## 1. Introduction: The Problem of Embodied Perception in Virtual Environments The question of how human beings perceive, inhabit, and ultimately constitute their worlds has long occupied phenomenological inquiry; yet the advent of virtual reality technologies has lent this question a renewed urgency that neither Merleau-Ponty nor his predecessors could have fully anticipated. When we consider the nature of embodied perception—the manner in which a living body orients itself within a surrounding environment, receives sensory impressions, and integrates them into a coherent field of experience—we encounter a profound philosophical tension. Virtual reality systems promise to replicate or simulate the conditions of perceptual life, generating what Thomas Metzinger describes as a complex phenomenal quality centered on identification, self-location, and temporal presence. Specifically, Metzinger argues that "'Presence' is a complex phenomenal quality, the three major dimensions of which are identification (i.e., being present as a self), self-location in a temporal frame of reference (i.e., being present as a self now, in this very moment), and self-location in space" (Metzinger, *Why Is Virtual Reality Interesting for Philosophers*, pp. 2–3). This tripartite structure of presence—selfhood, temporal immediacy, and spatial orientation—mirrors with remarkable precision the very coordinates that phenomenology has always taken to be constitutive of embodied perception. Thus the philosophical question becomes not merely whether virtual reality can reproduce perceptual experience but whether the conceptual apparatus inherited from Merleau-Ponty and his interlocutors can adequately account for the kinds of embodied engagement that virtual environments elicit. Indeed, the challenge of virtual reality is not simply technological but ontological; it demands that we revisit fundamental distinctions between the real and the virtual, the perceived and the imagined, and the body as lived and the body as represented. To pursue this inquiry, we must draw upon the resources of Heidegger's existential analytic, Aristotle's account of perception and phantasia, and contemporary philosophical reflections on virtual environments—resources that, taken together, illuminate the deep structural conditions under which embodied perception operates, whether in the so-called natural world or in digitally mediated spaces. The national park of phenomenological thought, as it were, must be explored anew if we are to protect our understanding of perception from both naive realism and uncritical technological enthusiasm. Accordingly, this section proceeds in three movements: first, an examination of the phenomenological structure of perception as world-disclosure; second, an analysis of how virtual reality problematizes the potentiality–actuality framework of perception; and third, a consideration of the role of phantasia and imagination in bridging embodied and virtual experience. 

## 2. Phenomenological Structures of Perception: World-Disclosure and Being-in-the-World The phenomenological tradition, from Husserl through Merleau-Ponty, insists that perception is never a passive reception of neutral data; it is always already structured by the perceiver's bodily orientation, practical concerns, and existential situation. Heidegger articulates this insight with particular force in his analysis of Dasein's Being-in-the-world, where perception is understood not as a detached contemplation of present-at-hand objects but as a mode of engaged encounter with ready-to-hand equipment. (Heidegger 2009, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. [PAGE NEEDED]), "When we have to do with anything, the mere seeing of the Things which are closest to us bears in itself the structure of interpretation, and in so primordial a manner that just to grasp something free, as it were, of the 'as', requires a certain readjustment" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 191). This passage is decisive for our inquiry, because it reveals that perception is always interpretive—always structured by the hermeneutical "as"—and that any attempt to isolate a layer of pure sensation beneath interpretive engagement represents a derivative abstraction rather than a primordial given. The perceiver does not first see a colorless shape and subsequently identify it as a door or a house; the door shows itself as a door within the practical context of dwelling, movement, and care. Similarly, Heidegger's early lectures on Aristotle elaborate this point by situating perception within the broader structure of life and world. In his reading of Aristotle's *De Anima*, (Heidegger 2009, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. [PAGE NEEDED]) thinking itself cannot be severed from the living body's engagement with the world: "If, however, even νοεῖν [the thorough deliberating of a matter, when I do not have it perceptually present] is something like a φαντασία or cannot be without φαντασία, then thinking too could not be without standing in the context of the entire life of a human being" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 149). Hence, even the most abstract cognitive operations remain tethered to the body's worldly situatedness through phantasia—the making-present-to-itself of the world, as Heidegger translates it. This is significant for the problem of virtual reality because any environment that successfully engages the perceiver's phantasia—that is, the capacity for making-present—will thereby engage the full structure of embodied cognition, regardless of whether the stimuli originate from physical or digital sources. The notion that perception is essentially world-disclosive rather than merely receptive finds further support in the existential concept of Dasein's fallenness. (Heidegger 2009, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. [PAGE NEEDED]) "Dasein has, in the first instance, fallen away from itself as an authentic potentiality for Being its Self, and has fallen into the 'world'" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 218–220). This absorption in the world—guided by idle talk, curiosity, and ambiguity—constitutes the everyday mode of perceptual engagement. Accordingly, if virtual reality environments can reproduce the conditions of such absorption, they do not merely simulate perception but instantiate a genuine mode of Being-in-the-world, albeit one mediated by technological apparatus. The question then becomes whether the virtual world possesses the ontological weight to sustain authentic or inauthentic engagement, or whether it remains a fundamentally different kind of disclosure. 

## 3. Potentiality and Actuality in Perception: The δύναμις/ἐνέργεια Framework The mandatory theoretical synthesis between Aristotle's δύναμις/ἐνέργεια framework and Heidegger's reading of this framework in his *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* proves indispensable for understanding how virtual reality engages embodied perception. In the Aristotelian account, perception involves a transition from potentiality to actuality: the sense organ possesses the capacity (δύναμις) to receive sensible forms, and this capacity is actualized (ἐνέργεια) through the agency of an external sensible object. Sensible qualities exist potentially below the threshold of perception and become actual only when encountered by an appropriately disposed perceiver. Heidegger's reading of ἐνέργεια deepens this picture considerably; as the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* note, Heidegger translates key Aristotelian terms in ways that foreground their existential significance: specifically, αἴσθησις becomes not "sensation" but "attending to" the world in the sense of having-it-there, and ἐνέργεια becomes not "reality" but "being-at-work" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 75–77). This reinterpretation transforms perception from a passive mirroring of external states into an active, engaged attending-to—a being-at-work that constitutes the perceiver's relation to the world. Heidegger's lectures on Aristotle confirm this interpretation by elaborating the meaning of ἐνέργεια in terms that bridge the ancient and the phenomenological: "It happened that the name ἐνέργεια also came to be carried over to the other, to what contrasted with determinations of movement; ἐνέργεια, specifically, is related in itself πρὸς τὴν ἐντελέχειαν" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 213–215). Thus ἐνέργεια is not merely the opposite of potentiality but is intrinsically related to ἐντελέχεια—the being-at-its-end, the state of completeness toward which every actualization strives. This framework illuminates the virtual reality problem in a crucial way: if perception is fundamentally the actualization of a capacity through engagement with a world, then the question of whether virtual stimuli can genuinely actualize perceptual capacities becomes a question about whether the virtual possesses sufficient reality to serve as a genuine efficient cause. The tension identified in the conceptual nodes—between κίνησις as incomplete actuality and ἐνέργεια as completed actuality—resurfaces here with force. Virtual reality environments may perpetually sustain perception in the mode of incomplete actuality, maintaining the user in a state of engaged potentiality without ever achieving the closure that characterizes full perceptual presence in a physical environment. Metzinger's philosophical analysis of virtual reality bears directly on this point; the very concept of virtual reality encompasses a paradox, noting that "virtual reality encompasses virtual unreality" (Metzinger, *Why Is Virtual Reality Interesting for Philosophers*, pp. 1–2). This the virtual is not simply a diminished copy of the real but contains within itself the possibility of unreality—a mode of appearance that discloses itself precisely as not fully actual. Similarly, a being that has evolved into what Metzinger calls "a biological organism that has evolved into a 'transreality interaction platform' by enabling causal interactions across physical and virtual reality" inhabits a condition where the boundary between potentiality and actuality is perpetually renegotiated (Metzinger, *Why Is Virtual Reality Interesting for Philosophers*, pp. 14–15). The perceiver in virtual reality is thus suspended between δύναμις and ἐνέργεια in a way that has no precise analogue in pre-technological experience. 

## 4. Phantasia, Imagination, and the Virtual Image The role of phantasia in mediating between perception and thought provides a further lens through which to examine virtual reality's phenomenological significance. Aristotle's account, as interpreted by both ancient commentators and modern scholars, positions phantasia as neither pure perception nor pure thought but as an intermediate capacity that enables the making-present of absent objects. Dorothea Frede observes that Aristotle characterizes phantasiai as somehow non-committing: "we speak of appearance when we do not have clear perception" and "phantasiai are somehow non-committing, in that we look at them 'as in a picture'" (Frede, *The Cognitive Role of Phantasia in Aristotle*, p. 3). This description of phantasia as a looking-at-something-as-in-a-picture is remarkably apposite for virtual reality, where the user engages with images that present themselves with perceptual vividness yet remain ontologically distinct from the physical objects they depict. Indeed, the conceptual tension identified in the corpus—between νοῦς as impassible and unmixed, and the φάντασμα upon which thought necessarily depends—becomes especially acute in virtual environments. If the soul never thinks without an image, as Aristotle maintains, then virtual images may serve the same cognitive function as images derived from physical perception; the question is whether the phantasma generated by virtual stimuli possesses the same cognitive reliability as one derived from αἴσθησις. Papachristou notes the complexity of translating phantasia, observing that "even though the Greek word 'phantasia' is usually translated it as 'imagination', I prefer to leave the word untranslated" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 9–10). This scholarly caution reminds us that phantasia is not mere imagination in the modern sense but a fundamental cognitive capacity that straddles the boundary between perception and thought—precisely the boundary that virtual reality technologies exploit and destabilize. The rhetorical dimension of this problem should not be overlooked. Kenneth Burke's analysis of how imagination mediates between reason and appetite offers a useful parallel; Burke discusses the classical view that rhetoric must "recommend the dictates of reason to imagination, in order to excite the appetite and will" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96). The virtual reality designer, like the rhetor, must engage the user's imagination in ways that mobilize desire, attention, and action; the persuasive power of virtual environments lies precisely in their capacity to present images that function as if they were perceptions, thereby activating the same affective and cognitive responses that genuine perceptual encounters elicit. Similarly, Rickert's work on ambient rhetoric connects this insight to Heidegger's ontology, suggesting that situated being-in-the-world is disclosed through "a primordial rhetorical making-known of our situated being-in-the-world" (Rickert 2013, p. [PAGE NEEDED]). Virtual reality, on this account, constitutes a rhetorical technology—a means of disclosing worlds through the manipulation of embodied perception. 

## 5. Conclusion: Toward a Phenomenology of Virtual Embodiment The analysis developed across the preceding sections reveals that the relationship between virtual reality and embodied perception is neither one of simple opposition nor of straightforward equivalence. Rather, virtual environments engage the same phenomenological structures—world-disclosure, interpretive perception, the actualization of perceptual capacities, and the mediating function of phantasia—that characterize embodied experience in the physical world, yet they do so under conditions of fundamental ontological instability. The tripartite structure of presence that Metzinger identifies—selfhood, temporal immediacy, and spatial location—maps onto the coordinates of Heidegger's Being-in-the-world and Aristotle's account of αἴσθησις as an attending-to-the-world in the mode of having-it-there. Yet virtual reality simultaneously suspends these structures by introducing a layer of virtual unreality that prevents the full actualization of perceptual potentiality. The user of virtual reality technology thus inhabits a distinctive ontological position: absorbed in a world that discloses itself with perceptual vividness, yet aware—at some level of phenomenal experience—that this world lacks the causal density of physical reality. The tension between κίνησις as incomplete actuality and ἐνέργεια as completed actuality thus finds its contemporary expression in the virtual perceiver's condition. To protect our understanding of embodied perception from both technological determinism and phenomenological nostalgia, we must recognize that virtual reality does not simply replicate or replace embodied perception; it transforms it, extending the scope of phantasia and world-disclosure into domains that Aristotle and Heidegger could not have foreseen but whose philosophical structures they nonetheless illuminated with remarkable prescience. --- # VALIDATION APPENDIX ## Claim Map | # | Claim | Source | Page(s) |
