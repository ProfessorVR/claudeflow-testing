# Phantasia and the Disclosure of Situated Environments: An Aristotelian-Rhetorical Investigation

## 1. Phantasia Aisthesis Perception Imagination φαντασία

The relationship between phantasia (φαντασία) and aisthesis (αἴσθησις) constitutes one of the most fundamental and contested sites in Aristotle's philosophical psychology, for it is precisely through the interplay of perception and imagination that the situated subject first encounters its environment as meaningful. Aristotle defines the soul as "the first actuality of a natural organic body," thereby establishing that any faculty belonging to the soul—including both perception and imagination—must be understood as the living body's active engagement with the surrounding world rather than as a disembodied mental operation (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[3]. This definition, drawn from *De Anima* II.1, lays the groundwork for understanding how phantasia emerges from perception and yet differs from it in decisive ways. As Hawhee observes, "phantasia is different from either perceiving or thinking, though it is not found without perception, nor judgment without it" (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 4–6)[4]. The formulation is crucial: phantasia depends causally upon aisthesis—it cannot arise in a creature utterly devoid of perceptual experience—yet it is not identical to perception, for it can persist in the absence of the perceived object and can represent what is not currently present to the senses. Indeed, the contrast between phantasia and aisthesis reveals something essential about how environments are disclosed to a situated subject. Perception delivers the immediate sensory content of the world—colors, sounds, textures—while phantasia transforms that content into something that can be retained, combined, and re-presented. This transformation is not merely derivative or secondary; it is the condition under which the perceptual world becomes a world of significance. Hawhee draws attention to Aristotle's own (somewhat dubious) etymology: "Phantasia is derived from phaos (light)," Aristotle remarks, "because without light it is impossible to see" (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 4–6)[5]. The analogy to light is telling, for just as light makes visible what would otherwise remain hidden, phantasia illuminates the perceptual field, rendering it available for further cognitive and practical engagement. Thus, phantasia does not simply copy aisthesis; it discloses the environment in a new register—one in which the absent can appear alongside the present, and in which the subject's own desires, memories, and judgments begin to shape what is seen. Burke, in his treatment of imagination within the rhetorical tradition, suggests that the very term "imagination" is irreducibly entangled with psychological and extraliterary origins, such that one cannot maintain categorical boundaries separating literary or rhetorical analysis from the operations of the perceiving mind (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 97–99)[6]. The question of whether phantasia is voluntarily controlled or involuntarily triggered—a question that will recur throughout this investigation—already surfaces here, for Aristotle notes that phantasia can arise even in dreams, when perception is inactive. The grades of phantasia, from the sensory (αἰσθητική) to the deliberative (βουλευτική), will be addressed more fully in subsequent sections; for now, it suffices to note that the foundational relationship between phantasia and aisthesis sets the stage for understanding how the world is not merely perceived but imaginatively constituted for the embodied subject. 

## 2. Phantasma Phantasia Imagination Image φαντασία φάντασμα

If phantasia names the capacity or faculty, then phantasma (φάντασμα) names its product—the image or appearance that persists after the perceptual encounter has ceased, and through which the absent object continues to exercise its hold upon the soul. The distinction between phantasia and phantasma is crucial for understanding how environments are structured for the situated subject, for it is through the phantasma that the subject can engage with what is no longer present, and indeed with what has never been present in immediate perception. Aristotle's account in *De Anima* III.3 establishes that phantasia is a specific kind of motion (κίνησις) generated by actual sensation, and the phantasma is the residual trace of that motion—an image that retains the qualitative character of the original perception while being detached from its immediate causal source (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[7]. Even though the Greek word phantasia is usually translated as "imagination," Papachristou prefers to leave the word untranslated, and similarly notes that doing so preserves the distinctive Aristotelian nuance that is lost when phantasia is assimilated to modern conceptions of the imagination as a purely subjective or creative faculty (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 9–10)[8]. The phantasma, accordingly, is not a mere copy of perception but a mediating structure through which the subject's relationship to the environment is sustained across time. Burke's rhetorical account illuminates this point from a complementary angle: he observes that imagination serves to "recommend the dictates of reason to imagination, in order to excite the appetite and will" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[9]. This Baconian formulation, which Burke adapts for his own rhetorical purposes, underscores the phantasma's role as an intermediary between rational judgment and the appetitive dimension of the soul; the image does not merely represent the world but solicits the subject's engagement with it. Subsequently, Hawhee's analysis of Aristotle's rhetorical vision reinforces this intermediary function by noting that through phantasia, "the less-than-visible becomes visible" (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 4–6)[10]. The phantasma thus operates at the threshold between perception and thought, between presence and absence, between the sensory and the deliberative grades of phantasia. Whether the phantasma is subject to voluntary control—as in deliberate recollection—or arises involuntarily—as in dreams or unbidden images—marks one of the unresolved tensions within Aristotle's account, a tension that we shall address more fully in subsequent sections concerned with the relation of phantasia to doxa and to desire. 

## 3. Phantasia Mneme Imagination Memory φαντασία

The relationship between phantasia and mneme (μνήμη, memory) represents a further unfolding of the image's capacity to structure the situated subject's experience of time and environment. Memory, for Aristotle, is not a self-contained faculty operating independently of phantasia; rather, it depends upon the phantasma as the vehicle through which past perceptual encounters are retained and subsequently made available for recollection. The phantasma serves as what we might call the temporal bridge between the original perceptual event and the subject's present engagement with its world, and it is precisely this bridging function that allows the environment to be disclosed not merely as what is now present but as what has been and what might yet be. Papachristou's analysis of the grades of phantasia is instructive here, for the retention of phantasmata is essential to the transition from sensory phantasia (αἰσθητική φαντασία), which remains close to immediate perception, to deliberative phantasia (βουλευτική φαντασία), which requires the subject to compare, combine, and evaluate images drawn from different temporal contexts (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[11]. Indeed, the mnemonic function of phantasia reveals a constitutive tension that Aristotle never fully resolves: phantasia is causally derived from actual sensation, as *De Anima* III.3 insists, yet its functional autonomy in the domain of memory allows the subject to operate upon images that are no longer tethered to any present perceptual stimulus. Nussbaum captures this point when she observes that "all men strive for the apparent good (phainomenon agathon): but no one is in control of the appearing (phantasia): the way the end appears (phainetai) to someone depends on what sort of a man he is" (Nussbaum, *The Role of Phantasia in Aristotle's Explanation of Action*, pp. 31–33)[12]. This formulation, drawn from the *Nicomachean Ethics*, underscores that the phantasmata retained in memory are not neutral representations but are shaped by the subject's character, habituation, and practical orientation. Hence, the environment disclosed through memory is always already an interpreted environment—a world colored by the subject's past experiences and dispositions. Burke's rhetorical framework similarly emphasizes that imagination is altered by "religion, opinion, and apprehension," and that the image-making faculty can be brought to the reinforcement of all three (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[13]. The convergence of these perspectives—Aristotelian, rhetorical, and ethical—suggests that the relationship between phantasia and mneme is not merely cognitive but deeply practical, shaping how the subject inhabits and navigates a temporally extended environment. 

## 4. Phantasia Doxa Imagination φαντασία

The relationship between phantasia and doxa (δόξα, opinion or belief) constitutes one of the most philosophically charged questions in Aristotle's *De Anima*, for it touches directly upon the epistemic status of the image and upon the question of whether the imaginative faculty can generate its own form of truth or falsity independent of perceptual accuracy. Aristotle insists, in *De Anima* III.3, that phantasia is not identical to doxa, for opinion involves conviction (πίστις) and logos, whereas phantasia can present images that the subject recognizes as false—as when one imagines a terrifying scene while knowing oneself to be safe (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[14]. This distinction is essential, because it means that the disclosure of the environment through phantasia is not constrained by the subject's rational assent in the same way that doxa is; the image can disclose a world that is, in a certain sense, at odds with what the subject believes to be the case. Specifically, this tension between phantasia and doxa has far-reaching implications for rhetorical theory. Gonzalez, analyzing the function of phantasia in *Rhetoric* III.1, notes the complex interplay of opinion and appearance in Aristotle's account, observing that doxa is "the proper rendering" at several key passages in the *Rhetoric* where the audience's response to a speaker depends not merely on the logical structure of the argument but on the way the subject-matter appears (φαίνεται) to them (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 14–15)[15]. In other words, a rhetorical demonstration "can be compelling to one, yet fail to convince another" precisely because the phantasia through which the audience receives the argument is shaped by prior dispositions, character, and habituated ways of seeing (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 14–15)[16]. Similarly, Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's rhetoric emphasizes that the aim of rhetorical λόγος is to construct a view—a δόξα—within the listener, and that this process is inseparable from the way the world appears through the listener's attunement and phantasia (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 107)[17]. Thus, phantasia and doxa are neither identical nor wholly independent; they exist in a dynamic relationship in which the image can both support and subvert the subject's settled opinions, disclosing the environment in ways that are voluntarily shaped in deliberative phantasia yet often involuntarily imposed in the sensory grade. 

## 5. Phantasia Pathos Imagination Emotion φαντασία πάθος / πάθη

The entanglement of phantasia with pathos (πάθος, emotion or affect) constitutes yet another dimension through which the situated subject's environment is disclosed—not as a neutral field of objects, but as a world suffused with significance, urgency, and evaluative weight. Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is perhaps the locus classicus for this entanglement, for it is there that he details how the emotions of the audience are aroused, modified, and directed through the speaker's capacity to bring things "before the eyes" (πρὸ ὀμμάτων ποιεῖν), a process that relies essentially upon the audience's phantasia. 5). The emotional force of rhetoric, on this account, depends not merely on the propositional content of the argument but on the vividness and immediacy with which the phantasma is made to appear in the audience's imagination. Hence, the relationship between phantasia and pathos reveals that the affective dimension of human existence is not an irrational supplement to cognition but is itself a mode of disclosure—a way in which the world shows up as mattering. Heidegger's later interpretation of the πάθη in his lectures on Aristotle supports this reading; he observes that the traditional tendency has been to partition affects into "bodily states and states of the soul—states that stand in some connection," but that such a partition distorts the phenomenon by severing what is existentially unified (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 134)[18]. The affects, Heidegger insists, must be understood as modes of being-in-the-world, not as internal psychological states that happen to have bodily correlates. This resonates with Burke's observation that imagination can be brought to the reinforcement of religion, opinion, and apprehension—and that the will itself is altered thereby (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[19]. Accordingly, the grades of phantasia—sensory and deliberative—play distinct roles in the generation of pathos: sensory phantasia produces immediate, vivid images that arouse fear, pity, or anger, while deliberative phantasia enables the subject to project future scenarios and thereby experience emotions directed at what has not yet occurred. Whether such emotional phantasmata are voluntarily summoned or involuntarily aroused remains a productive tension in Aristotle's account, one that has significant consequences for rhetorical theory and ethical practice alike. 

## 6. Phantasia Taking Imagination φαντασία

The concept of "taking-as" (φαίνεσθαι ὡς) opens a critical dimension of phantasia that moves beyond its role as a mere image-repository and reveals it as a mode of interpretive disclosure. To perceive something as something—to see the flickering light as a signal of the enemy's approach, or to hear the sound as a warning—requires a capacity that is neither pure perception nor pure judgment but something that mediates between them. This mediating function is precisely what Aristotle attributes to phantasia, and it is here that the Aristotelian account most clearly converges with Heidegger's existential analysis of the "as-structure" (Als-Struktur)[1] of understanding. The question that arises is whether we ever perceive anything without already taking it as something; and if not, then phantasia is not a secondary faculty layered upon perception but is constitutive of perceptual experience itself. Hawhee's reading of Aristotle's rhetorical vision foregrounds this point by observing that phantasia enables suasion precisely because it allows the audience to take what is described in discourse as if it were present to the senses: "[I call things] in accordance with reason what people long for on the basis of persuasion; for they desire to see and possess many things after hearing about them" (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 7–9)[20]. The "taking-as" function of phantasia is thus deeply implicated in the rhetorical process, for it is through this capacity that the speaker's words are transformed into vivid appearances in the listener's mind. Rickert's account of ambient rhetoric amplifies this point by suggesting that disclosure is never the work of a sovereign subject but emerges from the immersive interplay of body, environment, and language; indeed, he argues that religion, art, and other forms of knowledge "are already in deep relation to and disclosive for our way of being, for the way we dwell, for ethos" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 284–286)[21]. This observation aligns with Aristotle's insistence that phantasia is shaped by the character of the perceiver—that the way the end appears (φαίνεται) to someone depends on what sort of person he is (Nussbaum, *The Role of Phantasia in Aristotle's Explanation of Action*, pp. 31–33)[22]. Specifically, the distinction between sensory and deliberative phantasia is relevant here: sensory phantasia enables the immediate "taking-as" that structures perceptual experience, while deliberative phantasia enables a more reflective and voluntarily controlled form of interpretation, in which the subject weighs alternative ways of construing a situation. The relationship of this taking-as function to doxa remains complex; for while doxa involves rational commitment, phantasia's taking-as can operate beneath the threshold of explicit belief, disclosing the environment in ways that the subject may neither endorse nor be fully aware of. 

## 7. Orexis Phantasia Imagination Desire φαντασία ὄρεξις

The relationship between phantasia and orexis (ὄρεξις, desire or appetite) is central to Aristotle's explanation of animal and human action, for it is through the conjunction of image and desire that the soul is moved to act upon its environment. Aristotle's account in *De Anima* III.9–10 establishes that no movement occurs without both phantasia and orexis: the image presents something as desirable or avoidable, and desire supplies the motive force that translates the image into action. Nussbaum's careful analysis of this relationship emphasizes that phantasia is not merely an incidental accompaniment to desire but is functionally constitutive of it; as she observes, drawing upon the *Nicomachean Ethics*, "all men strive for the apparent good (phainomenon agathon): but no one is in control of the appearing (phantasia)" (Nussbaum, *The Role of Phantasia in Aristotle's Explanation of Action*, pp. 31–33)[23]. The implication is striking: the object of desire is not the good as such, but the good as it appears through phantasia—that is, the good as imaginatively constituted by the subject's prior experience, character, and habituation. This entanglement of desire and image has profound consequences for rhetorical theory. Hawhee's analysis of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* makes this explicit by noting that phantasia's relationship to suasion is inseparable from its relationship to desire, for the audience is moved not merely by the logical force of an argument but by the vividness with which the speaker makes the desirable or fearful object appear before the mind's eye (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 7–9)[24]. Burke similarly observes that imagination operates to "excite the appetite and will," thereby functioning as the bridge between rational deliberation and practical motivation (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[25]. Accordingly, the grades of phantasia play distinct roles in the structure of desire: sensory phantasia generates immediate appetitive responses to present objects, while deliberative phantasia enables the subject to project future goods and to weigh competing desires against one another. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle captures this temporal and practical dimension by noting that πρᾶξις, as the practical life, is oriented toward the ἀγαθόν as the end that is pursued "at each moment in the definite situation" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 221)[26]. The voluntariness of phantasia in the context of desire is thus a matter of degree: while sensory phantasia may present objects of desire involuntarily, deliberative phantasia allows the practically wise subject to exercise a measure of control over which images are entertained and how they are evaluated—though, as Nussbaum emphasizes, this control is never absolute. 

## 8. Phantasia Habituated Phantasia Imagination φαντασία

The notion of habituated phantasia introduces a crucial developmental and ethical dimension into Aristotle's account of imagination, for it suggests that the capacity to form, retain, and evaluate phantasmata is not fixed but is shaped by the subject's practices, training, and habituation over time. This idea is latent in Aristotle's broader ethical framework, where virtue is understood as a hexis (ἕξις, stable disposition) acquired through repeated practice, and it receives explicit articulation in the relationship between phantasia and practical wisdom (φρόνησις). Nussbaum's analysis highlights the way in which phantasia is conditioned by character: "the way the end appears (phainetai) to someone depends on what sort of a man he is" (Nussbaum, *The Role of Phantasia in Aristotle's Explanation of Action*, pp. 31–33)[27]. Thus, the habituated subject does not merely perceive the world differently from the unhabituated one; the very images that arise in the soul—the phantasmata through which the environment is disclosed—are shaped by the subject's accumulated experience and training. Papachristou's tripartite analysis of the grades of phantasia provides a framework for understanding this habituating process: the transition from sensory phantasia to deliberative phantasia is not an automatic cognitive development but requires the cultivation of practical and intellectual capacities that allow the subject to combine, compare, and evaluate images in increasingly sophisticated ways (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[28]. Hawhee's account of Aristotle's rhetorical vision reinforces this developmental perspective by emphasizing that the capacity to "see" through discourse—to be moved by the speaker's evocation of vivid images—is itself a trained capacity, one that the experienced auditor possesses to a greater degree than the naive listener (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 4–6)[29]. Similarly, Burke's treatment of imagination within the rhetorical tradition suggests that the power of the image to move the will is not merely a natural endowment but is cultivated through the subject's engagement with religion, opinion, and apprehension (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[30]. The implications for rhetorical theory are significant: if phantasia is habituated, then the persuasive efficacy of discourse depends not only on the speaker's skill but on the audience's imaginative training. The relation of habituated phantasia to doxa is likewise complex, for a well-habituated phantasia may generate images that align with true belief, while a poorly habituated one may produce phantasmata that systematically distort the subject's engagement with the environment. Whether the habituating of phantasia is a voluntary process or one that occurs largely beneath the threshold of conscious control remains a productive question that Aristotle's text invites but does not conclusively answer. 

## 9. Aristotle's *Metaphysics* offers the foundational insight that being is spoken of in many ways, and that the categories through which we articulate being correspond to the ways in which beings show themselves in our experience. Thus, substance, quantity, quality, and the other categories are not arbitrary classifications but modes of disclosure: ways in which the world appears to the knowing subject (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 116)[31]. The unity of being and unity, Aristotle argues, is clear "from the fact that it follows the categories in as many ways, and is not comprised within any category," and that "to be one is just to be a particular thing" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 116)[32]. This formulation establishes that the disclosure of beings is always the disclosure of particular beings in particular categorial modes—a point that resonates with Heidegger's later insistence that disclosure is never abstract but always situated. Indeed, Heidegger's reading of Aristotle in the *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* explicitly connects the disclosive function of λόγος to the categories, observing that "the λόγοι of the categories are such that they address the beings of the surrounding world with regard to the possibility of their being-there" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 221)[33]. This is not merely an epistemological claim but an ontological one: the categories disclose the world as a world of practical concern, a world in which things are encountered as available, usable, and significant for the subject's ongoing activity. Aristotle's *Physics* reinforces this point by showing that the coming-to-be of any determinate being—whether a house, a statue, or any shaped object—proceeds "from certain things in a certain state of separation instead of conjunction" and involves both order and composition (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 8–10)[34]. The disclosure of beings, then, is always a disclosure of beings in their structured, ordered, and composed character—not as bare matter but as formed, meaningful entities. For Heidegger, this Aristotelian insight anticipates the existential-analytic claim that Dasein's being-in-the-world is fundamentally disclosive, and that the structures through which beings appear—including phantasia—are not subjective impositions upon a neutral reality but are the very conditions under which beings can show themselves at all. The thesis that "the soul is in a way all existing things" (*De Anima* 432a1–3)[2] thus marks a point of profound convergence between Aristotle and Heidegger: the soul's capacity to become all things through perception, phantasia, and thought is the condition of the world's disclosure (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, pp. 16–18)[35]. 

## 10. Aristotle Phantasia Imagination φαντασία

We arrive, finally, at a synthetic consideration of Aristotle's account of phantasia as a whole—a faculty that, as the foregoing analysis has shown, is irreducible to any single cognitive function and serves instead as the nexus through which perception, memory, desire, emotion, belief, and practical deliberation are integrated into a unified disclosure of the situated environment. Papachristou's tripartite analysis of the grades of phantasia—sensory (αἰσθητική), combined or retentive, and deliberative (βουλευτική)—provides the structural backbone for this synthesis, for it reveals that phantasia is not a static capacity but a dynamic and developmentally articulated process through which the subject's engagement with the world becomes progressively more complex and reflective (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[36]. The sensory grade is tied closely to aisthesis and produces phantasmata that retain the qualitative character of immediate perception; the deliberative grade enables the subject to project future possibilities, combine images from different temporal contexts, and evaluate alternative courses of action—capacities that are essential for practical wisdom and rhetorical engagement alike. Aristotle's *Metaphysics* provides the ontological context for understanding this faculty, for the categories through which beings are disclosed—substance, quality, quantity, and the rest—are precisely the modes in which phantasia presents its objects. The potentiality that characterizes matter is, in Aristotle's formulation, "universal and indefinite," while "the actuality, being definite, deals with a definite object—being a 'this', it deals with a 'this'" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 168–169)[37]. Phantasia, accordingly, mediates between the indeterminate potentiality of sensory experience and the determinate actuality of practical judgment; it is the faculty through which the world's indefinite richness is structured into specific, actionable images. The *Physics* further illuminates this mediating function by establishing that every process of coming-to-be involves both order and composition—a principle that applies "equally to the production of a house, a statue, or anything else" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 8–10)[38]. Phantasia, we might say, performs an analogous composing function within the soul, gathering the raw materials of perception into structured images that orient the subject's practical engagement. As Rickert argues, rhetoric itself must be understood as disclosure—as the always-ongoing process through which the world shows up as mattering for the situated subject—and it is precisely through phantasia that this disclosive function is realized at the level of the individual soul (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 284–286)[39]. Thus, Aristotle's account of phantasia, far from being a marginal topic in the philosophy of mind, stands at the center of any adequate understanding of how environments are disclosed and structured for the embodied, desiring, and practically engaged subject. ---
