# Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Perception: Toward a Heideggerian Recovery of Kinēsis and Aisthēsis

## 1. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding

Any attempt to retrieve Aristotle's understanding of motion, time, and perception must first reckon with the manner in which these concepts have been sedimented by centuries of philosophical translation and reinterpretation, a process that has often obscured the originary Greek sense of the phenomena in question. The critical project of recovering what Aristotle meant—rather than what subsequent traditions have taken him to mean—demands that we attend not merely to the logical structure of his categories but to the lived, world-disclosing character of the experiences they name. As Rickert observes, the manner in which human beings comport themselves toward the world is enacted less as mastery or detached cognition than as "a 'letting be' that spares, preserves, and cultivates human beings in the world" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric- The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, pp. 53–54). This orientation, while explicitly Heideggerian in provenance, illuminates the broader stakes of rereading Aristotle: we are concerned not with static taxonomies of being but with the dynamic, situated, and affectively attuned character of existence. As the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* observe, "Passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Thus the Aristotelian categories of kinēsis, chronos, and aisthēsis are not merely theoretical abstractions but are modes of being-in-the-world, a point we must hold in view as we proceed. ## 2. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding (Continued)

The retrieval of Aristotle's understanding requires further precision regarding the ontological status of kinēsis as Heidegger reads it. In the conventional philosophical inheritance, motion is treated as a property of physical bodies, a change of place or quality that can be measured and predicted. Yet the Heideggerian recovery of Aristotle insists upon a more radical thesis: kinēsis is not merely something that happens to beings but constitutes a fundamental way that Dasein is. Indeed, "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). This claim transforms the entire landscape of Aristotelian interpretation, for it means that motion is not posterior to substance but is constitutive of the very being of that which exists. Such a reading resonates with Rickert's broader ambient framework, in which truth and reality are not reducible to social agonism or rhetorical constructionism alone; rather, the world retains an ontological thickness that exceeds merely discursive determination (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric- The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, p. 309). To articulate Aristotle's understanding, accordingly, is to resist the reduction of his thought either to a naive realism or to a thoroughgoing constructivism. Instead, we must trace how motion, time, and perception interweave as co-constitutive dimensions of the being that is there—Dasein—and, by extension, of every living being that moves, perceives, and dwells within a temporal horizon. ## 3. Aristotle on Motion

Aristotle's account of motion is anchored in a cosmological and metaphysical framework that distinguishes between kinds of change—generation, destruction, alteration, and locomotion—and seeks their ultimate origin. In the *Movement of Animals*, Aristotle establishes the principle that "the origin of other motions is that which moves itself, and that the origin of this is the immovable, and that the prime mover must of necessity be immovable" (Aristotle, *Movement of Animals*, pp. 1–4). This hierarchy of movers situates all particular motions within a causal chain that terminates in an unmoved source, a principle that will later be taken up and reinterpreted by Heidegger. At the level of living beings, motion is not merely physical displacement but is intimately bound to the soul's capacities of desire and aversion. As Heidegger's lectures on Aristotle emphasize, "Disposition, ἡδονή, has the two possibilities of δίωξις and φυγή, 'going-toward' the συμφέρον and 'shrinking-back before it.' Δίωξις and φυγή are basic ways of being moved for ψυχή, for being-there" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 205–206). Motion, then, is always already affectively charged; it is oriented by what attracts or repels. The *Metaphysics* further complicates this picture by insisting that any substance immune from change and in virtue of itself having attained the best must be considered an end, such that motion in the cosmos is teleologically structured toward the unmoved (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 148–149). Thus, for Aristotle, motion is at once physical, psychical, and cosmological—a phenomenon irreducible to any single register. ## 4. Aristotle on Time

Time, for Aristotle, is not an independent container within which events occur but is inextricably bound to motion and perception. The Aristotelian  we perceive time only insofar as we perceive change, and that the measurement of time depends upon the identification of determinate units marked out by regular motions. As Bowin explains, "we measure time by counting units of time (e.g., days) which are determinate because they are marked out by motions that are determinate in time (e.g., celestial motions)" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). Counting, in this schema, is the most exact form of measurement because it involves a measure that is absolutely indivisible—a standard not relative to the variability of sensory experience but grounded in the regularity of celestial phenomena (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). Yet this cosmological anchoring of temporal measurement does not exhaust the Aristotelian analysis; the perception of time also involves a subjective dimension, what later commentators would call the specious present. Bowin notes that the specious present is understood as "the short duration of which we are immediately and incessantly sensible" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 17–19). The interplay between objective celestial motion and the lived immediacy of temporal awareness constitutes the essential tension in Aristotle's account, a tension that will later be radicalized by Heidegger's existential analytic. Furthermore, the *Metaphysics* reminds us that being has two forms—"it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157)—and this duality informs the temporal structure of beings that are always in the process of actualizing what they potentially are. ## 5. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The intimate relation between motion and time in Aristotle's thought cannot be overstated, for neither phenomenon is intelligible without the other. Time is the number of motion with respect to before and after, a definition that makes temporal awareness parasitic upon the perception of change. As Bowin demonstrates, the measurement of time proceeds through the counting of determinate motions, and the very determinacy of these motions is what makes time measurable at all (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). The philosophical implication is that time does not exist independently of beings in motion; rather, time is disclosed in and through the kinetic character of the world. This point gains additional force when read through the Heideggerian lens, where kinēsis is understood not merely as physical movement but as a fundamental way of being. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* emphasize that passions—pathe—are themselves both motions and causes of motions, altering the very judgments through which we apprehend reality (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). When Aristotle argues in the *Metaphysics* that "the good and the beautiful are different (for the former always implies conduct as its subject, while the beautiful is found also in motionless things)" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157), he opens a distinction between temporal, action-oriented being and that which transcends motion. Thus, the relation between motion and time is not merely quantitative but is layered with axiological and existential significance. ## 6. Aisthēsis and Temporal Awareness

The perception of time presupposes aisthēsis—sense perception—as its necessary ground, for without the capacity to register change, the before and after that define time would remain undisclosed. Aristotle's treatment of perception as a discriminating power whose exercise is one and undivided with the time of its operation reveals that temporal awareness is not a secondary cognitive overlay but is woven into the very fabric of sensory experience. Burke's reflections on the temporality of essence illuminate this point from a rhetorical direction, for he discusses "the puns of logical and temporal priority whereby the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). This interplay between logical and temporal  our perception of what something is cannot be extricated from our perception of when and how it unfolds. Indeed, rhetorical and perceptual temporality converge insofar as naming a thing—designating its moral or political character—already implicates a temporal ordering of experience. Burke's analysis of the "question-begging appellative" as a "fallacy of confusion" used "for the designation of objects belonging to the field of moral science" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 107–108)  aisthēsis is never purely neutral but is always shaped by the categories and dispositions through which we encounter what is. ## 7. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

Phantasia occupies a pivotal position in Aristotle's account of the soul, mediating between aisthēsis and noēsis and enabling the temporal unification of perceptual experience. As O'Gorman notes, in the *De Anima*, "phantasia is intimately connected to the sense of sight, preserving visual perceptions in a sort of after-image, and is itself a sort of psychē-sight, the source of mental images or visual impressions" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric- Lexis, Appearance, and the Epideictic Function of Discourse*, p. 4). This preservative function is crucial, for it means that phantasia bridges the gap between the passing moment of sense perception and the enduring image that enables comparison, recognition, and judgment. Without phantasia, temporal experience would be a mere succession of disconnected instants; with it, the soul gains the capacity to hold together what has been perceived, what is being perceived, and what is anticipated. Burke's discussion of the temporizing of essence resonates here, for the translation of logical identity into narrative sequence depends upon precisely this capacity to retain and project images across time (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Phantasia, moreover, is not reducible to sensation or judgment but is a distinct faculty, one whose independence Aristotle insists upon against Platonic conflation. As Bowin observes, phantasia is implicated not only in special and common perception but also in incidental perception, extending its reach across the full spectrum of sensory awareness (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 17–19). Thus phantasia serves as the temporal ligature of the soul, binding perception, memory, and anticipation into a coherent experiential unity. ## 8. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's reading of Aristotle is decisive for the present inquiry because it transforms the categories of motion, time, and perception from objects of theoretical analysis into existential structures of Dasein. Central to Heidegger's interpretation is the recovery of ousia not as a static, substantial presence but as a mode of being-there disclosed through motion and self-expression. As Heidegger argues, "definitio is ορισμός, ορισμός is a λόγος, a 'self-expression' about being-there as being" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 25–26). This reading insists that definition is not merely conceptual circumscription but participates in the self-showing of beings—a process inseparable from the kinetic and temporal horizons within which beings appear. Furthermore, Heidegger draws attention to how Aristotle's predecessors, including Democritus, were "borne by the matter itself" in their discoveries, suggesting that philosophical inquiry is itself a mode of being-moved (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 176–177). The *Metaphysics* supports this existential reading insofar as Aristotle himself acknowledges that being exists in two forms—as fulfillment and as matter—and that the good always implies conduct, that is, temporal, active engagement (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). In this light, Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle reveals that the Greek understanding of being was always already oriented by the phenomena of motion and time, and that the categories of the soul—aisthēsis, phantasia, pathe—are not internal mental states but modes of worldly disclosure through which beings come to presence. ---
