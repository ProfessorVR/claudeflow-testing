## 1. The Taking-As Structure and the Ground of Resonant Motion To ask what it means for a living being to take something *as* something—to encounter an object not merely as a stimulus but as significant, threatening, or desirable—is to raise the question of the ground upon which all resonant motion depends. Motion, in the Aristotelian sense pertinent here, is not reducible to spatial displacement alone; it encompasses qualitative alteration and the soul's own responsive stirring when confronted with what matters. Indeed, the very possibility of an organism moving toward or away from an encountered thing presupposes that the thing has already been taken as bearing some affective valence—as pleasant or painful, safe or dangerous. This taking-as structure cannot be adequately explained by perception alone, nor by judgment alone, for it operates at a level where sensation, affect, and incipient action converge before any deliberate inference occurs. As Gibson observes in *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, "the visual world is a kind of experience that does not correspond to anything, not any possible picture, not any motion picture, and not even any 'panoramic' motion picture" (pp. 231–232); thus the organism's awareness already exceeds mere sensory registration, cutting across modalities in a manner that implies a prior structuring of significance. Similarly, the situational character of affect—what Gross calls the environmental and temporal specificity of "situations" (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, pp. 155–157)[1]—suggests that resonant motion is never occasioned by bare physical events but always by events already taken as meaningful within a lived context. The metaphysical stakes of this claim are considerable: if emergent properties at one time are "partly causally determinative" of subsequent states (O'Connor and Wong, *The Metaphysics of Emergence*, pp. 11–12), then the affective taking-as that structures an organism's present encounter with its world is itself a genuine causal factor in the temporal unfolding of behavior and experience. Hence the ground of resonant motion is neither raw perception nor disembodied cognition but the phantasmatic medium through which significance is retained, reconfigured, and projected forward in time—a medium to which we now turn. 

## 2. Phantasia as the Retentive Medium of Resonant Affect If the ground of resonant motion lies in the taking-as structure elaborated above, then the faculty that sustains that structure across time—holding the affective imprint of what has been encountered so that it may inflect what is yet to come—must be phantasia. Phantasia, understood as a distinct capacity irreducible to either sensation or judgment, operates as itself a kind of motion in the soul, one generated by the activity of perception yet persisting beyond the perceptual moment. It is precisely this retentive and generative character that distinguishes phantasia from aisthesis: where perception delivers the present object in its immediate sensory specificity, phantasia preserves and reconfigures the affective residue of that encounter, rendering it available for desire, deliberation, and anticipatory orientation. Thus perception, interpretation, desire, action, and judgment all cluster around phantasia as around a mediating axis. Indeed, one can discern in this clustering a form of emergence;, a whole's possessing an emergent property at one time "supervenes on its physical state" and "is partly causally determinative of its physical state" and mental state at a subsequent time (O'Connor and Wong, *The Metaphysics of Emergence*, pp. 11–12). The phantasmatic image, accordingly, is not a faded copy of sensation but a causally operative retention that shapes the organism's ongoing temporal navigation. Rickert captures something of this mediating function when it is better to understand affective environments as "complexly generating an overarching ambient environment that cradles, without forcing, particular affective responses or that fosters an attunement" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 172–174)[2]; phantasia, similarly, cradles the residue of past encounters without mechanically dictating the organism's next movement, instead disposing it toward certain affective orientations. Burke's attention to the rhetorical compression inherent in "gist"—whereby complex situational meanings are distilled into operative shorthand (Burke, *The War of Words*, pp. 187–188)—provides a further analogy: phantasia condenses experiential complexity into imagistic wholes that guide subsequent response. Specifically, the phantasmatic medium does not merely store images; it sustains the affective coloring through which the perceptual field is already organized before any discrete act of aisthesis takes place, a relationship whose fuller articulation requires attending to perception itself and the affordance landscape it discloses. 

## 3. Aisthesis, Perception, and the Affordance Landscape The transition from phantasia's retentive medium to the perceptual field it subtends requires a closer examination of aisthesis itself—not as isolated sensory reception but as the organism's active disclosure of an affordance landscape already saturated with significance. Aisthesis, unlike phantasia, delivers the present object in its immediate qualitative specificity; yet this delivery is never bare or neutral, for the perceiving organism encounters surfaces, edges, and events as structured by ecological regularities that solicit determinate modes of engagement. As Gibson observes in *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, ambient light structured at a point of observation constitutes "an ambient optic array" whose point "may be stationary or moving, relative to the persisting environment" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 34)[3]. Thus perception is already ecological—already an encounter with the layout of surfaces, their pigmentation, and the shadows they cast within a dynamically structured array (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 109–110). What the organism perceives, accordingly, is not a mosaic of discrete stimuli but an affordance landscape: an environment whose features present themselves as traversable or obstructing, nourishing or threatening, precisely because phantasia has already disposed the perceiver toward certain affective orientations. Indeed, the contrast between aisthesis and phantasia proves to be not an opposition but a reciprocal dependency; perception furnishes the immediate material upon which phantasia operates, while phantasia supplies the retentive horizon against which any present perception acquires its salience. 11–12), then the affordance landscape disclosed through aisthesis is itself shaped by the organism's emergent affective history. Hence perception never merely registers what is present; it opens a field of possible engagements whose directional character—toward or away from the encountered object—already implicates the structures of desire and avoidance to which we must now attend. 

## 4. Pursuit and Avoidance: Desire, Judgment, and the Kinaesthetic Arc The directional character of the affordance landscape—its implicit orientation toward or away from what the organism encounters—finds its fullest expression in the kinaesthetic arc of pursuit and avoidance, where desire and judgment converge upon the motile body. If aisthesis discloses an environment whose surfaces and edges solicit engagement, it is desire that transforms that solicitation into determinate movement; the organism does not merely register the traversable path but is drawn along it or repelled from it by an affective vector already shaped through phantasia's retentive operation. Thus the kinaesthetic arc is not a mechanical reflex but an emergent structure in which perception, retained affect, and evaluative judgment are synthesized into a single temporal gesture. 11–12)—applies with particular force here, for the organism's pursuit of a perceived good or avoidance of a perceived threat is never fully explicable by the immediate stimulus alone; it draws upon the accumulated affective sediment that phantasia has preserved from prior encounters. Similarly, Gibson's ecological framework underscores that the environment's significance is not projected onto inert matter but inheres in the structured array itself, in the layout and pigmentation and shadowing of surfaces that constitute "three causes" of ambient structuring (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 109–110). Pursuit and avoidance, accordingly, are not opposed to perception but are its kinetic consummation—the point at which the affordance landscape's latent directionality becomes overt bodily motion. Burke's analysis of rhetorical "gist" as a compressive mechanism whereby complex situations are distilled into actionable shorthand (Burke, *The War of Words*, pp. 187–188) illuminates an analogous compressive process within the kinaesthetic arc: the organism does not deliberate over every feature of its environment but acts upon a condensed affective evaluation that phantasia and aisthesis have jointly prepared. Hence desire is not a separate impulse appended to cognition; it is the very medium through which the taking-as structure achieves its practical realization, converting the perceived-as-significant into the pursued-or-avoided. Yet this account of pursuit and avoidance remains incomplete without examining the broader affective tonality within which any particular desire or aversion arises—the mood or attunement that colors the entire perceptual field prior to any discrete act of wanting or fleeing. 

## 5. Attunement, Mood, and the Affective Coloring of Experience The kinaesthetic arc of pursuit and avoidance, as the preceding analysis established, always unfolds within a more encompassing affective atmosphere—a mood or attunement that is not reducible to any single desire or aversion but that pervades the organism's entire field of experience, coloring each perception and each retained phantasmatic image before any discrete evaluative act can take place. How does this prior affective tonality arise, and what is its relationship to the emergent structures we have been tracing? As Rickert argues in *Ambient Rhetoric*, it is productive to understand affective environments as "complexly generating an overarching ambient environment that cradles, without forcing, particular affective responses or that fosters an attunement" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 173)[4]. Attunement, on this account, is not an interior psychological state superimposed upon a neutral world; it is the very medium through which the affordance landscape acquires its characteristic qualitative texture—its warmth or threat, its invitation or withdrawal. Thus mood functions as a kind of emergent property of the organism-environment system, irreducible to the sum of discrete perceptual inputs yet supervening upon them in the diachronic manner that O'Connor and Wong describe, whereby the system's affective configuration at one moment is "partly causally determinative of its physical state" at subsequent moments (O'Connor and Wong, *The Metaphysics of Emergence*, pp. 11–12). Accordingly, phantasia does not merely retain individual images or sensory traces; it sustains the tonal continuity within which those images acquire their affective charge. Similarly, Gibson's insistence that perceptual experience involves a "nonmodal form of perception, cutting across the perceptual systems and transcending the 'senses'" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 231)[5] suggests that attunement operates at precisely this transmodal level—not within sight or hearing alone but across the entire sensory manifold as an integrated atmospheric quality. Indeed, Gross's treatment of uncomfortable situations as environments in which material and social surroundings jointly "give rise to the world that he experiences or enacts" (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, p. 174)[6] further substantiates mood is situational rather than merely subjective; it emerges from the tight coupling of agent and environment that Burke's rhetorical analysis similarly presupposes when it identifies the compressive mechanisms through which complex situational forces are distilled into unified experiential orientations (Burke, *The War of Words*, pp. 187–188). Specifically, the affective coloring of experience determines which affordances will be salient and which will recede, which phantasmatic images will surface with compelling urgency and which will remain dormant; it is the condition under which pursuit and avoidance become not merely possible but directionally specific. Yet if attunement establishes the atmospheric preconditions for action, it does not by itself determine what the organism will do when confronted with genuinely novel or ambiguous situations—those moments in which the affective field must be actively interrogated through imagination and deliberative opinion. 

## 6. Imagination and Opinion: The Deliberative Horizon When the affective atmosphere analyzed above encounters genuine ambiguity—when the mood-saturated affordance landscape presents competing or indeterminate possibilities—the organism must engage a deliberative capacity that extends beyond both immediate perception and habitual pursuit or avoidance. It is here that imagination and opinion assume their distinctive role within the taking-as structure, for phantasia in its deliberative mode does not merely retain prior affective traces but actively recombines them, projecting possible futures against which present circumstances can be evaluated. This deliberative phantasia contrasts with aisthesis precisely in its capacity to entertain what is not yet present, to hold before the organism a range of scenarios whose affective valences can be compared and weighed without the organism committing to any particular kinaesthetic trajectory. Thus imagination operates as the medium through which opinion—provisional judgment about what is good, dangerous, or desirable—achieves its practical content; without the phantasmatic image, opinion would remain an empty logical form, devoid of the motivational force necessary to issue in action. 11–12). Specifically, the deliberative image is not produced ex nihilo but arises from the accumulated sediment of prior perceptual encounters, moods, and affective orientations that phantasia has preserved; it is, accordingly, an emergent synthesis rather than a simple retrieval. Gibson's ecological framework reinforces this point by reminding us that the structured environment itself constrains deliberation—the ambient optic array, with its invariant information specifying the layout, pigmentation, and shadowing of surfaces, provides the material from which imagination constructs its prospective scenarios (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 109–110). Indeed, Burke's analysis of rhetorical "gist" as a compressive mechanism through which complex discursive situations are distilled into headline-ready formulations—whereby an address can be "'honestly' headlined" through the resources of condensation (Burke, *The War of Words*, pp. 187–188)—offers a striking analogue to the deliberative compression that imagination performs upon the affordance landscape: the organism does not entertain every possible future in exhaustive detail but generates condensed affective scenarios whose evaluative charge is immediately legible. Hence imagination and opinion together constitute the deliberative horizon within which the organism navigates temporal uncertainty, and it is precisely this horizon that must now be understood as grounding the more fundamental structure of resonant motion and affect through which temporal experience itself is constituted. 

## 7. Resonant Motion and Affect as the Fundamental Structure of Temporal Navigation The preceding sections have demonstrated that phantasia, as a distinct faculty of retentive motion in the soul, sustains the kinaesthetic arc of pursuit and avoidance within an affective atmosphere that both colors perception and constrains deliberative imagination. What remains is these interlocking structures—resonant motion and affect—constitute the fundamental architecture through which the organism navigates temporal experience itself. To perceive time and to notice that time has passed, it is sufficient to perceive a number of motion stages; yet this perception of temporal passage is never affectively neutral, for each retained motion-stage carries the phantasmatic charge that attunement has deposited within it. Thus temporal navigation is not a bare cognitive registration of sequential moments but an affectively saturated process in which the organism continually orients itself toward futures whose valence has already been shaped by the sediment of prior experience. Indeed, the situational character of this temporal structure becomes evident when we recognize, with Gross, that human emotions must be treated with attention to environments that are simultaneously social and "temporal, which sometimes means historical in the precise, professional sense of the word" (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, p. 155)[7]. Resonant affect, accordingly, is not merely individual but historically and environmentally embedded; the organism's temporal horizon is always already inflected by the situated conditions from which its phantasmatic repertoire has emerged. Similarly, Rickert's account of ambient environments that foster attunement without forcing particular responses (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 173) confirms that resonant motion unfolds within a field of possibility rather than along a single deterministic trajectory. The organism navigates time by inhabiting an emergent relational structure wherein,, diachronic emergence ensures that present configurations are "partly causally determinative" of subsequent states (O'Connor and Wong, *The Metaphysics of Emergence*, pp. 11–12). Gibson's ecological insistence that awareness of "out there" and "here" are complementary dimensions of a nonmodal perceptual experience (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 231) further substantiates this claim: temporal navigation requires the organism to hold together its situated present and its projected horizons within a single resonant field. Hence resonant motion and affect, far from being secondary accompaniments to cognition, constitute the very medium through which temporal experience achieves its coherent, directional, and fundamentally lived character.
