## Overarching Thesis

Aristotle's accounts of motion (kinesis), time (chronos), and temporal cognition through aisthesis and phantasia constitute an integrated philosophical framework in which objective physical structures and subjective psychological capacities are mutually constitutive, producing an emergent account of temporality that resists reduction to either pure objectivity or pure subjectivity.

## C1. Motion as Irreducible Ontological Category

**Claim:** Aristotle's definition of motion (kinesis) as "the actualization of potentiality qua potential" establishes motion as a distinctive ontological category that resists reduction to conventional classifications, occupying an intermediate mode of being between pure potentiality and complete actuality.

**Claim type:** DEFINITIONAL — establishes the foundational concept upon which subsequent claims depend

**Toulmin structure:**

| Component | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **Grounds** | See evidence table below |
| **Warrant** | The Greek formulation *hē tou dunamei ontos entelecheia* distinguishes motion from static actuality by preserving the character of ongoing realization rather than completed achievement, maintaining the tension between what is and what is becoming. |
| **Backing** | Heidegger's phenomenological reading confirms that kinesis preserves incompleteness even while actualizing, distinguishing it from energeia (complete actuality). |
| **Qualification** | "This interpretation represents the consensus reading of Physics III among contemporary scholars, though substantive debates persist about whether kinesis should be understood primarily as a temporal process unfolding through successive stages or as an ontological state characterized by constitutive incompleteness." |
| **Rebuttal** | The resolution of this debate "bears directly on whether Aristotle's account privileges the kinetic dimension of change or the structural condition of being-in-motion as a distinctive mode of existence." |

## C2. Time as Relational and Emergent

**Claim:** Aristotle defines time (chronos) as "the number of motion with respect to before and after," establishing it as fundamentally relational — dependent upon both kinetic processes and the soul's capacity to enumerate — and therefore neither purely objective nor purely subjective but emergent.

**Claim type:** DEFINITIONAL-ANALYTICAL — establishes the concept of time and immediately analyzes its ontological status

**Toulmin structure:**

| Component | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **Grounds** | See evidence table below |
| **Warrant** | The definition crucially distinguishes time from motion itself: temporal structure emerges not from movement per se but through the cognitive act of numbering motion's successive phases. |
| **Backing** | Bowin's analysis confirms the dual-dependency reading: time requires both the material substrate of motion and a rational soul capable of enumeration. |
| **Qualification** | "This hybrid account has generated extensive scholarly debate regarding whether time can exist in the absence of a soul, with interpreters divided between those who advocate for a more realist reading... and those who emphasize the constitutive role that psychic enumeration plays." |
| **Rebuttal** | "The interpretive tension reflects a fundamental ambiguity in Aristotle's own formulation, specifically concerning whether the soul's counting activity merely recognizes a temporal structure already present in motion or whether it actively constitutes time through the very act of numerical determination." |

## C3. Motion–Time Interdependence

**Claim:** Motion and time in Aristotle's framework are essentially interdependent through a bidirectional relationship that preserves their conceptual distinction while acknowledging their mutual necessity, mediated by the 'now' (*to nun*) which both divides and unifies temporal succession.

**Claim type:** ANALYTICAL-RELATIONAL — articulates the structural relationship between two previously defined concepts

**Toulmin structure:**

| Component | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **Grounds** | See evidence table below |
| **Warrant** | Time functions as the measure of motion while motion provides the necessary substrate without which temporal determination cannot occur; this creates mutual dependence that resists reduction to identity or complete separation. |
| **Backing** | Heidegger's interpretation of the 'now' as simultaneously a principle of differentiation and a unifying element confirms the dual function required for motion-time reciprocity. |
| **Qualification** | "While Aristotle maintains their conceptual distinction throughout his analysis, the precise nature of their ontological relationship remains subject to interpretation, particularly regarding whether motion has ontological priority over time or whether they constitute mutually dependent aspects of a single phenomenon." |
| **Rebuttal** | "This interpretive ambiguity stems from Aristotle's insistence that time cannot exist without change while simultaneously characterizing time as something distinct from motion itself." |

## C4. Aisthesis and Phantasia as Infrastructure for Temporal Cognition

**Claim:** Temporal awareness in Aristotle depends fundamentally upon two complementary psychological capacities — aisthesis (perception) provides direct access to temporal succession, while phantasia (imagination) enables retention and comparison of successive moments — together constituting the complete infrastructure for temporal cognition.

**Claim type:** ANALYTICAL-PSYCHOLOGICAL — identifies the cognitive mechanisms enabling the phenomena described in C2 and C3

**Toulmin structure:**

| Component | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **Grounds** | See evidence table below |
| **Warrant** | Without perception, there is no access to the motion that grounds time; without phantasia, there is no capacity to retain and compare successive moments — both are necessary and neither alone is sufficient for temporal cognition. |
| **Backing** | Aristotle's seal-impression metaphor (*De Anima*) shows how a percept becomes a phantasma, preserving perceptual content for temporal retention beyond the immediate sensory encounter. |
| **Qualification** | "The precise mechanisms by which phantasia operates in temporal cognition remain somewhat underdetermined in Aristotle's texts, leading to ongoing scholarly debate about its exact function." |
| **Rebuttal** | (None explicitly stated — the claim is presented as the standard reading.) |
