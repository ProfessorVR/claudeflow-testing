# Phantasia as Disclosive Mediation in Aristotelian Psychology

Aristotle's phantasia functions as a disclosive capacity that makes present what is not immediately given in perception, operating as what Heidegger characterizes as a "'making-present-to-itself' of the world, in which what is made present is not actually there, but instead is, say, in memory" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 112). This characterization reveals phantasia's fundamental role in mediating between sensory experience and thought, a necessity underscored by Aristotle's insistence that theoretical reasoning "cannot do without 'images'" (phantasmata), thereby indicating that phantasia provides the representational content indispensable for intellectual activity (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 112). Indeed, phantasia serves as a crucial component in "the meaning-making process" within Aristotelian psychology, bridging the gap between perception and conceptual understanding in a manner that transcends mere reproductive or causal derivation from sensation (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 112). Thus, the characterization of phantasia as "making-present-to-itself" reveals its disclosive function: it renders accessible to consciousness what is temporally or spatially absent from immediate perception, establishing a mediating structure that discloses the world in a mode fundamentally distinct from both the receptivity of sensation and the abstractive operations of intellection (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 112). This making-present of the non-present establishes phantasia as fundamentally disclosive rather than merely reproductive, yet this interpretation confronts the tension between phantasia's causal derivation from actual sensation in *De Anima* III.3 and its functional autonomy in domains such as dreaming, deliberation, and rhetorical reception, where it operates independently of immediate perceptual input (Aristotle, *De Anima*, III.3; *Rhetoric*). Subsequently, the question emerges whether phantasia's disclosive capacity genuinely constitutes an ontologically distinct mode of world-access or whether its apparent autonomy remains parasitic upon prior sensory actualization, a tension that Aristotle's framework never fully resolves and that threatens to collapse the distinction between embodied perception and separable intellection (Aristotle, *De Anima*, III.4, III.7).

The structural parallel between Aristotelian *phantasia* and Heideggerian *Erschlossenheit* must be carefully qualified, for while both establish pre-predicative conditions for cognitive access to beings, they operate within fundamentally different ontological registers: Aristotle conceives *phantasia* as a psychic capacity (*dynamis*) belonging to the soul's faculties alongside sensation and intellection, whereas Heidegger reconceives disclosure as an existential structure constitutive of Dasein's very being-in-the-world (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 112). This distinction becomes acute when examining Heidegger's interpretive emphasis on the temporal and presentational dimensions of *phantasia*—particularly its capacity to make absent beings present through *phantasmata*—which, while illuminating the phenomenological structure of imagination and memory, may extend considerably beyond what Aristotle explicitly formulates in *De Anima* III.3, where *phantasia* is defined primarily as "a movement (*kinēsis*) produced by actual sensation" (Aristotle, *De Anima*, 428b10-429a9). Moreover, Heidegger's reading privileges the disclosive function of *phantasia* as temporally ecstatic—as enabling Dasein's projection toward future possibilities and retrieval of past encounters—yet Aristotle's own account remains ambiguous regarding whether *phantasia's* mediating role between sensation and thought constitutes genuine temporal ekstasis or merely psychological succession, leaving unresolved whether the structural parallel reflects genuine conceptual convergence or whether Heidegger's existential reinterpretation transforms *phantasia* into something Aristotle himself would not recognize (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 111-113).

The frameworks diverge fundamentally in their ontological commitments: Aristotle's *phantasia* operates within a substance ontology where the soul possesses distinct faculties, while Heidegger's *Erschlossenheit* rejects faculty psychology entirely, positioning disclosure as the existential structure of Dasein's Being itself. Aristotle's hylomorphic framework treats *phantasia* as a *dynamis* (capacity) that the soul (*psyche*) possesses as the form of a living body, situating it within a taxonomy of psychic faculties alongside perception, desire, and intellect, where each faculty has its proper object and operation within the teleological structure of the organism (Aristotle, *De Anima*, 414a29-414b19). The Aristotelian "expression οὐσία, as the fundamental term of Aristotelian research, stems from an expression that has a customary meaning in natural language," grounding Aristotle's ontology in substance (*ousia*) that Heidegger explicitly rejects (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 18). Consequently, Aristotle's framework treats "the being whose being constitutes the τί ἤν εἶναι" as "ἕκαστον, 'the particular,'" operating within a substance ontology where beings are understood through essence and particularity (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 47). Heidegger, by contrast, dismantles the substance-ontology underlying faculty psychology, arguing that Dasein cannot be understood as a substance with properties or capacities; he insists that if Dasein "lacks its 'there', it is not factically the entity which is essentially Dasein; indeed, it is not this entity at all," making disclosure constitutive of Dasein's Being rather than a property it possesses (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 133). *Erschlossenheit* is not something Dasein has but what Dasein is—the "there" (*Da*) that constitutes its existence—transforming disclosure from a psychological capacity into an ontological structure, from a feature of the soul to the Being of Dasein itself (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 171-172).

Heidegger's engagement with Aristotelian λόγος in his lectures reveals a hermeneutic strategy that simultaneously retrieves Greek phenomenological insights and transforms their ontological framework, suggesting that the connection between *phantasia* and *Erschlossenheit* constitutes neither straightforward historical continuity nor arbitrary contemporary imposition but rather productive philosophical reconstruction. Heidegger explicates "the being-there of the human being as being-in-the-world, in such a way that λόγος came forth as the basic character of this being," demonstrating his appropriation of Aristotelian λόγος as foundational to disclosure rather than merely a logical or linguistic faculty (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 133). This retrieval locates in Aristotle's account of λόγος—where "as λόγος is what governs, I draw my knowledge from hearing-saying"—a medium through which beings become accessible, anticipating *Erschlossenheit* as the ontological structure by which Dasein encounters world (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 35). Subsequently, Heidegger identifies a dimension of Greek thought where "for uncovering no possibility of dissimulating, of mis-leading" exists, suggesting he recovers in Aristotle an original understanding of *aletheia* (unconcealment) that precedes later epistemological frameworks centered on correspondence or representation (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 35). Yet this hermeneutic approach involves reading Aristotle against the grain of subsequent metaphysical interpretations, seeking phenomenological insights obscured by the tradition while fundamentally reconceiving their ontological ground—positioning λόγος as "the basic character" of Being-in-the-world transforms Aristotelian concepts even as Heidegger claims to retrieve their originary meaning, exposing the productive tension between historical fidelity and philosophical reconstruction that characterizes his entire engagement with Greek thought (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 133).

The extent to which Heidegger's reading represents Aristotle's own intentions versus Heidegger's philosophical reconstruction remains contested, particularly when examining his treatment of foundational Aristotelian concepts through the lens of existential analytic. The hermeneutic circle thus tightens: Heidegger's retrieval of Greek ontology necessarily proceeds through the existential analytic developed in *Being and Time*, making it methodologically impossible to separate "what Aristotle meant" from "what becomes visible through Heidegger's questioning"—a productive ambiguity that generates philosophical insight precisely by refusing to resolve into either pure historical reconstruction or arbitrary philosophical appropriation.

The importance Heidegger places on world transforms the locus of agency: "the world not only frees the ready-to-hand as entities encountered within-the-world; it also frees Dasein, and the Others in their Dasein-with" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 364), indicating that world itself performs the disclosive function rather than a faculty possessed by the subject. This represents the most radical divergence between the frameworks, one that cannot be harmonized through developmental narrative or hermeneutic charity: Aristotle's faculty psychology presupposes a subject (the ensouled organism) that possesses capacities for engaging with an external world, whereas Heidegger's existential analytic dissolves this subject-object structure entirely, making *Erschlossenheit* not a capacity Dasein exercises but the way Dasein exists.
