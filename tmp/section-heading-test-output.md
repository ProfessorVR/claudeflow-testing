# Environmental Disclosure and the Situated Subject: Four Traditions

The question of how an environment is disclosed to, and structured for, a situated subject has occupied philosophers from antiquity to the present day. To inhabit a world is never simply to stand within a neutral container of objects; rather, it is to find oneself always already attuned to an environment that solicits perception, action, and meaning in determinate ways. This dissertation section examines four traditions that illuminate the phenomenon of environmental disclosure: Aristotle's account of phantasia as the capacity through which the soul renders its surroundings present even in the absence of immediate sensation; Heidegger's ontological analysis of Erschlossenheit, mood, and attunement as the pre-cognitive disclosure of Being-in-the-world; Jakob von Uexküll's biosemiotic concept of the Umwelt, in which each organism inhabits a perceptual world structured by species-specific perception tones and effect tones; and Thomas Rickert's theory of ambient rhetoric, which extends these insights into a contemporary account of how rhetoric itself is constituted by the immersive, affective, and material conditions of dwelling. By tracing the convergences and tensions among these four approaches, we aim to show that the environment is never a brute given but is always already shaped by the perceptual, affective, and ontological capacities of the being who inhabits it. The synthesis that concludes this section demonstrates how these traditions jointly illuminate the situated character of environmental disclosure; specifically, they reveal that perception, mood, biological organization, and rhetorical ambiance are not separable layers imposed upon a neutral world but interwoven dimensions of a single, ongoing event of disclosure. 

## 1. Aristotle on Phantasia as Environmental Disclosure

Aristotle's treatment of phantasia in *De Anima* constitutes one of the earliest systematic attempts to account for the way in which the soul renders its environment present to itself; indeed, phantasia occupies a critical intermediary position between bare sensation (aisthēsis) and full-fledged thought (noēsis), enabling the organism to sustain contact with its surroundings even when the objects of perception are no longer immediately before it. As Aristotle observes, "imagination takes place in the absence of both" faculty and activity of sense, "as e.g. in dreams" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)[1]*, p. 42)[6]. This formulation is decisive, for it establishes phantasia as a capacity that is not reducible to sensation yet remains causally derived from it — a tension that pervades the entire account and that we shall identify as productive rather than merely problematic. The phantasma, the image or appearance generated by phantasia, thus functions as a quasi-perceptual presentation of the environment; it retains the qualitative character of sensory experience while operating independently of the external object that originally occasioned it. Thus, the organism that possesses phantasia is disclosed to its environment in a richer sense than the organism that possesses sensation alone, for it can hold its environment before itself in deliberation, memory, and desire. Aristotle further distinguishes between kinds or grades of phantasia, a distinction that bears directly on the question of environmental disclosure. In *De Anima* III.11, he differentiates sensitive imagination from deliberative imagination: "Sensitive imagination, as we have said, is found in all animals, deliberative imagination only in those that are calculative: for whether this or that shall be enacted is already a task requiring calculation; and there must be a single standard to measure by, for that is pursued which is greater" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)[2]*, p. 52)[7]. Sensitive phantasia discloses the environment at the level of immediate relevance — the prey animal perceives the predator as fearful, and an image of flight is generated automatically; deliberative phantasia, by contrast, discloses the environment as a field of possibilities that can be weighed against one another according to a standard of comparison. The deliberative grade thus introduces a temporal and evaluative dimension into environmental disclosure; the situated subject does not merely register what is present but projects future states of affairs and selects among them. This distinction is critical because it reveals that phantasia is not a single, homogeneous faculty but a graded capacity whose higher forms presuppose calculative reason (logismos). The relationship between phantasia and belief (doxa) is accordingly complex: while doxa involves a propositional commitment — a "yes" to the matter at hand, as Heidegger's reading of Aristotle suggests (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 108)[8] — phantasia can present appearances that diverge from what the subject believes to be true. Hence, one may imagine something fearful while believing oneself to be safe; the environment as disclosed by phantasia need not coincide with the environment as judged by doxa. The voluntariness of phantasia introduces a further complication. While sensitive phantasia arises automatically in response to sensory stimulation, deliberative phantasia is subject to a measure of voluntary control; the deliberator actively composes images of possible futures. As Christina Papachristou notes, the Greek word phantasia resists straightforward translation as "imagination," since such a rendering obscures the faculty's deep connection to appearance (phantasma) and its complex relationship to truth and falsity (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 9–10)[9]. The full elaboration of these internal distinctions — including the precise conditions under which phantasia can be true or false, and the manner in which deliberative phantasia cooperates with practical syllogism — will be developed at greater length in the following chapter. For now, it suffices to observe that Aristotle's account of phantasia establishes a foundational insight: the environment is never simply given to the organism as a collection of bare stimuli but is always already structured by a presentational capacity that organizes, retains, and projects sensory content in ways shaped by the organism's form of life and its practical engagements. 

## 2. Heidegger on Erschlossenheit, Mood, and Attunement

Martin Heidegger's analysis of disclosure (Erschlossenheit)[10] in *Being and Time* radicalizes the Aristotelian insights examined above by relocating the question of environmental presentation from the faculty psychology of *De Anima* to a fundamental ontology of Being-in-the-world. For Heidegger, the environment is not first encountered as a set of objects that are subsequently colored by mood or interpreted by thought; rather, the world is always already disclosed through Dasein's state-of-mind (Befindlichkeit)[11], which is ontically manifested as mood (Stimmung)[12]. As Heidegger writes, what "we indicate ontologically by the term 'state-of-mind' is ontically the most familiar and everyday sort of thing; our mood, our Being-attuned" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 172–173)[13]. This deceptively simple observation carries profound consequences for our understanding of environmental disclosure: mood is not an inner psychological state that is subsequently projected onto an outer world, but the very medium through which the world as a whole becomes accessible. Accordingly, attunement precedes and conditions every act of perception, cognition, and volition; the situated subject is always already attuned before it can deliberate or choose. The connection to Aristotle's account of phantasia is both deep and revisionary. Where Aristotle treats phantasia as a faculty of the soul that mediates between sensation and thought, Heidegger re-reads the Aristotelian insight in ontological rather than psychological terms. Specifically, Heidegger interprets Aristotle's treatment of the affects (pathē) not as states "of the soul" that happen to be accompanied by "bodily symptoms," but as disclosive orientations of the whole human being toward its world (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 134)[14]. The partitioning of experience into inner mental states and outer physical symptoms is, on Heidegger's reading, a derivative abstraction that obscures the originary unity of human being-in-the-world. Indeed, Heidegger goes so far as to praise Aristotle's *Rhetoric* as "the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being with one another," a remark that Thomas Rickert cites to underscore how mood "assails us," arising "out of Being-in-the-world, as a way of such Being," so that "the mood has already disclosed, in every case, Being-in-the-world as a whole, and makes it possible first of all to direct itself towards something" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric- The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, p. 33)[15]. Thus, Heidegger transforms Aristotle's faculty of phantasia — which discloses the environment through images retained from sensation — into the broader phenomenon of attunement, which discloses the environment as a totality of significance prior to any act of imaging or judging. Similarly, Heidegger's re-reading of doxa (belief) sheds light on the environmental dimensions of disclosure. Where Aristotle treats doxa as a propositional orientation toward the matter at hand, Heidegger reinterprets this orientation as an existential modality of being-situated. As he argues, rhetoric aims at "constructing a view: a δόξα is to be cultivated," and this cultivation is not merely a matter of forming correct propositions but of being attuned to the shared world of everyday practical life (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 108)[16]. The notion of Umwelt — literally, the "world around" — which Heidegger borrows from biology and transforms into an existential concept, further underscores this point: the expression "'environment' [Umwelt] contains in the 'environ' ['um'] a suggestion of spatiality" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 94)[17]. Accordingly, the environment is not a container in which a subject happens to find itself but a structured horizon of significance that is opened up by Dasein's attunement. This insight connects directly to Aristotle's graded phantasia; just as deliberative phantasia discloses the environment as a field of weighted possibilities, Heidegger's attunement discloses the environment as a totality of practical relevance, where entities show up as ready-to-hand (zuhanden), available and usable, constituting the "there-character" of everyday being (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 248–249)[18]. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* observe that Heidegger's sustained engagement with Aristotelian concepts during the early 1920s shaped many of the key terms of *Being and Time*, including attunement itself; hence the list of concepts molded during Heidegger's ruminations on rhetoric "does read like a glossary of key terms from Being and Time" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 7–9)[3]. Subsequently, we can see that Heidegger's ontological analysis of disclosure does not abandon the Aristotelian framework but deepens it, revealing mood and attunement as the conditions of possibility for every form of environmental encounter. 

## 3. Uexküll's Umwelt: Perception Tones and Effect Tones

Jakob von Uexküll's concept of the Umwelt provides a biological and semiotic framework for understanding how an organism's environment is not a neutral, objective space but is constituted by the organism's own perceptual and effector capacities. Where Aristotle's account of phantasia addresses the psychological faculties through which the soul renders its surroundings present, and Heidegger's analysis of attunement addresses the ontological conditions under which the world is disclosed to Dasein, Uexküll's biosemiotics addresses the structural organization of an organism's perceptual and active life as a functional circle (Funktionskreis)[19] that binds perception and action into a unified cycle of meaning. Every animal, Uexküll argues, inhabits its own Umwelt, a perceptual world composed of perception marks (Merkmale)[20] and effect marks (Wirkmale)[21] that together define the range of objects and events that are significant for that organism. Objects in the environment are not encountered as neutral substrates but are invested with what Uexküll calls "tones" — perception tones that mark an object as perceptually salient and effect tones that mark it as a target of action. As Uexküll's index of concepts indicates, the organism's use of stimuli and the transfer of stimuli are mediated by species-specific perceptual organs and effector systems, such that the same physical object may carry entirely different tones for different species (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 276–278). Specifically, a stone may carry a "throwing tone" for a human being — it shows up as something to be hurled — while for a tick it carries no tone at all; it is simply invisible to that organism's perceptual apparatus (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 276–278). This notion of tonal investment bears a productive affinity to Aristotle's account of how phantasia structures environmental disclosure. Just as Aristotle observes that "sensitive imagination" is "found in all animals" while "deliberative imagination" belongs "only in those that are calculative" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)[4]*, p. 52)[22], Uexküll's framework suggests that the complexity of an organism's Umwelt is directly proportional to the richness of its perceptual and effector repertoire. The tick's Umwelt, for example, is impoverished relative to that of a dog or a human being because the tick possesses only a handful of perception marks — warmth, butyric acid, and the tactile quality of skin — whereas the dog's Umwelt is structured by a far richer array of olfactory, visual, and auditory tones. Hence, the environment is never an objective given; it is always a species-specific disclosure, a world constituted by the organism's biological form. James Gibson's ecological psychology provides a complementary perspective. Gibson's concept of affordances — the action possibilities that the environment offers to a particular organism — resonates with Uexküll's perception tones and effect tones, though Gibson develops his account within a framework of direct perception rather than biosemiotics. For Gibson, the environment is structured by surfaces, substances, and media that afford specific behaviors; thus, a shelter "affords protection from rain" and "walls afford protection from wind," while "a doorway" affords "entry and exit" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 62)[23]. Similarly, Gibson insists on the ecological scale of environmental description: "I shall talk about changes, events, and sequences of events but not about time as such" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 36)[24]. This ecological orientation parallels Uexküll's insistence that the organism's Umwelt is not a slice of objective physical reality but a functionally organized perceptual world; indeed, both thinkers reject the assumption that perception begins with meaningless stimuli that must be subsequently interpreted by cognitive processes. Rickert draws on both Uexküll and Gibson to argue that the environment is always already meaningful, always already toned or afforded in ways that precede any act of conscious deliberation (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric- The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, pp. 23–25)[25]. The question of ambience is thus also a question of perception and recognition, or, as Rickert paraphrases Heidegger, of "wakefulness" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric- The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, pp. 23–25)[26]. Subsequently, the Umwelt concept reveals that environmental disclosure is not a uniquely human phenomenon but a fundamental feature of all organismic life, grounded in the structural coupling of perception and action. 

## 4. Rickert's Ambient Attunement and Rhetorical Ambiance

Thomas Rickert's theory of ambient rhetoric draws together the Aristotelian, Heideggerian, and Uexküllian strands examined above into a unified account of how rhetoric is constituted by the immersive, affective, and material conditions of environmental disclosure. For Rickert, rhetoric is not merely a set of techniques deployed by an autonomous human agent upon passive audiences; rather, rhetoric is an ongoing event of disclosure that encompasses the material environment, affective attunement, and bodily engagement as co-constitutive dimensions of persuasion. Thus, rhetorical theory is itself "a variable in its own system," so that "changes in rhetorical understanding unpredictably alter how we inhabit and practice rhetoric in the world" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric- The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, p. 10)[27]. This claim is not merely epistemological but ontological: the ambience within which rhetoric operates is not a background that can be subtracted from the rhetorical act but is the very ground from which rhetorical meaning emerges. Rickert's account of attunement extends Heidegger's analysis of Befindlichkeit into an explicitly rhetorical register. Where Heidegger shows that mood discloses Being-in-the-world as a whole, Rickert argues that this disclosure has a fundamentally rhetorical character; the world that is disclosed through attunement is always already a world of significance, solicitation, and persuasion. Rickert explicitly retrieves Heidegger's praise of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* as the first systematic hermeneutic of everyday being-with-one-another, and he develops this insight by arguing that mood "assails us" in a way that makes possible all subsequent orientation toward things (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric- The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, p. 33)[28]. Furthermore, Rickert draws on the notion of embodied cognition to argue that the body is not a bounded container of mind but a "newly plastic" entity whose boundaries are blurred by its engagement with tools and environments; as Andy Clark argues, "the tools we use start 'dovetailing back' and blur the distinction between 'mind' and 'tool'" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric- The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, p. 57)[29]. Accordingly, ambient rhetoric cannot be understood within a framework that separates the human subject from its material surroundings; the environment is not a scene upon which the rhetorical act takes place but a co-agent in the constitution of rhetorical meaning. The connection to Uexküll's Umwelt is crucial for Rickert's argument. Uexküll's demonstration that each organism inhabits a species-specific perceptual world structured by perception tones and effect tones provides Rickert with a biological model for the way in which environments are always already meaningful prior to any act of interpretation. Similarly, Aristotle's account of nature as "a principle or cause of being moved and of being at rest in that to which it belongs primarily, in virtue of itself and not accidentally" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 15–17)[30] provides Rickert with an ontological foundation for the claim that things possess an inherent directedness or tendency toward their proper end. Hence, the rhetorical ambiance of an environment is not something imposed upon inert matter by a human agent but something that emerges from the dynamic interplay of organisms, things, and the material conditions of dwelling. As Aristotle further observes, the principle of order applies equally to "the production of a house, a statue, or anything else," since "each of these objects" is "partly order and partly composition" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 8–10)[31]. Rickert's ambient rhetoric takes up this insight by arguing that the material composition and spatial arrangement of an environment are themselves rhetorically significant; they structure the possibilities of encounter, attunement, and persuasion in ways that exceed the intentions of any individual rhetor. Thus, materiality is not merely the vehicle of rhetoric but is "appearance as shaped in advance by culture, and only in this guise, where valence is established with 'emotion, conviction, and judgment,' is it rhetorical" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric- The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, p. 13)[32]. The notion of ambient attunement thus synthesizes Heideggerian disclosure, Aristotelian natural philosophy, and Uexküllian biosemiotics into a comprehensive account of how environments are disclosed and structured for situated subjects. 

## 5. Synthesis — How These Four Jointly Structure a Situated Environment

How, then, do these four traditions jointly illuminate the phenomenon of environmental disclosure? The synthesis we propose here does not seek to flatten the significant differences among Aristotle, Heidegger, Uexküll, and Rickert into a single, homogeneous framework; rather, it traces the lines of convergence and productive tension that emerge when these approaches are brought into dialogue. Each tradition contributes an indispensable dimension of the full picture: Aristotle's phantasia provides the psychological mechanism through which the environment is rendered present to the soul; Heidegger's attunement provides the ontological condition under which the environment is disclosed as a totality of significance; Uexküll's Umwelt provides the biological structure that binds perception and action into a species-specific world; and Rickert's ambient rhetoric provides the rhetorical framework within which these dimensions are understood as co-constitutive of persuasion, meaning, and dwelling. The first axis of convergence concerns the priority of meaning over bare stimulus. All four traditions reject the assumption that the organism or subject first encounters a world of meaningless data that must subsequently be endowed with significance through cognitive processing. For Aristotle, phantasia already invests sensory content with a qualitative character that reflects the organism's form of life and practical concerns; for Heidegger, mood discloses the world as significant before any theoretical attitude can be adopted; for Uexküll, perception tones and effect tones ensure that the organism's Umwelt is structured by relevance from the outset; and for Rickert, ambient attunement reveals that rhetoric is not applied to a pre-rhetorical world but emerges from the always-already-meaningful conditions of environmental engagement. Daniel Gross's analysis of "situated conceptualization" further illuminates this point; he argues that the background of any situation "recedes indefinitely into what we would consider non-conceptual affordances located in the environment, including the social environment" (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, pp. 150–151)[33]. Thus, meaning is not a layer added to an otherwise neutral world but is constitutive of the environment as such. The second axis concerns the role of affect and attunement. Both Heidegger and Aristotle insist that the affects are not mere accompaniments of cognition but are disclosive in their own right. Heidegger's early lectures on Aristotle, as the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* observe, were decisive in shaping his mature account of Befindlichkeit, and the rhetorical perspective is "ineluctably temporal," attending to the "importance of sensitivity to time" and the "difficulty" of "relating universal to particular" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 130–131)[5]. Similarly, Gross insists that emotions such as racialized fear are "not just a concept" but something situated within an entire social and material environment that resists reduction to a discrete cognitive state (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, pp. 150–151)[34]. The affective dimension of environmental disclosure is thus irreducible: mood, pathos, and attunement are not distortions of an otherwise rational encounter with the world but are the very conditions under which the world becomes accessible. 77–80). Accordingly, the situated subject is never a neutral observer of its environment but is always already implicated in a web of motives, affects, and material conditions. The third axis concerns the structure of the perceptual world. Uexküll's functional circle and Gibson's affordances converge with Aristotle's graded phantasia and Heidegger's readiness-to-hand in revealing that perception is always perception-for-action: the environment shows up as a field of practical possibilities rather than a collection of detached objects. Heidegger's insistence that everyday entities are encountered as χρήματα — "that with which one has to do everyday," "what stands in use" — echoes Uexküll's perception tones and effect tones, in which every object is invested with a functional significance relative to the organism's needs and capacities (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 248–249)[35]. The convergence of these traditions suggests that the environment, far from being a fixed, objective container, is a dynamic, relational structure that is always already organized around the practical concerns of the situated subject. Indeed, Gross's critique of situated cognition theory signals that even contemporary cognitive science remains caught between a modular, funcionalizing account of conceptualization and the recognition that the background of any situation extends beyond the conceptual into non-conceptual affordances (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, pp. 25–27)[36]. Subsequently, the synthesis advanced here points toward a conception of environmental disclosure in which perception, mood, biological organization, and rhetorical ambiance are not separable layers but interwoven dimensions of a single ongoing event — the event by which a situated subject and its environment are mutually constituted. In sum, the four traditions examined in this section jointly reveal that to be a situated subject is to inhabit an environment that is always already disclosed, structured, and meaningful. ---
