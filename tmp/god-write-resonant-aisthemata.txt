## Resonant Aisthemata and Resonant Affect: The Persisting Formal Motions That Ground Phantasia, Desire, and Rhetorical Pathos

#

## 1. The Residual Motion of Sense: Aisthemata as the Material Ground of Phantasia

To understand how phantasia operates within Aristotle's broader psychological and rhetorical framework, we must first attend to the persisting formal motions—the residual aisthemata—that sense-perception leaves behind in the perceiving organism. Aristotle's account of phantasia is grounded in the prior operation of sense-perception, such that the image-making faculty cannot function independently of aisthesis; indeed, the very possibility of phantasmata depends upon motions that were originally initiated by external objects acting upon the sense organs but that continue to reverberate within the organism even after the sensible object has been removed. These persisting motions are not mere copies or static representations; they are kinetic remnants, formal impressions that retain the qualitative character of the original perception while being susceptible to distortion, combination, and reactivation under conditions that differ markedly from those of the initial sensory encounter. As the anonymous test on rhetorical phantasia observes, drawing upon the *De Memoria*, "all objects of which there is imagination are in themselves objects of memory". This formulation is crucial because it reveals that the domain of imagination and the domain of memory share a common material substratum: namely, the residual aisthemata that persist in the sensory apparatus and the common sense faculty. Thus the resonance of these perceptual motions constitutes the ground upon which both recollection and anticipatory imagination are built, and it is precisely this resonance that enables phantasia to function not merely as a passive repository of past impressions but as a generative faculty capable of presenting objects and scenarios that are not currently perceived.

The importance of this kinetic residue for Aristotle's psychology cannot be overstated. Victor Caston, in his examination of why Aristotle requires a distinct faculty of imagination, argues that phantasia addresses a specific explanatory problem: the need for a form of "intentionality" that "makes error possible" in a way that bare sense-perception, when operating under standard conditions, does not (Caston, *Why Aristotle Needs Imagination*, p. 18). Specifically, the residual motions that constitute aisthemata are no longer constrained by the immediate causal presence of the external object; hence they can misrepresent, combine heterogeneous sensory elements, and generate appearances that diverge from what is actually the case. The capacity for error is thus not an accidental defect of phantasia but rather a structural consequence of the fact that the motions grounding imagination are formally identical to—yet causally detached from—the original perceptual events. This detachment is what allows the organism to entertain possibilities, to deliberate about absent objects, and to be moved affectively by what is merely imagined rather than directly perceived.

## 2. Grades of Phantasia: Sensory, Deliberative, and Their Relation to Doxa

Aristotle distinguishes between at least two grades of phantasia, a distinction that bears directly upon the relationship between imagination and belief (doxa), and that has profound implications for rhetorical theory. The lower grade, sensory phantasia (phantasia aisthetike), is shared by humans and non-human animals alike; it arises directly from the residual motions of perception and does not require the mediation of rational judgment or linguistic articulation. Animals act on the basis of sensory phantasia when they pursue prey or flee predators, responding to persisting impressions that trigger appetitive or aversive responses without the intervention of deliberative reasoning. The higher grade, deliberative phantasia (phantasia bouleutike or logistike), is available only to rational beings and involves the capacity to construct, compare, and evaluate multiple phantasmata in the service of practical reasoning about what is to be done. This deliberative imagination presupposes the capacity for belief and judgment—doxa—because the rational agent must be able to assess the phantasma's veridicality or appropriateness in relation to the agent's goals and circumstances.

The relationship between phantasia and doxa is accordingly complex. Phantasia is not identical to belief, because one can entertain an image that one simultaneously judges to be false—the sun appears small, yet we believe it to be larger than the inhabited earth. Similarly, phantasia possesses a degree of voluntariness that doxa does not: we can summon images before our eyes at will, placing things "before our eyes" as mnemonic practitioners do, whereas beliefs are not subject to the same volitional control. As Debra Hawhee observes, the language of "paths of vision" that pervades rhetorical and perceptual discourse reveals how deeply Aristotle's account of imagination is intertwined with the energeia of bodily movement and visual orientation; indeed, Dan Slobin's research, cited by Hawhee, shows that "verbs of perception appear in the same syntactic and semantic constructions as verbs of motion" (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, p. 24)[1]. This convergence of perception and motion at the level of linguistic structure mirrors the Aristotelian insight that phantasia is itself a kind of motion—a kinesis produced by the activity of sense—and that the images it generates carry with them the dynamic, directional quality of the original perceptual engagement. Kevin White's observation, noted by Hawhee, that "the physiological study of the 'imagination' seems to have its origins in Galen's medical researches, which make use of Aristotle's remarks on to phantastikon" further underscores the embodied, materially grounded character of this faculty, while also cautioning us that later connotations of "imagination" do not correspond to Aristotle's phantasia (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, p. 23). A more thorough treatment of the voluntariness of phantasia and its precise demarcation from doxa will be developed in the chapter devoted to deliberative phantasia and practical reasoning; here, essential structural point that the capacity for resonant aisthemata makes possible both grades of imagination, with the deliberative grade adding a rational, evaluative layer to the basic sensory residue.

## 3. From Resonant Affect to Rhetorical Pathos: The Persuasive Force of Persisting Motions

The persisting formal motions that ground phantasia do not merely furnish cognitive content; they are also the substrate of affective experience, and it is through this affective dimension that they enter the domain of rhetoric. The anonymous source on rhetorical phantasia makes this connection explicit by quoting Aristotle's *Rhetoric*: "both the man who remembers and the man who hopes will be attended by an imagination of what he remembers or hopes". This passage reveals that the temporal orientations of memory and hope—past-directed and future-directed respectively—are both mediated by phantasia, and that the affective coloring of these temporal experiences depends upon the quality of the phantasmata that attend them. The individual who remembers a pleasant experience is moved by the residual impression of that pleasure, while the individual who hopes for a future good is moved by an anticipatory phantasma that carries affective force precisely because it resonates with prior perceptual and emotional experiences. Thus, rhetorical pathos—the strategic mobilization of audience emotion—depends fundamentally upon the orator's capacity to activate, shape, and direct the resonant aisthemata and their attendant affects within the souls of listeners.

Ned O'Gorman's work on Aristotle's phantasia in the *Rhetoric* deepens this analysis by emphasizing that the appetites and emotions in Aristotle are not "irrational" in a way that would place them beyond the reach of discursive influence; rather, as O'Gorman notes following Nussbaum, they "may be educated and shaped by discourse" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 11)[2]. This capacity for education through discourse is possible precisely because the emotions are grounded in phantasmata that are themselves susceptible to rhetorical manipulation—through vivid description, metaphor, and the technique of placing things "before the eyes" (pro ommaton) that Aristotle discusses in Book III of the *Rhetoric*. Indeed, Aristotle's own rhetorical principle when he catalogues the elaborate metaphorical substitutions through which orators amplify the emotional resonance of their speech: "he does not say 'sweat', but 'the moist sweat'; not 'to the Isthmian games', but 'to the world-concourse of the Isthmian games'; not 'laws', but 'the laws that are monarchs of states'" (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 92)[3]. Each substitution works by enriching the phantasma that the listener constructs, layering additional sensory and evaluative content upon the basic perceptual residue so that the resulting image carries greater affective weight and greater persuasive force. The technique thus exploits the structure of resonant aisthemata: because the residual motions of sense are susceptible to combination and amplification, the skilled rhetor can compose verbal stimuli that activate and intensify these motions in ways that serve the persuasive purpose at hand.

Kenneth Burke's conception of rhetoric as fundamentally concerned with "identification" and "address" further illuminates how resonant affect operates at the level of rhetorical encounter. Burke frames his project as one of "carving out a rhetoric, often from materials not generally thought to fall under the head" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, p. 7)[4], and his attention to the ways in which symbolic action works upon the "individual soul" suggests an implicit recognition that rhetorical persuasion depends upon affective resonances that operate beneath or alongside rational judgment. Similarly, Burke's analysis in *The War of Words* of the way organizational rhetoric deploys stories about the formation of a "nucleus" and the adopting of "principles" while actual conditions remain unspecified illustrates how rhetorical pathos can be sustained through the manipulation of anticipatory phantasmata—images of future solidarity, progress, and collective achievement that carry affective force even in the absence of corresponding realities (Burke, *The War of Words*, p. 104). The resonance here is temporal as well as affective: the audience's hope is grounded in phantasmata that project a desirable future, and these projections are themselves composed of residual impressions drawn from past experience.

## 4. Ambient Attunement and the Situational Character of Resonant Affect

The foregoing analysis of resonant aisthemata and their role in grounding phantasia, desire, and rhetorical pathos would be incomplete without attending to the ambient and situational character of affective experience. Thomas Rickert's work on ambient rhetorical being is always already attuned to its environment in ways that exceed the intentional control of the speaking subject; rhetorical invention, on Rickert's account, emerges from the interplay between agent and milieu, a claim developed through his attention to kairos and emplacement (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 14). This ambient dimension of rhetoric resonates with Aristotle's account of how aisthemata are shaped by the perceptual environment and how phantasmata carry the qualitative character of the situations in which they were originally formed. Subsequently, Daniel Gross's work on "uncomfortable situations" extends this environmental attunement into the domain of affect theory, arguing that "situations" are "environmental, including a social environment that can be quite specific like empathy exhaustion, and temporal, which sometimes means historical in the precise, professional sense of the word" (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, p. 154)[5]. The resonance of aisthemata is thus never purely individual or psychological; it is shaped by the social, material, and temporal environment in which perception occurs and in which phantasmata are subsequently reactivated.

Jacob von Uexküll's concept of the Umwelt, extended by later biosemiotic theorists, provides a complementary framework for understanding how organisms inhabit perceptual worlds that are structured by species-specific capacities and environmental affordances (von Uexkull, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans*, pp. 254–256). The resonant aisthemata of a given organism are always aisthemata of its Umwelt—its meaningful environment—and the phantasmata that arise from these residual motions are accordingly shaped by the particular perceptual-motor repertoire of the organism in question. For human beings, this Umwelt is constitutively social and linguistic, which means that the resonant motions grounding phantasia and desire are already inflected by the rhetorical situations in which they were formed and in which they are reactivated.

#

## 5. Conclusion: Resonance as the Architectonic Principle of Phantasia and Pathos

The argument developed in the preceding the persisting formal motions of sense—the resonant aisthemata—constitute the indispensable material ground upon which phantasia, desire, and rhetorical pathos are built. These residual kinetic impressions, generated by the activity of perception but persisting beyond the immediate presence of the sensible object, furnish the content of imagination across both its sensory and deliberative grades. They make possible the constructive, combinatory, and evaluative operations of phantasia that underlie practical reasoning and emotional response alike. The affective dimension of these resonant motions is what enables rhetoric to move audiences: by activating, shaping, and intensifying the phantasmata of listeners through vivid description, metaphor, and strategic narrative, the skilled orator exploits the very structure of perceptual residue to produce the emotional responses that Aristotle identifies as central to persuasion. The situational and ambient character of this resonance, illuminated by contemporary work in affect theory, ambient rhetoric, and biosemiotics, further reveals that the motions grounding phantasia are never isolated psychological events but are always already embedded in the material, social, and temporal environments that constitute the rhetor's and the audience's shared world. Accordingly, any adequate theory of rhetorical pathos must attend not only to the propositional content of persuasive discourse but also, and perhaps more fundamentally, to the resonant aisthemata through which discourse achieves its affective and imaginative force.
