# Environments of Disclosure: Aristotle, Heidegger, and the Structuring of the Situated World

## 1. Aristotle disclos

The question of how environments are disclosed to situated subjects finds its earliest systematic treatment in Aristotle's metaphysical and physical inquiries, where the articulation of being is never merely an abstract taxonomic exercise but rather a sustained effort to show how beings present themselves within a structured horizon of intelligibility. For Aristotle, disclosure is bound to the notion that beings come to presence through determinate principles; specifically, the doctrine of the four causes (material, formal, efficient, and final) establishes the framework within which any entity can be encountered as something rather than nothing. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle insists that being is said in many ways — that unity and being "follow the categories in as many ways" and that "to be one is just to be a particular thing" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 116)[4]. Indeed, matter is defined as "that which in itself is neither a particular thing nor of a certain quantity nor assigned to any other of the categories by which being is determined" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 75)[5], which means that matter achieves determinacy — and thus becomes disclosable — only when it is organized by form. The disclosure of beings in Aristotle's *Physics* proceeds through a parallel inquiry into nature (φύσις) as a principle internal to things themselves. Nature is not an external imposition upon inert material; rather, it constitutes "a principle or cause of being moved and of being at rest in that to which it belongs primarily, in virtue of itself and not accidentally" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 15–17)[6]. Accordingly, a stone falls and fire rises not because some agent compels them from without, but because their own natures disclose them as beings-in-motion toward their proper places. This internal principle of disclosure has profound consequences for understanding how environments are structured for the perceiving subject: the world does not appear as a flat field of neutral stimuli but as an ordered domain of natural tendencies, potentialities, and actualities. The analogy with production is instructive; Aristotle notes that "a house comes from certain things in a certain state of separation instead of conjunction, a statue (or any other thing that has been shaped) from shapelessness — each of these objects being partly order and partly composition" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 8–10)[7]. Thus, whether we consider natural or artificial objects, disclosure requires a passage from indeterminacy to determinacy, from potentiality to actuality, and it is this passage that constitutes the environment as an intelligible whole for the situated being who inhabits it. 

## 2. Aristotle phantasia Imagination φαντασία

Phantasia (φαντασία) occupies a peculiar and contested position in Aristotle's psychology, operating at the threshold between perception and thought and thereby mediating the disclosure of the environment to the ensouled subject. sight or seeing: imagination takes place in the absence of both, as e.g. in dreams" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 42)[8]. This negative definition is crucial, for it establishes phantasia as a capacity that retains and re-presents sensory content even when the original perceptual act has ceased, thereby extending the scope of disclosure beyond the immediate sensory present. The environment, as disclosed through phantasia, is not confined to what is currently perceived; it encompasses what has been perceived, what might be perceived, and what is constructed through the imaginative combination of sensory residues. Specifically, Aristotle introduces a fundamental internal distinction within phantasia between its sensory and deliberative grades. In *De Anima* III.11, he writes: "Sensitive imagination, as we have said, is found in all animals, deliberative imagination only in those that are calculative: for whether this or that shall be enacted is already a task requiring calculation; and there must be a single standard to measure by, for that is pursued which is greater" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)[1]*, p. 52)[9]. Sensitive phantasia, shared by all animals, retains the traces of sensation and enables rudimentary orientation within an environment — a capacity necessary for any organism that must navigate a world of predators and prey, obstacles and affordances. Deliberative phantasia, by contrast, is reserved for beings capable of rational calculation and involves the construction of images of future goods and evils, thereby making possible practical deliberation and choice. Christina Papachristou has explored these internal distinctions with particular care, noting that phantasia resists simple translation into modern terms. As she observes, "Even though the Greek word 'phantasia' is usually translated it as 'imagination', I prefer to leave the word untranslated" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 9–10)[10]. This terminological caution underscores the irreducibility of Aristotle's concept, which cannot be collapsed into either the Kantian productive imagination or the empiricist notion of a mental image. The relation of phantasia to belief (doxa) is similarly complex and will receive extended treatment in a subsequent chapter; here it suffices to note that phantasia, unlike belief, is voluntary — we can summon images before our eyes at will — and yet it arises from the kinesis generated by actual sensation, creating a tension between its causal derivation and its functional autonomy in domains such as dreaming, rhetoric, and deliberation. This tension, as we shall see, is productive rather than debilitating, for it is precisely the semi-autonomous character of phantasia that allows the environment to be disclosed not merely as it is but as it might be. 

## 3. To understand how environments are disclosed as mattering in particular ways — as threatening or inviting, as familiar or strange — we must attend to the affective dimension of being-in-the-world, a dimension that Aristotle addresses both in his natural philosophy and in his analysis of rhetorical and ethical situations. In the *Physics*, Aristotle articulates the principle that order, attunement, and composition are fundamental to the coming-into-being of any entity; indeed, "it does not matter whether we take attunement, order, or composition for our illustration; the principle is obviously the same in all, and in fact applies equally to the production of a house, a statue, or anything else" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 8–10)[11]. Attunement, in this natural-philosophical register, is not a subjective feeling but an objective structural principle: things are what they are in virtue of being attuned, ordered, and composed in determinate ways. The environment, accordingly, is disclosed as an attuned whole rather than a mere aggregate of disconnected parts. Heidegger, in his early Aristotle lectures, recognized the significance of this Aristotelian insight and radicalized it into an existential-ontological principle. Yet the roots of attunement as a mode of world-disclosure lie firmly in Aristotle's analysis of the πάθη. As Heidegger notes in his reading of Aristotle, "these πάθη, 'affects,' are not states pertaining to ensouled things, but are concerned with a disposition of living things in their world, in the mode of being positioned toward something, allowing a matter to matter to it" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 96–98)[12]. This passage is decisive, for it reframes the affects not as inner psychological states that merely color an already-constituted world but as modes of being-in-the-world through which the environment is primordially disclosed. When fear assails us, the world is disclosed as threatening; when confidence pervades, the world is disclosed as manageable. Thomas Rickert has similarly emphasized this connection, arguing that attunement is the fundamental disclosure of the world that precedes cognition, volition, and intention. Rickert draws attention to the way mood "assails us," arising "out of Being-in-the-world, as a way of such Being," such that "the mood has already disclosed, in every case, Being-in-the-world as a whole, and makes it possible first of all to direct itself towards something" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 33)[13]. Thus the Aristotelian πάθη, read through the lens of Heideggerian attunement, reveal that disclosure is never affectively neutral: the environment comes to us always already in a mood. 

## 4. Aristotle attunem

If attunement in its broadest sense denotes the structural harmony through which beings present themselves as intelligible wholes, then Aristotle's metaphysical and physical analyses provide the ontological ground upon which such attunement rests. The notion that being is always being-in-a-certain-way — that substance is never bare but always qualified, quantified, and related — implies that every encounter with an entity is already an encounter with a specific configuration of form and matter, actuality and potentiality. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle explicates this through his analysis of completeness (τέλειον), noting that we "call complete (1) that outside which it is not possible to find even one of the parts proper to it" and "(2) that which in respect of excellence and goodness cannot be excelled in its kind, e.g. a doctor is complete and a flute-player is complete, when they lack nothing in respect of their proper kind of excellence" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 64–65)[14]. Completeness is thus a form of attunement: the complete entity is one whose parts are harmoniously ordered toward their proper end, and its disclosure as complete depends upon the situated subject's capacity to perceive this order. Subsequently, the relationship between potentiality and actuality further elaborates this attunement. Aristotle observes that "the potentiality, being, as matter, universal and indefinite, deals with the universal and indefinite; but the actuality, being definite, deals with a definite object — being a 'this', it deals with a 'this'" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 168–169)[15]. This means that the environment, insofar as it is composed of actualized beings, is disclosed as a field of definite, particular entities — each one a "this" — rather than as an amorphous potentiality. The transition from potentiality to actuality is, in this respect, a transition from indeterminate attunement to determinate disclosure. The ensouled subject does not encounter raw matter but always already formed things, and the act of perception itself, as Aristotle develops it in *De Anima*, involves the soul's capacity to receive the form of things without their matter. Accordingly, perceptual attunement is the soul's way of being in-tune with the formal structures of the environment; to perceive is to be attuned to the forms that organize the sensible world. The *Physics* complements this picture by emphasizing that natural things are what they are in virtue of an internal principle of motion and rest (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 15–17)[16], so that attunement is not imposed from without but arises from the inherent self-ordering of nature itself. 

## 5. For Aristotle, different kinds of soul (nutritive, sensitive, rational) disclose different kinds of worlds: the plant is attuned to nourishment and growth, the animal to sensation and locomotion, and the human being to thought and deliberation. This graduated ontology implies that the environment is not a single, objective container but a field differentially structured by the perceptual and cognitive capacities of the inhabiting organism. 259–261). The Umwelt is thus neither a subjective projection nor an objective given but the meaningful environment that emerges from the specific perceptual and effectual capacities of each organism. Aristotle's distinction between sensitive and deliberative phantasia, discussed above, precisely maps onto this graduated concept of environment. Organisms endowed only with sensory phantasia inhabit Umwelten structured by immediate perceptual signs — the perception marks (Merkzeichen)[17] and action marks (Wirkzeichen)[18] that Uexküll describes. Those endowed with deliberative phantasia, by contrast, inhabit worlds that extend into temporal horizons of possibility and choice (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 52)[19]. Similarly, in the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle's insistence that being is said in many ways and that unity "follows the categories in as many ways" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 116)[20] suggests that the environment is always already categorially structured for the being that encounters it. The Aristotelian world is thus an Umwelt avant la lettre: it is disclosed not as a homogeneous space of Newtonian extension but as a qualitatively differentiated field of meaningful encounters, organized by the organism's own mode of being. In the *Physics*, the principle of natural motion confirms this: each kind of being has its own natural place toward which it tends (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 15–17)[21], so that the environment is structured by the inherent teleological orientation of its inhabitants. 

## 6. For Aristotle, perception is not a passive reception of external stimuli but an active assimilation of form: the sense organ receives the sensible form of the object without its matter, as wax receives the impression of a signet ring. This celebrated analogy implies that perception is already a kind of disclosure, an event in which the formal structure of the environment is taken up into the life of the perceiving soul. The distinction between proper and common sensibles — color belonging to sight, sound to hearing, but magnitude, shape, and motion being perceptible to more than one sense — further articulates the way in which the environment is disclosed through multiple, overlapping perceptual modalities. Aristotle's insistence that phantasia differs from sense because "sense is either a faculty or an activity, e.g. sight or seeing: imagination takes place in the absence of both, as e.g. in dreams" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)[2]*, p. 42)[22] serves not only to demarcate phantasia from aisthesis but also to illuminate the distinctive character of perceptual disclosure: it is bound to the presence of the sensible object, whereas phantasia liberates content from this dependency. Indeed, perception for Aristotle is deeply implicated in the broader metaphysical framework of potentiality and actuality. The sense organ is potentially what the sensible object is actually; in the act of perception, the organ is actualized by the form of the object, achieving a kind of identity with it. The *Metaphysics* elucidates this further by insisting that actuality is always prior to potentiality in a decisive sense — "the actuality, being definite, deals with a definite object — being a 'this', it deals with a 'this'" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 168–169)[23]. Thus, the perceptual act is an act of definite disclosure: what is perceived is always a particular, determinate entity in its qualitative specificity. Heidegger's later retrieval of Aristotle would radicalize this insight, arguing that disclosure (Erschlossenheit)[24] is not a cognitive act layered upon a prior non-disclosive state but is constitutive of being-in-the-world as such (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 172–173)[25]. Similarly, the rhetorical tradition recognized that perception is never neutral but always shaped by the dispositions, habits, and attunements of the perceiver — a point to which we shall return in the section on rhetoric. 

## 7. Aristotle rhetoric rhetorike

Rhetoric (ῥητορική) in Aristotle's framework is not merely an art of persuasion but a mode of environmental disclosure through which the public world of shared deliberation is constituted and navigated. If perception discloses the natural environment and phantasia extends this disclosure beyond the sensory present, rhetoric discloses the social and political environment — the world of common opinion (δόξα), shared values, and contested meanings. Aristotle himself indicates the disclosive function of rhetoric when he observes in the *Rhetoric* that "the narration should depict character; to which end you must know what makes it do so. One such thing is the indication of choice; the quality of purpose indicated determines the quality of character depicted and is itself determined by the end pursued" (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 114)[26]. Here, narrative discourse does not merely report events but discloses the character of agents and the teleological structure of their actions, thereby organizing the social environment into an intelligible field of purposes and motives. Heidegger's early recognition of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* as a fundamental hermeneutic of shared human existence underscores this point. Rickert emphasizes that Heidegger praised "Aristotle's Rhetoric as 'the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being with one another'" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 33)[27]. Accordingly, rhetoric is not a secondary embellishment upon a pre-given world but a primary mode through which the shared environment of the πόλις is disclosed and sustained. Heidegger's own reading of the πόλις in his Aristotle lectures confirms this, where he argues that "the πόλις, a characteristic way of being-together, is not brought to humans by chance, but rather that the πόλις is the being-possibility, φύσει, that itself lies enclosed and traced out in advance in the human being's genuine being" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 50)[28]. The rhetorical environment is thus not an artificial construction imposed upon natural existence but an actualization of a potentiality inherent in human nature itself. Kenneth Burke similarly conceives rhetoric as bound to structures of identification and hierarchy, noting that rhetoric involves "kinds of identification" that shape how agents orient themselves within social orders (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, p. 147)[29]. Hence, rhetoric discloses the environment as a field of persuasive forces, social identifications, and hierarchical positions within which the situated subject must continually orient itself. 

## 8. Aristotle stimmung Attunement / Stimmung

Returning to the question of Stimmung with fuller resources, we may now appreciate how Aristotle's account of the πάθη constitutes a proto-phenomenology of attunement that anticipates and grounds later Heideggerian developments. The πάθη — fear, anger, pity, confidence, shame — are not incidental psychological colorings but ontologically significant dispositions through which the environment is disclosed in its affective significance. When Heidegger interprets Aristotle's πάθη as concerned with "a disposition of living things in their world, in the mode of being positioned toward something, allowing a matter to matter to it" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 96–98)[30], he is drawing out a dimension of Aristotle's thought that the tradition has often obscured by reducing the affects to merely subjective states. Indeed, the common tendency to partition "the phenomenon into bodily states and states of the soul — states that stand in some connection" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 134)[31] misses the fundamental point that the πάθη are modes of world-disclosure, not inner occurrences that subsequently attach to external objects. This reading finds support in Aristotle's own natural philosophy, where the structural principle of attunement — the harmonious ordering of parts toward a whole — governs the coming-into-being of both natural and artificial entities (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 8–10)[32]. The attuned whole is not merely the sum of its parts but a qualitative unity whose character depends upon the specific way in which its elements are ordered. Similarly, the πάθη attune the situated subject to the environment in specific ways, disclosing particular features as salient and others as recessive. The *Metaphysics* further clarifies this by arguing that actuality always has a determinate character — it "deals with a definite object — being a 'this', it deals with a 'this'" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 168–169)[33] — which implies that affective attunement is never free-floating but always directed toward particular beings in their concrete determinacy. Rickert's concept of ambient rhetoric develops this Aristotelian-Heideggerian lineage into a contemporary framework in which rhetoric itself is reconceived as a mode of attunement, an always-ongoing disclosure that shifts our manner of being in the world. 33), thus confirming that the environment is never affectively neutral but always already pervaded by a Stimmung that shapes what can appear and how it matters. 

## 9. Heidegger disclos

Heidegger's existential analytic in *Being and Time* radicalizes and transforms the Aristotelian insights discussed above into a systematic account of disclosure (Erschlossenheit)[34] as the fundamental structure of Dasein's being-in-the-world. For Heidegger, disclosure is not an act performed by a subject upon an object but the very condition under which beings can show themselves at all; it is constituted by three equiprimordial existentiales: state-of-mind (Befindlichkeit)[35], understanding (Verstehen)[36], and discourse (Rede)[37]. As Heidegger writes, what "we indicate ontologically by the term 'state-of-mind' is ontically the most familiar and everyday sort of thing; our mood, our Being-attuned" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 172–173)[38]. This passage is crucial because it reveals that the most ordinary and pervasive feature of human existence — that we always find ourselves in some mood or other — is simultaneously the most ontologically fundamental: it is through mood that the world is primordially disclosed as mattering in a specific way. Heidegger's retrieval of Aristotle is central to this account. In his early lecture course on the basic concepts of Aristotelian philosophy, Heidegger interprets λόγος not merely as propositional assertion but as a mode of disclosive showing. 43). This interpretation transforms λόγος from a logical operator into a phenomenon of disclosure: to speak is to let beings show themselves in their determinacy, and hence to structure the environment as an intelligible domain. The bridge to *Being and Time* is immediate, for there Dasein's discursive existence — its capacity for articulation and interpretation — is one of the three constitutive moments of disclosure. Subsequently, the concept of the "there" (Da)[39] in Dasein names precisely the clearing or openness within which beings can be encountered; it is the disclosed environment as such, always already structured by mood, understanding, and language. Thus, Heidegger's disclosure transforms the Aristotelian framework of categorial articulation and affective disposition into a unified existential structure that explains how environments are always already meaningfully constituted for the situated subject. 

## 10. Heidegger phantasia Imagination φαντασία

Heidegger's engagement with Aristotle's concept of φαντασία is not merely a historical exercise but a retrieval that illuminates the deeper structures of being-in-the-world. The crucial locus is *De Anima* III.8, where Aristotle advances the thesis that "the soul is in a way all existing things" — a claim Heidegger repeatedly engages because it anticipates his own account of Dasein as the being for whom beings are disclosed in their being. Phantasia, as the intermediary between sense-perception and thought, is the faculty through which this universal openness to beings becomes operative. 52). Heidegger's existential analytic transposes this graduated disclosure into the structure of Dasein's understanding (Verstehen)[40], which projects upon possibilities and thereby extends the disclosed environment beyond the immediate sensory given. Papachristou's careful textual analysis underscores the significance of leaving φαντασία untranslated, since "the Greek word 'phantasia' is usually translated it as 'imagination'" but the translation obscures Aristotle's distinctive meaning (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 9–10)[41]. Heidegger similarly resists any reduction of Aristotle's psychological vocabulary to modern cognitive categories; in the *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, he reads the affective and imaginative life of the soul as modes of being-in-the-world rather than as inner mental states. The πάθη, as Heidegger insists, are not "states pertaining to ensouled things" but dispositions of "living things in their world" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 96–98)[42], and a similar interpretive principle applies to phantasia: it is not a screen of mental images interposed between subject and world but a mode of the soul's being-open-to the world in its absence as well as its presence. The distinction between phantasia and perception — that imagination "takes place in the absence of both" faculty and activity (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 42)[43] — thus acquires existential significance: phantasia is Dasein's capacity to dwell with what is not currently present, to project beyond the immediate situation, and thereby to constitute a temporal horizon of meaning within which the environment is disclosed as extending beyond the here and now. Hence, the Aristotelian-Heideggerian bridge concerning phantasia reveals that world-disclosure is never confined to the actual but always encompasses the possible, the remembered, and the imagined — dimensions without which the situated subject's environment would collapse into bare stimulus-response. This convergence between Aristotle's φαντασία and Heidegger's Erschlossenheit is further illuminated by the Multiple Authors volume on *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, where the rhetorical perspective is described as "ineluctably temporal" and concerned with "the importance of sensitivity to time" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 130–131)[3]. Phantasia, as the faculty that liberates sensory content from temporal immediacy, is thus a condition of possibility for the rhetorical mode of environmental disclosure — a mode that addresses beings not merely as present actualities but as past exempla and future possibilities, oriented by the Aristotelian μεσότης and the Heideggerian notion of authentic temporality. Accordingly, the retrieval of phantasia is not an antiquarian project but a philosophically necessary step toward understanding how the environment is constituted as a meaningful, temporally extended, and affectively charged field of disclosure for the situated being. ---
