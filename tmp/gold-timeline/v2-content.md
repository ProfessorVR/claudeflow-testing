# Aristotle on Motion, Time, and the Faculties of Temporal Awareness: An Inquiry into Phantasia, Aisthēsis, and the Heideggerian Inheritance

## 1. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding

Aristotle's philosophical enterprise is distinguished by a persistent commitment to disclosing the being of beings through careful attention to the structures of experience, language, and worldly engagement. Kenneth Burke, in his exploration of dramatistic terminology, identifies the deep interrelation between essence and temporality in Aristotle's thought, noting the ways in which logical priority and temporal priority become interchangeable in narrative and philosophical discourse; as Burke writes, we may observe "the puns of logical and temporal priority whereby the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 28). This observation is significant because it reveals how Aristotle's metaphysics is never merely static or abstractly categorical but is, from the outset, attuned to the dynamic unfolding of things in time. Indeed, Thomas Rickert situates this attunement within a broader ontological horizon, arguing that rhetoric itself must be understood as an emergent phenomenon inseparable from material, temporal, and environmental conditions; for Rickert, rhetoric is "an emergent result of environmentally situated and interactive engagements, redolent of a world that affects us, that persuades us prior to symbolicity" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 54). Thus, Aristotle's understanding encompasses both a formal analysis of substance and an awareness that being is disclosed through change, motion, and the temporal comportments of living creatures. Similarly, the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* emphasize that Heidegger's recovery of Aristotle's thought foregrounds kinesis as a fundamental mode of being, whereby "kinesis gives the authentic Da-Charakter to Dasein, the vital place-time of Being" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Accordingly, to articulate Aristotle's understanding is to confront a philosophical vision in which substance, change, perception, and temporality are woven into an indissociable fabric. ## 2. Aristotle on Motion

Motion, or kinesis, occupies a central place in Aristotle's natural philosophy, serving not merely as an observable phenomenon but as an ontological category through which being itself is disclosed. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle insists that the study of nature must attend to the principles and causes of change, since the natural world is constituted by substances that are in motion or at rest; hence, the inquiry into motion is simultaneously an inquiry into being as such (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 176). The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* illuminate this point by demonstrating that Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's *Physics* recasts kinesis not as something superadded to beings but as a fundamental way that Dasein is; as stated directly, "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 27). The interanimation of pathēsis and poiētikos in Aristotle's definition of motion is thus not simply a technical matter but one that, as Heidegger recognized, "reaches into the true center of the Greek view of the world and life" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 27). Ned O'Gorman further develops this trajectory by showing how motion in the Aristotelian corpus extends beyond physical displacement to encompass psychic processes; specifically, phantasia is itself a kind of motion in the soul, one that mediates between sensation and thought, thereby enabling the deliberative and cognitive activities essential to rhetorical practice (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). Thus, Aristotle's account of motion traverses the domains of physics, psychology, and rhetoric, disclosing in each case the dynamic character of being. ## 3. Aristotle on Time

Time, for Aristotle, is intimately related to motion and yet possesses its own distinctive structure as a measure of change. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle inquires into the relationship between temporal sequence and the principles of substance, asking how the before and after of time correspond to the ontological priority of form, matter, and actuality (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 176). Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotelian temporality proves indispensable here, for in *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, Heidegger reads the Greek conception of being as fundamentally oriented toward presence—what he calls Anwesenheit—such that time is not an empty container but rather the horizon within which beings show themselves. Heidegger observes that Aristotle's study of nature yields insight into the manner in which truth itself compels the researcher toward a disclosure of beings; (Hawhee 2011), Empedocles was "led by truth itself," and Democritus was "borne by the matter itself" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 176–177). The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* elaborate this account by noting that Aristotle's treatment of the passions reveals a profound sensitivity to temporal dimensions of affect, wherein fear, for instance, is oriented toward a future that is not yet present but which nevertheless intimately shapes one's being-in-the-world; specifically, fear possesses a tense that is future, a voice that is subjunctive, and a mode that is the possible (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Hence, Aristotle's account of time is not merely quantitative; it is bound up with the lived, affective, and perceptual experience of temporal beings. ## 4. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The relation between motion and time in Aristotle's philosophy is one of mutual implication: time is the number of motion with respect to the before and after, while motion cannot be conceived apart from the temporal horizon within which it unfolds. John Bowin's analysis clarifies that for Aristotle, the perception and cognition of time depend upon the soul's capacity to discriminate between successive phases of a motion, such that time is neither purely objective nor purely subjective but emerges at the intersection of worldly change and psychic awareness (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 1). Rickert extends this insight by arguing that the temporality of rhetorical engagement—what he theorizes as kairos—is itself inseparable from the material, spatial, and environmental conditions in which a situation arises; (Hawhee 2011), "kairos needs to be thought of ambiently, akin to Heidegger's understanding of the Umwelt, or the surrounding world" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 109). Burke, similarly, addresses the temporal character of transformation when (Hawhee 2011) essence can be stated narratively in terms of fulfillment or fruition: "Thus,  man's timeless essence in temporal terms if, instead of calling him 'by nature a criminal,' you say, 'he will end on the gallows'" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 28). Accordingly, the relation between motion and time is not one of simple correlation but of ontological entanglement, wherein the experience of temporal flow is constituted through the soul's engagement with a world in motion. ## 5. Aisthēsis and Temporal Awareness

The faculty of aisthēsis, or sense perception, provides the primary ground upon which temporal awareness is built in Aristotle's psychology. It is through perception that the soul is first brought into contact with change, for without the registering of successive sensory states there could be no apprehension of the before and after that constitutes time. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* underscore this point by noting that fear, as Aristotle analyzes it, becomes present to us not by direct experience alone but through the mediating activity of imagination: "Fear becomes present to us not by direct experience, aisthesis, but through our imagination, phantasia; fancies replace sensations as stimulus" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). This displacement of aisthēsis by phantasia in the temporal experience of fear reveals the complexity of Aristotle's perceptual psychology; indeed, existence is disclosed not through brute sensation but through signs and anticipatory images that furnish the immediate character of our affective life. Burke, for his part, demonstrates how the symbolic and perceptual dimensions of experience are interwoven in human motivation, emphasizing that our engagement with the world is always mediated by imagery and interpretive frameworks that shape what we perceive and how we respond (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Thus, aisthēsis is not a passive reception of data but an active, temporally inflected mode of being-in-the-world, one that cooperates with higher faculties to generate the continuity of temporal experience. We might therefore ask: does temporal awareness arise solely within perception, or does it require the synthetic activity of a further faculty? This question leads us directly to the role of phantasia. ## 6. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

Phantasia, as Aristotle articulates it in *De Anima* and as it is deployed in the *Rhetoric*, serves as the faculty by which discrete sensory impressions are unified into a continuous temporal experience and made available for deliberation, memory, and imagination. 4). Phantasia thus underlies and mediates between sense perception and the mind, furnishing the images upon which deliberation—what Aristotle calls bouleutikē—depends. O'Gorman  appearances are inherently unstable and often unreliable, since phantasia may misconstrue its objects under the influence of time, temperament, and emotion; accordingly, "there is no need to assume any precise correspondence between a phantasma and that which it is a phantasma of" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* reinforce this point by observing that Aristotle's account of fear depends on phantasmata as the medium through which a destructive future evil is made present, noting the discursive mode of this phenomenon: "existence is an announcement, and it is announced through signs, semeia, not facts" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Burke's analysis of symbolic transformation adds a further dimension, showing how imagistic and narrative structures shape the way temporal experience is given meaning and coherence in both literary and rhetorical contexts (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Hence, phantasia enables the unification of temporal experience by bridging the gap between perception and thought, furnishing the imaginative substrate upon which the continuity of past, present, and future is constructed. ## 7. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle transforms the latter's natural philosophy and psychology into a proto-existential analytic of Dasein, disclosing dimensions of Aristotle's thought that had been obscured by centuries of scholastic reception. In *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, Heidegger argues that the Greek study of nature is not merely an empirical investigation but an ontological inquiry into the manner in which beings disclose themselves; (Hawhee 2011) Aristotle shifted the weight of philosophical inquiry onto the examination of physis as being, thereby attaining "the ground for the study of being as such" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 177). Heidegger's reading of kinesis as constitutive of Dasein's being transforms the Aristotelian categories from static logical structures into dynamic existential modes; as the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* emphasize, the ontological significance of the passions lies in the capacity for change, and the pathē are modes of being-taken with respect to being-in-the-world (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Bowin's analysis supplements this reading by showing that the perception and cognition of time, as Aristotle articulates them, require the soul to discriminate between successive phases of motion, a capacity that Heidegger would later thematize as ecstatic temporality (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 1). Furthermore, Heidegger reads Aristotle's definition of motion through the interanimation of poiētikos and pathētikos, demonstrating that the very structure of kinesis entails an ambiguity of activity and passivity that prefigures his own account of Dasein's being-in-the-world (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 27). Aristotle, in the *Metaphysics*, provides the conceptual ground for this recovery by maintaining that the inquiry into first principles is inseparable from the investigation of change and becoming (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 176). Thus, Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle does not simply appropriate ancient philosophy for modern ends; rather, it reveals the latent existential and temporal dimensions of Aristotle's thought, demonstrating that the question of being was already, in the Greek beginning, a question of time. ---
