# Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Temporal Perception: From Aisthēsis to Phantasia

## 1. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding

Aristotle's philosophical project, taken in its fullest scope, endeavors to articulate the being of beings not as static essences abstractly detached from the world of change and appearance but as structures of disclosure fundamentally entangled with the lived, situated, temporal character of human existence. To grasp what Aristotle understands by being, one must attend to the manner in which his inquiry proceeds through the concrete phenomena of motion, perception, and the soul's capacities for holding together the manifold of experience—capacities that, as we shall see, centrally involve phantasia and aisthēsis. Kenneth Burke, in his analysis of dramatistic motivation, draws our attention to the ways in which essence and temporality are intertwined in philosophical discourse: the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent, such that we  person's nature not through abstract predication but through the trajectory of their becoming and fulfillment (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 28). This insight is not merely a modern rhetorical observation; it reaches back to the heart of Aristotle's metaphysics, wherein the being of a thing is disclosed through its entelecheia—its maintaining-itself-in-being-completed, as Heidegger renders it (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 263). The ambient, embedded character of Aristotelian inquiry thus resists any reduction to static categories. As Thomas Rickert argues, rhetoric itself must be understood as an emergent result of environmentally situated and interactive engagements, a formulation that retrieves the Aristotelian insight that knowing and being are never separable from the concrete situations in which disclosure takes place (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 54). The understanding of being that Aristotle provides is therefore inseparable from the phenomena of motion and time, which together constitute the horizon against which all entities appear and are known. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle in the 1924 lectures underscores that the concepts of being-in-the-polis have their foundation in concepts of nature, and that Aristotle shifted the weight of his inquiry onto the examination of physis as being, thereby attaining the ground for the study of being as such (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 177). ## 2. Aristotle on Motion

Motion, kinēsis, occupies a central and irreducible place in Aristotle's natural philosophy, for it is the fundamental way in which beings disclose themselves as what they are. Aristotle defines kinēsis not as a property extrinsic to the thing moved but as a mode of being intrinsic to the entity undergoing change. As the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* emphasize, kinesis gives the authentic Da-Charakter to Dasein, the vital place-time of Being, such that the ontological significance of the passions lies in our capacity for change, the Veränderlichkeit of Being (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Motion is therefore never simply the displacement of an object across space; it is the very medium through which the world becomes present, the mode in which beings maintain or alter their characteristic form. Heidegger's reading of the Physics captures this point with remarkable precision, rendering the Aristotelian definition thus: motion is always motion of something moved, which is moved by another mover—a formulation in which the passive and active dimensions are fundamentally interanimate (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). The interanimation of pathēsis and poiētikos within kinēsis is, for Heidegger, no incidental feature; rather, it reaches into the true center of the Greek view of the world and life, such that the understanding of how the Greeks apprehended being depends upon how one understands kinēsis (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 27). 4). Thus motion is not merely a physical fact but a psychic and rhetorical reality, the very condition of all appearance and judgment. ## 3. Aristotle on Time

Time, for Aristotle, is not an empty container within which events happen to occur but is instead intimately bound up with motion and with the soul's capacity to perceive and mark change. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle presses the question of being toward its temporal dimensions, asking what it means for something to be present, to persist, or to pass away; the inquiry into time is thus an inquiry into the most fundamental structures of being. Heidegger's lectures on Aristotle bring this temporal dimension to the fore, reading being as being-present, Anwesenheit, in the Greek understanding—a reading that discloses the deep complicity between time and the disclosure of entities (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 263). Time is not, on this account, a sequence of discrete instants but a continuous unfolding that is disclosed only insofar as a perceiving soul marks the before and after of change. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* elaborate this point in their account of Aristotle's rhetoric as a radically timeful practice of changes, alterations designed to take account of past, present, and future in the respective genres of judicial, epideictic, and deliberative oratory (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Time is thus always already rhetorical—structured by the concerns, dispositions, and anticipations of the living being who experiences it. The Aristotelian thesis that the soul is in some sense the condition of time's reality is not, therefore, a form of idealism; it is rather an acknowledgment that time's disclosure depends upon the soul's capacity for aisthēsis and phantasia, capacities that are themselves temporal through and through. Heidegger captures the existential weight of this temporality when (Hawhee 2011) peculiar character of fear: it is oriented toward the future, conditioned by signs rather than present facts, and thus reveals the deep temporal structure of human being-in-the-world (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). ## 4. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The relation between motion and time in Aristotle's framework is one of mutual dependence and co-disclosure: motion is the substrate through which time becomes perceptible, and time is the measure or number through which motion acquires determinacy. Neither can be apprehended independently of the other, and both require the activity of a perceiving soul. Thomas Rickert's account of kairos as something ontological—inseparable from new forms of becoming, new forms of disclosure—captures this Aristotelian insight in a rhetorical key, for the kairotic moment is precisely the point at which motion and time converge in a singularity of disclosure that cannot be reduced to either subjective will or objective determination (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 108–109). John Bowin's analysis of Aristotle's account of temporal cognition further specifies the relation: the perception of time requires that the soul discriminate between the before and after of a given change, and this discrimination is itself a kind of psychic motion that unifies what would otherwise be a disconnected series of impressions (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*). Kenneth Burke's dramatistic framework provides an additional lens through which to view this relation, for the temporalizing of essence—the translation of logical priority into narrative sequence—mirrors the Aristotelian thesis that being is disclosed only through the unfolding of motion in time (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 28). The relation between motion and time is thus not merely a topic for physics; it is a fundamental condition of all rhetorical and philosophical disclosure, the ground upon which appearance, judgment, and persuasion rest. Indeed, to sever motion from time would be to render both unintelligible, for it is only in their mutual interplay that the world shows up as a structured, meaningful environment within which human beings orient themselves and act. ## 5. Aisthēsis and Temporal Awareness

Aisthēsis, or sense perception, is the primary mode through which the living being encounters the world as a temporal, changing reality. For Aristotle, perception is never merely passive reception; it is an active, discriminating capacity through which the soul grasps not only the qualities of things but also the temporal relations that structure experience. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* make this point forcefully, noting that the passions—which are themselves modes of perceiving and being affected—are not static knowledge, Wissen, but Befindlichkeiten, dispositions that orient us in and toward the world (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Perception, in its fullest Aristotelian sense, is always already a mode of being-in-the-world, and temporal awareness is an irreducible dimension of this mode. Fear, for instance, becomes present to us not by direct experience, aisthēsis, but through our imagination, phantasia, such that fancies replace sensations as stimulus; and existence is announced through signs, semeia, not facts (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Burke's account of identification and transformation in rhetorical motivation provides a complementary perspective: the imagery of life and death, of killing and being killed, functions rhetorically precisely because it engages the deepest temporal structures of human awareness—the sense of beginning and ending, of emergence and dissolution—that aisthēsis first opens up (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Temporal awareness is thus not an abstract cognition layered atop perception but is woven into the very texture of perceptual life; we perceive change, duration, and succession as features of the world itself, and this perceptual temporality is the foundation upon which all higher forms of temporal cognition—memory, anticipation, deliberation—are built. ## 6. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

Phantasia, the faculty of appearing or image-making, occupies a pivotal position in Aristotle's account of how the manifold of temporal experience is unified into coherent wholes available for judgment, deliberation, and action. As O'Gorman demonstrates, phantasia in Aristotle is not mere fantasy or illusion but a distinct psychic capacity that underlies and mediates between sense perception and the mind, preserving visual perceptions in a sort of after-image and serving as the source of mental images or visual impressions (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). It is through phantasia that the residues of past perceptions are held together with present experience and anticipations of the future, enabling the kind of deliberative synthesis that Aristotle identifies as essential to both practical reasoning and rhetorical persuasion. O'Gorman  Aristotle describes this phantasmatic mental activity as bouleutikē, designating deliberation as entailing the combination of mental images (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). The similarity between bouleutikē as a mental activity and as an activity of discourse is suggestive, as both may be understood as processes of taking appearances into account (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). Heidegger's reading of Aristotle in the 1924 lectures reinforces this point by showing that fear, as a paradigmatic pathos, depends upon phantasmata—mental pictures of future evil—rather than upon present sensation, and that it is through such phantasmatic projection that the temporal horizon of human existence is constituted (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Burke's analysis of the ritualistic and symbolic dimensions of transformation likewise depends upon phantasia's capacity to hold together disparate temporal moments—origin and ending, crime and punishment, death and rebirth—within a single imaginative act (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Phantasia is thus the faculty through which temporal experience achieves unity, the psychic capacity that makes possible both the coherence of individual experience and the shared, rhetorical construction of meaningful worlds. ## 7. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle, developed most fully in the 1924 summer semester lectures, represents a decisive retrieval of the temporal and kinetic dimensions of Aristotle's thought that had been obscured by centuries of scholastic and metaphysical reception. For Heidegger, Aristotle's categories are not static classificatory schemes but modes of disclosing the being of entities in their movement and temporality. 263). Aristotle himself, in the *Metaphysics*, presses the question of being toward its most fundamental determinations, asking what it means for something to be present and to persist through change; and Heidegger's reading retrieves this question as the unasked but operative question of all Greek philosophy (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*). Heidegger insists that kinēsis is not merely a physical phenomenon but a fundamental way that Dasein is, and (Hawhee 2011) the understanding of how the Greeks apprehended being depends upon how one understands kinēsis (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 27). Bowin's analysis of Aristotle's account of temporal perception provides a further specification: the soul's capacity to discriminate the before and after of change is itself a mode of being-in-time that cannot be abstracted from the concrete conditions of embodied, situated life (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*). Thus Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle is not merely a historical exercise but a philosophical retrieval—an effort to recover the original, lived sense of being, motion, and time that Aristotle articulated and that subsequent metaphysics progressively concealed. In this retrieval, the rhetorical, perceptual, and temporal dimensions of Aristotle's thought are shown to be inseparable, disclosing a conception of human existence as fundamentally kinetic, temporal, and world-involved. ---
