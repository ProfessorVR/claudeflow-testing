# Phantasia as the Soul's Temporal Medium

## I. Introduction: Phantasia as the Soul's Temporal Medium

The question of phantasia — what it is, how it functions, and why Aristotle accords it such a pivotal role in the architecture of the soul — has long remained one of the most contested topics in Aristotelian scholarship. Indeed, the difficulty arises not merely from the semantic breadth of the term, which resists any simple translation into "imagination" or "mental imagery," but from the structural position phantasia occupies between perception (aisthesis) and thought (noesis), between the body's reception of sensible form and the soul's capacity to deliberate, judge, and act. Thus phantasia is neither a mere residue of sensation nor an autonomous cognitive act; it is, rather, the mediating power through which sensory motion persists, transforms, and becomes available for higher-order operations of the soul. To understand phantasia adequately, we must situate it within the broader ontological framework of motion and time — the very conditions under which anything can appear, endure, and recur within the soul's experience. This chapter proposes a temporal reading of phantasia, one that traces its genesis through a sequence of ontological moments we designate T0 through T3. At T0, motion and time constitute the irreducible horizon within which all being and becoming unfold; at T1, the soul receives the first motion of perception, the pure affection of aisthesis; at T2, the residual motion that persists after the sensible object has departed generates an intermediate kinetic trace; and at T3, this trace crystallizes into the phantasma proper, a dual inscription of resonant motion and resonant affect. The temporal framework is not imposed externally upon Aristotle's account but is drawn from his own insistence, in the *Physics*, that time is the "number of motion in respect of the before and after" and that all change is intelligible only within the continuum of temporal succession (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 39). Specifically, the movement from T0 to T3 represents not a series of discrete instants but a continuous unfolding in which each stage is the necessary condition for the next, much as Heidegger's analysis of temporality reveals Dasein's Being as always already stretched along between past, present, and future (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 499). What distinguishes phantasia from aisthesis on the one hand and noesis on the other is precisely its temporal character — its capacity to hold and reproduce what is no longer present, to make the absent sensorially available. Phantasia is not a mixture of sensation and judgment, as Plato believed, but a distinct faculty rooted in the soul's kinetic constitution. As Papachristou has demonstrated, a careful reading of *De Anima* reveals that Aristotle discriminates not merely two but "three kinds or grades of phantasia: (a) Indefinite/indeterminate phantasia (ἀόριστος φαντασία)" and further grades connected to perception and deliberation respectively (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 25–27). The internal distinctions among sensory phantasia, which humans share with other animals, and deliberative or bouleutike phantasia, which belongs to rational beings alone, will be examined in detail in Chapter IV; here we note only that these grades correspond to different temporal depths of the phantasma's formation, ranging from the near-immediate afterimage to the complex deliberative image that informs practical reasoning. Similarly, the relation between phantasia and belief (doxa) — a relation Aristotle carefully circumscribes — and the question of phantasia's voluntariness, its susceptibility to being summoned at will, will receive sustained treatment in subsequent chapters. For now, it suffices to establish the ontological ground: phantasia is the soul's temporal medium, the faculty through which motion becomes memory, and memory becomes the material of thought.

## II. T0–T3: Motion, Time, and the Genesis of Phantasmata

Before we can analyze the individual stages of phantasmatic genesis, we must grasp the overarching logic that binds them into a coherent sequence. The movement from T0 to T3 is not a mechanical process of information transfer but an ontological unfolding in which the soul's capacity for being-affected (paschein) generates progressively more complex forms of kinetic presence. Aristotle's account of motion in the *Physics* provides the foundational grammar for this process: motion is the actuality of the potential qua potential, the passage from what something can be to what it is becoming (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 39). Accordingly, each transition from one temporal moment to the next is itself a motion — a further actualization of a capacity that was latent in the preceding stage. At T0, the world moves, and time measures that movement; at T1, the soul is moved by the sensible object; at T2, the soul continues to be moved even after the object's departure; at T3, this continuing motion crystallizes into a phantasma that bears both a kinetic trace and an affective valence. The philosophical challenge here is to explain how a motion that begins outside the soul — in the physical alteration of the sensory medium — comes to reside within the soul as an enduring image possessed of qualitative character. This is, in essence, the problem of emergence: how do novel properties arise from the interaction of lower-level components? The parallel with Aristotle's account is striking; the phantasma is an emergent property of the soul's kinetic system, arising from the complex interaction of sensory reception, residual motion, and the soul's own affective disposition. Thus the T0–T3 framework captures not merely a temporal sequence but an ontological stratification, each level of which introduces properties that cannot be reduced to those of the preceding level. Heidegger's existential analytic provides an additional lens through which to view this stratification. In *Being and Time*, temporality is not simply one feature of Dasein's existence among others; it is the "temporal way of describing this phenomenon" that discloses Dasein's Being as fundamentally stretched along the ecstatic horizons of past, present, and future (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 499). When we apply this insight to Aristotle's account of phantasia, we see that the phantasma is not merely an image that happens to occur in time but is itself a temporal structure — a holding-present of what has passed, a making-available of what might yet come. The phantasma, seemingly a simple image, subsumes within itself the complexity of an entire kinetic and temporal history. The sections that follow trace this history from its origin in motion and time (T0) through its culmination in the dual trace of the phantasma (T3).

### II.1 T0 — Motion and Time as Ontological Horizon

The ontological horizon within which phantasia becomes possible is constituted by motion (kinesis) and time (chronos). For Aristotle, motion is the most fundamental category of becoming; it is the actuality of what exists potentially insofar as it exists potentially, and it pervades every domain of natural existence. In the *Physics*, Aristotle insists that the infinite itself — the boundless continuum within which motion unfolds — "has this mode of existence: one thing is always being taken after another, and each thing that is taken is always finite, but always different" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 39–40). This characterization is crucial for understanding the temporal medium of phantasia, because it establishes that the continuum of motion is not a static substrate but a perpetual process of succession, each moment giving way to the next in an unbroken flow. Time, as the number of this motion with respect to before and after, is accordingly not a container within which events occur but the very measure of their occurrence — the articulation of the "before" and "after" that makes any succession intelligible. The now is not a fixed boundary but a potential division within the continuous flow of time, actualized only when a soul attends to the transition from one phase of motion to another. Hence, even at the most fundamental level — the level of T0, before any particular sensation has occurred — the conditions for phantasia are already in place, because time itself requires a soul capable of marking the now, and this marking is already a proto-perceptual act. The soul does not stand outside time and then enter it through perception; rather, the soul is always already within the temporal continuum, attuned to the motion that constitutes the world's becoming. Heidegger's reading of Greek ontology extends this insight by showing that Being, for the Greeks, is fundamentally understood as presence (Anwesenheit), the coming-to-presence of what is within the horizon of time. What Heidegger suggests is that the Greeks' understanding of Being as temporal presence already implies a perceptual subject for whom things appear — a subject whose encounter with the world is structured by the before and after of temporal succession. At T0, then, we have not merely physical motion and its temporal measure but the ontological readiness of the soul to receive the motions of the world. Aristotle's account in *Sense and Sensibilia* of how sensory media transmit motion between object and organ presupposes this readiness; specifically, the parts of the medium between a sensory organ and its object are affected in a sequential manner that mirrors the temporal structure of motion itself (Aristotle, *Sense and Sensibilia*, pp. 19–20). The medium's sequential affection is the physical counterpart of the soul's temporal receptivity, and together they constitute the horizon — T0 — within which phantasia will subsequently emerge.

### II.2 T1 — Aisthesis: First Motion and Pure Reception

At T1, the ontological horizon of motion and time becomes concretely actualized in the event of aisthesis — the soul's first reception of sensory motion from an external object. This is the moment at which the world impresses itself upon the soul, not by transferring matter but by communicating form through the sensory medium. Aristotle's account in *Sense and Sensibilia* makes clear that perception involves a genuine motion, an alteration of the sense organ by the sensible quality of the object; indeed, "the parts of media between a sensory organ and its object are not all affected at once — except in the case of light" (Aristotle, *Sense and Sensibilia*, pp. 19–20). The sequential character of this affection is significant: it means that even the first moment of perception unfolds in time, that reception is itself a temporal process rather than an instantaneous event. The soul at T1 is not merely passive in the sense of being inert; it is rather receptive in the sense of being ontologically prepared — attuned, in Heidegger's vocabulary — to receive the motion that the medium communicates. Heidegger's analysis of Dasein's thrownness (Geworfenheit) provides a structural parallel to this moment of pure reception. Dasein does not choose its attunement to the world; it finds itself always already disposed in a particular way, affected by what it encounters before any reflective act of judgment. Similarly, aisthesis at T1 is pre-reflective and pre-judgmental; it is the soul's being-affected by the world prior to any interpretive overlay. This is why Aristotle insists that phantasia is distinct from both perception and judgment: perception gives us the first motion, judgment evaluates it, but phantasia occupies the interval between them, holding the motion in a form that is neither raw sensation nor conceptual thought. Subsequently, the motion received at T1 does not simply vanish when the external object is removed; it persists as a residual kinetic trace, a continuing vibration within the soul's sensory apparatus. This persistence is the key to the transition from T1 to T2, and it is what distinguishes the living soul from a merely physical medium that ceases to be affected once the stimulus is withdrawn. Indeed, aisthesis at T1 is not a merely internal event but an ecological encounter in which the soul and the world interpenetrate through the medium of sensory motion. At T1, the complex configuration is the intersection of sensory organ, medium, and sensible object; the emergent state is the perceptual act itself, irreducible to any of its physical components taken in isolation. Thus T1 marks the genesis of experiential life — the moment at which mere physical motion becomes the soul's lived encounter with the world.

### II.3 T2 — Residual Motion and Apoleipomene Kinesis

The transition from T1 to T2 is marked by a phenomenon that Aristotle terms apoleipomene kinesis — the residual or "left-behind" motion that persists in the sensory apparatus after the external object has been removed. This concept is central to Aristotle's account of afterimages, dreams, and the broader phenomenon of phantasia, because the soul can entertain appearances in the absence of the objects that originally caused them. In *Sense and Sensibilia*, Aristotle provides a vivid illustration of this residual motion: the phenomenon of visual persistence, in which an image lingers after one has looked at a bright object, occurs because the motion imparted to the sense organ continues to propagate even when the stimulus ceases. As Aristotle notes, "the phenomenon does not occur unless the motion is rapid and takes place in darkness" (Aristotle, *Sense and Sensibilia*, pp. 5–6). The conditions he specifies — rapidity and darkness — reveal that residual motion is not a marginal epiphenomenon but a lawful consequence of the kinetic nature of perception; when the external stimulus is strong enough and subsequent stimulation is minimized, the persisting motion becomes perceptible in its own right. What is philosophically significant about T2 is that it represents a moment of ontological ambiguity: the soul is moved, but not by an external object currently present; it is affected, but the affection is a trace rather than a direct impression. This ambiguity is precisely what makes phantasia possible and also what makes it susceptible to error — for the soul may take a residual motion for a fresh perception, or may confuse the trace of one object with the presence of another. The motion at T2, in Aristotle's terms, exists in an intermediate mode; it is neither the full actuality of aisthesis nor the complete absence of motion, but rather a potentiality that persists in the sense organ and in the common sense. Indeed, Aristotle's broader account of perceptual thresholds in *Sense and Sensibilia* supports this reading; "the increment of sense-perception is not perceptible in itself, nor capable of separate existence (since it exists only potentially in the more distinctly perceivable whole of sense-perception)" (Aristotle, *Sense and Sensibilia*, pp. 17–18). The residual motion at T2 similarly exists in a potential mode — it is part of the perceptual whole without being distinctly perceivable as a separate entity. The motion that persists at T2 retains something of the informational structure of the original perception; it is not random noise but a patterned residue that preserves, however imperfectly, the formal character of the sensible object. Hence, the transition from T2 to T3 is not a leap from chaos to order but a further articulation of a kinetic pattern that was already nascent in the moment of original reception. Accordingly, T2 serves as the crucial intermediate stage at which the raw motion of aisthesis begins its transformation into the complex, form-bearing phantasma.

### II.4 T3 — Phantasmata and the Dual Trace: Resonant Motion and Resonant Affect

At T3, the residual motion of T2 crystallizes into the phantasma proper — an internal appearance that bears a dual character, being at once a kinetic trace (a persisting pattern of motion in the soul's sensory apparatus) and an affective inscription (a qualitative coloring that disposes the soul toward pursuit or avoidance, desire or aversion). The concept of a dual trace is essential because it captures what is distinctive about phantasmata as opposed to mere afterimages or mechanical reverberations: the phantasma is not only a motion but a motion that matters, a motion that carries with it the soul's affective response to the original object. Gonzalez's reading of Aristotle's *De Anima* underscores this point; "the movers of the animal are reasoning and phantasia and choice and wish and appetite" (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 24–25). Phantasia thus stands at the intersection of cognition and desire; the phantasma is not merely an image to be contemplated but an appearance that moves the soul toward action. Translating this into Aristotelian terms: the emergent perceptual state at T1 (the first motion of aisthesis) generates a residual kinetic trace at T2 that in turn gives rise to a further emergent state at T3 — the phantasma, which is irreducible to the physical motion that underlies it because it possesses qualitative and affective properties not present in the mere vibration of the sense organ. The phantasma at T3 is thus a genuinely novel ontological entity, one that emerges from the soul's kinetic history but cannot be fully explained by any single stage of that history taken in isolation. Aristotle's own account in the *Physics* of how substances retain their identity through change provides a further gloss on the dual trace. "when a thing has been affected and altered in any way we still call it by the original name: thus we speak of the bronze or the wax being is fluid or hard or hot (not only that — we also call the fluid and the hot stuff bronze), giving the matter the same name as the affection" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 100). The analogy to the phantasma is instructive: just as the bronze retains its name even when it has been melted and reshaped, the phantasma retains the formal imprint of the original sensible object even as it has been transformed by the processes of residual motion and affective coloring. The dual trace is thus not a composite of two separate elements glued together but a single ontological structure in which kinetic pattern and affective quality are inseparably intertwined — much as the bronze is at once a substance and a bearer of its current affection. Hawhee's work on Aristotle's rhetorical vision further illuminates this duality; when Aristotle discusses the epideictic function of lofty language, the term megethos invokes both physical stature and qualitative greatness, a doubling that mirrors the phantasma's own dual character as both kinetic magnitude and affective significance (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 12–13). Finally, the emergence of phantasmata at T3 establishes the conditions for all subsequent operations of the soul — memory, deliberation, desire, and action. The phantasma is the medium through which the past persists into the present and the present is projected into the future; it is, in the fullest sense, the soul's temporal medium. Aristotle's primary substance, the unmoved mover, "produces the primary eternal and single movement" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 146–147); by analogy, the phantasma, once formed, produces within the soul a continuing movement that organizes experience, motivates action, and makes thought possible. Thus the T0–T3 framework reveals phantasia not as a marginal psychological curiosity but as the foundational faculty through which the soul inhabits time — receiving, retaining, and transforming the motions of the world into the appearances that constitute its inner life. The deliberative and rational grades of phantasia, which Aristotle distinguishes as bouleutike phantasia in rational beings (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 22–23), represent further elaborations of this temporal medium, articulations that presuppose the kinetic and affective ground we have traced from T0 to T3. Accordingly, the analysis developed in this chapter provides the necessary foundation for the investigations that follow, in which the phantasma's role in memory, rhetoric, and practical deliberation will be examined in their full complexity.

## III. T4–T6: Memory, Judgment, Emotion, and Habituated Phantasia

### III.1 T4 — Mneme and Phantasma Activation: The Past as Past

Having established in the preceding section the manner in which the dual trace—resonant motion and resonant affect—crystallizes into phantasmata at T3, we are now positioned to trace how these foundational operations of the soul unfold in real-time perceptual-motor engagement with the world. The outfielder tracking a fly ball offers a paradigmatic case for understanding how the complete T1–T6 loop operates not as a single, linear progression from perception to action but rather as a continuously iterated cycle of micro-adjustments, each one a compressed recapitulation of the soul's full arc of sensing, imagining, desiring, judging, and moving. Indeed, the outfielder's task is remarkable precisely because it seems, from the outside, to be a single fluid motion—a run, a reach, a catch—when in fact it constitutes an unbroken series of perceptual updates, phantasmatic recalibrations, and locomotive corrections that together reveal the soul's temporal structure in its most dynamic register.

We must begin by considering what the outfielder perceives at the moment of the bat's contact with the ball. At T1, the initial sensory impression is registered: the crack of the bat, the visual detection of the ball's trajectory as it leaves the infield, and the ambient optical information that specifies the ball's direction and velocity relative to the fielder's position. The outfielder's visual system is thus engaged from the start as a whole-body perceptual apparatus; the eyes track the ball not in isolation but as part of a postural, locomotive, and kinesthetic totality. This ecological framing allows us to understand why T1, the moment of initial perception, is never merely receptive but is always already oriented toward movement—toward the affordance structure of the environment as it presents itself to a body capable of running, adjusting, and catching.

From this initial perception, the T2 moment of phantasmatic activation unfolds almost simultaneously. The outfielder does not perceive the ball's trajectory as a bare geometric line; rather, the trajectory is immediately overlaid with anticipatory phantasmata—images of where the ball will land, kinesthetic impressions of how the body must move to intercept it, and affective tonalities of urgency or ease depending on the difficulty of the play. Aristotle's account of phantasia as a motion engendered by actual perception is crucial here, for, among mortal beings "those which possess calculation have all the other powers above mentioned, while the converse does not hold—indeed some live by imagination alone, while others have not even imagination" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 22). The outfielder, as a rational animal, possesses all the layers of the soul's operation, yet in the heat of the play it is phantasia rather than deliberative calculation that governs the immediate response. The ball's arc generates a phantasma—a sensory-motor image that is at once a representation of the ball's probable landing point and a felt imperative to move. This phantasma is not static; it is itself a kinesis, a movement within the soul that corresponds to and anticipates the movement of the ball through external space.

The crystallization at T3 that we discussed in the preceding section takes on a distinctive character in this athletic context. The phantasma that crystallizes is not a fixed image but what we might call a dynamic schema—a continuously updated anticipatory model of the ball's trajectory and the body's required response. Accordingly, T3 in the outfielder's experience is not a single moment of crystallization but a rapidly iterated series of crystallizations, each one refining the phantasmatic image in response to new perceptual information. The outfielder's perception of the ball's flight is precisely a marking off of motion by the before and after; each successive moment of the ball's trajectory provides a new "before" against which the current position is measured, and the phantasmatic projection of where the ball will be constitutes the "after" toward which the body moves. Thus, the T1–T3 sequence is not traversed once but is looped continuously, each iteration yielding a slightly more refined phantasma that guides the next micro-adjustment of the fielder's stride, angle, and speed.

### III.2 T5 — Doxa, Judgment, and the Affective Architecture

What, then, of T4 and T5—the moments of deliberation and judgment that, in more leisured contexts, intervene between phantasmatic crystallization and overt action? In the outfielder's case, these stages are not absent but are compressed to the point of near-invisibility. The outfielder does not pause to deliberate about whether to run left or right; the phantasma itself carries a directional imperative that functions as a compressed judgment. The outfielder's running is not merely instrumental—a means to the end of catching the ball—but is a mode of being-in-the-situation, a way of existing as a perceiving-moving agent whose deliberation is folded into the very quality of the movement. The judgment at T5 is similarly compressed: the outfielder does not consciously evaluate and select among alternative courses of action but rather enacts a kinesthetic judgment that is inseparable from the ongoing adjustment of stride and gaze. Hence, the T4–T5 compression is not a skipping of stages but a radical condensation in which deliberation and judgment are performed by the body's ongoing attunement to the perceptual field rather than by explicit cognitive operations.

### III.3 T6 — Pathos, Orexis, and Action

This brings us to T6, the moment of overt motor action—the final adjustment of the glove hand, the leap or the slide, the closure of the fingers around the ball. Yet even T6 is not truly final in the outfielder's case, for the micro-adjustments continue until the very instant of the catch. The ball may drift in the wind, may carry farther than anticipated, may sink more rapidly than the initial phantasma projected; each of these variations triggers a new iteration of the T1–T6 loop, each one faster and more compressed than the last as the margin for error narrows. Von Uexküll's concept of the functional cycle between perception signs and effect signs is relevant here, for as he describes, the organism's perceptual apparatus and motor apparatus are linked in a continuous loop in which "the self-tones that respond serve as perception signs of external events" and these perception signs are in turn "stamped as perception marks corresponding to the respective source of stimulus" (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, p. 210). The outfielder's perceptual marks—the visual registration of the ball's position, the proprioceptive sense of the body's orientation—are continuously transposed into motor responses, which in turn alter the perceptual field, generating new marks that demand new responses. The functional cycle is thus a biological instantiation of the T1–T6 loop, and the outfielder's performance reveals this cycle operating at maximal speed and precision.

We should also consider the role of temporal infinity in the outfielder's experience. Aristotle's observation in the *Physics* that "a thing in a finite time cannot come in contact with things quantitatively infinite" but "can come in contact with things infinite in respect of divisibility; for in this sense the time itself is also infinite" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 82) bears directly on the structure of the outfielder's micro-adjustments. The flight of the ball occupies a finite duration, yet within that duration the outfielder's perceptual-motor adjustments are, in principle, infinitely divisible—each adjustment can be further refined, each moment of the trajectory can be further parsed. The contact with the infinite through divisibility rather than quantity is precisely what gives the outfielder's performance its quality of fluid continuity; the discrete iterations of the T1–T6 loop blend into a seamless motion because the temporal intervals between them approach the infinitely small. The T1–T6 loop, iterated at the frequency of perceptual updating, thus produces not a series of discrete actions but a single, temporally extended act whose unity is given by the phantasmatic anticipation that threads through each iteration.

Specifically, this analysis reveals that the outfielder's performance is not well described by the traditional stimulus-response model, in which a perception triggers a fixed motor program that is then executed. Rather, the outfielder embodies a mode of being in which perception, phantasia, desire, judgment, and movement are continually co-constituting one another in a temporal loop whose iterations are too rapid and too tightly integrated to be parsed into discrete stages. Subsequently, we can see that the T1–T6 framework, when applied to real-time athletic performance, does not describe a sequence of stages so much as a recursive structure—a loop that folds back upon itself with each new perceptual datum, refining the phantasma and adjusting the motor response in an ongoing dialogue between soul and world. The outfielder, in this light, is not merely catching a ball; the outfielder is enacting the full temporal architecture of the ensouled body's engagement with a moving world, revealing in compressed and accelerated form the same perceptual-phantasmatic-motor cycle that operates in every act of embodied cognition.

### III.4 Habituated Phantasia, Hexis, and “Muscle Memory”

If the outfielder reveals the T1–T6 loop in its iterative, real-time dynamism, the pianist reveals something equally important: how the loop itself is transformed through practice, such that what begins as a slow, stepwise progression through each stage becomes, in the expert, a radically compressed arc in which intermediate stages are absorbed into kinesthetic phantasmata. The contrast between the novice and the expert pianist is thus not merely a difference in speed or accuracy but a difference in the very structure of the soul's engagement with the musical task—a restructuring that illuminates the nature of phantasia, the role of habituation, and the relationship between deliberation and embodied skill.

Consider first the novice. Seated at the keyboard, confronting a musical score, the novice pianist engages the T1–T6 loop in its most explicit and disaggregated form. At T1, the novice perceives the notation on the page—a visual stimulus that must be decoded rather than immediately understood. The perception is effortful and halting; each note must be identified, its position on the staff correlated with a key on the keyboard, its duration parsed from the surrounding rhythmic context. This perceptual labor is already a far cry from the outfielder's fluid visual tracking; the novice's T1 is characterized by a fragmented, piecemeal engagement with the perceptual field in which the affordances of the score are not yet transparent. Von Uexküll's framework is again instructive here, for he describes how "every animal harbors a certain number of tones, which enter into" the functional cycle of perception and effect (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, p. 194). For the novice, the "perception tones"—the perceptual signs that the score presents—are not yet fluently connected to the corresponding "effect tones"—the motor impulses that produce the correct keystrokes. The functional cycle is incomplete or, more precisely, is traversed only with great deliberative effort.

At T2, the novice generates a phantasma of the intended sound—an auditory image of what the note or phrase should sound like when correctly played. This phantasma, however, is often vague or incomplete; the novice may have a general sense of the melody but lacks the detailed auditory-kinesthetic representation that would allow for precise execution. The phantasmatic crystallization at T3 is accordingly tentative: the novice's image of the target sound and the corresponding finger movement is not yet a stable schema but a fragile, effortful construction that must be consciously maintained. Gonzalez's analysis of phantasia in Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is relevant here, for "phantasia is present even in the study of geometry" and hence is not reducible to mere show or ostentation but functions as a cognitive capacity operative in all forms of structured understanding (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 15). The novice pianist's phantasia operates in precisely this cognitive register: it is not a vivid, automatic image but a laboriously constructed representation that mediates between the perceived score and the intended action.

From T3, the novice must proceed through T4 and T5 in their fully explicit forms. T4, the moment of deliberation, involves a conscious evaluation of alternatives: Which finger should play this note? How should the hand be positioned for the upcoming passage? What dynamic level is indicated? These questions are not rhetorical for the novice; they are genuine deliberative challenges that require the kind of reasoning Aristotle associates with *bouleusis*—practical deliberation about matters that admit of being otherwise. The novice must weigh options, consider consequences, and select a course of action before executing it. the *Metaphysics* that "one must be already trained to know how to take each sort of argument, since it is absurd to seek at the same time knowledge and the way of attaining knowledge; and neither is easy to get" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 23) applies with particular force to the novice musician, who is simultaneously trying to learn the skill and to exercise it—seeking at once the knowledge and the way of attaining it. T5, the moment of judgment or *krisis*, follows from this deliberation: the novice decides on a fingering, a hand position, a dynamic approach, and commits to executing it. Only then does T6 arrive—the overt motor action of striking the key—and even this final stage is often awkward, imprecise, and accompanied by a sense of uncertainty about whether the intended result has been achieved.

The expert pianist, by contrast, inhabits the T1–T6 loop in a fundamentally different manner. What is most striking about expert performance is not merely its speed—though speed is certainly a factor—but its structural reorganization of the loop itself. The expert does not simply traverse T1 through T6 more quickly; rather, the expert compresses the intermediate stages, specifically T4 and T5, into the phantasmatic crystallization at T3, such that the loop effectively runs T1→T3→T6 with deliberation and judgment absorbed into kinesthetic phantasmata. This compression is the hallmark of what Aristotle would recognize as *hexis*—a stable dispositional state of the soul acquired through habituation, in which the capacity for correct action is no longer dependent on explicit deliberation but is embedded in the body's learned responsiveness. The expert pianist perceives the score (T1) and, almost simultaneously, generates a rich, multi-modal phantasma (T3) that encompasses not merely the auditory image of the intended sound but also the kinesthetic feel of the required finger movements, the proprioceptive sense of hand position and weight distribution, and the affective quality of the musical phrase. This phantasma is not merely an image in the narrow visual sense; it is what we might call a kinesthetic phantasma—a full-bodied, multi-sensory anticipation that carries within itself the deliberative and judicative functions that the novice must perform explicitly.

How does this compression occur? The process is one of habituation through repetition, in which the functional cycle between perception signs and effect signs is progressively tightened until the two become nearly simultaneous. Von Uexküll's metaphor of the organism as a kind of musical instrument is apposite: he speaks of "perception tones" and "effect tones" that, like the tones of a carillon, "enter into" structured patterns of response (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, p. 194). For the expert pianist, the perception tones—the visual and auditory signs of the score and the instrument—and the effect tones—the motor impulses that drive the fingers—have been tuned, through practice, into a harmonious functional unity. The carillon metaphor is particularly apt because it captures the way in which expert musical performance has a quality of resonance: each perception tone calls forth its corresponding effect tone not through deliberation but through a kind of sympathetic vibration, a direct coupling of perception and action that bypasses the explicit calculative steps of T4 and T5.

This is not to say that the expert pianist never deliberates; in interpretive decisions, in confronting a new or unusual passage, in responding to the acoustics of an unfamiliar hall, the expert may re-engage the full T1–T6 loop with its explicit deliberative stages. But in the performance of well-practiced repertoire, the compression to T1→T3→T6 is the norm, and it is this compression that gives expert performance its quality of effortless fluency. Accordingly, we can understand why Aristotle insists in the *Rhetoric* on the connection between phantasia and persuasion, for just as the skilled rhetorician brings the audience "before the eyes" through vivid language—making them see and feel the matter at hand—so too the skilled pianist brings the music "before the fingers" through kinesthetic phantasia, making the body enact the musical intention without the intervention of explicit deliberation. Gonzalez emphasizes that phantasia in Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is not mere ornamental display but a cognitive and perceptual operation: it is "not πρὸς αὑτόν, not πρὸς τὸν ἀκροατήν," as in different contexts, but functions wherever teaching and persuasion take place (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 15). Similarly, the expert pianist's kinesthetic phantasia is not a subjective embellishment of the musical experience but the very medium through which musical meaning is realized in sound.

The expert's playing is not merely a means of producing correct notes—an instrumental relation of agency to purpose—but a way of being musical, a mode of existence in which the body, the instrument, the score, and the audience are gathered into a unified act that transcends the sum of its mechanical parts. Hence the expert's compressed loop is not a diminished version of the novice's full loop but an enriched one, in which the deliberative and judicative functions have been internalized so thoroughly that they operate as qualities of the movement itself rather than as discrete preparatory stages.

Finally, we should consider what the contrast between novice and expert reveals about the nature of phantasia as such. Papachristou's analysis of three grades of phantasia in Aristotle's *De Anima* is suggestive here, for Aristotle "discriminates not two—as it is commonly argued—but three kinds or grades of phantasia" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, p. 25). If we map these grades onto the pianist's development, we can see that the novice operates primarily at the level of indeterminate phantasia—a vague, effortful imaging that requires explicit deliberative support—while the expert operates at a higher grade in which phantasia is determinate, richly structured, and functionally integrated with both perception and motor action. The progression from novice to expert is thus not merely a matter of motor learning but a transformation in the very grade of phantasia that governs the soul's engagement with the musical task. Subsequently, this transformation illuminates the deeper philosophical point that phantasia is not a fixed faculty with a single mode of operation but a dynamic capacity that develops, refines, and restructures itself through habituation and practice—a capacity that, in its highest expression, compresses the full arc of the soul's temporal engagement with the world into a single, fluid, kinesthetically rich act.

Thus, the outfielder and the pianist together reveal the T1–T6 loop as both iteratively dynamic and developmentally plastic. The outfielder shows us the loop in its rapid, real-time iteration, each cycle a micro-adjustment guided by continuously updated phantasmata; the pianist shows us the loop in its developmental compression, the novice's laborious stepwise progression yielding, through practice, to the expert's compressed arc from perception to kinesthetic phantasma to action. In both cases, it is phantasia—understood not as mere mental imagery but as the soul's kinetic, anticipatory, multi-modal engagement with the world—that serves as the linchpin of the loop, the faculty that transforms bare perception into meaningful, directed, embodied action. These two cases accordingly prepare us for the broader theoretical question that follows: how the T1–T6 loop, in its various modes and compressions, constitutes the temporal structure of the ensouled body's being-in-the-world and grounds the possibility of rhetorical, aesthetic, and practical engagement with the situations we encounter.

## IV. Phantasia, Umwelt, and the Soap Bubble of World-Disclosure

Having examined in the preceding section how phantasia operates through memory, judgment, emotion, and habituated practice—as illustrated by the outfielder tracking a fly ball and the pianist whose fingers find their way across the keys—we are now positioned to ask a broader question: if phantasia is the faculty through which the soul renders the world present, making appearances appear as something determinate, does it not follow that phantasia also constitutes a kind of perceptual horizon, a bounded disclosure of the world shaped by the organism's own capacities and concerns? To pursue this question, we must turn to a conceptual resource that may seem, at first glance, far removed from Aristotle's psychology: the biosemiotic framework of Jakob von Uexküll, specifically his famous metaphor of the "soap bubble" that encloses every organism within its own species-specific world of meaning. The juxtaposition is not as strained as it might appear; indeed, both Aristotle and Uexküll share a conviction that perception is not passive reception of a pre-given environment but an active, organismic disclosure of a world shaped by the perceiving creature's own capacities, needs, and modes of engagement. As Uexküll writes, describing the fundamental relationship between subject and environment, "the sensation 'blue' becomes the 'blueness' of the sky, the sensation 'green' becomes the 'greenness' of the lawn, and so forth" (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 52–53). The organism does not encounter a neutral field of data; it encounters a world already colored by its own perceptual and effector organs, a world that is, in a profound sense, its own. For Aristotle, phantasia occupies a structurally analogous position. It is neither mere sensation nor intellection but, as Gonzalez insists, a distinct faculty through which the soul makes things appear as determinate, meaningful phenomena. Specifically, Gonzalez demonstrates that in *Rhetoric* III.1, phantasia names not a subjective illusion but the fundamental condition under which logos reaches its audience: "for logos [is] a kind of sign, so that it will not accomplish its goal unless" it is brought before the hearer through phantasia (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 8–9). Thus logos itself depends upon the phantasmatic rendering of things—their being made to appear as something to someone. Phantasia, then, is not a static image but a dynamic movement of the soul, a kinesis that constitutes a horizon of appearance. It is worth noting that phantasia admits of internal distinctions—grades ranging from indeterminate or sensory phantasia to deliberative phantasia (the *bouleutikē phantasia* operative in rational beings)—as well as a complex relation to belief (*doxa*) and a notable voluntariness in its higher forms; these distinctions, which Papachristou has delineated as comprising not two but three grades of phantasia (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 25–27), will receive full elaboration in a subsequent chapter. For the present argument, what matters is that phantasia, in all its grades, constitutes the perceptual bubble through which the human soul discloses its world.

### IV.1 Uexküll's Soap Bubble: Species-Specific Bubbles of Meaning

Uexküll's central contribution to the biological sciences—and, subsequently, to philosophy—lies in his demonstration that every organism inhabits not "the" world but its own *Umwelt*, a species-specific environment constituted by the interplay of its perception organs (*Merkorgan*) and its effector organs (*Wirkorgan*). The soap bubble metaphor captures this insight with particular vividness: each animal lives enclosed in a bubble of perception marks and effect marks that together compose its meaningful world, and this bubble is, in principle, impenetrable from the outside. We cannot inhabit the tick's Umwelt any more than the tick can inhabit ours; we can only reconstruct it through careful attention to the organism's anatomical and behavioral capacities. As Uexküll demonstrates through his analysis of the fly's visual field, "the coarser the mosaic, the greater the loss of the details of the things, and the world as seen through a fly's eye must seem significantly coarsened as compared to its being seen through a human eye" (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 66–69). The fly's soap bubble is not a degraded version of the human world; it is a different world altogether, one in which certain features of the environment are salient and others simply do not exist for the organism. Aristotle observes that "being has two forms—it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157), and indeed the way in which being shows itself to any given creature depends upon the form of that creature's perceptual and cognitive engagement. Accordingly, what Uexküll calls the perception mark (*Merkmal*) can be understood as the biosemiotic analogue of the phantasma: both name the way in which the organism's own activity transforms raw contact with the environment into meaningful appearance. The tick responds to butyric acid, warmth, and the tactile quality of skin—three perception marks that compose its entire Umwelt. The human, by contrast, inhabits a vastly more complex bubble, one in which phantasia operates across sensory, mnemonic, emotional, and deliberative registers. Hawhee underscores this complexity when observing that epideictic speeches "take up actions that are agreed upon, so that what remains is to clothe the actions with greatness (megethos) and beauty" (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 12–13); the rhetorical amplification of actions into visions of magnitude and beauty is itself a function of the human phantasmatic bubble, a capacity for world-disclosure that far exceeds the tick's three-dimensional Umwelt while remaining, in structural terms, analogous to it. Thus Uexküll's framework helps us to see that phantasia is not merely an internal psychological event but a world-constituting capacity—a soap bubble of meaning that surrounds the organism and determines the horizon within which things can appear. Specifically, the human soap bubble is characterized by its extraordinary richness: because phantasia operates in conjunction with logos, memory, desire, and habituated practice, the human Umwelt encompasses not only the immediately sensible but also the remembered, the anticipated, the imagined, and the desired. The human soap bubble is thus temporally extended in a way that the tick's is not; it includes not only what is present but what has been and what may yet come to pass.

### IV.2 Human Phantasia and Mood as an Anthropological "Soap Bubble"

If every organism inhabits a soap bubble of meaning, what distinguishes the human bubble? The answer, we propose, lies in the intersection of phantasia and mood—or, to use the Heideggerian term, *Befindlichkeit* (state-of-mind, attunement). Heidegger's early lectures on Aristotle, delivered in the summer semester of 1924, reveal the depth of his engagement with precisely this question. As Gross observes in his account of Heidegger's Marburg lectures, "the determination of Being-with-one-another in politike founds that which rhetoric brings to language" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 45–46). Rhetoric, for the early Heidegger, is not mere persuasion but a fundamental mode of disclosing the shared world of *Mitsein*; and it accomplishes this disclosure through the passions (*pathē*), which Heidegger interprets not as transient feelings but as fundamental ways of finding oneself in the world. Indeed, as the same volume records, "passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). The passions, understood as modes of *Befindlichkeit*, are thus themselves kinetic—they are movements of the soul that alter the horizon within which things appear. Hence they are intimately connected to phantasia, which is likewise a form of kinesis, a movement that renders the world determinate. What mood accomplishes, on this reading, is a modulation of the human soap bubble. When one is afraid, the world appears threatening; when one is confident, it appears manageable; when one is in a state of wonder, it appears strange and worthy of inquiry. Accordingly, Rickert argues that a worldview "constitutes how the world appears 'out there' as colored by cultural lenses, a position that insinuates a disengagement between people and world, so that ultimately 'objective' reason becomes the means to eliminate cultural distortion and get at the actual essence of things" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 239–240). Yet as Heidegger insists, dwelling is not a worldview but a principle of being; we do not stand outside our soap bubble and observe it from a neutral vantage point. We are always already within it, and it is phantasia—operating in concert with mood, habit, memory, and desire—that constitutes the bubble's contours. The "rhetorical situation," as Gross has framed it, involves an exigence, an audience, and a set of constraints (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, pp. 102–103); but these elements are themselves disclosed through the phantasmatic-affective bubble of the situated agent. The exigence appears as exigent, the audience as responsive or resistant, the constraints as enabling or limiting, only because phantasia renders them so within the mood that currently attunes the agent to the world. Gonzalez's analysis of *Rhetoric* III.1 reinforces this point. phantasia in its deliberative or logistic grade—the *logistikē phantasia* that operates "in rational beings"—is what enables the human agent to weigh alternative courses of action, to consider what is beneficial or harmful, noble or base (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 22–23). This deliberative phantasia is not detached from mood; on the contrary, it operates within and through the affective attunement that constitutes the agent's current soap bubble. Subsequently, the full elaboration of phantasia's internal grades—sensory, indeterminate, and deliberative—and their respective relations to belief (*doxa*) and to the voluntary will be developed in a dedicated chapter. Thus we arrive at the central thesis of this section: the human Umwelt—the anthropological soap bubble—is constituted by the interplay of phantasia and mood. Phantasia, as the faculty that renders the world present under determinate appearances, provides the perceptual and cognitive content of the bubble; mood, as the attunement that colors and orients that content, provides its affective tonality. Together, they compose what Heidegger calls "thrownness," the condition in which Dasein always already finds itself disclosed to a world that matters, a world in which things appear as significant, urgent, or indifferent (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 400–401). The soap bubble is never neutral, never a transparent medium through which an objective world is passively received; it is always already shaped by the organism's own capacities, concerns, and current affective state. Hence the human soap bubble, while structurally analogous to the tick's or the fly's, is distinguished by its extraordinary complexity—a complexity rooted in the multiple grades of phantasia, the richness of human mood, and the capacity of logos to articulate, amplify, and transform the appearances that phantasia makes available.

## V. Mood, Ambience, and Dwelling: Heidegger and Rickert

In Section IV we traced the structural homology between the animal's Funktionskreis—its closed circuit of Merkbild and Wirkbild through which environmental carriers of significance are encountered—and the human capacity for phantasia operating in concert with Stimmung, whereby Dasein's disclosive "soap bubble" of meaning is perpetually generated. What emerged was a picture in which taking-as, the fundamental structure of encountering things as something, is not a purely intellectual act but a dynamic, affectively saturated movement of the soul that mirrors, at a higher register, the perceptual-motor loop Uexküll describes for the tick or the sea urchin. We must now deepen the affective side of this account. Specifically, if phantasia furnishes the image-laden medium through which things appear, mood (Stimmung) provides the atmospheric ground within which any such appearing first becomes possible. The question that guides the present section is accordingly twofold: How does Heidegger's ontological treatment of mood disclose the primordial openness of Dasein to a world, and how does Thomas Rickert's concept of ambience extend and rhetorically enrich that disclosure so as to illuminate what it means for Dasein to dwell amid the things that matter? Heidegger's analysis in *Being and Time* establishes mood not as a psychological coloring laid over an already disclosed world but as an existential structure—Befindlichkeit—through which Dasein finds itself always already situated in a world that presses upon it. The "downward plunge" (Absturz) that Heidegger describes, wherein "Dasein remains in the throw, and is sucked into the turbulence of the 'they's' inauthenticity," reveals that thrownness is not a single event completed at birth but the ongoing facticity of being delivered over to a world one did not choose (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 223). Mood, in other words, is the way thrownness is lived: it is the medium in which Dasein's there is perpetually opened and attuned. Indeed, without Befindlichkeit there would be no clearing in which entities could show up as anything at all; entities within-the-world can be encountered only because Dasein is always already in a mood that renders it receptive to what it meets. Heidegger's own term for the everyday mode of such encountering is "dealings" (Umgang) with entities in the environment, and the Being of those entities is exhibited phenomenologically when "we take as our clue our everyday Being-in-the-world, which we also call our 'dealings' in the world and with entities within-the-world" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 96). Thus the environmental things nearest to Dasein—the hammer, the doorway, the path through the national park—are not first present-at-hand objects subsequently invested with value; they are discovered within a mood-laden totality of involvements. Rickert's contribution is to press Heidegger's insight beyond the traditional boundaries of the human subject and toward a broader ecology of attunement. In *Ambient Rhetoric*, Rickert argues that affects and environments are not separable poles—subject here, world there—but are "complexly generating an overarching ambient environment that cradles, without forcing, particular affective responses or that fosters an attunement" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 172). The verb "cradles" is telling: ambience does not determine a response causally but holds open a space within which certain affective orientations become likely, relevant, or resonant. Similarly, Rickert's reading of Heidegger's fourfold underscores that "the human remains an important site for disclosure, but the world also discloses itself in its own ways—this too is part of the world's 'worlding'" (Rickert, *Towards Ecosophy in a Participating World*, p. 12). Hence mood is never merely subjective; it is the joint product of Dasein's thrown projection and the self-showing of the world. The implications for rhetoric are profound: persuasion, on this account, does not operate solely through propositional content or even through the arousal of discrete emotions but through the cultivation of an ambient attunement that enables the user to encounter a situation as calling for a particular kind of response.

### V.1 Befindlichkeit and Stimmung: Mood as Ontological Ground

Heidegger's existential analytic in *Being and Time* insists that Dasein is never encountered in a mood-free state. Every cognitive act, every practical engagement, every encounter with an entity in the environment presupposes a prior attunement that has already disclosed the world in a particular tonality. This is the meaning of Befindlichkeit—literally, the state of finding-oneself—which Heidegger distinguishes sharply from any merely ontic psychology of emotion. Befindlichkeit names the ontological condition of possibility for moods (Stimmungen), while Stimmung names the specific existentiell manner in which Dasein is attuned at any given time. Together they constitute one of the three equiprimordial structures of the "there" (Da) alongside understanding (Verstehen) and discourse (Rede). What is decisive for our argument is that Befindlichkeit is not derivative of cognition; it is co-original with it, and in many respects more primordial, since Dasein must already find itself disposed toward the world before any theoretical or practical apprehension can begin. The ontological weight of this claim can be grasped by attending to Heidegger's account of thrownness (Geworfenheit). As Heidegger writes, thrownness "is neither a 'fact that is finished' nor a Fact that is settled" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 223). The ongoing, unsettled character of thrownness means that Dasein is perpetually being delivered over to its own situatedness, and this delivery is experienced precisely as mood. Fear, anxiety, boredom, elation—each of these moods discloses the world differently, not by adding a subjective coloring to a neutral set of facts but by opening up or closing down specific regions of significance. In anxiety, for instance, the familiar totality of involvements collapses, and Dasein finds itself confronting the sheer that-it-is of its existence; in everyday absorbed coping, by contrast, mood recedes into the background precisely because its disclosive work proceeds so smoothly. Heidegger's analysis of "everydayness" (Alltäglichkeit) as "that kind of Being in which Dasein maintains itself proximally and for the most part" underscores that what this expression "signifies at bottom when delimited ontologically, remains obscure" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 421). The obscurity is itself significant: everyday mood is inconspicuous because it pervades everything, functioning as the atmospheric medium in which entities show up as available, threatening, inviting, or indifferent. What is crucial here is the distinction between mood understood ontologically and mood understood as an emergent psychological state. Mood, on the Heideggerian account, is precisely not ontologically reducible in this way; it cannot be decomposed into a set of neurological firings plus environmental stimuli and thereby explained. Rather, Stimmung is the prior whole within which any such decomposition is subsequently carried out. Mood is thus an emergent existential totality that cannot be traced back to its ontic components without remainder, just as the care-structure (Sorge) that Heidegger identifies as the Being of Dasein "cannot be traced back to some ontical" element (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 241). Accordingly, the ontological irreducibility of mood signals that Befindlichkeit occupies a level of analysis distinct from both folk psychology and cognitive science: it names the condition under which any affective episode, any perceptual encounter, any rhetorical situation first comes to be what it is. If phantasia is the kinetic movement of the soul through which images are generated and retained—the movement that Aristotle connects to perception, desire, and judgment—then Stimmung is the atmosphere within which that movement acquires its directionality. Without a prior attunement, phantasia would be, as it were, a mechanism spinning freely; it is mood that gives phantasia its traction, its bearing on the particular situation in which Dasein finds itself. Indeed, the passions (pathē) that Heidegger interprets through Aristotle's *Rhetoric* are understood not as static cognitive states but as modes of Befindlichkeit, as ways of being moved that disclose the world in determinate ways (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Thus Heidegger's own reading of Aristotle's affective categories underscores the inseparability of mood and the image-forming capacity we have been calling phantasia: passion is "both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Phantasia, as itself a motion in the soul, is accordingly conditioned by the pathē that attune Dasein to what matters.

### V.2 Rickert's Ambience, Attunement, and Rhetorical Dwelling

If Heidegger provides the ontological architecture of mood, Thomas Rickert furnishes its rhetorical habitation. Rickert's concept of ambience extends Befindlichkeit beyond the existential analytic of Dasein and into the broader ecology of places, things, and atmospheric conditions within which rhetorical persuasion operates. In doing so, Rickert does not abandon Heidegger but radicalizes the insight that mood is never merely internal; it is always already shared, distributed, and environmentally enmeshed. The result is a notion of rhetoric that can no longer be confined to the speaker's strategic deployment of appeals but must encompass the entire ambient surround—the lighting of a room, the hum of a city, the silence of the national park at dusk—that disposes an audience toward certain modes of responsiveness. Central to Rickert's project is the claim that affective environments are not passive backdrops against which rhetorical action occurs but are themselves generative of attunement. As Rickert argues, such environments are "complexly generating an overarching ambient environment that cradles, without forcing, particular affective responses or that fosters an attunement" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 172). The phrase "without forcing" is essential: ambience operates below the threshold of explicit argumentation, shaping the conditions under which arguments gain or lose traction. Hence Rickert proposes that rhetorical theory must attend not only to logos, pathos, and ethos but to the choric, material, and atmospheric dimensions that precede and condition those classical appeals. This move does not render the classical categories obsolete but reveals them as embedded within a wider field of disclosive forces. Similarly, Rickert's engagement with Heidegger's later topology of Being—in which poetic thinking becomes, as the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* note, "the dwelling place [Ortschaft] of essence" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 166)—suggests that rhetoric is not merely an art of persuasion but a mode of dwelling, a way of inhabiting and being inhabited by the world's self-disclosure. If ambience is the atmospheric field within which attunement arises, then phantasia can be understood as the faculty through which that atmospheric field is rendered determinate—through which the vague press of mood becomes a specific image, a particular scene, a concrete taking-as. Rickert's insight that the body is "newly plastic," that the merging of mind and tool "blur the distinction between 'mind' and 'tool'" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 57), resonates deeply with the Aristotelian picture of phantasia as a power that is neither purely sensory nor purely intellectual but operates at the mobile boundary between organism and environment. What Rickert adds that is distinctive, however, is a rhetorical sensibility: the recognition that ambience is not merely given but is also cultivated, designed, and contested. Our national parks, for example, are deliberately managed to produce certain ambient experiences—the silence, the grandeur, the sense of temporal depth—that attune visitors to a particular mode of dwelling. The rhetoric of environmental conservation thus operates not only through explicit arguments about biodiversity or climate change but through the ambient fostering of attunements that dispose people to protect our national parks as places of irreplaceable significance. Hence Rickert's ambient rhetoric discloses a dimension of persuasion that classical rhetorical theory, focused as it is on the discrete speech act, has tended to overlook; and this dimension is precisely the one in which Stimmung and phantasia collaborate to generate the world-disclosing "soap bubble" of meaning we have been describing.

### V.3 Integrating Mood and Phantasia: Antichesis and Ontological Completion

The term antichesis—literally, a placing-against or counter-positioning—is employed here to name the dynamic interplay between mood and phantasia through which Dasein's world achieves its determinate character. If Stimmung opens the atmospheric clearing, phantasia fills that clearing with images, scenes, and anticipatory projections; and the ongoing tension between these two dimensions—the diffuse and the determinate, the atmospheric and the imagistic—constitutes the fundamental rhythm of world-disclosure. Aristotle's account of phantasia, as we noted in earlier sections, situates it between perception and thought: it is a motion in the soul occasioned by actual perception but capable of persisting and operating in perception's absence. Gonzalez observes that in Aristotle's treatment "phantasia is present even in the study of geometry" and accordingly "cannot be 'mere show' or 'ostentation'" (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 15). This passage is significant because phantasia is not confined to the realm of appearance or deception but operates wherever cognition engages with form—whether mathematical, perceptual, or deliberative. Thus phantasia possesses a genuine cognitive function: it is the faculty through which forms are held before the soul in a quasi-perceptual mode, enabling comparison, deliberation, and judgment. Gonzalez further notes that "the phantasma is what others say about us; and that the persuasion operative in the phantasia is enhanced in proportion as the speakers are trustworthy" (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 27). This observation reveals phantasia's deeply rhetorical character: the images through which we grasp ourselves and our situations are socially mediated, shaped by the discourse and testimony of others, and accordingly susceptible to the influence of ethos. We note that Aristotle distinguishes between sensory phantasia (aisthētikē), which arises directly from perceptual encounters and is shared with many animals, and deliberative phantasia (bouleutikē), which involves the combining and comparing of images in the service of practical reasoning and is proper to rational beings. The relation of phantasia to belief (doxa) is complex: while phantasia can produce appearances that conflict with what one believes to be the case—as when the sun appears small though one knows it to be large—it is not identical to belief, since belief requires assent and phantasia is in some measure voluntary, subject to the will's capacity to summon or dismiss images. These internal distinctions within the faculty of phantasia—its grades, its relation to doxa, and its voluntariness—will receive fuller systematic treatment in a subsequent chapter; for present purposes, it suffices to mark them as structurally important for understanding how phantasia collaborates with mood. Mood, as we have seen, is ontologically irreducible; it cannot be decomposed into component parts without losing its essential character as a disclosive attunement. Phantasia, for its part, is kinetic—a motion that generates images, holds them before the soul, and projects them into anticipated futures. The antichesis between mood and phantasia is accordingly the counter-positioning of atmosphere and image, of the diffuse ground and the determinate figure, through which Dasein's world acquires its characteristic texture. When I walk through the national park at dusk and am seized by a mood of melancholic wonder, the phantasmatic images that arise—the fading light on the ridgeline, the remembered voice of a companion, the anticipated darkness—are not merely decorations added to a neutral perceptual field. They are the very medium through which the mood realizes itself as a disclosure of particular things; and conversely, the mood is the atmospheric ground without which those images would lack their specific affective charge. Hence antichesis names not a static opposition but a dynamic reciprocity: mood attunes phantasia, and phantasia articulates mood. The ontological completion that this reciprocity achieves is the full disclosure of a world in which entities show up as mattering in determinate ways. Heidegger's insistence that Dasein's Being is care (Sorge)—the being-ahead-of-itself-already-in-a-world-alongside-entities—finds its concrete phenomenological content in this interplay. The "turbulence of the 'they's' inauthenticity" into which Dasein is "sucked" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 223) is itself a mood-phantasia complex: the they-self is sustained by shared images, habitual fantasies, and ambient attunements that together compose the familiar, unreflective texture of everyday existence. Conversely, authentic disclosure—the moment in which Dasein takes over its own thrownness—involves a transformation of both mood and phantasia, a shift in attunement that reconfigures the images through which the world appears. Time, mood, and image are woven into a single disclosive fabric; and antichesis is the name for the tension that keeps that fabric taut.

## VI. Conclusion: Phantasia as the Temporal Medium of Affective World-Disclosure

The foregoing analysis has sought to demonstrate that phantasia, understood in its full Aristotelian scope, functions as the temporal medium through which affective world-disclosure is accomplished. Mood opens the clearing; phantasia populates it with determinate images, scenes, and projections; and their dynamic interplay—what we have called antichesis—constitutes the ontological completion of Dasein's world. Rickert's concept of ambience has shown that this interplay is not confined within the boundaries of a solitary subject but extends into the material, atmospheric, and rhetorical surround—an insight that transforms our understanding of how persuasion operates and how dwelling is sustained. As Rickert reminds us, the world discloses itself in its own ways, and this disclosure is part of the world's "worlding" (Rickert, *Towards Ecosophy in a Participating World*, p. 12). Phantasia, on this account, is not a private mental theater but an ecologically embedded, rhetorically conditioned, and temporally extended capacity through which the soul and the world meet in the medium of the image. We have noted that the internal distinctions within phantasia—its sensory and deliberative grades, its complex relation to belief (doxa), and the degree to which it is voluntary—require more extensive treatment than the present chapter has been able to provide. These distinctions will be taken up systematically in the chapter that follows, where the focus will shift from the ontological conditions of phantasia's operation to its specific cognitive mechanics and its role in practical reasoning. The framework developed here — phantasia as temporal medium, Stimmung as atmospheric horizon, antichesis as their dynamic interplay — thus provides the conceptual apparatus for analyzing any environment deliberately designed to shape appearance and attunement. In subsequent chapters, this apparatus will be brought to bear on video games, and specifically on *Red Dead Redemption 2*, as a case study in the deliberate construction of ambient worlds that script phantasia, mood, and taking-as through the careful orchestration of visual, auditory, and kinesthetic experience.

For now, it is sufficient to have established the framework: phantasia is not opposed to mood but is its kinetic complement, and together they generate the "soap bubble" of significance through which Dasein encounters, inhabits, and is claimed by its world.
