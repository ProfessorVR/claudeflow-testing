## II.5. The Outfielder: Micro-Adjustments as Continual Updates of the T1–T6 Loop

Having established in the preceding section the manner in which the dual trace—resonant motion and resonant affect—crystallizes into phantasmata at T3, we are now positioned to trace how these foundational operations of the soul unfold in real-time perceptual-motor engagement with the world. The outfielder tracking a fly ball offers a paradigmatic case for understanding how the complete T1–T6 loop operates not as a single, linear progression from perception to action but rather as a continuously iterated cycle of micro-adjustments, each one a compressed recapitulation of the soul's full arc of sensing, imagining, desiring, judging, and moving. Indeed, the outfielder's task is remarkable precisely because it seems, from the outside, to be a single fluid motion—a run, a reach, a catch—when in fact it constitutes an unbroken series of perceptual updates, phantasmatic recalibrations, and locomotive corrections that together reveal the soul's temporal structure in its most dynamic register.

We must begin by considering what the outfielder perceives at the moment of the bat's contact with the ball. At T1, the initial sensory impression is registered: the crack of the bat, the visual detection of the ball's trajectory as it leaves the infield, and the ambient optical information that specifies the ball's direction and velocity relative to the fielder's position. Gibson's ecological account of perception is instructive here, for it reminds us that what the outfielder perceives is not a collection of isolated sensory data points subsequently assembled by cognition but rather a structured array of information already specifying the affordances of the situation. As Gibson argues, natural vision depends not on the processing of punctiform stimuli but on "the eyes in the head on a body supported by the ground, the brain being only the central organ of a complete visual system" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 12). The outfielder's visual system is thus engaged from the start as a whole-body perceptual apparatus; the eyes track the ball not in isolation but as part of a postural, locomotive, and kinesthetic totality. This ecological framing allows us to understand why T1, the moment of initial perception, is never merely receptive but is always already oriented toward movement—toward the affordance structure of the environment as it presents itself to a body capable of running, adjusting, and catching.

From this initial perception, the T2 moment of phantasmatic activation unfolds almost simultaneously. The outfielder does not perceive the ball's trajectory as a bare geometric line; rather, the trajectory is immediately overlaid with anticipatory phantasmata—images of where the ball will land, kinesthetic impressions of how the body must move to intercept it, and affective tonalities of urgency or ease depending on the difficulty of the play. Aristotle's account of phantasia as a motion engendered by actual perception is crucial here, for (Hawhee 2011), among mortal beings "those which possess calculation have all the other powers above mentioned, while the converse does not hold—indeed some live by imagination alone, while others have not even imagination" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 22). The outfielder, as a rational animal, possesses all the layers of the soul's operation, yet in the heat of the play it is phantasia rather than deliberative calculation that governs the immediate response. The ball's arc generates a phantasma—a sensory-motor image that is at once a representation of the ball's probable landing point and a felt imperative to move. This phantasma is not static; it is itself a kinesis, a movement within the soul that corresponds to and anticipates the movement of the ball through external space.

The crystallization at T3 that we discussed in the preceding section takes on a distinctive character in this athletic context. The phantasma that crystallizes is not a fixed image but what we might call a dynamic schema—a continuously updated anticipatory model of the ball's trajectory and the body's required response. Accordingly, T3 in the outfielder's experience is not a single moment of crystallization but a rapidly iterated series of crystallizations, each one refining the phantasmatic image in response to new perceptual information. Bowin's analysis of Aristotle's account of temporal perception is illuminating in this regard: "time, too, we apprehend when we mark off motion, marking it off by the before and after, and we say that time has passed when we get a perception of the before and after in motion" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 2). The outfielder's perception of the ball's flight is precisely a marking off of motion by the before and after; each successive moment of the ball's trajectory provides a new "before" against which the current position is measured, and the phantasmatic projection of where the ball will be constitutes the "after" toward which the body moves. Thus, the T1–T3 sequence is not traversed once but is looped continuously, each iteration yielding a slightly more refined phantasma that guides the next micro-adjustment of the fielder's stride, angle, and speed.

What, then, of T4 and T5—the moments of deliberation and judgment that, in more leisured contexts, intervene between phantasmatic crystallization and overt action? In the outfielder's case, these stages are not absent but are compressed to the point of near-invisibility. The outfielder does not pause to deliberate about whether to run left or right; the phantasma itself carries a directional imperative that functions as a compressed judgment. Burke's dramatistic framework helps us understand this compression, for (Hawhee 2011), "action is not merely a means of doing but a way of being" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 330). The outfielder's running is not merely instrumental—a means to the end of catching the ball—but is a mode of being-in-the-situation, a way of existing as a perceiving-moving agent whose deliberation is folded into the very quality of the movement. The judgment at T5 is similarly compressed: the outfielder does not consciously evaluate and select among alternative courses of action but rather enacts a kinesthetic judgment that is inseparable from the ongoing adjustment of stride and gaze. Hence, the T4–T5 compression is not a skipping of stages but a radical condensation in which deliberation and judgment are performed by the body's ongoing attunement to the perceptual field rather than by explicit cognitive operations.

This brings us to T6, the moment of overt motor action—the final adjustment of the glove hand, the leap or the slide, the closure of the fingers around the ball. Yet even T6 is not truly final in the outfielder's case, for the micro-adjustments continue until the very instant of the catch. The ball may drift in the wind, may carry farther than anticipated, may sink more rapidly than the initial phantasma projected; each of these variations triggers a new iteration of the T1–T6 loop, each one faster and more compressed than the last as the margin for error narrows. Von Uexküll's concept of the functional cycle between perception signs and effect signs is relevant here, for as he describes, the organism's perceptual apparatus and motor apparatus are linked in a continuous loop in which "the self-tones that respond serve as perception signs of external events" and these perception signs are in turn "stamped as perception marks corresponding to the respective source of stimulus" (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, p. 210). The outfielder's perceptual marks—the visual registration of the ball's position, the proprioceptive sense of the body's orientation—are continuously transposed into motor responses, which in turn alter the perceptual field, generating new marks that demand new responses. The functional cycle is thus a biological instantiation of the T1–T6 loop, and the outfielder's performance reveals this cycle operating at maximal speed and precision.

We should  role of temporal infinity in the outfielder's experience. Aristotle's observation in the *Physics* that "a thing in a finite time cannot come in contact with things quantitatively infinite" but "can come in contact with things infinite in respect of divisibility; for in this sense the time itself is also infinite" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 82) bears directly on the structure of the outfielder's micro-adjustments. The flight of the ball occupies a finite duration, yet within that duration the outfielder's perceptual-motor adjustments are, in principle, infinitely divisible—each adjustment can be further refined, each moment of the trajectory can be further parsed. The contact with the infinite through divisibility rather than quantity is precisely what gives the outfielder's performance its quality of fluid continuity; the discrete iterations of the T1–T6 loop blend into a seamless motion because the temporal intervals between them approach the infinitely small. Similarly, the Gibsonian insight that optical information is structured by the persistence of surface elements over time supports this reading: the outfielder perceives not discrete snapshots of the ball's position but a continuous optical flow in which "the nonpermutation of the adjacent order of these persisting units over time" specifies the ball's trajectory as a unified event (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 212). The T1–T6 loop, iterated at the frequency of perceptual updating, thus produces not a series of discrete actions but a single, temporally extended act whose unity is given by the phantasmatic anticipation that threads through each iteration.

Specifically, this analysis reveals that the outfielder's performance is not well described by the traditional stimulus-response model, in which a perception triggers a fixed motor program that is then executed. Rather, the outfielder embodies a mode of being in which perception, phantasia, desire, judgment, and movement are continually co-constituting one another in a temporal loop whose iterations are too rapid and too tightly integrated to be parsed into discrete stages. Subsequently, we can see that the T1–T6 framework, when applied to real-time athletic performance, does not describe a sequence of stages so much as a recursive structure—a loop that folds back upon itself with each new perceptual datum, refining the phantasma and adjusting the motor response in an ongoing dialogue between soul and world. The outfielder, in this light, is not merely catching a ball; the outfielder is enacting the full temporal architecture of the ensouled body's engagement with a moving world, revealing in compressed and accelerated form the same perceptual-phantasmatic-motor cycle that operates in every act of embodied cognition.

## II.6. The Pianist: Novice versus Expert and the Compression of the T1–T6 Loop

If the outfielder reveals the T1–T6 loop in its iterative, real-time dynamism, the pianist reveals something equally important: how the loop itself is transformed through practice, such that what begins as a slow, stepwise progression through each stage becomes, in the expert, a radically compressed arc in which intermediate stages are absorbed into kinesthetic phantasmata. The contrast between the novice and the expert pianist is thus not merely a difference in speed or accuracy but a difference in the very structure of the soul's engagement with the musical task—a restructuring that illuminates the nature of phantasia, the role of habituation, and the relationship between deliberation and embodied skill.

Consider first the novice. Seated at the keyboard, confronting a musical score, the novice pianist engages the T1–T6 loop in its most explicit and disaggregated form. At T1, the novice perceives the notation on the page—a visual stimulus that must be decoded rather than immediately understood. The perception is effortful and halting; each note must be identified, its position on the staff correlated with a key on the keyboard, its duration parsed from the surrounding rhythmic context. This perceptual labor is already a far cry from the outfielder's fluid visual tracking; the novice's T1 is characterized by a fragmented, piecemeal engagement with the perceptual field in which the affordances of the score are not yet transparent. Von Uexküll's framework is again instructive here, for he describes how "every animal harbors a certain number of tones, which enter into" the functional cycle of perception and effect (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, p. 194). For the novice, the "perception tones"—the perceptual signs that the score presents—are not yet fluently connected to the corresponding "effect tones"—the motor impulses that produce the correct keystrokes. The functional cycle is incomplete or, more precisely, is traversed only with great deliberative effort.

At T2, the novice generates a phantasma of the intended sound—an auditory image of what the note or phrase should sound like when correctly played. This phantasma, however, is often vague or incomplete; the novice may have a general sense of the melody but lacks the detailed auditory-kinesthetic representation that would allow for precise execution. The phantasmatic crystallization at T3 is accordingly tentative: the novice's image of the target sound and the corresponding finger movement is not yet a stable schema but a fragile, effortful construction that must be consciously maintained. Gonzalez's analysis of phantasia in Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is relevant here, for (Hawhee 2011) "phantasia is present even in the study of geometry" and hence is not reducible to mere show or ostentation but functions as a cognitive capacity operative in all forms of structured understanding (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 15). The novice pianist's phantasia operates in precisely this cognitive register: it is not a vivid, automatic image but a laboriously constructed representation that mediates between the perceived score and the intended action.

From T3, the novice must proceed through T4 and T5 in their fully explicit forms. T4, the moment of deliberation, involves a conscious evaluation of alternatives: Which finger should play this note? How should the hand be positioned for the upcoming passage? What dynamic level is indicated? These questions are not rhetorical for the novice; they are genuine deliberative challenges that require the kind of reasoning Aristotle associates with *bouleusis*—practical deliberation about matters that admit of being otherwise. The novice must weigh options, consider consequences, and select a course of action before executing it. (Aristotle 2014) the *Metaphysics* that "one must be already trained to know how to take each sort of argument, since it is absurd to seek at the same time knowledge and the way of attaining knowledge; and neither is easy to get" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 23) applies with particular force to the novice musician, who is simultaneously trying to learn the skill and to exercise it—seeking at once the knowledge and the way of attaining it. T5, the moment of judgment or *krisis*, follows from this deliberation: the novice decides on a fingering, a hand position, a dynamic approach, and commits to executing it. Only then does T6 arrive—the overt motor action of striking the key—and even this final stage is often awkward, imprecise, and accompanied by a sense of uncertainty about whether the intended result has been achieved.

The expert pianist, by contrast, inhabits the T1–T6 loop in a fundamentally different manner. What is most striking about expert performance is not merely its speed—though speed is certainly a factor—but its structural reorganization of the loop itself. The expert does not simply traverse T1 through T6 more quickly; rather, the expert compresses the intermediate stages, specifically T4 and T5, into the phantasmatic crystallization at T3, such that the loop effectively runs T1→T3→T6 with deliberation and judgment absorbed into kinesthetic phantasmata. This compression is the hallmark of what Aristotle would recognize as *hexis*—a stable dispositional state of the soul acquired through habituation, in which the capacity for correct action is no longer dependent on explicit deliberation but is embedded in the body's learned responsiveness. The expert pianist perceives the score (T1) and, almost simultaneously, generates a rich, multi-modal phantasma (T3) that encompasses not merely the auditory image of the intended sound but also the kinesthetic feel of the required finger movements, the proprioceptive sense of hand position and weight distribution, and the affective quality of the musical phrase. This phantasma is not merely an image in the narrow visual sense; it is what we might call a kinesthetic phantasma—a full-bodied, multi-sensory anticipation that carries within itself the deliberative and judicative functions that the novice must perform explicitly.

How does this compression occur? The process is one of habituation through repetition, in which the functional cycle between perception signs and effect signs is progressively tightened until the two become nearly simultaneous. Von Uexküll's metaphor of the organism as a kind of musical instrument is apposite: he speaks of "perception tones" and "effect tones" that, like the tones of a carillon, "enter into" structured patterns of response (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, p. 194). For the expert pianist, the perception tones—the visual and auditory signs of the score and the instrument—and the effect tones—the motor impulses that drive the fingers—have been tuned, through practice, into a harmonious functional unity. The carillon metaphor is particularly apt because it captures the way in which expert musical performance has a quality of resonance: each perception tone calls forth its corresponding effect tone not through deliberation but through a kind of sympathetic vibration, a direct coupling of perception and action that bypasses the explicit calculative steps of T4 and T5.

This is not to say that the expert pianist never deliberates; in interpretive decisions, in confronting a new or unusual passage, in responding to the acoustics of an unfamiliar hall, the expert may re-engage the full T1–T6 loop with its explicit deliberative stages. But in the performance of well-practiced repertoire, the compression to T1→T3→T6 is the norm, and it is this compression that gives expert performance its quality of effortless fluency. Accordingly, we can understand why Aristotle insists in the *Rhetoric* on the connection between phantasia and persuasion, for just as the skilled rhetorician brings the audience "before the eyes" through vivid language—making them see and feel the matter at hand—so too the skilled pianist brings the music "before the fingers" through kinesthetic phantasia, making the body enact the musical intention without the intervention of explicit deliberation. Gonzalez emphasizes that phantasia in Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is not mere ornamental display but a cognitive and perceptual operation: it is "not πρὸς αὑτόν, not πρὸς τὸν ἀκροατήν," as in different contexts, but functions wherever teaching and persuasion take place (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 15). Similarly, the expert pianist's kinesthetic phantasia is not a subjective embellishment of the musical experience but the very medium through which musical meaning is realized in sound.

Burke's insight that "action is not merely a means of doing but a way of being" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 330) finds its most vivid illustration in the expert pianist's performance. The expert's playing is not merely a means of producing correct notes—an instrumental relation of agency to purpose—but a way of being musical, a mode of existence in which the body, the instrument, the score, and the audience are gathered into a unified act that transcends the sum of its mechanical parts. Burke's dramatistic vocabulary helps us see that the compression of the T1–T6 loop is not merely a cognitive-motor achievement but an ontological transformation: the pianist moves from a mode of being in which action is effortful and deliberate to a mode of being in which action is expressive and revelatory—a being-in-the-world through music. Hence the expert's compressed loop is not a diminished version of the novice's full loop but an enriched one, in which the deliberative and judicative functions have been internalized so thoroughly that they operate as qualities of the movement itself rather than as discrete preparatory stages.

Finally, we should consider what the contrast between novice and expert reveals about the nature of phantasia as such. Papachristou's analysis of three grades of phantasia in Aristotle's *De Anima* is suggestive here, for  Aristotle "discriminates not two—as it is commonly argued—but three kinds or grades of phantasia" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, p. 25). If we map these grades onto the pianist's development, we can see that the novice operates primarily at the level of indeterminate phantasia—a vague, effortful imaging that requires explicit deliberative support—while the expert operates at a higher grade in which phantasia is determinate, richly structured, and functionally integrated with both perception and motor action. The progression from novice to expert is thus not merely a matter of motor learning but a transformation in the very grade of phantasia that governs the soul's engagement with the musical task. Subsequently, this transformation illuminates the deeper philosophical point that phantasia is not a fixed faculty with a single mode of operation but a dynamic capacity that develops, refines, and restructures itself through habituation and practice—a capacity that, in its highest expression, compresses the full arc of the soul's temporal engagement with the world into a single, fluid, kinesthetically rich act.

Thus, the outfielder and the pianist together reveal the T1–T6 loop as both iteratively dynamic and developmentally plastic. The outfielder shows us the loop in its rapid, real-time iteration, each cycle a micro-adjustment guided by continuously updated phantasmata; the pianist shows us the loop in its developmental compression, the novice's laborious stepwise progression yielding, through practice, to the expert's compressed arc from perception to kinesthetic phantasma to action. In both cases, it is phantasia—understood not as mere mental imagery but as the soul's kinetic, anticipatory, multi-modal engagement with the world—that serves as the linchpin of the loop, the faculty that transforms bare perception into meaningful, directed, embodied action. These two cases accordingly prepare us for the broader theoretical question that follows: how the T1–T6 loop, in its various modes and compressions, constitutes the temporal structure of the ensouled body's being-in-the-world and grounds the possibility of rhetorical, aesthetic, and practical engagement with the situations we encounter.
