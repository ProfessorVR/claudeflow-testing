# Phantasia, Attunement, and Environment: The Architectonic of Appearance from Aristotle through Heidegger and Uexküll

The question of how beings appear to consciousness—and how that appearing is structured by faculties, moods, and environments—has animated philosophical inquiry from antiquity through phenomenology and into contemporary rhetorical theory. Aristotle's account of phantasia as a mediating motion between sensation and judgment, Heidegger's existential analytic of Befindlichkeit as the horizon within which beings disclose themselves, and Jakob von Uexküll's biosemiotic concept of the Umwelt as a species-specific perceptual bubble together constitute a genealogy of appearance that challenges representationalist epistemologies and opens rhetorical studies to ambient, ecological modes of thinking. Indeed, these three frameworks share a commitment to understanding appearance not as a passive mirroring of external reality but as an active, temporally unfolding process in which the perceiving subject is already situated within a world that conditions what can show itself and how it can matter. Thus, the present inquiry traces the architectonic of appearance through Aristotle's temporal sequence from sensation to action, Heidegger's existential reinterpretation of Aristotelian pathe as attunements that disclose being-in-the-world, and Uexküll's and Rickert's accounts of environmental shaping, wherein the dwelling-place and perceptual capacities of an organism co-constitute the phenomenal field. By examining these interlocking frameworks, we may articulate a more robust understanding of how rhetoric operates not merely through symbolic persuasion but through the ambient conditions that shape the very possibility of appearance and significance; accordingly, this  rhetorical being is fundamentally attuned being, embedded in environments that precede and exceed conscious intention yet remain open to inventional transformation through dwelling and attunement.

## 1. Aristotle's T0–T6 Structure: From Aisthesis to Action through Phantasmata

Aristotle's account of phantasia in *De Anima* establishes a temporal and causal sequence that moves from initial sensory contact with an object through residual motion in the soul to the formation of phantasmata, which subsequently enable judgment and motivate action. This sequence, which we may schematize as T0 (sensory encounter) through T6 (completed action), reveals phantasia to be not a static repository of images but a dynamic process of motion within the soul that bridges the gap between passive reception and active response. As Papachristou observes, "phantasia (φαντασία) is a kind of motion (κίνησις) in the soul that cannot exist apart from sensation (αἴσθησις)—it is caused by the activity of sensation" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 12-13). Thus, phantasia is fundamentally derivative from aisthesis yet distinct from it; it arises only after the sensory organ has been acted upon by a sensible object, yet it persists beyond the moment of contact, enabling the soul to retain and manipulate what has been perceived even in the absence of the original stimulus. The temporal structure of this process is crucial: at T0, the sense organ receives an impression from the external object; at T1, the sensory apparatus undergoes alteration (alloiosis) as the form of the object is received without its matter; at T2, the motion initiated by sensation continues within the soul as a residual kinesis, which Aristotle identifies as the operation of phantasia itself; subsequently, at T3, this motion gives rise to phantasmata, which are not mere copies of sensory data but transformed presentations that can be combined, compared, and manipulated by the soul. Indeed, Aristotle insists that phantasia plays an integral role in both sense perception and thought, as O'Gorman notes when summarizing the scholarly consensus that *De Anima* grants phantasia a mediating function between sensation and intellection (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, pp. 2-3). The phantasma thus becomes the material with which the soul works when it engages in deliberation, comparison, and practical reasoning; at T4, the soul exercises judgment (krisis) by comparing phantasmata and discerning similarities or differences among them, a process that requires the discriminating power to be "qua undivided one, and active in a single moment of time, while qua divisible it twice over uses the same dot at one and the same time" (Aristotle, *On The Soul*, pp. 40-42). This paradoxical unity-in-division of the judging faculty ensures that the soul can hold multiple phantasmata in view simultaneously and thereby perceive relations among them, which is essential for practical deliberation and for the formation of desire. At T5, desire (orexis) arises in response to the phantasmata presented by the soul; as Gonzalez explains, "motion must be traced to desire, desire that is not without phantasia; and that phantasia can be categorized as to its connection with either logos or perception" (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 22-23). Finally, at T6, action follows from desire as the soul moves the body toward or away from the object that has been phantasmatically presented, completing the circuit from initial sensation to embodied response. This temporal unfolding reveals that phantasia is not a static faculty but a kinetic process that structures the soul's engagement with the world; it is, as Papachristou argues, a motion that includes multiple grades or kinds, ranging from indefinite phantasia that merely retains sensory residues to deliberative phantasia that enables rational beings to weigh options and choose courses of action (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 25-27). Moreover, the fact that phantasia cannot exist apart from sensation yet is not identical to it underscores its status as a mediating faculty that transforms the passive reception of sensory data into the active material of thought and desire. Hence, the T0–T6  appearance is not a simple given but a temporally extended process in which the soul's capacity for motion—its ability to be moved by and to move itself—determines what can show itself and how it can matter for action. This Aristotelian framework thus provides the foundation for subsequent phenomenological investigations into the temporal and existential structures of disclosure, as we shall see when we turn to Heidegger's reinterpretation of Aristotelian pathe as existential attunements.

## 2. Heidegger's Befindlichkeit and Stimmung: The Existential Horizon of Disclosure

Heidegger's existential analytic in *Being and Time* radically reinterprets the Aristotelian account of phantasia and pathe by situating them within the broader structure of Dasein's being-in-the-world, thereby transforming epistemological questions about perception and judgment into ontological questions about the conditions under which beings can appear at all. Central to this transformation is Heidegger's concept of Befindlichkeit, which is often translated as "state-of-mind" or "affectedness" but which more fundamentally designates the existential structure by which Dasein always already finds itself in a world that matters, a world in which things and options have significance prior to any explicit act of cognition or volition. As Heidegger states, "What we indicate ontologically by the term 'state-of-mind' is ontically the most familiar and everyday sort of thing; our mood, our Being-attuned" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 173). Thus, Befindlichkeit is not a psychological property that Dasein occasionally possesses but an existential structure that constitutes Dasein's very being; it is the primordial disclosedness of being-in-the-world, the "there" (Da) of Dasein's existence, which is always already affectively toned or attuned (gestimmt) by a mood (Stimmung). Indeed, Heidegger insists that mood is not a subjective coloring that Dasein projects onto an otherwise neutral world but the very medium through which the world discloses itself; only because Dasein is attuned can entities within the world matter, can they show themselves as threatening or inviting, as familiar or strange, as significant or insignificant. This existential priority of attunement over cognition represents a decisive break with the Cartesian tradition, which treats affective states as secondary modifications of a primarily cognitive relation to the world; for Heidegger, by contrast, "Being there as State-of-mind" means that Dasein's cognitive and practical engagements are always already conditioned by an affective disclosure that cannot be reduced to or derived from theoretical knowledge. Accordingly, Heidegger reinterprets the Aristotelian pathe—the passions or affections that Aristotle analyzes in the *Rhetoric* and *De Anima*—not as episodic emotional states but as existential modes of Befindlichkeit, as ways in which Dasein is disclosed to itself and to the world. As the *Heidegger and Rhetoric* volume notes, Heidegger reads Aristotle's *Rhetoric* as "the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness (Alltaglichkeit) of being amongst one another" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 174-175), thereby transforming rhetoric from a techne of persuasion into an existential analytic of how Dasein is always already attuned in its being-with-others. This reinterpretation allows Heidegger to connect Aristotelian phantasia to existential temporality: where Aristotle sees phantasia as a residual motion that persists after sensation, Heidegger sees a structure of retention and protention by which Dasein's past and future are held together in the present; where Aristotle sees phantasmata as the material for judgment and deliberation, Heidegger sees the existential projection of possibilities that are disclosed through attunement. Specifically, the Aristotelian sequence from aisthesis to phantasia to judgment is recast as the existential structure of thrownness (Geworfenheit), projection (Entwurf), and falling (Verfallen), which together constitute the care (Sorge) that defines Dasein's being. Thus, the initial sensory encounter (T0–T1 in the Aristotelian schema) corresponds to Dasein's thrownness, its finding itself always already in a world that it did not choose and cannot fully master; the residual motion and formation of phantasmata (T2–T3) correspond to Dasein's projection, its ability to hold open possibilities and to understand itself in terms of what it can be; and the movement from judgment to desire and action (T4–T6) corresponds to Dasein's falling, its tendency to lose itself in the busy-ness of everyday concern and to understand itself in terms of the "they" (das Man). Moreover, Heidegger's emphasis on mood as the primordial disclosure of the "that-it-is" of Dasein's existence—what he calls the "thrownness" of Dasein—directly parallels the Aristotelian insight that phantasia discloses not merely the "what" of an object but the "that" of its presence or absence. As Heidegger writes, "the mood brings Dasein before the 'that-it-is' of its 'there', which, as such, stares it in the face with the inexorability of an enigma" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 176-177). This "that-it-is" is not a fact that Dasein can cognize objectively but an existential weight that it must bear, a burden of existence that is disclosed affectively rather than cognitively; similarly, Aristotelian phantasia discloses the presence or absence of an object not as a neutral datum but as something that moves the soul toward or away from it, that elicits desire or aversion. Hence, Heidegger's existential analytic reveals that the Aristotelian structure of phantasia and pathe is not merely a psychological mechanism but an ontological condition: appearance is always already attuned appearance, and the world discloses itself only to a being that is affectively situated within it. Furthermore, Heidegger's concept of Stimmung extends the Aristotelian insight that phantasia is a kind of motion (kinesis) by showing that attunement is not a static state but a dynamic process of being-moved and being-attuned; as Rickert observes, Dreyfus translates Befindlichkeit as "affectedness" in order to convey "being found in a situation where things and options already matter" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 302-303), thereby emphasizing the passive dimension of attunement, the sense in which Dasein is always already claimed by a world that exceeds its control. Thus, the existential horizon of disclosure that Heidegger articulates through Befindlichkeit and Stimmung is not a neutral framework within which beings appear but an affectively charged field in which significance and mattering are primordial; only on the basis of this existential attunement can entities show themselves as the kinds of beings they are, and only through the transformation of attunement can new possibilities for understanding and action emerge. Accordingly, Heidegger's reinterpretation of Aristotelian phantasia and pathe as existential structures of  rhetoric operates not merely at the level of symbolic persuasion but at the deeper level of affective attunement, where the very conditions of appearance and significance are at stake.

## 3. Uexküll's Umwelt and Rickert's Ambience: Environmental Shaping of Appearance

The biosemiotic concept of the Umwelt, developed by Jakob von Uexküll in the early twentieth century, extends the Aristotelian and Heideggerian insights into the conditioned nature of appearance by demonstrating that every organism inhabits a species-specific perceptual world that is constituted by its sensory capacities and practical needs, such that what appears as significant to one organism may be entirely invisible to another. Uexküll's famous metaphor of the "soap bubble" captures this insight: each organism lives within a phenomenal bubble that includes only those features of the environment that its sensory organs can detect and that its action repertoire can engage, so that the objective physical environment is refracted into a multiplicity of distinct Umwelten, each of which is the only world that exists for the organism in question. As Uexküll explains, "everything a subject perceives belongs to its perception world [Merkwelt], and everything it produces, to its effect world [Wirkwelt]" (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans*, pp. 45-46). This distinction between the perception world and the effect world underscores the active, selective nature of appearance: the organism does not passively receive stimuli from a neutral environment but actively constructs a meaningful world by filtering sensory data through species-specific perceptual organs and by acting upon the environment in ways that are determined by its biological needs and capacities. Thus, the Umwelt is not a subjective projection onto an objective reality but the only reality that exists for the organism; it is the world as it appears within the constraints and affordances of the organism's embodied existence. This biosemiotic framework resonates deeply with the Aristotelian account of phantasia as a faculty that mediates between sensation and action, for Uexküll's Merkwelt corresponds to the domain of phantasmata—the retained and transformed sensory data that the soul manipulates in the course of deliberation—and his Wirkwelt corresponds to the domain of action that follows from desire and judgment. Indeed, Uexküll's emphasis on the organism's active construction of its perceptual world parallels Aristotle's insistence that phantasia is a kind of motion, a dynamic process rather than a passive reception; both frameworks reject the notion that appearance is a simple mirroring of external reality and insist instead that what appears is always already shaped by the capacities and needs of the perceiving subject. Moreover, Uexküll's concept of the Umwelt anticipates Heidegger's existential analytic by demonstrating that the world is not a container within which organisms are placed but a structure of significance that is co-constituted by the organism's being-in-the-world; as Rickert notes, Heidegger argues that "dwelling indicates not a worldview but a principle of being: culture is materially enworlded" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 239-240), thereby rejecting the subject-object dualism that treats the world as an external object to be cognized and emphasizing instead that Dasein's being is fundamentally a being-in-a-world that is already meaningful and significant. Rickert's concept of ambient rhetoric builds on both Uexküll's biosemiotic framework and Heidegger's existential analytic by arguing that rhetorical effects are not merely the result of symbolic persuasion but are produced by the ambient conditions—the material, spatial, and affective environments—within which discourse unfolds and within which subjects are attuned.
