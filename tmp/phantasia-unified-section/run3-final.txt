## IV. Phantasia, Umwelt, and the Soap Bubble of World-Disclosure

Having examined in the preceding section how phantasia operates through memory, judgment, emotion, and habituated practice—as illustrated by the outfielder tracking a fly ball and the pianist whose fingers find their way across the keys—we are now positioned to ask a broader question: if phantasia is the faculty through which the soul renders the world present, making appearances appear as something determinate, does it not follow that phantasia also constitutes a kind of perceptual horizon, a bounded disclosure of the world shaped by the organism's own capacities and concerns? To pursue this question, we must turn to a conceptual resource that may seem, at first glance, far removed from Aristotle's psychology: the biosemiotic framework of Jakob von Uexküll, specifically his famous metaphor of the "soap bubble" that encloses every organism within its own species-specific world of meaning. The juxtaposition is not as strained as it might appear; indeed, both Aristotle and Uexküll share a conviction that perception is not passive reception of a pre-given environment but an active, organismic disclosure of a world shaped by the perceiving creature's own capacities, needs, and modes of engagement. As Uexküll writes, describing the fundamental relationship between subject and environment, "the sensation 'blue' becomes the 'blueness' of the sky, the sensation 'green' becomes the 'greenness' of the lawn, and so forth" (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 52–53). The organism does not encounter a neutral field of data; it encounters a world already colored by its own perceptual and effector organs, a world that is, in a profound sense, its own. For Aristotle, phantasia occupies a structurally analogous position. It is neither mere sensation nor intellection but, as Gonzalez insists, a distinct faculty through which the soul makes things appear as determinate, meaningful phenomena. Specifically, Gonzalez demonstrates that in *Rhetoric* III.1, phantasia names not a subjective illusion but the fundamental condition under which logos reaches its audience: "for logos [is] a kind of sign, so that it will not accomplish its goal unless" it is brought before the hearer through phantasia (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 8–9). Thus logos itself depends upon the phantasmatic rendering of things—their being made to appear as something to someone. 5). Phantasia, then, is not a static image but a dynamic movement of the soul, a kinesis that constitutes a horizon of appearance. It is worth noting that phantasia admits of internal distinctions—grades ranging from indeterminate or sensory phantasia to deliberative phantasia (the *bouleutikē phantasia* operative in rational beings)—as well as a complex relation to belief (*doxa*) and a notable voluntariness in its higher forms; these distinctions, which Papachristou has delineated as comprising not two but three grades of phantasia (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 25–27), will receive full elaboration in a subsequent chapter. For the present argument, what matters is that phantasia, in all its grades, constitutes the perceptual bubble through which the human soul discloses its world. ## IV.1 Uexküll's Soap Bubble: Species-Specific Bubbles of Meaning

Uexküll's central contribution to the biological sciences—and, subsequently, to philosophy—lies in his demonstration that every organism inhabits not "the" world but its own *Umwelt*, a species-specific environment constituted by the interplay of its perception organs (*Merkorgan*) and its effector organs (*Wirkorgan*). The soap bubble metaphor captures this insight with particular vividness: each animal lives enclosed in a bubble of perception marks and effect marks that together compose its meaningful world, and this bubble is, in principle, impenetrable from the outside. We cannot inhabit the tick's Umwelt any more than the tick can inhabit ours; we can only reconstruct it through careful attention to the organism's anatomical and behavioral capacities. As Uexküll demonstrates through his analysis of the fly's visual field, "the coarser the mosaic, the greater the loss of the details of the things, and the world as seen through a fly's eye must seem significantly coarsened as compared to its being seen through a human eye" (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 66–69). The fly's soap bubble is not a degraded version of the human world; it is a different world altogether, one in which certain features of the environment are salient and others simply do not exist for the organism. Aristotle  "being has two forms—it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157), and indeed the way in which being shows itself to any given creature depends upon the form of that creature's perceptual and cognitive engagement. Accordingly, what Uexküll calls the perception mark (*Merkmal*) can be understood as the biosemiotic analogue of the phantasma: both name the way in which the organism's own activity transforms raw contact with the environment into meaningful appearance. The tick responds to butyric acid, warmth, and the tactile quality of skin—three perception marks that compose its entire Umwelt. The human, by contrast, inhabits a vastly more complex bubble, one in which phantasia operates across sensory, mnemonic, emotional, and deliberative registers. Hawhee underscores this complexity when  epideictic speeches "take up actions that are agreed upon, so that what remains is to clothe the actions with greatness (megethos) and beauty" (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 12–13); the rhetorical amplification of actions into visions of magnitude and beauty is itself a function of the human phantasmatic bubble, a capacity for world-disclosure that far exceeds the tick's three-dimensional Umwelt while remaining, in structural terms, analogous to it. Thus Uexküll's framework helps us to see that phantasia is not merely an internal psychological event but a world-constituting capacity—a soap bubble of meaning that surrounds the organism and determines the horizon within which things can appear. Specifically, the human soap bubble is characterized by its extraordinary richness: because phantasia operates in conjunction with logos, memory, desire, and habituated practice, the human Umwelt encompasses not only the immediately sensible but also the remembered, the anticipated, the imagined, and the desired. Bowin's analysis of Aristotle's account of time-perception illustrates this point, noting that "Aristotle calls this perceiving time because he does not distinguish between remembering time and perceiving time as it is passing" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 9–10). The human soap bubble is thus temporally extended in a way that the tick's is not; it includes not only what is present but what has been and what may yet come to pass. ## IV.2 Human Phantasia and Mood as an Anthropological "Soap Bubble"

If every organism inhabits a soap bubble of meaning, what distinguishes the human bubble? The answer, we propose, lies in the intersection of phantasia and mood—or, to use the Heideggerian term, *Befindlichkeit* (state-of-mind, attunement). Heidegger's early lectures on Aristotle, delivered in the summer semester of 1924, reveal the depth of his engagement with precisely this question. As Gross observes in his account of Heidegger's Marburg lectures, "the determination of Being-with-one-another in politike founds that which rhetoric brings to language" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 45–46). Rhetoric, for the early Heidegger, is not mere persuasion but a fundamental mode of disclosing the shared world of *Mitsein*; and it accomplishes this disclosure through the passions (*pathē*), which Heidegger interprets not as transient feelings but as fundamental ways of finding oneself in the world. Indeed, as the same volume records, "passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). The passions, understood as modes of *Befindlichkeit*, are thus themselves kinetic—they are movements of the soul that alter the horizon within which things appear. Hence they are intimately connected to phantasia, which is likewise a form of kinesis, a movement that renders the world determinate. What mood accomplishes, on this reading, is a modulation of the human soap bubble. When one is afraid, the world appears threatening; when one is confident, it appears manageable; when one is in a state of wonder, it appears strange and worthy of inquiry. Accordingly, Rickert argues that a worldview "constitutes how the world appears 'out there' as colored by cultural lenses, a position that insinuates a disengagement between people and world, so that ultimately 'objective' reason becomes the means to eliminate cultural distortion and get at the actual essence of things" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 239–240). Yet as Heidegger insists, dwelling is not a worldview but a principle of being; we do not stand outside our soap bubble and observe it from a neutral vantage point. We are always already within it, and it is phantasia—operating in concert with mood, habit, memory, and desire—that constitutes the bubble's contours. The "rhetorical situation," as Gross has framed it, involves an exigence, an audience, and a set of constraints (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, pp. 102–103); but these elements are themselves disclosed through the phantasmatic-affective bubble of the situated agent. The exigence appears as exigent, the audience as responsive or resistant, the constraints as enabling or limiting, only because phantasia renders them so within the mood that currently attunes the agent to the world. Gonzalez's analysis of *Rhetoric* III.1 reinforces this point. (Hawhee 2011) phantasia in its deliberative or logistic grade—the *logistikē phantasia* that operates "in rational beings"—is what enables the human agent to weigh alternative courses of action, to consider what is beneficial or harmful, noble or base (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 22–23). This deliberative phantasia is not detached from mood; on the contrary, it operates within and through the affective attunement that constitutes the agent's current soap bubble. Subsequently, the full elaboration of phantasia's internal grades—sensory, indeterminate, and deliberative—and their respective relations to belief (*doxa*) and to the voluntary will be developed in a dedicated chapter. Burke, for his part, illuminates the broader stakes of this claim when (Hawhee 2011) individual passions may unconsciously further designs that exceed the agent's own awareness, such that "Absolute Spirit is said to act by using the blind passions of individual men as its medium" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 288–289). While Burke's Hegelian idiom differs from Aristotle's, the structural insight is analogous: the passions, as modulations of the phantasmatic bubble, do not merely reflect the world but actively constitute it, and they do so in ways that may exceed the agent's own deliberate intentions. Thus we arrive at the central thesis of this section: the human Umwelt—the anthropological soap bubble—is constituted by the interplay of phantasia and mood. Phantasia, as the faculty that renders the world present under determinate appearances, provides the perceptual and cognitive content of the bubble; mood, as the attunement that colors and orients that content, provides its affective tonality. Together, they compose what Heidegger calls "thrownness," the condition in which Dasein always already finds itself disclosed to a world that matters, a world in which things appear as significant, urgent, or indifferent (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 400–401). The soap bubble is never neutral, never a transparent medium through which an objective world is passively received; it is always already shaped by the organism's own capacities, concerns, and current affective state. Hence the human soap bubble, while structurally analogous to the tick's or the fly's, is distinguished by its extraordinary complexity—a complexity rooted in the multiple grades of phantasia, the richness of human mood, and the capacity of logos to articulate, amplify, and transform the appearances that phantasia makes available.
