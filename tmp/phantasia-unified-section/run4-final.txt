## V. Mood, Ambience, and Dwelling: Heidegger and Rickert

In Section IV we traced the structural homology between the animal's Funktionskreis—its closed circuit of Merkbild and Wirkbild through which environmental carriers of significance are encountered—and the human capacity for phantasia operating in concert with Stimmung, whereby Dasein's disclosive "soap bubble" of meaning is perpetually generated. What emerged was a picture in which taking-as, the fundamental structure of encountering things as something, is not a purely intellectual act but a dynamic, affectively saturated movement of the soul that mirrors, at a higher register, the perceptual-motor loop Uexküll describes for the tick or the sea urchin. We must now deepen the affective side of this account. Specifically, if phantasia furnishes the image-laden medium through which things appear, mood (Stimmung) provides the atmospheric ground within which any such appearing first becomes possible. The question that guides the present section is accordingly twofold: How does Heidegger's ontological treatment of mood disclose the primordial openness of Dasein to a world, and how does Thomas Rickert's concept of ambience extend and rhetorically enrich that disclosure so as to illuminate what it means for Dasein to dwell amid the things that matter? Heidegger's analysis in *Being and Time* establishes mood not as a psychological coloring laid over an already disclosed world but as an existential structure—Befindlichkeit—through which Dasein finds itself always already situated in a world that presses upon it. The "downward plunge" (Absturz) that Heidegger describes, wherein "Dasein remains in the throw, and is sucked into the turbulence of the 'they's' inauthenticity," reveals that thrownness is not a single event completed at birth but the ongoing facticity of being delivered over to a world one did not choose (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 223). Mood, in other words, is the way thrownness is lived: it is the medium in which Dasein's there is perpetually opened and attuned. Indeed, without Befindlichkeit there would be no clearing in which entities could show up as anything at all; entities within-the-world can be encountered only because Dasein is always already in a mood that renders it receptive to what it meets. Heidegger's own term for the everyday mode of such encountering is "dealings" (Umgang) with entities in the environment, and the Being of those entities is exhibited phenomenologically when "we take as our clue our everyday Being-in-the-world, which we also call our 'dealings' in the world and with entities within-the-world" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 96). Thus the environmental things nearest to Dasein—the hammer, the doorway, the path through the national park—are not first present-at-hand objects subsequently invested with value; they are discovered within a mood-laden totality of involvements. Rickert's contribution is to press Heidegger's insight beyond the traditional boundaries of the human subject and toward a broader ecology of attunement. In *Ambient Rhetoric*, Rickert argues that affects and environments are not separable poles—subject here, world there—but are "complexly generating an overarching ambient environment that cradles, without forcing, particular affective responses or that fosters an attunement" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 172). The verb "cradles" is telling: ambience does not determine a response causally but holds open a space within which certain affective orientations become likely, relevant, or resonant. Similarly, Rickert's reading of Heidegger's fourfold underscores that "the human remains an important site for disclosure, but the world also discloses itself in its own ways—this too is part of the world's 'worlding'" (Rickert, *Towards Ecosophy in a Participating World*, p. 12). Hence mood is never merely subjective; it is the joint product of Dasein's thrown projection and the self-showing of the world. The implications for rhetoric are profound: persuasion, on this account, does not operate solely through propositional content or even through the arousal of discrete emotions but through the cultivation of an ambient attunement that enables the user to encounter a situation as calling for a particular kind of response. ## V.1 Befindlichkeit and Stimmung: Mood as Ontological Ground

Heidegger's existential analytic in *Being and Time* insists that Dasein is never encountered in a mood-free state. Every cognitive act, every practical engagement, every encounter with an entity in the environment presupposes a prior attunement that has already disclosed the world in a particular tonality. This is the meaning of Befindlichkeit—literally, the state of finding-oneself—which Heidegger distinguishes sharply from any merely ontic psychology of emotion. Befindlichkeit names the ontological condition of possibility for moods (Stimmungen), while Stimmung names the specific existentiell manner in which Dasein is attuned at any given time. Together they constitute one of the three equiprimordial structures of the "there" (Da) alongside understanding (Verstehen) and discourse (Rede). What is decisive for our argument is that Befindlichkeit is not derivative of cognition; it is co-original with it, and in many respects more primordial, since Dasein must already find itself disposed toward the world before any theoretical or practical apprehension can begin. The ontological weight of this claim can be grasped by attending to Heidegger's account of thrownness (Geworfenheit). As Heidegger writes, thrownness "is neither a 'fact that is finished' nor a Fact that is settled" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 223). The ongoing, unsettled character of thrownness means that Dasein is perpetually being delivered over to its own situatedness, and this delivery is experienced precisely as mood. Fear, anxiety, boredom, elation—each of these moods discloses the world differently, not by adding a subjective coloring to a neutral set of facts but by opening up or closing down specific regions of significance. In anxiety, for instance, the familiar totality of involvements collapses, and Dasein finds itself confronting the sheer that-it-is of its existence; in everyday absorbed coping, by contrast, mood recedes into the background precisely because its disclosive work proceeds so smoothly. Heidegger's analysis of "everydayness" (Alltäglichkeit) as "that kind of Being in which Dasein maintains itself proximally and for the most part" underscores that what this expression "signifies at bottom when delimited ontologically, remains obscure" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 421). The obscurity is itself significant: everyday mood is inconspicuous because it pervades everything, functioning as the atmospheric medium in which entities show up as available, threatening, inviting, or indifferent. What is crucial here is the distinction between mood understood ontologically and mood understood as an emergent psychological state. O'Connor and Wong, in their analysis of emergence, observe that a structural property obtains when "there is nothing more to having the structural property than being composed by parts having certain other properties and bearing certain relations to one another—it is ontologically reducible" (O'Connor and Wong, *The Metaphysics of Emergence*, p. 5). Mood, on the Heideggerian account, is precisely not ontologically reducible in this way; it cannot be decomposed into a set of neurological firings plus environmental stimuli and thereby explained. Rather, Stimmung is the prior whole within which any such decomposition is subsequently carried out. Mood is thus an emergent existential totality that cannot be traced back to its ontic components without remainder, just as the care-structure (Sorge) that Heidegger identifies as the Being of Dasein "cannot be traced back to some ontical" element (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 241). Accordingly, the ontological irreducibility of mood signals that Befindlichkeit occupies a level of analysis distinct from both folk psychology and cognitive science: it names the condition under which any affective episode, any perceptual encounter, any rhetorical situation first comes to be what it is. If phantasia is the kinetic movement of the soul through which images are generated and retained—the movement that Aristotle connects to perception, desire, and judgment—then Stimmung is the atmosphere within which that movement acquires its directionality. Without a prior attunement, phantasia would be, as it were, a mechanism spinning freely; it is mood that gives phantasia its traction, its bearing on the particular situation in which Dasein finds itself. Indeed, the passions (pathē) that Heidegger interprets through Aristotle's *Rhetoric* are understood not as static cognitive states but as modes of Befindlichkeit, as ways of being moved that disclose the world in determinate ways (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Thus Heidegger's own reading of Aristotle's affective categories underscores the inseparability of mood and the image-forming capacity we have been calling phantasia: passion is "both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Phantasia, as itself a motion in the soul, is accordingly conditioned by the pathē that attune Dasein to what matters. ## V.2 Rickert's Ambience, Attunement, and Rhetorical Dwelling

If Heidegger provides the ontological architecture of mood, Thomas Rickert furnishes its rhetorical habitation. Rickert's concept of ambience extends Befindlichkeit beyond the existential analytic of Dasein and into the broader ecology of places, things, and atmospheric conditions within which rhetorical persuasion operates. In doing so, Rickert does not abandon Heidegger but radicalizes the insight that mood is never merely internal; it is always already shared, distributed, and environmentally enmeshed. The result is a notion of rhetoric that can no longer be confined to the speaker's strategic deployment of appeals but must encompass the entire ambient surround—the lighting of a room, the hum of a city, the silence of the national park at dusk—that disposes an audience toward certain modes of responsiveness. Central to Rickert's project is  affective environments are not passive backdrops against which rhetorical action occurs but are themselves generative of attunement. As Rickert argues, such environments are "complexly generating an overarching ambient environment that cradles, without forcing, particular affective responses or that fosters an attunement" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 172). The phrase "without forcing" is essential: ambience operates below the threshold of explicit argumentation, shaping the conditions under which arguments gain or lose traction. Hence Rickert proposes that rhetorical theory must attend not only to logos, pathos, and ethos but to the choric, material, and atmospheric dimensions that precede and condition those classical appeals. This move does not render the classical categories obsolete but reveals them as embedded within a wider field of disclosive forces. Similarly, Rickert's engagement with Heidegger's later topology of Being—in which poetic thinking becomes, as the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* note, "the dwelling place [Ortschaft] of essence" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 166)—suggests that rhetoric is not merely an art of persuasion but a mode of dwelling, a way of inhabiting and being inhabited by the world's self-disclosure. If ambience is the atmospheric field within which attunement arises, then phantasia can be understood as the faculty through which that atmospheric field is rendered determinate—through which the vague press of mood becomes a specific image, a particular scene, a concrete taking-as. Rickert's insight that the body is "newly plastic," that the merging of mind and tool "blur the distinction between 'mind' and 'tool'" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 57), resonates deeply with the Aristotelian picture of phantasia as a power that is neither purely sensory nor purely intellectual but operates at the mobile boundary between organism and environment. Indeed, Gibson's ecological approach to perception similarly challenges the interiority of cognition; even Gibson's account of the permanent objects of the world as "actually only objects that persist for a very long time" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 37) suggests that what we take to be stable features of an objective world are in fact dynamically sustained through an ongoing perceptual engagement that is always situated within a broader ambient field. Thus Rickert, Heidegger, and Gibson converge on a picture in which the subject-object divide is insufficient to account for the way things show up: it is the ambient, mooded, ecologically embedded character of perception that enables the user to encounter entities as meaningful. What Rickert adds that is distinctive, however, is a rhetorical sensibility: the recognition that ambience is not merely given but is also cultivated, designed, and contested. Our national parks, for example, are deliberately managed to produce certain ambient experiences—the silence, the grandeur, the sense of temporal depth—that attune visitors to a particular mode of dwelling. The rhetoric of environmental conservation thus operates not only through explicit arguments about biodiversity or climate change but through the ambient fostering of attunements that dispose people to protect our national parks as places of irreplaceable significance. Hence Rickert's ambient rhetoric discloses a dimension of persuasion that classical rhetorical theory, focused as it is on the discrete speech act, has tended to overlook; and this dimension is precisely the one in which Stimmung and phantasia collaborate to generate the world-disclosing "soap bubble" of meaning we have been describing. The term antichesis—literally, a placing-against or counter-positioning—is employed here to name the dynamic interplay between mood and phantasia through which Dasein's world achieves its determinate character. If Stimmung opens the atmospheric clearing, phantasia fills that clearing with images, scenes, and anticipatory projections; and the ongoing tension between these two dimensions—the diffuse and the determinate, the atmospheric and the imagistic—constitutes the fundamental rhythm of world-disclosure. Aristotle's account of phantasia, as we noted in earlier sections, situates it between perception and thought: it is a motion in the soul occasioned by actual perception but capable of persisting and operating in perception's absence. Gonzalez observes that in Aristotle's treatment "phantasia is present even in the study of geometry" and accordingly "cannot be 'mere show' or 'ostentation'" (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 15). This passage is significant because (White 1985) phantasia is not confined to the realm of appearance or deception but operates wherever cognition engages with form—whether mathematical, perceptual, or deliberative. Thus phantasia possesses a genuine cognitive function: it is the faculty through which forms are held before the soul in a quasi-perceptual mode, enabling comparison, deliberation, and judgment. Gonzalez  "the phantasma is what others say about us; and that the persuasion operative in the phantasia is enhanced in proportion as the speakers are trustworthy" (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 27). This observation reveals phantasia's deeply rhetorical character: the images through which we grasp ourselves and our situations are socially mediated, shaped by the discourse and testimony of others, and accordingly susceptible to the influence of ethos. We  Aristotle distinguishes between sensory phantasia (aisthētikē), which arises directly from perceptual encounters and is shared with many animals, and deliberative phantasia (bouleutikē), which involves the combining and comparing of images in the service of practical reasoning and is proper to rational beings. The relation of phantasia to belief (doxa) is complex: while phantasia can produce appearances that conflict with what one believes to be the case—as when the sun appears small though one knows it to be large—it is not identical to belief, since belief requires assent and phantasia is in some measure voluntary, subject to the will's capacity to summon or dismiss images. These internal distinctions within the faculty of phantasia—its grades, its relation to doxa, and its voluntariness—will receive fuller systematic treatment in a subsequent chapter; for present purposes, it suffices to mark them as structurally important for understanding how phantasia collaborates with mood. Mood, as we have seen, is ontologically irreducible; it cannot be decomposed into component parts without losing its essential character as a disclosive attunement. 5) helps us see, by contrast, what mood is not: mood is not a structural property of Dasein's component parts but an emergent existential whole that conditions those parts. Phantasia, for its part, is kinetic—a motion that generates images, holds them before the soul, and projects them into anticipated futures. The antichesis between mood and phantasia is accordingly the counter-positioning of atmosphere and image, of the diffuse ground and the determinate figure, through which Dasein's world acquires its characteristic texture. When I walk through the national park at dusk and am seized by a mood of melancholic wonder, the phantasmatic images that arise—the fading light on the ridgeline, the remembered voice of a companion, the anticipated darkness—are not merely decorations added to a neutral perceptual field. They are the very medium through which the mood realizes itself as a disclosure of particular things; and conversely, the mood is the atmospheric ground without which those images would lack their specific affective charge. Hence antichesis names not a static opposition but a dynamic reciprocity: mood attunes phantasia, and phantasia articulates mood. The ontological completion that this reciprocity achieves is the full disclosure of a world in which entities show up as mattering in determinate ways. Heidegger's insistence that Dasein's Being is care (Sorge)—the being-ahead-of-itself-already-in-a-world-alongside-entities—finds its concrete phenomenological content in this interplay. The "turbulence of the 'they's' inauthenticity" into which Dasein is "sucked" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 223) is itself a mood-phantasia complex: the they-self is sustained by shared images, habitual fantasies, and ambient attunements that together compose the familiar, unreflective texture of everyday existence. Conversely, authentic disclosure—the moment in which Dasein takes over its own thrownness—involves a transformation of both mood and phantasia, a shift in attunement that reconfigures the images through which the world appears. Bowin's observation that Aristotle "does not distinguish between remembering time and perceiving time as it is passing" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 9) is pertinent here, for (White 1985) the temporal dimension of phantasia—its capacity to hold the past and project the future within the present perception—is itself inseparable from the kind of attunement in which Dasein finds itself. Time, mood, and image are woven into a single disclosive fabric; and antichesis is the name for the tension that keeps that fabric taut. ## VI. Conclusion: Phantasia as the Temporal Medium of Affective World-Disclosure

The foregoing analysis has sought (Aristotle 2014) phantasia, understood in its full Aristotelian scope, functions as the temporal medium through which affective world-disclosure is accomplished. Mood opens the clearing; phantasia populates it with determinate images, scenes, and projections; and their dynamic interplay—what we have called antichesis—constitutes the ontological completion of Dasein's world. Rickert's concept of ambience has shown that this interplay is not confined within the boundaries of a solitary subject but extends into the material, atmospheric, and rhetorical surround—an insight that transforms our understanding of how persuasion operates and how dwelling is sustained. As Rickert reminds us, the world discloses itself in its own ways, and this disclosure is part of the world's "worlding" (Rickert, *Towards Ecosophy in a Participating World*, p. 12). Phantasia, on this account, is not a private mental theater but an ecologically embedded, rhetorically conditioned, and temporally extended capacity through which the soul and the world meet in the medium of the image. We have noted that the internal distinctions within phantasia—its sensory and deliberative grades, its complex relation to belief (doxa), and the degree to which it is voluntary—require more extensive treatment than the present chapter has been able to provide. These distinctions will be taken up systematically in the chapter that follows, where the focus will shift from the ontological conditions of phantasia's operation to its specific cognitive mechanics and its role in practical reasoning. For now, it is sufficient to have established the framework: phantasia is not opposed to mood but is its kinetic complement, and together they generate the "soap bubble" of significance through which Dasein encounters, inhabits, and is claimed by its world.
