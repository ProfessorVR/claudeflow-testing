# A₀: Motion and Time as First Actuality

## 1. A₀ as First Actuality

The architecture of the actualization chain begins not with perception but with the ontological conditions that make perception possible. A₀ designates the first actuality of the chain: motion and time as the co-implicated structures whose prior actuality is the necessary condition for everything that follows. Before the sensible object can exercise its sensible power, before the sense-organ can hold its first actuality as capacity, before the rational soul can actualize the countability through which temporal succession becomes articulated, motion and time must already be in act. The chain, in other words, cannot begin with a static potentiality to be awakened from without; it must begin with an actuality that is itself a process, one whose ongoingness grounds everything downstream.

This architectural claim rests on Aristotle's doctrine of the priority of actuality over potentiality, developed most fully at *Metaphysics* IX.8. Actuality, Aristotle argues, is prior to potentiality in three senses: in account (λόγῳ), in time (χρόνῳ), and in substance (οὐσίᾳ). The potential is intelligible only through the actuality it is potential for (1049b12-17); any individual potentiality presupposes a prior actuality capable of bringing it into play (1049b17-1050a3); the end (τέλος) is more fully real than the process directed toward it (1050a4-16). Each mode of priority has architectural consequences. Motion and time are prior in all three senses to the actualities A₁ will specify, and this threefold priority is what licenses the chain's iterated structure: each completed actuality serves as the unmoved originator of the motion that produces the next, and the first such originator — the ground upon which the whole chain depends — is A₀.

A further architectural principle, articulated at *Physics* V.5, 229b25, clarifies how the chain's stages are named: each motion takes its name from its terminus, not from its starting point. The motion from potentiality to the completed house is called building; the motion from the sensible object's exercise to the completed perceptual act is called *aisthēsis*; the motion from the stabilized *phantasma* to the committed *doxa* is called opining. The chain, accordingly, is a sequence of motions each named by its target actuality, and each target actuality in turn becomes the unmoved originator that initiates the next motion. A₀ is the first link in this sequence. It is the actuality from which the first named motion (perceptual motion at M₁→A₂) inherits its intelligibility, and it is the ground without which none of the subsequent stages could be more than formal abstractions. Indeed, A₀ does not merely precede A₁ temporally; it is structurally prior in the stronger sense Aristotle reserves for actuality — it is what A₁'s potentialities are potentialities *for*.

The soul's inherent rhetorical nature — its capacity for being-affected (πάσχειν) that generates the progressively complex kinetic and affective actualizations the chain will trace — is what motivates placing this ontological analysis at the front of the chain rather than treating it as mere background. The full defense of that rhetorical framing is developed in [Chapter X: The Motion-Inducing Soul — Rhetoric, Attunement, and Actualization (Heidegger, Rickert, Burke)].

## 2. Kinesis as Energeia Ateles

Aristotle's definition of motion at *Physics* III.1, 201a10-14 is compact and notoriously difficult: motion is "the fulfilment of what is potentially, as such, ... e.g. the fulfilment of what is alterable, as alterable, is alteration; of what is increasable and its opposite, decreasable, increase and decrease; of what can come to be and pass away, coming to be and passing away; of what can be carried along, locomotion." The difficulty lies not in the examples but in the governing formula: motion is the actuality of the potential *qua* potential. Neither pure potentiality nor completed actuality, neither what is not yet begun nor what is already finished — motion occupies an intermediate ontological register where the potential is being actualized *as* potential, where the capacity is held in the state of its exercise without being exhausted.

The decisive warrant for reading motion as this kind of intermediate register — what Aristotle elsewhere calls *energeia ateles*, "incomplete actuality" — is the contrast he draws at *Metaphysics* IX.6, 1048b18-34 between *kinesis* and *energeia* proper. Aristotle frames the distinction as a tense test. "At the same time we are seeing and have seen, are understanding and have understood, are thinking and have thought; but it is not true that at the same time we are learning and have learnt, or are being cured and have been cured." Seeing, thinking, and living well are *energeiai* because they contain their end within themselves; the moment one has seen, one is seeing, and the activity does not cease on reaching its terminus. Learning, building, walking are *kineseis* because their terminus lies beyond the activity itself; the moment the house exists, the building has ceased. "Of these processes, then, we must call the one set movements, and the other actualities. For every movement is incomplete — making thin, learning, walking, building; these are movements, and incomplete movements" (*Metaphysics* IX.6, 1048b28-30).

Why must motion occupy this intermediate register? The answer follows from the structure of actuality and potentiality themselves. If motion were the actuality of what is already actual, motion would have already ceased — once the house stands, no more building. If motion were merely the potential without actualization, nothing would be happening — the bronze sitting in the workshop is potentially a statue but is not thereby in motion. Motion, accordingly, must be a mode of being that is neither completed actuality nor inert potentiality, but the ongoing exercise of the potential as potential. Aristotle's buildable example makes the structural point decisively: "the actuality of the buildable *as* buildable is the process of building. For the actuality must be either this or the house. But when there is a house, the buildable is no longer there. On the other hand, it *is* the buildable which is *being* built. Necessarily, then, the actuality is the process of building" (*Physics* III.1, 201b7-14). The bronze example reinforces the point: the motion of bronze becoming a statue is not the fulfilment of bronze *as bronze* but the fulfilment of bronze *as potentially a statue* (*Physics* III.1, 201a28-31). The *qua* structure governs everything. What is actualized in motion is actualized *as* potential, not as achieved completion; motion is unfolding, not destination.

Grasping this requires holding together two Greek terms that Aristotle treats as coordinate but distinct: *dunamis* and *energeia*. *Dunamis* names the capacity — the state of being-able that a thing possesses but is not currently exercising. *Energeia* names the being-at-work — the actualization of a capacity in its proper exercise. The two are mutually definitional: one cannot specify what a capacity is except by reference to the actuality it is a capacity for, and one cannot specify what an actuality is except by reference to the capacity it fulfils. The capacity to build is defined by the act of building; the act of building is defined by its relation to the buildable. Motion, within this coordination, is the peculiar mode of being in which *dunamis* is not replaced by *energeia* but is enacted *as dunamis*, held in the very activity of its unfolding. The Greek *ateles*, "incomplete" or "lacking a *telos*," names not a defect in motion's being but a structural feature of it. Motion's incompleteness is precisely what makes it motion rather than finished being; the not-yet-having-arrived is constitutive.

This structural feature is what licenses A₀'s architectural role. An actuality that is complete and self-contained — an *energeia* in the strict sense — does not ground a chain of further actualizations; it terminates in itself. An incomplete actuality that is ongoing, by contrast, is exactly the kind of being that can serve as the unmoved originator of subsequent motion. Motion's *ateles* character is not a deficiency but the structural condition of its grounding role. Indeed, when Aristotle organizes the types of motion — alteration, increase, decrease, coming-to-be, passing-away, locomotion — he does so around the different ways in which a potentiality can be *qua* potentiality in the course of fulfillment. Each type presupposes a subject capable of a determinate kind of change, and each is defined by the kind of capacity being actualized, not by the terminus toward which it moves. *Kinesis* is an ontological category, not a defective approximation to something else.

## 3. Priority of Actuality and the Chain Structure

The chain structure of A₀ → A₁ → A₂ → A₃ → A₄ inherits its logic from the priority of actuality over potentiality. This doctrine, articulated most fully at *Metaphysics* IX.8, 1050a4-16, runs as follows: "everything that comes to be moves towards a principle, i.e. an end. For that for the sake of which a thing is, is its principle, and the becoming is for the sake of the end; and the actuality is the end, and it is for the sake of this that the potentiality is acquired." The end (τέλος) is not merely the terminus of a process but its *principle* (ἀρχή): the completed form furnishes the reason why the process occurs at all and the measure by which its stages are ordered. Aristotle is explicit: "the actuality is the end, and it is for the sake of this that the potentiality is acquired. For animals do not see in order that they may have sight, but they have sight that they may see" (*Metaphysics* IX.8, 1050a10-12). Sight is not a capacity that becomes actualized as seeing; seeing is the actuality for the sake of which sight exists as capacity at all.

Kenneth Burke's reading of this doctrine illuminates its architectural implications. Burke observes that Aristotle's entelechy introduces "another kind of priority, namely the 'principle' involved in a given form" — a priority that runs opposite to temporal sequence. A stage that follows another in time may be prior in form and substantiality: "man is 'prior' to boy because man has already attained its complete form whereas boy has not" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 261). The distinction Burke draws — between temporal priority and priority in form and substantiality — is crucial for the chain structure. Each actuality in the chain stands in both relations to its neighbors. A₀ is temporally prior to A₁ (motion and time must be in act before sensible objects and sense-organs can actually exist), but A₁ is formally prior to the specific perceptual motions it will ground, just as the completed form of man is formally prior to the boy who is becoming a man. The chain is, accordingly, not a linear sequence of self-sufficient stages but a nested architecture in which each actuality licenses and is licensed by others.

Aristotle's illustration at *Metaphysics* IX.9, 1051a29-33 sharpens the point. "The potentially existing constructions are discovered by being brought to actuality; the reason is that the geometer's thinking is an actuality; so that the potency proceeds from an actuality; and therefore it is by making constructions that people come to know them." The geometer who actively proves a theorem discovers potentialities that were already latent in the figure but could not be seen until actualization made them manifest. Potentiality, on this reading, is not discovered by introspection but by enactment; the actual reveals what the potential is. For the chain, this means that each stage's potentialities are not abstract capacities waiting to be filled in but structural possibilities that become intelligible only when the prior actuality has already licensed them. A₀'s actuality is what lets A₁'s potentialities be what they are.

Aristotle's etymological gloss at *Metaphysics* IX.8, 1050a21-23 provides the final architectural piece: "For the action (ἔργον) is the end, and the actuality (ἐνέργεια) is the action. Therefore even the word 'actuality' is derived from 'action,' and points to the fulfilment." *Energeia* is "being-at-work," and the term is extended at *entelecheia* — the having-one's-end-within-oneself — to the completed actuality that holds its *telos* internally. This etymological coordination is decisive for understanding A₀. Motion, as *energeia ateles*, is *energeia* in the primary sense — it is being-at-work — but its *telos* lies beyond itself. Completed actuality, as *entelecheia*, is *energeia* whose *telos* is internal. The chain runs from the one to the other: from actuality-whose-end-lies-beyond (motion) through a succession of actualities-whose-ends-are-internal (A₁, A₂, and so on). At each stage, a motion whose end lies beyond itself produces an actuality whose end is internal; that actuality then functions as the principle for the next motion.

The tension between the priority of actuality and the *ateles* character of motion thus resolves into coordination rather than contradiction. The *telos* gives motion its intelligibility — we know what building is because we know what a house is, and the house is formally prior to the building. But the *ateles* character of motion gives it its distinct mode of being — motion is not a deficient approach to completion but a genuine mode of being-at-work whose very incompleteness is what allows it to ground further actualizations. Motion is, in Burke's terms, an actuality that precedes another actuality in time: the first link in a chain whose subsequent links depend upon it.

## 4. The Relational Structure of Kinesis

A further feature of A₀ must be established before we can trace the chain forward: *kinesis* is irreducibly relational. Motion is not a property inhering in a single substance; it is a joint actualization of two capacities, the capacity to act and the capacity to be acted upon. Aristotle develops this at *Physics* III.2, 202a13-20. "Motion is in the movable. It is the fulfilment of this potentiality by the action of that which has the power of causing motion; and the actuality of that which has the power of causing motion is not other than the actuality of the movable; for it must be the fulfilment of *both*. A thing is capable of causing motion because it *can* do this, it is a mover because it actually *does* it. But it is on the movable that it is capable of acting. Hence there is a single actuality of both alike." The mover and the moved share a single *energeia*, and this shared *energeia* *is* the motion.

The same section specifies the dyadic structure in categorial terms. Motion involves both a *poiētikon* (what can act) and a *pathētikon* (what can be acted upon); the actuality of the agent is *poiesis* and the actuality of the patient is *pathesis*. "It is necessary that there should be an actuality of the agent and of the patient. The one is agency and the other patiency; and the outcome and end of the one is an action, that of the other a passion" (*Physics* III.3, 202a21-24). The motion is a single event, yet it has two formal aspects under which it can be described — agency and patiency, *poiesis* and *pathesis* — and these aspects are mutually implicated without being identical. Aristotle clarifies with the Thebes-Athens analogy: "Thebes and Athens are different cities, yet the road from Thebes to Athens and the road from Athens to Thebes are the same" (*Physics* III.3, 202b13-14). One road, two descriptions. One motion, two formal aspects. The distinction is in account, not in substrate.

This relational structure supplies A₀'s dyadic architecture to the chain. A₀ establishes that motion is always *pros ti* — always a being-in-relation — and A₁ inherits this structure: the sensible object's active potency (*poiesis*) meets the sense-organ's receptive potency (*pathesis*) in a single perceptual actualization. The perceptual event at A₂ is not two events conjoined but one event described under two *logoi*, as Aristotle will state explicitly at *De Anima* III.2, 425b26-27. The dyadic structure is inherited from A₀ to A₁ to A₂, and it repeats at every subsequent stage of the chain. What the realizable good moves at M₃→M₄ is *orexis* — the moved mover that is at once agent (of the animal's locomotion) and patient (of the apparent good); what *orexis* moves is the animal via its bodily instrument. The same dyadic schema iterates.

A further feature of *kinesis* must be registered for the chain's subsequent stages: motion is the kind of process that can persist even as its originating cause withdraws. The full analysis of this residual structure belongs to the subsequent section on *aisthēsis* (A₁→A₂), where the residual motion in the sense-organs will be developed as the material condition for the *phantasma* at A₃. Here it suffices to note that A₀ supplies the ontological possibility of residual motion as a structural feature of *kinesis* itself. Motion, once initiated, does not require the continuous presence of its cause in order to continue; it has a kind of temporal autonomy, grounded in its *ateles* character, that allows residual traces to persist in the moved thing even after the mover has withdrawn.

## 5. Chronos as the Number of Motion

Aristotle's definition of time at *Physics* IV.11, 219b1-2 is structurally consequential. Time is "the number of motion with respect to before and after" (ἀριθμὸς κινήσεως κατὰ τὸ πρότερον καὶ ὕστερον). Time is not a substance, not a container within which events transpire, not an independent metaphysical entity. Time is an aspect of motion — specifically, the aspect of motion under which it admits of enumeration. Where there is no motion there is no time, and where motion can be numbered there is time as that numbering.

Aristotle adds a distinction that is easy to miss but load-bearing. "Time, then, is a kind of number. Number, we must note, is used in two ways — both of what is counted or countable and also of that with which we count. Time, then, is what is counted, not that with which we count: these are different kinds of things" (*Physics* IV.11, 219b5-10). Time belongs to the first category: it is the counted number of motion, not the counting activity. This distinction will prove decisive when we turn to the soul's role, for it allows Aristotle to locate time in the structure of motion itself (as what *can* be counted) while still requiring a counter (the soul) for time's full actualization as numbered.

The definition is grounded experientially. "We perceive movement and time together," Aristotle observes at *Physics* IV.11, 219a4-8; "even when it is dark and we are not being affected through the body, if any movement takes place in the mind we at once suppose that some time has indeed elapsed; and not only that but also, when some time is thought to have passed, some movement also along with it seems to have taken place." Neither motion nor time is epistemically prior to the other; we apprehend them together. And the reciprocity is not merely epistemic but ontological: "the movement goes with the magnitude, and time, as we maintain, goes with motion" (*Physics* IV.11, 219a10-13). Magnitude is continuous; motion inherits continuity from magnitude; time inherits continuity from motion. The continuity of time is therefore not a feature imposed on an otherwise discrete medium but a property derived from the continuity of spatial magnitude transmitted through the continuity of motion.

The "now" (τὸ νῦν) occupies a crucial position within this structure. It is what makes time available to enumeration at all. "The 'now' determines time, insofar as time involves the before and after" (*Physics* IV.11, 219b12-13). The now is not a part of time but its boundary — just as the point is not a part of the line but its limit. Aristotle develops this analogy carefully at *Physics* IV.11, 220a5: "Time, then, also is both made continuous by the 'now' and divided at it." The now is at once the principle of continuity (it is the moving boundary that links successive durations) and the principle of division (it marks the point at which past ends and future begins). "What is *before* is in time; for we say 'before' and 'after' with reference to the distance from the 'now', and the 'now' is the boundary of the past and the future" (*Physics* IV.14, 223a4-6). Accordingly, the now is always the same in substrate — it is whatever bears the before-and-after relation at any given moment — while differing in being as it traverses successive positions.

Aristotle illuminates the now's structure through an experiential correlate: "the now corresponds to the body that is carried along, as time corresponds to the motion. For it is by means of the body that is carried along that we become aware of the before and after in the motion, and if we regard these as countable we get the 'now'" (*Physics* IV.11, 219b22-25). The body-carried-along analogy captures the now's peculiar mode of being. The moving body is always the same body even as it traverses different positions; the now is always the same now even as it marks different moments. One in substrate, different in being. The body moves; the now marks; time is what we get when we attend to the motion as countable.

## 6. The Soul's Constitutive Role and the Motion-Time Parallel

Here the architectural weight of A₀ becomes most explicit. Aristotle asks at *Physics* IV.14, 223a25-27 whether time could exist if soul did not, and his answer is carefully qualified: "But if nothing but soul, or in soul reason, is qualified to count, it is impossible for there to be time unless there is soul, but only that of which time is an attribute — i.e., if movement can exist without soul." The conditional is precise. Motion, Aristotle allows, may exist without soul; natural bodies move whether or not anyone observes them. But time — which is the *numbered* aspect of motion — seems to require a counter, and where there is no counter there can be no number counted. Thus while motion does not depend on soul, time does: time is real as the countable structure of motion but becomes fully actual as time only in relation to a soul capable of counting.

This chapter adopts a stronger interpretive reading of Aristotle's conditional. On the reading advanced here, time without a counting soul is not merely unnumbered but *ontologically incomplete* — present as countability, structurally real as the before-and-after of motion, yet not fully actualized as time. The interpretive move goes beyond what Aristotle explicitly states. Aristotle's own formulation is epistemically cautious (if-there-is-no-counter-then-no-number); the stronger ontological claim (that time's full actuality requires soul) is the reading this chapter takes up. The reading is defensible because it coheres with Aristotle's broader priority-of-actuality commitments: if time is fully actual only as numbered, then what makes the numbering possible — the soul — is constitutive of time's full actuality, not merely its epistemic availability.

This interpretive reading supports the architectural claim that A₀ is not a neutral substrate but an actuality whose full actualization is achieved only with the arrival of the soul at A₁. The chain, on this reading, is not a sequence of stages running sequentially upon an already-complete foundation; it is an iterated structure in which each stage is what makes the prior stage what it fully is. A₀ is structurally present from the beginning — motion and time are co-eternal, always already in act — but time's full actualization as numbered requires the soul that A₁ introduces. The soul, in turn, exists only because motion and time have already made natural beings possible. The interdependence is reciprocal; the stages are not linearly ordered but nested and co-constitutive.

The decisive architectural payoff lies in the parallel between motion's mode of being and time's mode of being. Motion, as *energeia ateles*, occupies an intermediate ontological register: neither pure potentiality nor complete actuality, but the active exercise of the potential *as* potential. Time, on the interpretive reading just advanced, occupies a structurally analogous register: neither brute succession nor fully articulated numerical order, but the countable aspect of motion that is on its way to being counted. Motion is actual as ongoing unfolding; time is actual as countable structure. Neither is a static, self-contained *energeia*; both are ongoing processes whose very incompleteness is what allows them to ground further actualities.

The parallel licenses everything downstream. Because motion is *energeia ateles*, it can serve as the unmoved originator of the motions that produce completed actualities at A₁, A₂, and beyond — it is ongoing enough to be the source, incomplete enough not to terminate in itself. Because time is the countable structure of motion, it can become fully articulated only as the soul actualizes the counting — it is real enough to condition sequential ordering, open enough to be developed as the numbered continuum in which the subsequent motions unfold. A₀'s mode of being is thus not static grounding but *active grounding*: the ongoing process whose very incompleteness makes further completion possible.

Every A₍ₙ₎ in the chain inherits this intermediate-register character. Each completed actuality at A₁, A₂, A₃ is both the terminus of its antecedent motion and the unmoved originator of its successor — an *entelecheia* in relation to what precedes it, an *arche* in relation to what follows. The chain is iterated because each of its nodes has the same dual structure that A₀ establishes as primitive: completion looking backward, potentiality looking forward, actuality all the way down. The fundamental motion of the chain — what Aristotle might call its καθόλου structure — is not sequential passage from stage to stage but the repeated exercise of a single ontological pattern: *energeia ateles* generating *entelecheia* generating the conditions for a new *energeia ateles*. A₀ is where this pattern first emerges and from which the chain inherits its primitive structure.

## 7. Synthesis: A₀ as Ground of A₁

A₀, then, is what lets A₁ be what it is. Motion being actual is what allows sensible objects to actually exercise their sensible powers — the color visible, the sound audible, the warm warming. Motion being actual is what allows the medium to transmit the sensible form from object to organ. Time being actual is what allows the temporal continuum within which the transmission unfolds; and time becoming *fully* actual with the arrival of the rational soul is what allows the sequential articulation of perceptual, imaginative, and cognitive acts that the subsequent chain stages will trace. A₁ names the world in which all three conditions obtain: rational souls exist, sensible objects actually exercise their sensible powers, sense-organs hold their first actuality as capacities to receive form without matter. Each of these actualities depends on A₀, and each will itself be the unmoved originator of further motions as the chain proceeds.

The generalization that licenses this iterated structure is that each transition from one actuality to the next is itself a motion — a further actualization of a capacity that was latent in the preceding stage. A₀ → A₁ is the actualization of the world's natural beings (made possible by motion and time); A₁ → A₂ is the actualization of perception (made possible by the sensible object and sense-organ); A₂ → A₃ is the actualization of the *phantasma* (made possible by the residual trace of perception); A₃ → A₄ is the actualization of appetitive action (made possible by the completed cognitive act together with the realizable good it discloses). Each transition is a motion in the *energeia ateles* sense; each terminus is a completed actuality that becomes the unmoved originator of the next. The chain is not a collection of discrete events but a single iterated structure whose primitive unit — actuality grounding the motion that produces the next actuality — is specified at A₀.

One final bridge connects A₀'s ontological horizon to the physical correlate that the subsequent section will develop. The medium's sequential affection in transmitting sensible form from object to organ (*Sense and Sensibilia* 6, 447a3-7, with light as the exception at 446b8-9) is the physical counterpart of the ontological structure A₀ establishes. Where A₀ supplies the general conditions — motion and time as co-actual — the medium's sequential affection supplies the specific physical mechanism through which one particular kind of motion, the perceptual kind, unfolds in time. The full analysis of the medium belongs to the next section. Here it suffices to note that A₀'s ontological horizon is not an abstract framework but the very condition under which the sensible world's physical transmission of form becomes possible. The reader is deposited at the threshold of A₁.[^1]

[^1]: The Heideggerian analysis of temporality — particularly Heidegger's reading of Greek Being as presence (*Anwesenheit*) in *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* and his account of Dasein's ecstatic temporality in *Being and Time* §78 — illuminates what this section establishes. Heidegger argues that the Aristotelian definition of time captures what he calls *Innerzeitigkeit*, "innertimeness" or time-as-publicly-encountered, while the more primordial structures of temporality and historicality that ground this derivative time-conception remain concealed. A full integration of this Heideggerian reappropriation with the Aristotelian architecture developed here lies beyond the scope of the present chapter and is taken up in [Chapter X: The Motion-Inducing Soul — Rhetoric, Attunement, and Actualization (Heidegger, Rickert, Burke)].

## Development Note

Three items flag future development. First, the motion-time parallel advanced in §6 depends on an interpretive reading of *Physics* IV.14, 223a25-27 that treats time-without-soul as ontologically incomplete rather than merely unnumbered. The reading is defensible given Aristotle's broader priority-of-actuality commitments, but the textual warrant is cautious-conditional, and alternative readings that treat time as fully actual independently of soul merit engagement in a more extended treatment. Ursula Coope's work on Aristotelian time and Sarah Broadie's commentary on *Physics* IV are the principal interlocutors for this debate.

Second, the relationship between time as a common sensible (*De Anima* III.1, 425a16), the discriminating faculty's unity across the now (*De Anima* III.2, 426b17-427a1), and the role of *phantasia* in producing continuity across discrete perceptual instants (developed in subsequent sections) bears on how A₀'s time becomes A₁'s cognitively articulated temporal manifold. The *On Memory* passages at 449b22-25 and 450a11-13 on memory requiring the recognition of past-*qua*-past, together with *De Anima* III.10, 433b5-10 on the sense of time co-implicated with deliberative *phantasia*, specify the psychological apparatus through which the cognitive articulation of time occurs. This chapter defers analysis of that apparatus to the sections on *aisthēsis*, *phantasia*, and the orientational modes at M₂→M₃.

Third, the etymological-architectural claim advanced via Burke's reading of entelechy — the distinction between temporal priority and priority in form and substantiality — suggests a deeper affinity between Aristotle's entelechial structure and the rhetorical theory of motivated action that subsequent chapters will develop. The full articulation of this affinity — how the completed actuality at each chain stage functions as both *telos* (end) and *arche* (principle) in the sense of rhetorical motivation — lies beyond the scope of the present section and is developed in the chapter indicated in the closing footnote.
