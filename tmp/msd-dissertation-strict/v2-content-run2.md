# Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Temporal Consciousness: Toward a Rhetorical Ontology

## 1. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding

To articulate Aristotle's understanding of motion, time, and the perceptual faculties that bind them together is to enter a domain of inquiry in which ontology, rhetoric, and phenomenology converge in ways that remain profoundly generative for contemporary thought. Aristotle did not treat motion and time as mere physical phenomena awaiting quantitative description; rather, he understood them as disclosive structures through which beings reveal themselves in the fullness of their being. As the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* emphasize, kinesis for Heidegger is not simply a category of natural philosophy but rather a way that Dasein exists in the world, such that motion constitutes the very manner in which entities come to presence and disclose their character (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Thus, the Aristotelian treatment of motion already carries within it an implicit rhetorical dimension, insofar as motion is bound up with how things appear, how they are encountered, and how they affect those who perceive them. Specifically, Rickert's account of kairos helps us appreciate that Aristotle's philosophical vocabulary was never divorced from the spatial and temporal conditions of human engagement with the world. Rickert notes that kairos originally possessed a spatial dimension, though this early usage was gradually lost in favor of meanings such as opportunity and decorum, and most of these senses are keyed to a subject who functions as an active, willing, conscious agent (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 108–109). This observation is significant because it reveals how Aristotle's conceptual apparatus was always already attuned to the situational, environmental, and embodied character of human experience. Similarly, Aristotle himself recognized that causes must be understood in their full multiplicity; in the *Metaphysics*, he insists that when causes are being looked for, either all four must be sought or they must be sought in one of these four ways (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 14–15). Accordingly, the understanding we seek to articulate must honor the complexity inherent in Aristotle's own approach—an approach that refuses to separate the physical from the perceptual, or the ontological from the rhetorical. ## 2. Aristotle on Motion

Aristotle's account of motion, or kinesis, constitutes one of the most consequential contributions to Western metaphysics, precisely because it refuses to treat motion as an accidental or secondary feature of reality. For Aristotle, motion is not something that happens to beings from the outside; it is rather a fundamental expression of what it means for a being to exist in a state of potentiality moving toward actuality. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle outlines the basic modes of being through which motion must be understood, specifying that being exists both as potentiality (dynamei) and as actuality (entelecheia), and that these modes implicate the categories and the relational structures of beings in the world (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). Indeed, Aristotle is emphatic that motion is not something separate from things themselves; as the *Physics* discussion relayed in Heidegger's lectures makes clear, kinesis is not para ta pragmata—not apart from things—but is rather the very how of the being of beings (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263). This formulation is essential because it grounds motion in ontology rather than in mere mechanics; motion becomes the way in which potentiality is realized and beings achieve the fullness of their form. Subsequently, the rhetorical implications of this understanding become visible when we consider how Heidegger takes up Aristotle's notion of pathe in the *Rhetoric*. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* observe that passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions, a capacity for altering, and that Heidegger reads (Aristotle 2014) passions alter judgments as the point at which passions intrude upon logos—articulated judgments that are frequently mistranslated by historians of philosophy simply as reason (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Hence, motion for Aristotle is not confined to the physical realm but extends into the domain of affect, persuasion, and discursive engagement. Aristotle's *Rhetoric*  breadth, offering examples in which narrative motion—the unfolding of a story's events—serves as a mode of persuasive action; the mention of actual past facts and the invention of facts by the speaker both depend on the temporal and kinetic structure of rhetorical address (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 71). Thus, Aristotle's concept of motion bridges the gap between natural philosophy and the art of persuasion in ways that subsequent thinkers have only begun to appreciate fully. ## 3. Aristotle on Time

Aristotle's treatment of time is inseparable from his account of motion, yet time possesses its own distinctive character as a phenomenon that requires perceptual and cognitive engagement for its apprehension. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle establishes that being has two forms—it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter—and this dual character of being provides the ontological ground upon which temporal experience unfolds (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). Time, for Aristotle, is not an independent substance or an empty container within which events simply occur; rather, it is the number of motion with respect to the before and after, and as such it is always already bound to the perceiving soul that counts and discriminates temporal intervals. John Bowin's careful analysis illuminates this point with precision: Aristotle says that we measure time by counting units of time, such as days, which are determinate because they are marked out by motions that are determinate in time, and that counting is the type of measurement that is most exact because the measure it involves is absolutely indivisible rather than indivisible merely in relation to perception (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). This passage reveals that the measurement of time is not a purely abstract operation but one that is grounded in determinate motions—specifically celestial motions—that provide the framework within which temporal units become intelligible. Accordingly, we must ask: what role does perception play in the constitution of temporal awareness? Aristotle's answer, as Bowin reconstructs it, involves a complex interplay between sensation, memory, and the cognitive faculties that unify discrete moments into a continuous temporal flow. The notion of the specious present—the short duration of which we are immediately and incessantly sensible—gestures toward this dimension of temporal experience, though this formulation owes more to William James than to Aristotle directly (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 17–19). Nevertheless, it captures something essential about the Aristotelian position, namely that time is not merely objective but is also a phenomenon of lived awareness. Similarly, the *Rhetoric* demonstrates that temporal awareness is integral to persuasive discourse, since the rhetor must navigate the audience's sense of past, present, and future in order to construct effective arguments (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 71). Thus, time for Aristotle is simultaneously cosmological and phenomenological, objective and subjective, measured and lived. ## 4. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The relation between motion and time in Aristotle's thought is one of mutual implication: neither can be understood in isolation from the other, and both require the activity of a perceiving soul for their determination. As Bowin notes, the measurement of time through the counting of determinate temporal units presupposes that there are motions regular and stable enough to serve as standards—celestial motions being paradigmatic—and that there is a cognitive agent capable of recognizing and enumerating these regularities (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). Hence, the relation between motion and time is not merely one of correlation but one of ontological co-dependence; time is disclosed through motion, and motion is rendered intelligible through the temporal framework that structures its unfolding. This co-dependence is precisely what distinguishes Aristotle's account from later mechanistic treatments in which time is an independent parameter and motion a mere function of spatial displacement over temporal intervals. Specifically, the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* help us appreciate the deeper ontological stakes of this relation. When Heidegger insists that kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein exists, he is drawing out an implication already present in Aristotle's own texts: that motion is not merely a physical occurrence but a mode of being that shapes the temporal horizon within which entities appear (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Heidegger's interpretive framework reveals that the Aristotelian relation between motion and time is ultimately a relation between modes of disclosure and the temporality that conditions all disclosure. Indeed, Aristotle himself was attuned to the way in which different forms of being—potential and actual—implicate different temporal structures; the movement from potentiality to actuality is inherently a temporal process, and it is through this process that beings achieve their definitive form (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). Furthermore, Gibson's ecological framework, though developed in a different context, provides a complementary insight: when we consider that an object can be resting on a surface of support without being attached to it, and that these distinctions are essential for understanding motions as ecological events, we see that the perception of motion and time is always environmentally situated (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 114–116). Thus, the relation between motion and time is not abstract but deeply embedded in the perceptual and ecological conditions of embodied existence. ## 5. Burke's rhetorical philosophy offers a productive lens through which to examine this connection, particularly in his treatment of how logical and temporal priority become entangled in discourse. Burke discusses what he calls the temporizing of essence, whereby the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). This insight is directly relevant to our understanding of aisthesis and temporal awareness, for (White 1985) the perceptual encounter with a thing is never simply a reception of present data but always involves an implicit narrative structure—a movement from origin to present state—that is itself temporal in character. Accordingly, sensory perception is already a form of temporal engagement with the world, one that anticipates the more complex operations of memory and phantasia. Furthermore, Burke's analysis of identification illuminates how aisthesis functions within rhetorical situations to generate shared temporal experience. (Hawhee 2011) the expressing of a proposition in one or another rhetorical form involves identification, first by inducing the auditor to participate in the form as a universal locus of appeal, and then by including a partisan statement within this same pale of assent (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 73–74). The process of identification that Burke describes is fundamentally temporal: it requires the auditor to track the unfolding of a rhetorical form over time, to anticipate its completion, and to experience the satisfaction or disruption of that expectation. Aristotle's own recognition that perception involves not only the reception of sensory qualities but also the discrimination of magnitude and relational structure points in this same direction, suggesting that aisthesis is never merely passive but is always an active, temporally structured engagement with the perceptual field. Thus, temporal awareness emerges not as a secondary cognitive achievement but as an intrinsic feature of sensory perception itself. ## 6. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

Phantasia occupies a distinctive position in Aristotle's psychology of perception, serving as the faculty that preserves, transforms, and unifies the deliverances of sense perception into coherent images that sustain temporal continuity. As O'Gorman explains in his analysis of Aristotle's treatment of phantasia in the *Rhetoric*, phantasia is intimately connected to the sense of sight, preserving visual perceptions in a sort of after-image, and it is itself a sort of psychic sight, the source of mental images or visual impressions (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). This characterization is significant because it reveals that phantasia is not simply a reproductive faculty that mechanically copies sensory data; rather, it is a generative capacity that produces images that may diverge from their sensory origins, thereby enabling the soul to engage with what is absent, past, or merely possible. Indeed, phantasia thus functions as the bridge between present perception and temporal awareness, unifying discrete sensory moments into a continuous experiential flow. The implications of this account for rhetoric are considerable. Burke's notion of identification, when read alongside Aristotle's phantasia, suggests that rhetorical persuasion depends upon the audience's capacity to generate and sustain mental images that extend beyond the immediate moment of perception. Burke notes that the expression of a proposition in rhetorical form induces the auditor to participate in the form itself, and this participation is mediated by the phantastic capacity to project images, anticipate outcomes, and connect present speech to past experience and future expectation (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 73–74). Subsequently, Bowin's analysis adds further depth by observing that phantasia is implicated in special, common, and incidental perception alike, which means that the temporal unification achieved by phantasia extends across all modalities of perceptual engagement (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 17–19). Aristotle's insight, then, is that temporal experience is not given to us whole but is actively constructed through the phantastic power of the soul—a power that is simultaneously perceptual, cognitive, and, as the rhetorical tradition makes clear, deeply persuasive. ## 7. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle constitutes one of the most consequential philosophical retrieval projects of the twentieth century, and it is through this interpretation that the full rhetorical and ontological significance of Aristotle's accounts of motion and time comes into view. Heidegger reads Aristotle not as a dry systematizer of categories but as a thinker profoundly attentive to the ways in which beings disclose themselves in the lived world of human concern. In the *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, Heidegger takes up Aristotle's account of how early thinkers such as Democritus were led to philosophical insight not by abstract reasoning alone but by being, as Aristotle puts it, borne by the matter itself, leading to the discovery that beings must be apprehended not merely with regard to material composition but also with regard to their form or shape (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 176–177). This formulation captures something essential about Heidegger's own approach: the conviction that philosophy begins not with method but with the compulsion of things themselves. Specifically, Heidegger's reading of kinesis transforms Aristotle's natural philosophy into an existential analytic. As the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* observe, kinesis for Heidegger is a fundamental way that Dasein exists (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). This radical reinterpretation means that motion is no longer confined to the domain of physics but becomes the very structure of human existence—the way in which Dasein moves toward its possibilities, is affected by its world, and comports itself toward other beings. Hence, Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is not incidental to his philosophical project but central to it, because the *Rhetoric* provides the most sustained ancient analysis of how human beings are moved by passions, persuaded by arguments, and situated within particular temporal and spatial contexts. Aristotle's observation that contradictories cannot simultaneously hold of the same subject without dissolving the possibility of determinate discourse—as when (Hawhee 2011) those who speak of the indeterminate while fancying themselves to be speaking of being are in fact speaking about non-being (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 42–43)—resonates with Heidegger's own insistence that being must be understood in its determinacy, as presence, rather than as mere abstraction. Similarly, Rickert's ambient understanding of rhetoric extends this Heideggerian trajectory by emphasizing the environmental and embodied conditions of rhetorical engagement, insisting that we understand concepts like kairos not merely as subjective opportunities but as ambient attunements to the world (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 108–109). Thus, Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle opens a pathway from ancient ontology to contemporary rhetorical theory, revealing that the questions of motion, time, and perception that animated Aristotle's thought remain indispensable for understanding the situatedness and temporality of human discourse. ---
