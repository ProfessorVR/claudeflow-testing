# Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Temporal Awareness: Toward a Heideggerian Interpretation

## 1. Articulate Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Temporal Awareness

Aristotle's philosophical project, particularly as it unfolds across the *Physics*, the *Metaphysics*, and *De Anima*, constitutes one of the most sustained and internally coherent attempts in Western thought to articulate the nature of motion, time, and the perceptual awareness through which temporal experience becomes intelligible to human beings. Indeed, the significance of Aristotle's account lies not merely in its systematic character but in the manner by which it binds together ontological categories—potentiality and actuality, substance and change—with the phenomenological dimensions of perception and cognition. As Heidegger recognized, and as subsequent commentators have elaborated, Aristotle's treatment of motion, or kinesis, is not simply a physics of displacement in space; rather, kinesis names a fundamental mode of being that encompasses alteration, growth, and every transition from potentiality to actuality. Specifically, Heidegger's reading of Aristotle reframes kinesis as deeply intertwined with the very character of Dasein's existence, such that, as the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* observe, "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Thus, the Aristotelian inquiry into motion is simultaneously an inquiry into the being of beings in their most concrete manifestation. We must accordingly recognize that Aristotle's understanding of time is inseparable from his account of motion. Time, for Aristotle, is the number of motion with respect to the before and after; it is not an independent container within which events occur but is rather constituted by the perception and counting of change. The temporal dimension is thus phenomenologically grounded in aisthēsis, the perceptual awareness that registers the succession  moving things. Similarly, phantasia—the faculty of imagination or appearance—plays a crucial mediating role, preserving and unifying perceptual impressions so that the soul can cognize temporal continuity and duration. Thomas Rickert reminds us that concepts such as kairos, which originally carried a spatial dimension, gradually shifted toward temporal meanings like "opportunity," and that such shifts are "keyed to a subject who is an active, willing, conscious agent" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 108–109). This observation underscores the broader Aristotelian insight that temporal awareness is not passive reception but active comportment toward the world. Aristotle  being has multiple forms, existing "not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157), and it is precisely this ontological duality that animates the relation between motion and time, between the potential and the actual as they unfold in temporal experience. 

## 2. Aristotle defines motion as the actualization of what exists in potency insofar as it is in potency, a definition that resists reduction to mere spatial displacement and instead encompasses qualitative alteration, quantitative growth and diminution, and substantial generation and corruption. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle emphasizes that the principles of nature must be sought not merely in material substrata but in the interplay of form and matter, potentiality and actuality; (Hawhee 2011), being "exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). Specifically, the category of motion is what enables Aristotle (Aristotle 2014) beings change while nonetheless retaining an underlying identity, and it is for this reason that kinesis is not simply one topic among many in Aristotle's philosophy but rather the very fulcrum upon which his ontology turns. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle deepens and radicalizes this account by interpreting kinesis not as a category of physical science alone but as a mode of being. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* note that, in Heidegger's interpretation, "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Hence, motion is not something that merely happens to entities in the world; it is constitutive of the manner in which Dasein exists, comports itself, and discloses the world. This reinterpretation transforms the Aristotelian understanding of motion from a physics of change into a phenomenology of existence, wherein the passions themselves—pathos, or what Heidegger would render as Befindlichkeit—are recognized as forms of motion. Indeed, as Heidegger observes in his lectures on Aristotle, "Passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). This characterization reveals that motion is not limited to the external displacement of bodies; it pervades the affective and cognitive life of the soul, shaping judgment and perception from within. Furthermore, Aristotle's account of motion is intimately linked to his teleological framework, in which every natural being possesses an internal principle of motion directed toward its proper end. Aristotle discusses in the *Metaphysics* how earlier thinkers, such as those who posited friendship or reason as causes, did not  movements started from such principles, noting that "when the causes are being looked for, either all four must be sought thus or they must be sought in one of these four ways" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 14–15). Accordingly, the four causes—material, formal, efficient, and final—are all implicated in the full analysis of motion, and it is only by attending to their interconnection that the nature of kinesis can be properly grasped. 

## 3. Aristotle on Time

Aristotle's treatment of time in the *Physics* establishes the  time is the number of motion with respect to the before and after, a formulation that binds temporal succession inextricably to the phenomenon of change. Time, on this account, is not an independently subsisting entity but rather a measure, a way of counting and ordering the phases of kinesis. John Bowin clarifies this point by observing that, for Aristotle, "we measure time by counting units of time (e.g., days) which are determinate because they are marked out by motions that are determinate in time (e.g., celestial motions)" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). Thus, the determinacy of temporal units is parasitic upon the regularity of certain motions, specifically the celestial revolutions that provide the most exact and uniform standard of measurement. Indeed, Bowin  counting constitutes the most exact form of measurement "because the measure that it involves is absolutely indivisible, rather than indivisible in relation to perception" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). This distinction between absolute and perceptual indivisibility is crucial, for it reveals that Aristotle is not merely offering a subjective account of temporal experience but is also concerned with the ontological conditions that make measurement possible. Yet it would be a mistake to suppose that Aristotle's account of time is purely cosmological or mathematical. Time is also deeply connected to the perceptual and cognitive faculties of the soul. Without perception—without the awareness of a before and an after in the flow of change—there would be no time in the sense in which Aristotle means it. The soul's capacity for aisthēsis, for registering the difference between successive states, is what enables the counting that constitutes temporal measurement. Aristotle himself, in the *Metaphysics*, insists on the multiplicity of being, noting that those who speak of the indeterminate "while fancying themselves to be speaking of being, they are speaking about non-being; for that which exists potentially and not actually is the indeterminate" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 42–43). This remark, while directed at a broader metaphysical context, illuminates the temporal problematic by underscoring that what is merely potential—what has not yet been actualized—occupies a peculiar ontological status that time, as the measure of motion, serves to articulate. Accordingly, Aristotle's account of time demands attention not only to cosmological regularity but also to the perceptual and cognitive apparatus through which temporal order is apprehended. 

## 4. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The relationship between motion and time in Aristotle's philosophy is one of mutual implication rather than simple derivation. Time does not exist apart from motion, nor motion apart from time; rather, they are co-given in the experience of change. Bowin emphasizes that the determinacy of temporal units depends on motions that are themselves determinate in time, creating a reciprocal structure in which neither concept can be fully articulated without the other (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). This reciprocity is philosophically significant because it means that any attempt to define time in purely objective, cosmological terms will necessarily circle back to the question of perception, and any account of perception will in turn presuppose a temporal framework within which perceptual acts are situated. Hence, the Aristotelian analysis of motion and time opens onto a phenomenological domain that later thinkers, specifically Heidegger, would develop into a full-scale existential analytic. Heidegger's interpretation of the motion-time relation in Aristotle transforms what might appear to be a technical discussion in natural philosophy into a fundamental question about the being of Dasein. As the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* emphasize, kinesis is not merely a physical phenomenon but "a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Similarly, Heidegger reads Aristotle's pathos as a form of motion that discloses the world affectively, noting that passions alter judgments by intruding upon logos—a point developed in the observation that "passions alter judgements" and that "this is where passions intrude on logos" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). This intertwining of affect, motion, and  time, for Aristotle, is not merely counted but felt, not merely measured but lived. The Heideggerian reading thus recovers an experiential dimension that purely formal analyses of the time-motion relation tend to obscure. Furthermore, the ecological and ambient dimensions of this relationship should not be overlooked. Gibson's account of the visual perception of motion and rest within an environment provides a useful parallel, noting that "an object can be resting on a surface of support, in contact with it, without being attached to it" and that "these distinctions will be used in discussing motions as ecological events" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 114–116). While Gibson is not directly commenting on Aristotle, his ecological framework illuminates the manner in which motion is always perceived within a structured environment, an insight that resonates with (Aristotle 2014) time is the number of motion and hence depends upon the perceptual registration of change within a surrounding world. 

## 5. Aisthēsis and Temporal Awareness

The Aristotelian concept of aisthēsis—sense perception—serves as the indispensable ground upon which temporal awareness is built, for without the perception of change, there can be no awareness of the before and after that constitutes time. Aisthēsis, in Aristotle's framework, is not a passive reception of data but an active discrimination of qualities, magnitudes, and relations within the perceptual field. It is through the senses that the soul first registers the movement of things and thereby becomes aware of temporal succession. Accordingly, the question of temporal awareness is, at bottom, a question about the nature and scope of perceptual activity. Burke's discussion of identification in rhetoric illuminates an analogous structure, for just as aisthēsis involves the active participation of the perceiver in the world, rhetorical identification induces the auditor to participate in a shared form; Burke argues that the expression of a proposition in rhetorical form involves "identification, first by inducing the auditor to participate in the form, as a 'universal' locus of appeal, and next by trying to include a partisan statement within this same pale of assent" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 73–74). This parallel is instructive because it highlights the active, participatory character of both perception and persuasion. Similarly, Burke's treatment of the temporizing of essence provides a framework for understanding how temporal awareness is not merely a matter of registering succession but also of interpreting the significance of what is perceived. Burke observes that "the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Thus, temporal awareness involves not only the perception of change but also a kind of hermeneutic activity in which the perceiver situates present experience within a narrative of origin and development. For Aristotle, this hermeneutic dimension is inseparable from the operation of the common sense and phantasia, which together unify discrete perceptual impressions into a continuous temporal experience. Bowin underscores that the specious present—that brief duration of which we are immediately sensible—is constituted precisely through such perceptual and cognitive synthesis (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 17–19). Hence, aisthēsis is not merely the starting point of temporal awareness but its ongoing condition of possibility. 

## 6. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

If aisthēsis provides the raw material of temporal awareness, it is phantasia—the faculty of imagination or appearance—that accomplishes the unification of discrete perceptual moments into a coherent temporal experience. Aristotle distinguishes phantasia from both sensation and judgment, treating it as a distinctive capacity of the soul that preserves and re-presents sensory impressions in the absence of the external object. 4). This characterization reveals that phantasia operates at the intersection of perception and cognition, retaining the traces of what has been perceived and making them available for the soul's further processing. Specifically, phantasia enables the soul to hold together the before and after of motion, thus providing the continuity without which temporal awareness would dissolve into disconnected instants. The rhetorical significance of phantasia is equally important, for it is through phantasia that discourse acquires the power to make things appear before the mind's eye, rendering absent realities present and future possibilities vivid. 4). This observation is critical for understanding the temporal dimension of phantasia, for it means that the faculty is not merely reproductive but also productive, capable of generating images that anticipate future states or reconstruct past ones. Accordingly, phantasia serves as the bridge between perception and deliberation, between the immediate registration of change and the reflective apprehension of temporal order. Burke's discussion of narrative and temporal equivalents of logical essences further supports this point, insofar as the temporizing of essence requires precisely the kind of imaginative projection that phantasia enables (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). The cooperation of aisthēsis and phantasia thus constitutes the perceptual-cognitive apparatus through which Aristotle's account of time achieves its phenomenological depth, demonstrating that temporal awareness is not a single faculty but a complex interplay of sensory registration, imaginative retention, and cognitive synthesis. 

## 7. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's engagement with Aristotle constitutes one of the most consequential episodes in twentieth-century philosophy, for it is through this engagement that Heidegger develops the existential analytic of Dasein that animates *Being and Time* and his subsequent thought. Heidegger reads Aristotle not as a historical curiosity but as a thinker who comes closest to articulating the question of being in its full radicality, even as the subsequent tradition covers over the original insights that Aristotle's work contains. Specifically, Heidegger interprets Aristotle's account of kinesis as disclosing the fundamental mobility of Dasein's existence, and he reframes the Aristotelian categories of potentiality and actuality as existential structures rather than merely logical or physical concepts. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* make clear that, for Heidegger, "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27), a claim that transforms the Aristotelian physics of motion into an ontology of existence. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is particularly revealing, for (White 1985) Heidegger understands the passions—the pathē—not as mere psychological states but as fundamental attunements, or Befindlichkeiten, through which Dasein finds itself disposed toward the world. Heidegger observes that passion is "both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115), and this insight enables him to connect Aristotle's rhetorical analysis of affect with his own existential account of mood and disposition. Thus, the Heideggerian interpretation reveals that Aristotle's rhetoric is not merely a techne of persuasion but a phenomenology of affective disclosure, in which the passions open up the world in determinate ways and thereby shape the possibilities of judgment and action. Aristotle himself, in the *Rhetoric*, provides the concrete analyses of fear, anger, pity, and other emotions that serve as the basis for Heidegger's retrieval; the famous example of the fox swept into a hole in the rocks, suffering from "the swarms of fleas that fastened on her" (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 71), illustrates precisely the kind of vivid, emotionally charged discourse through which phantasia and pathos collaborate to produce rhetorical effect. Heidegger's retrieval of Aristotle is thus not a mere commentary but a creative appropriation that transforms the Aristotelian legacy. He reads the Greek conception of being as fundamentally tied to presence—being as Anwesenheit—and interprets Aristotle's analysis of motion, time, and perception as revealing the temporal structure of this presence. Rickert's observation that Heidegger understands dwelling as a "letting be" that "spares, preserves, and cultivates human beings in the world" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 53–54) captures the spirit of Heidegger's Aristotelian inheritance, for (White 1985) the Heideggerian project is, at its deepest level, an attempt to recover the Aristotelian insight that being is always being-in-motion, being-in-time, and being-in-the-world. Heidegger's own foundational work, *Being and Time*, identifies the worldly character of the environment as it "announces itself in entities within-the-world" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 494), thereby connecting his existential analytic back to the Aristotelian problematic of how motion, time, and perception disclose the structures of the world in which Dasein dwells. ## Conclusion

The foregoing analysis has traced the intricate connections among motion, time, and temporal awareness in Aristotle's philosophy, demonstrating that these concepts form a tightly interwoven fabric that extends from natural philosophy through psychology to rhetoric and phenomenology. Aristotle's definition of time as the number of motion with respect to the before and after establishes the ontological dependence of temporality on change, while his accounts of aisthēsis and phantasia reveal the perceptual and cognitive conditions under which temporal experience becomes possible. Heidegger's retrieval of these Aristotelian insights transforms them into the foundations of an existential analytic in which motion, affect, and temporality are understood not as properties of objects but as fundamental structures of Dasein's being-in-the-world. Thus, the dialogue between Aristotle and Heidegger illuminates the deep continuity between ancient Greek ontology and modern phenomenology, suggesting that the question of time is inseparable from the question of being and that any adequate account of temporal awareness must attend to the full complexity of perception, imagination, and affective attunement. Accordingly, this inquiry serves as a necessary prolegomenon to any study that seeks to understand how human beings inhabit, navigate, and make sense of the temporal horizons within which all experience unfolds. ---
