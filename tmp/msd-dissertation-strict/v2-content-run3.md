# Phantasia, Perception, and the Problem of Being: Aristotle's Imagination from De Anima to Heidegger's Phenomenological Reinterpretation

## 1. Unlike Plato, who treated phantasia as a composite of sensation and judgment—thus rendering it derivative and epistemologically suspect—Aristotle endeavored to establish phantasia as a distinct capacity of the soul, one that could not be reduced to either perception or opinion alone. As Papachristou argues in her detailed study of the notion of phantasia in *De Anima*, Book III, the Aristotelian position leads us to the conclusion that the "Stageirite philosopher discriminates not two —as it is commonly argued— but three kinds or grades of phantasia: (a) Ιndefinite/indeterminate phantasia (ἀόριστος φαντασία)" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 25–27). This tripartite  Aristotle recognized a graduated spectrum of imaginative activity, ranging from the rudimentary image-making shared with non-rational animals to the more complex deliberative phantasia operative in human practical reasoning. Indeed, the significance of phantasia within the Aristotelian framework extends well beyond the narrow confines of *De Anima*; it permeates the *Rhetoric*, where the capacity to bring things "before the eyes" of an audience is central to persuasion. O'Gorman clarifies this rhetorical dimension by arguing that "bringing before the eyes" is a lexical form of energeia, a type of motion realized through the capacity of the mind for seeing objects in action (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 5). Thus phantasia operates simultaneously as a psychological faculty and a rhetorical instrument, connecting the internal movements of the soul to the external efficacy of discourse. Aristotle himself, in his discussion of causation and knowledge, reminds us that the master-workers in each craft are considered "more honourable and know in a truer sense and are wiser than the manual workers, because they know the causes of the things that are done" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 4–5). Similarly, phantasia enables a kind of knowing that grasps causes and appearances together, situating the user within a field of meaning that extends from bare sensation to deliberative choice. Accordingly, any comprehensive account of Aristotle's phantasia must attend to its role as a faculty that mediates between the immediacy of perception and the universality of thought, a faculty whose significance is amplified when we consider how it was subsequently reinterpreted by phenomenological thinkers such as Heidegger. 

## 2. Aristotle and Imagination

The relationship between phantasia and what we might broadly call "imagination" is itself a matter of considerable scholarly dispute, not least because the English term introduces connotations of creative fancy and productive invention that are largely absent from the Greek original. For Aristotle, phantasia is fundamentally tied to the capacity for image-formation—the production of phantasmata, or appearances that arise from prior perceptual experience and persist in the soul even in the absence of the external object. This generative capacity is not, however, a free play of the mind; it is constrained by the causal structures of perception, and the phantasma retains a residual connection to the sensory encounter from which it arose. Specifically, Caston observes that the scholarly tradition has long struggled with the compositional structure of *De Anima*, noting, through Lewes's earlier assessment, that "we shall note here, as in almost every one of [Aristotle's] scientific works, the want of masterly and logical arrangement of subject, and the want of the elementary requisites of good composition" (Caston, *Why Aristotle Needs Imagination*, pp. 2–3). This organizational complexity has contributed to deep disagreement about whether phantasia is a single unified power or a family of related capacities operating at different levels of cognitive sophistication. What remains clear, however, is that imagination for Aristotle is not a faculty of creative fabrication; it is a preservative and representative function that enables the soul to retain and manipulate the sensory content of past encounters. Indeed, Gonzalez's analysis of phantasia in the *Rhetoric* makes explicit that the phantasma can even encompass social and discursive content: "the phantasma is what others say about us; and that the persuasion operative in the phantasia is enhanced in proportion as the speakers are trustworthy" (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 27–28). Hence the Aristotelian imagination must be understood not as an isolated mental act but as a deeply relational capacity, one that situates the user within a network of perceptions, memories, and social significances. Similarly, Burke's dialectical method of successive redefinition—his notion that "an ever closer approximation to truth by successive redefinition is sometimes offered as the opposite of the dialectical method" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 422–424)—resonates with the way phantasia continuously refines and re-presents the content of experience, circling back upon itself in a process of imaginative clarification. Aristotle's insistence that the good and the beautiful are different, since "the former always implies conduct as its subject, while the beautiful is found also in motionless things" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157),  imagination operates across both the practical and the contemplative domains of human life, and thus cannot be confined to any single category of mental activity. 

## 3. Aristotle and Perception

Perception, for Aristotle, constitutes the foundational stratum upon which phantasia and all higher cognitive operations are built, yet perception itself is far from a passive reception of external stimuli. In the Aristotelian framework, aisthesis involves a genuine actualization of the sensible form without its matter; the soul takes on the form of the perceived object, and this act of reception is simultaneously an act of discrimination and engagement. Aristotle makes clear in the *Physics* that "each of them has within itself a principle of motion and of stationariness" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 7), a claim that applies not only to natural bodies but, by extension, to the perceptive soul that encounters those bodies. The perceptual act is itself a kind of motion, a qualitative change that transpires in the organ of sense and radiates inward toward the common sense and, subsequently, toward the faculty of phantasia. Thus perception and phantasia are intimately linked: the former provides the raw content that the latter preserves, recombines, and presents to the deliberative intellect. Gibson's ecological approach to perception offers a useful point of comparison here, for Gibson similarly insists that perception is not a matter of constructing internal representations from meaningless sensory data but rather a direct engagement with structured information in the environment. As Gibson notes, "when ambient light at a point of observation is structured it is an ambient optic array" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 114–116), and this structuring of the perceptual field is what makes meaningful perception possible. While Gibson's framework departs from Aristotle in significant ways—specifically by dispensing with internal representations altogether—the shared emphasis on the active and relational character of perception is noteworthy. Von Uexküll's concept of the Umwelt provides yet another productive lens, for his account of how "each component of an organic or inorganic object is brought into connection with, let us say, a 'complement,' in the body of the subject, which serves as a consumer of meaning" (von Uexkull, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 151–152) captures the Aristotelian insight that perception is always already a meaningful encounter, never a mere registration of neutral data. Gross's work on situated affect similarly underscores that perceptual immersion involves rhetorical and phenomenological dimensions, as demonstrated by his observation that experimental descriptions were designed to induce the ability to "be there," which "begs literary and rhetorical questions about identification" (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, pp. 128–129). Accordingly, perception for Aristotle is the initial moment of a continuous process that runs through phantasia toward deliberation and action, and its structure determines the quality and reliability of everything that follows. 

## 4. Aristotle and Being

The question of being pervades Aristotle's account of phantasia at every level, for the capacity to imagine is ultimately grounded in the soul's mode of being in the world—its way of existing as an embodied, perceiving, and thinking substance. Aristotle's ontological commitments, particularly his rejection of Parmenidean monism and his insistence on the multiplicity of being, create the philosophical space within which phantasia becomes both possible and necessary. In the *Physics*, Aristotle argues against the idea that being means only one thing, asserting that "even if what is not cannot be without qualification, there is no reason why it should not be something or other" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 7). This pluralistic ontology is crucial, for it allows phantasia to operate in the domain of what-is-not-yet or what-is-no-longer—the domains of possibility, memory, and anticipation that constitute the temporal horizon of practical life. Indeed, in the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle elaborates the distinction between fulfillment and potentiality by affirming that "being has two forms—it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157), and it is precisely this double character of being that phantasia exploits when it renders absent objects present to the mind. Heidegger's phenomenological reinterpretation radicalizes this connection between phantasia and being. As Rickert observes, Heidegger's concept of dwelling conveys "active comportments toward the world, such as building, enacted less as the perpetuation of mastery and control than as a 'letting be' that spares, preserves, and cultivates human beings in the world" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 53–54). This notion of "letting be" resonates profoundly with the way phantasia allows what is absent to appear, not through the coercive imposition of representation but through a receptive openness to the self-showing of phenomena. Furthermore, Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's pathe as Befindlichkeiten—as affective attunements rather than static propositional states—transforms the understanding of phantasia from a cognitive faculty into a mode of being-in-the-world. The *Heidegger and Rhetoric* volume confirms this interpretive trajectory: "Passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering: veranderliche Beschaffenheit" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Hence, phantasia as Heidegger reads it is not merely a representational mechanism but a constitutive feature of Dasein's disclosive engagement with beings, a capacity through which being itself is made manifest in the space between perception and thought. 

## 5. Aristotle and Time

Time occupies a crucial position in the Aristotelian analysis of phantasia, for the very possibility of imagination depends upon the temporal persistence of perceptual content and its availability for subsequent retrieval, combination, and deliberative use. Aristotle's treatment of time in the *Physics* locates temporality in the relationship between motion and the counting soul; time is the number of motion with respect to before and after, and it requires a perceiving subject capable of distinguishing temporal intervals. The discriminating power of the soul and the time of its exercise must be, as Aristotle insists, one and undivided—a unity that underlies the coherence of the phantasma as a temporally extended representation. Specifically, (Aristotle 2014) the *Physics* that "in thought they are separable from motion, and it makes no difference, nor does any falsity result, if they are separated" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 19) points to the capacity of the mind to abstract mathematical and formal properties from the flux of temporal experience, a capacity that is itself dependent upon the intermediary work of phantasia. Without phantasia, the soul could not retain the traces of past perception, nor could it project those traces forward into deliberative scenarios about future action; thus the temporal dimension of phantasia is inseparable from its practical and cognitive functions. The question of time in Aristotle also intersects with Heidegger's radicalization of temporality in *Being and Time*, where the ecstatic structure of Dasein's temporal existence—its being-ahead-of-itself, already-being-in, and being-alongside—provides a phenomenological framework within which the Aristotelian analysis of phantasia can be reinterpreted. Rickert's ambient approach to rhetoric, informed by Heidegger, suggests that kairos needs to be thought of in terms of world-openness rather than mere opportunistic timing, and that the tools and environments with which we merge start,  through Rickert, to blur the distinction between inner and outer, mind and world (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 57–60). Similarly, Gibson's observation that "the motion of a detached object is not the prototype of an event that we have been led to think it was" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 265–266) challenges the reductive treatment of temporal events as successions of discrete atomic motions, inviting us to reconsider how phantasia apprehends temporal wholes rather than punctual instants. Aristotle's insight, articulated in the *Metaphysics*, that "if there is movement, and something moved, and everything is moved out of something and into something, it follows that that which is moved must first be in that out of which it is to be moved" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 130–131), reinforces the fundamentally transitional and processual character of phantasia as a faculty that inhabits the space between what was and what is yet to come, thereby constituting the temporal architecture of deliberative thought. 

## 6. Frede, Phantasia, and the Scholarly Tradition

The interpretive tradition surrounding Aristotle's phantasia has been shaped decisively by the work of Dorothea Frede, whose analyses of Aristotle's philosophy of mind have clarified the relationship between phantasia, perception, and belief. Frede's contribution is particularly significant in delineating how phantasia functions as a faculty distinct from both aisthesis and doxa—a point that Papachristou elaborates by arguing that a detailed study of phantasia in *De Anima*, Book III, reveals three distinct kinds or grades of the faculty, rather than the two that are commonly attributed to Aristotle (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 25–27). This tripartite scheme—comprising indeterminate phantasia, sensory phantasia proper, and deliberative or calculative phantasia—owes much to Frede's insistence that Aristotle's psychological vocabulary be taken seriously in its philosophical specificity rather than being collapsed into modern cognitive categories. Indeed, the question of whether phantasia involves propositional content has been one of the most persistent debates in the secondary literature. Caston addresses this question by noting that the grounds adduced for the propositional character of phantasia are "largely undermined by the differences she develops between the character of 'sensory representation' (which she attributes to phantasia) and symbolic representation" (Caston, *Why Aristotle Needs Imagination*, pp. 13–14), thereby suggesting that phantasia operates below or alongside the level of linguistic articulation. Gonzalez complicates this picture further by demonstrating that in the rhetorical context, phantasia is deeply intertwined with the persuasive efficacy of discourse and the social standing of speakers (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, pp. 27–28). Thus the scholarly conversation initiated by Frede and extended by Caston, Papachristou, and Gonzalez reveals phantasia to be an irreducibly complex phenomenon that resists univocal definition. The phenomenological reinterpretation offered by Heidegger—wherein the pathe are understood as Befindlichkeiten and phantasia becomes a mode of disclosive attunement—adds yet another layer of complexity. As the contributors to the *Heidegger and Rhetoric* volume demonstrate, Heidegger's observation that passions "alter judgements" reveals the point where "passions intrude on logos—here articulated judgements, although frequently translated by historians of philosophy as 'reason'" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Von Uexküll's framework similarly enriches our understanding by showing that perceptual engagement always involves an organism-specific environment of meaning, wherein "perception cells and their perception signs take charge and transpose a perception mark into the environment (a quasi-mental process)" (von Uexkull, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 121–124). Accordingly, the trajectory from Frede's analytical clarification through Heidegger's phenomenological reappropriation reveals that phantasia remains one of the most fertile and contested concepts in the entire Aristotelian corpus, one whose implications extend from ancient psychology to contemporary theories of rhetoric, ecology, and being. ---
