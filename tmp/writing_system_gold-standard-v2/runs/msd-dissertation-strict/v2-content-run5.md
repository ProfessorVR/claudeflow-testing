# Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Temporal Awareness: Toward a Heideggerian Rhetorical Ontology

## 1. Articulate Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Rhetorical Being

Aristotle's philosophical project, as it has been received and reinterpreted across the history of Western thought, provides the foundational vocabulary through which motion, time, and the perceptual life of human beings have been conceptualized; indeed, his ontological commitments regarding these phenomena remain indispensable for any serious inquiry into the relationship between rhetoric and being. Specifically, Aristotle understood the natural world as constituted by entities whose very being is disclosed through their modes of change and persistence, such that motion (*kinēsis*) is never merely an accidental feature of things but rather belongs to the fundamental structure of what it means for something to exist as a natural being. As Heidegger's reading of Aristotle makes clear, *kinēsis* is not to be understood as mere physical displacement; instead, as the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* observe, "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). This interpretation underscores the depth of Aristotle's account: motion belongs not only to the physics of bodies but also to the ontological constitution of living, perceiving, and speaking beings. Similarly, Aristotle's treatment of temporal awareness—how we perceive and cognize the passage of time—cannot be disentangled from his account of motion, for time is always the measure or number of change perceived by a soul capable of discrimination.

Thomas Rickert, in his account of ambient rhetoric, draws attention to the way in which Aristotle's philosophical inheritance is transformed when we attend to the spatial and temporal dimensions of rhetorical engagement. Rickert argues that kairos, a concept deeply rooted in Aristotelian rhetorical thought, "originally had a spatial dimension, though this early usage was gradually lost in favor of meanings such as 'opportunity' and 'decorum,' among others" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 108–109). This observation is significant because (White 1985) Aristotle's understanding of time and motion in rhetoric is not limited to the temporal dimension of opportunity but encompasses a broader attunement to the world—a mode of being-in-the-world that Heidegger would subsequently elaborate. Accordingly, to articulate Aristotle's understanding of motion, time, and temporal awareness is to trace the lineaments of an ontology in which beings are always already moving, always already temporally situated, and always already disclosing themselves through the perceptual and cognitive capacities of the soul. Aristotle himself insists, in the *Metaphysics*, that "being has two forms—it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157), thus grounding the dynamic character of reality in the very structure of being itself.

## 2. Aristotle on Motion

Motion, for Aristotle, is one of the most fundamental categories through which beings manifest themselves; it is neither a mere predicate of substance nor a secondary quality but rather the actuality of what exists potentially insofar as it is potential. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle elaborates the basic modes of being as *dynamis* (potentiality) and *entelecheia* (actuality), and he insists that *kinēsis* is not something that exists apart from things but rather constitutes the very manner in which beings are. Heidegger, in his reading of Aristotle's *Physics*, underscores this point by noting that the fundamental topic of the inquiry into nature is "κίνησις and what is cogiven in it," including the basic modes of being as "ὄν δυνάμει and ἐντελεχείᾳ" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263). Thus, motion is not something superadded to beings but the very process by which they achieve their proper form and fulfillment; it is the actualization of potential being as such.

This conception of motion extends well beyond the merely physical. In the *Rhetoric*, Aristotle employs narrative and temporal movement as constitutive features of persuasive discourse, drawing upon fables and historical examples to illustrate how the movement of argument mirrors the movement of events in the world (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 71). The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* further clarify the rhetorical significance of motion by noting that passion (*pathos*) is itself "both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Hence, the passions that move an audience are not merely psychological states but ontological movements—transformations in the being of those who are affected. This interpretation aligns with Heidegger's  *kinēsis* is a way of being, not simply a physical event; specifically, it reveals how the rhetorical situation is always already a situation of motion, change, and becoming. Accordingly, Aristotle's account of motion provides the necessary groundwork for understanding how temporal awareness and rhetorical persuasion are ontologically intertwined, for without motion there would be neither time nor the possibility of persuasion through discourse.

## 3. Aristotle on Time

Time, in Aristotle's philosophical framework, is inseparable from motion and perception; indeed, Aristotle defines time as the number or measure of motion with respect to the before and after, a definition that immediately implicates the perceiving soul in the constitution of temporal experience. John Bowin, in his careful study of Aristotle's account, observes that "we measure time by counting units of time (e.g., days) which are determinate because they are marked out by motions that are determinate in time (e.g., celestial motions)" (Bowin, "Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time," pp. 7–9). Counting, Bowin notes, is "the type of measurement that is most exact because the measure that it involves is absolutely indivisible, rather than 'indivisible in relation to perception'" (Bowin, "Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time," pp. 7–9). This passage is crucial, for it reveals that Aristotle's understanding of time is not merely cosmological—rooted in the regular cycles of celestial motion—but also deeply epistemological, requiring a discriminating soul capable of perceiving and counting the units by which time is measured. Thus, without perception, there is, for Aristotle, no time in the strict sense; time is always time-for-a-perceiver.

In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle further complicates this picture by attending to the relationship between being and non-being, actuality and potentiality, in the constitution of temporal phenomena. He warns against those who speak "of the indeterminate, and, while fancying themselves to be speaking of being, they are speaking about non-being; for that which exists potentially and not actually is the indeterminate" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 42–43). This  time, insofar as it is bound up with the actualization of potentiality, always involves a passage from indeterminacy to determinacy—a passage that is itself perceived and measured by the soul. Similarly, the rhetorical dimension of time is evident in Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, where temporal sequence and narrative structure serve as the medium through which persuasion unfolds; the speaker arranges events in time, and the audience perceives the movement of argument as a temporal progression (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 71). Accordingly, Aristotle's account of time is at once physical, perceptual, and rhetorical, binding together the structure of the cosmos, the activity of the soul, and the movement of discourse.

## 4. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The relation between motion and time in Aristotle's thought is one of mutual implication: time does not exist apart from motion, and motion is always measured and perceived through time. Bowin's analysis clarifies this interdependence by emphasizing that the determinacy of temporal units depends upon the determinacy of the motions that mark them out; hence, the regular motions of the heavens provide the standard by which all other temporal measurements are calibrated (Bowin, "Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time," pp. 7–9). Yet this cosmological account does not exhaust the relation, for Aristotle insists that time is perceived only by a soul capable of discriminating the before and after in motion. Bowin  role of *phantasia* in this process, observing that the "specious present" constitutes a short duration of which we are immediately and incessantly sensible, and that this perceptual synthesis implicates *phantasia* in the common and incidental perception of time (Bowin, "Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time," pp. 17–19).

The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* deepen this analysis by showing that, for Heidegger, the Aristotelian relation between motion and time is not merely a matter of physics but discloses the very structure of Dasein's being.  "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27) entails that time, as the measure of motion, is equally fundamental to Dasein's mode of existence. Thus, the relation between motion and time is not one of external correspondence but of ontological co-constitution: beings are temporal precisely because they are in motion, and they are in motion precisely because their being is characterized by potentiality and actualization. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle, as articulated in *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, foregrounds the way in which the investigation of nature takes *kinēsis* and its concomitants as its basic topic, revealing that the inquiry into time is always already an inquiry into being (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263). Subsequently, the rhetorical significance of this relation becomes evident: persuasion unfolds in time, through the motion of argument, and the perception of temporal order is itself a condition of rhetorical engagement.

## 5. Aisthēsis and Temporal Awareness

Perception (*aisthēsis*), for Aristotle, is the primary capacity by which temporal awareness is constituted; without the discriminating activity of the senses, neither motion nor time can be apprehended. Kenneth Burke, in his analysis of the rhetorical dimensions of perception, draws attention to the way in which the logical and the temporal are intertwined in the act of perceiving and interpreting the world. Burke discusses "the puns of logical and temporal priority whereby the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). This insight is significant because it reveals that temporal awareness is not a passive reception of stimuli but an active, interpretive process—one that is deeply implicated in the rhetorical construction of meaning. Similarly, Burke's account of  the perceiver is always already participating in the forms through which experience is organized; specifically, (Hawhee 2011) importance of "inducing the auditor to participate in the form, as a 'universal' locus of appeal" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 73–74). Hence, *aisthēsis* is not merely a physiological event but a rhetorical and ontological one, in which the perceiver's engagement with the world is shaped by the temporal and formal structures of discourse.

James Gibson's ecological approach to perception further illuminates the Aristotelian account by emphasizing the structured character of ambient information. Gibson notes that "an object can be resting on a surface of support, in contact with it, without being attached to it," and that "these distinctions will be used in discussing motions as ecological events" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 114–116). This ecological perspective resonates with Aristotle's insistence that perception is always situated within a world of moving, changing beings, and that the perceiver's temporal awareness is constituted by the structure of the ambient environment. Indeed, the discriminating power of *aisthēsis* is precisely what allows the perceiver to mark the before and after in motion, and thus to perceive time as such. Accordingly, temporal awareness is not a faculty that operates in isolation but is always already embedded in the broader field of rhetorical and ecological engagement.

## 6. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

*Phantasia*, or the power of imagination, occupies a pivotal position in Aristotle's account of temporal unification, for it is through *phantasia* that discrete perceptual moments are synthesized into a coherent experience of temporal duration. Ned O'Gorman, in his study of Aristotle's *phantasia* in the *Rhetoric*, emphasizes that in the *De Anima*, "phantasia is intimately connected to the sense of sight, preserving visual perceptions in a sort of after-image, and is itself a sort of psyche-sight, the source of mental images or visual impressions" (O'Gorman, "Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric," p. 4). This characterization is essential: *phantasia* is not mere fancy or imagination in the modern sense but a faculty that retains and transforms sensory impressions, enabling the soul to hold together the before and after of experience in a unified temporal field. O'Gorman , though *phantasia* depends on sense perceptions, "there is no need to assume any precise correspondence between a phantasma and that which it is a phantasma of" (O'Gorman, "Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric," p. 4), thus underscoring the creative and interpretive dimension of this faculty.

The internal distinctions within *phantasia*—between its sensory and deliberative grades, its relation to belief (*doxa*), and its voluntariness—are matters of considerable scholarly debate; a fuller treatment of these distinctions, including the way in which deliberative *phantasia* enables practical reasoning and choice, will be developed in a subsequent chapter. For present purposes, it suffices (Aristotle 2014) *phantasia* is itself a kind of motion in the soul, intimately connected to *kinēsis* and thus to the temporal structures analyzed above. Burke's discussion of the temporizing of essence, in which logical priority is translated into temporal narrative (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28), provides a rhetorical analogue to this process: just as *phantasia* unifies discrete perceptions into a temporal whole, so too does rhetorical discourse organize discrete claims into a narrative that moves through time. Subsequently, the rhetorical function of *phantasia* is not merely illustrative but constitutive—it is through the imaginative synthesis of temporal experience that persuasion becomes possible.

## 7. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle is, in many respects, the philosophical event that makes possible the integration of the foregoing analyses into a unified ontological account of rhetorical temporality. Heidegger reads Aristotle's understanding of *kinēsis* not as a mere physics of bodies but as an ontology of Dasein, insisting that motion is a fundamental way of being rather than an accidental property; hence, in *Being and Time*, he attends to the way in which "the Worldly Character of the Environment Announces itself in Entities Within-the-world" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 494), thereby re-reading Aristotle's account of motion as a disclosure of Dasein's situated, temporal existence. Specifically, Heidegger's re-reading of motion reinterprets the Aristotelian conception of *kinēsis* as the dynamic self-disclosure of being, not merely as physical change but as the manner in which Dasein finds itself always already in motion within a world of significance.

With respect to Aristotle's account of time, Heidegger transforms the Aristotelian definition of time as the number of motion into an existential analytic of temporality, arguing that Dasein's being is fundamentally temporal and that the perception of time is grounded in Dasein's existential concern with past, present, and future. Regarding the relation between motion and time, Heidegger insists that these are co-constitutive features of Dasein's being, not external measures imposed upon an indifferent substance; (Hawhee 2011), the basic topic of inquiry into nature is "κίνησις and what is cogiven in it" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263). With respect to *aisthēsis* and temporal awareness, Heidegger re-reads Aristotelian perception as *Befindlichkeit*—a mode of attunement or disposition through which Dasein is always already open to the world. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* note that Heidegger interprets Aristotle's observation that passions alter judgments as the point where "passions intrude on logos" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115), thus revealing perception and affect as ontological rather than merely psychological phenomena. Similarly, Heidegger's re-reading of *phantasia* and temporal unification transforms Aristotle's account of imagination into an existential structure of Dasein's temporal projection—what Rickert describes as active comportments toward the world, "enacted less as the perpetuation of mastery and control than as a 'letting be' that spares, preserves, and cultivates human beings in the world" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 53–54). Thus, Heidegger's interpretation gathers the Aristotelian themes of motion, time, perception, passion, and imagination into a single ontological framework, one in which rhetoric is not merely a technique of persuasion but a fundamental mode of being-in-the-world. As Aristotle himself insists, the causes of being must be sought in all their forms, for "when the causes are being looked for, either all four must be sought thus or they must be sought in one of these four ways" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 14–15).
