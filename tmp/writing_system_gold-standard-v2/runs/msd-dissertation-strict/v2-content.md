# Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Temporal Awareness: Kinesis, Aisthesis, and Phantasia in Rhetorical and Ontological Perspective

## 1. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding

To articulate Aristotle's understanding of motion, time, and their interrelation within both rhetorical and ontological registers, we must first recognize that these concepts are not mere technical categories isolated within the domain of natural philosophy; rather, they permeate the very structure of human experience, disclosure, and persuasion. Aristotle's account of kinesis, as it has been received and reinterpreted across the philosophical tradition, reveals a concept far richer than simple locomotion or physical displacement. As the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* observe, kinesis should be understood not as an external property of objects but as something more fundamental: a way of being itself, such that motion constitutes the very character of entities in their becoming and self-disclosure (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). This reading, inflected through Heidegger's engagement with Aristotle, suggests that motion is not separable from the being of beings but is rather internal to the way entities come to presence. Accordingly, when we approach Aristotle's framework, we must attend to the ambient and world-disclosing dimensions of kinesis, which inform not only natural philosophy but also rhetorical theory. Thomas Rickert's work on ambient rhetoric further deepens our appreciation of the environmental and relational character of Aristotle's thought by arguing that concepts like kairos originally possessed a spatial dimension, though this early usage was gradually lost in favor of meanings such as opportunity and decorum, and most of these senses are keyed to a subject who is an active, willing, conscious agent (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 108–109). Specifically, Rickert's insight invites us to reconsider the extent to which Aristotle's key temporal and modal concepts—including motion—bear within them an ambient, situational quality that cannot be reduced to subjective intentionality alone. Indeed, the interplay between kinesis, aisthesis, and phantasia in Aristotle's  holistic account of human engagement with the world, one in which perception, imagination, and the experience of time are intertwined with rhetorical practice and ontological structure. Thus, any effort to understand Aristotle's treatment of these themes must draw upon both the rhetorical and the metaphysical dimensions of his philosophy, attending to the ways in which motion, time, and perception converge in the lived experience of the human being. ## 2. Aristotle on Motion

Aristotle's treatment of motion—kinesis—occupies a central position in his natural philosophy and metaphysics, serving as the conceptual hinge between potentiality and actuality, matter and form, and ultimately between being and becoming. In the *Physics*, as Heidegger's reading makes explicit, kinesis is not something alongside or external to things but rather a mode of the being of beings themselves: it is not separate from things but is rather the how of their being (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263). The basic topic of the inquiry into nature, as Aristotle frames it, concerns kinesis and what is co-given in it, including the fundamental modes of being that emerge in its analysis—being in potentiality and being in actuality, the categories, and the relational structure that pervades natural entities (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263). Hence, motion is not reducible to mere change of place; it encompasses generation, corruption, alteration, and growth, all of which are modes in which beings disclose themselves within the world. The rhetorical significance of kinesis becomes apparent when we consider how passions—pathe—function within Aristotle's account of persuasion. As the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* note, passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions, possessing the capacity for altering the dispositions and judgments of those who experience it (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Aristotle's observation that passions alter judgments reveals the point at which motion and logos intersect, for it is precisely through the kinetic force of affect that rhetorical discourse achieves its persuasive effect. Similarly, Aristotle in the *Metaphysics* reflects on the indeterminate character of what exists potentially but not actually, warning that those who speak of the indeterminate as if it were being are in fact speaking about non-being (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 42–43). This distinction between the potential and the actual is essential for understanding kinesis as the transition from one to the other, the movement by which what is merely possible comes into fulfillment and presence. Aristotle further clarifies that being has two forms—it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter—thereby underscoring the dynamic, processual character of reality itself (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). Subsequently, we can appreciate that motion, for Aristotle, is not a deficiency or privation but the very medium through which the world becomes intelligible and present to us. ## 3. Aristotle on Time

Time, in Aristotle's philosophy, is inseparable from motion; indeed, it is defined in relation to kinesis, serving as the measure or number of motion with respect to before and after. Yet the perception and cognition of time involve more than simple counting; they require a complex integration of sensory awareness, memory, and anticipation. As John Bowin argues, Aristotle holds that we measure time by counting units of time—such as days—which are determinate because they are marked out by motions that are determinate in time, for example celestial motions (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). This means that time is not a freestanding entity but is always disclosed through the perception of determinate motions, and the precision of temporal measurement depends upon the kind of units employed. Bowin  counting is the type of measurement that is most exact because the measure that it involves is absolutely indivisible, rather than indivisible merely in relation to perception (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). Thus, Aristotle distinguishes between the phenomenological experience of temporal passage and the more rigorous, cognitive act of measuring time through determinate units, a distinction that has profound implications for rhetorical theory insofar as the persuasive force of discourse depends upon the hearer's temporal awareness and capacity for discrimination. The metaphysical ground of this account is clarified in the *Metaphysics*, where Aristotle discusses the relationship between being, potentiality, and actuality, noting that the good and the beautiful are different, for the former always implies conduct as its subject, while the beautiful is found also in motionless things (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). This observation is pertinent to the question of time because (White 1985) temporal experience is bound up with the practical and the ethical—with conduct and action—rather than with the contemplation of static forms alone. In the *Rhetoric*, Aristotle's treatment of narrative and factual recollection further illustrates this point, as the rhetor's art consists in the arrangement and presentation of past facts, which are themselves temporal phenomena requiring the audience's capacity for temporal synthesis and judgment (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 71). ## 4. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The intimate relation between motion and time in Aristotle's philosophy is not merely formal or definitional; it is experiential and cognitive, rooted in the perceiver's capacity to discriminate temporal intervals through the awareness of change. Bowin's analysis underscores this point by drawing attention to the role of phantasia in the perception of time, noting that the specious present—the short duration of which we are immediately and incessantly sensible—serves as the experiential ground upon which temporal cognition is built (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 17–19). This concept of the specious present reveals that temporal awareness is not a matter of discrete, punctual instants but of a lived, flowing duration in which past and future are co-present within the sensory field. Hence, the perception of motion and the perception of time are not two separate cognitive acts but are rather aspects of a single, integrated mode of awareness. The ontological implications of this relation are drawn out in Heidegger's reading of Aristotle, where kinesis is interpreted as a fundamental way that Dasein is, such that motion gives the authentic character to human existence as being-in-the-world (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). On this view, the experience of time is not something that befalls a static subject from without but is constitutive of the very structure of human being. Specifically, Heidegger's  time and motion are co-originary, each disclosing the other in the lived experience of the human being who is always already engaged in a world of change and becoming. Rickert's ambient rhetoric further illuminates this point by insisting that concepts like kairos, which bind time to situational opportunity, must be thought of in terms of the broader environment and attunement within which human beings find themselves, rather than as mere subjective judgments (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 108–109). Thus, the relation between motion and time is not confined to the domain of physics but extends into the rhetorical, ethical, and existential dimensions of human life. ## 5. Aisthesis and Temporal Awareness

Aisthesis—sensory perception—serves as the foundational stratum upon which temporal awareness is built. Without perception, there can be no awareness of change, and without awareness of change, there can be no experience of time. Kenneth Burke's rhetorical theory, though not framed in the idiom of Aristotelian natural philosophy, provides a suggestive parallel. Burke discusses the relationship between logical and temporal priority, observing that the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Indeed, Burke's insight into the puns of logical and temporal  aisthesis is always already structured by conceptual and narrative frameworks that shape how we perceive the passage of time and the order of events. Subsequently, we  Gibson's ecological approach to perception offers a complementary perspective, emphasizing that perception is embedded in the ambient optic array and the persisting environment in which the perceiver moves and acts. Gibson notes that when ambient light at a point of observation is structured, it constitutes an ambient optic array, and the point of observation may be stationary or moving relative to the persisting environment (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 114–116). This ecological  temporal awareness is not merely a function of internal cognitive processes but is shaped by the structure of the environment itself, including the motions and events that constitute the perceiver's surroundings. Similarly, the fact that an object can be resting on a surface of support, in contact with it, without being attached to it, draws our attention to the fine-grained distinctions that perceptual awareness must make in order to track change and motion over time (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, pp. 114–116). Thus, aisthesis, as the ground of temporal awareness, is always already ecological and ambient, embedded in the world rather than sealed within the mind. ## 6. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

Phantasia—the faculty of imagination or appearance—plays a crucial role in Aristotle's account of temporal unification, bridging the gap between discrete sensory impressions and the continuous experience of time. As Ned O'Gorman observes, in Aristotle's *De Anima*, phantasia is intimately connected to the sense of sight, preserving visual perceptions in a sort of after-image, and is itself a sort of psychic sight, the source of mental images or visual impressions (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). This capacity for preserving and re-presenting sensory data is essential for temporal awareness, since without the ability to retain past perceptions and compare them with present ones, there could be no experience of duration, succession, or change. O'Gorman  phantasia depends on sense perceptions, yet there is no need to assume any precise correspondence between a phantasma and that which it is a phantasma of, indicating that the imaginative faculty introduces a degree of creative synthesis into the process of temporal unification (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). Burke's rhetorical theory enriches this account by drawing attention to the narrative dimension of temporal experience. By translating logical essences into temporal and narrative equivalents, the rhetor enables the audience to experience the unfolding of argument as a temporal event, thereby engaging phantasia in the act of persuasion (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Bowin's discussion of the specious present further supports this interpretation, suggesting that phantasia mediates between the immediate deliverances of sense and the broader cognitive synthesis required for the perception and cognition of time (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 17–19). Thus, phantasia is not a mere repository of images but an active, unifying faculty that enables the human being to integrate the fragmentary data of perception into a coherent temporal experience—a function that is no less essential for rhetorical discourse than for natural philosophy. ## 7. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle represents one of the most consequential philosophical appropriations of the classical tradition in the twentieth century, transforming the understanding of kinesis, pathos, and temporal awareness in ways that resonate deeply with contemporary rhetorical theory. In his *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, Heidegger reads Aristotle's treatment of motion and being as disclosing the fundamental ontological structures of human existence, insisting that beings must be apprehended not merely with regard to their material constitution but also with regard to their form and appearance (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 176–177). Heidegger draws attention to the way in which Democritus was borne by the matter itself, led to the discovery that the being of beings exceeds mere materiality—a point that Heidegger generalizes into a broader claim about the disclosive character of kinesis and physis. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* elaborate this interpretation by emphasizing that passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions, and that Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's pathe reveals the point at which affective experience, rhetorical persuasion, and ontological disclosure converge (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Specifically, Heidegger's observation that passions alter judgments and intrude upon logos reframes the traditional rhetorical understanding of pathos, showing it to be not merely a tool of persuasion but a fundamental mode of being-in-the-world. Accordingly, Heidegger interprets Aristotle's account of motion and time as disclosing the temporal and affective structure of Dasein, the being for whom being is always an issue. Rickert extends this line of thought by arguing that kairos and other temporal-rhetorical concepts must be understood ambiently, in terms of the broader environmental and relational context within which human beings dwell—a context that Heidegger describes as a letting be that spares, preserves, and cultivates human beings in the world (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 53–54). Thus, Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle opens up a space in which rhetoric, ontology, and temporal experience are seen as deeply intertwined, each illuminating the others in the ongoing task of understanding what it means to be a being in motion, in time, and in the world. ---
