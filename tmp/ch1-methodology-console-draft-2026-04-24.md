# Methodology: The Actualization Chain as Interpretive Apparatus

## 1. Chapter Frame

This chapter advances a sustained interpretive reading of Aristotle's account of perception, imagination, cognition, and appetitive action as a single architectural sequence: the *actualization chain*. The chain reads across multiple treatises — *De Anima*, *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *Sense and Sensibilia*, *On Dreams*, *On Memory*, *De Motu Animalium*, and *Rhetoric* — and treats the passage from sensible object to completed action as a series of iterated motions linking completed actualities. Each node in the chain is an actuality; each link is a motion; and each motion produces the actuality that the next motion will presuppose. The chain is not a doctrine Aristotle hands us pre-formed; it is an interpretive construction that integrates his scattered analyses of perception and locomotion into a unified architectural reading. The present section specifies the methodological apparatus that makes that reading possible: the three-factor kinetic schema, the priority of actuality, the structural principle that each motion is named by its terminus, the scale-invariance of relative unmoved originators, the interlocking of actuality-first architecture with the iterated chain, and the citation and interpretive conventions the chapter observes throughout.

One overarching frame motivates the project of which this chapter is a component: the soul's inherent rhetorical nature. The soul is not, on the reading this dissertation develops, a cognitive faculty that subsequently enters into rhetorical or affective relations with its world; it is constituted, from the moment of its first perceptual encounter, as a faculty whose kinetic and affective dimensions are constitutive rather than supplementary. The capacity for being-affected (πάσχειν) is not a secondary feature added to cognition but the mode of being that cognition itself specifies under a different *logos*. The full defense of this rhetorical framing is developed in *Chapter X: The Motion-Inducing Soul — Rhetoric, Attunement, and Actualization (Heidegger, Rickert, Burke)*. For the present chapter's purposes, the framing supplies the motivating stakes: the actualization chain is not a metaphysically neutral apparatus but the structure through which the rhetorically constituted soul unfolds its perceptual and appetitive capacities in engagement with a world that matters to it. What this chapter traces is the architectural sequence through which that mattering is generated.

## 2. The Actualization Chain as Interpretive Construction

The chain advanced here organizes Aristotle's analyses of perception and action into five principal nodes: $A_0$ (motion and time as the first actuality), $A_1$ (the preconditions under which perception can occur — sensible object actually exercising its sensible power, sense-organ in its first actuality as capacity, rational souls existing in the world), $A_2$ (completed perception together with the dual residual trace it deposits), $A_3$ (the *phantasma* proper, crystallized from the resonant *kinēsis* at A₂), and $A_4$ (the completed cognitive actuality produced when the rational cognitive apparatus engages the *phantasma* — intellection, memory, deliberative or speculative synthesis, or the committal dimension of *doxa*). Subsequent stages develop the orectic motion through which the cognitive actuality, when it discloses a realizable good, mobilizes *orexis* into determinate appetitive action; these are traced in the downstream sections on emotion and action and are accordingly not numbered in the present methodology's enumeration.

The chain is an *interpretive construction* in the sense that no single Aristotelian treatise presents it as such. Aristotle supplies the pieces: the three-factor kinetic schema at *De Anima* III.10, 433b12-25; the definition of motion at *Physics* III.1, 201a10-14; the definition of time at *Physics* IV.11, 219b1-2; the priority-of-actuality doctrine at *Metaphysics* IX.8, 1049b12-1050a16; the hylomorphic account of perception at *De Anima* II.12, 424a17-24; the treatment of residual motion at *On Dreams* 2, 459a24-460b3; the dependence of thought on images at *De Anima* III.7, 431a15-17 and III.8, 432a3-9; the enmattered-accounts framework at *De Anima* I.1, 403a25-b19; the practical-syllogism at *De Motu Animalium* 7, 701a7-25. The architectural work of integrating these into a sustained reading of perception-to-action as a single iterated motion-structure is the present chapter's contribution. Where this chapter advances interpretive readings that go beyond what Aristotle explicitly states — the ontological incompleteness of time without soul, the dual-trace thesis, the orthogonal committal dimension of *doxa*, the gatekeeping condition for doxastic commitment — the reading is flagged as interpretive rather than presented as direct exegesis.

## 3. The Three-Factor Structure of Perceptual Motion

Three ontological components constitute the perceptual event: **actuality**, **motion**, and **time**. Their relation is asymmetric. Actuality is prior in account and substance (*Metaphysics* IX.8, 1049b4-10): the potential is intelligible only through the actuality it is potential *for*, and each motion takes its name from its terminus, not its starting point (*Physics* V.5, 229b25). Motion is the fulfilment of a potential *qua* potential (*Physics* III.1, 201a10) — the ontological hinge connecting one completed actuality to the next. Time is the number of motion with respect to before and after (*Physics* IV.11, 219b1) — derivative from motion and therefore doubly dependent on actuality. The chain's architectural sequence proceeds chronologically, because that is how the ensouled body lives through the event: motion and time are experienced together as the passage from one completed state to the next. But at each node, the completed actuality is what makes the preceding motion — and its temporal measure — intelligible. The list reads forward; the explanation runs in reverse.

## 4. The Kinetic Schema

Each motion in the chain possesses an identical internal structure. Aristotle provides the schema at *De Anima* III.10, 433b12-25:

> "All movement involves three factors, (1) that which originates the movement, (2) that by means of which it originates it, and (3) that which is moved. The expression 'that which originates the movement' is ambiguous: it may mean either something which itself is unmoved or that which at once moves and is moved. Here that which moves without itself being moved is the realizable good, that which at once moves and is moved is the faculty of appetite (for that which is moved is moved insofar as it desires, and appetite in the sense of actual appetite *is* a kind of movement), while that which is in motion is the animal. The instrument which appetite employs to produce movement is bodily: hence the examination of it falls within the province of the functions common to body and soul. To state the matter summarily at present, that which is the instrument in the production of movement is to be found where a beginning and an end coincide as e.g. in a ball and socket joint; for there the convex and the concave sides are respectively an end and a beginning (that is why while the one remains at rest, the other is moved): they are separate in definition but not separable spatially. For everything is moved by pushing and pulling."

Aristotle gives three factors: (1) that which originates movement (τὸ κινοῦν) — the unmoved originator, i.e., the realizable good (τὸ ὀρεκτόν / τὸ πρακτὸν ἀγαθόν); (2) that by means of which it originates movement (ᾧ κινεῖ) — the faculty of appetite (ὀρεκτικόν), which "at once moves and is moved"; and (3) that which is moved (τὸ κινούμενον) — the animal, via the bodily instrument (the joint as beginning-and-end). This is not merely a classification of physical locomotion. It is Aristotle's schema for *any* psycho-somatic movement — and crucially, the internal psychic transitions that constitute the chain (aisthēsis → phantasia → cognition → desire → action) are movements. The three-factor schema therefore supplies a structural principle that recurs at every level of the chain.

One qualification deserves registration. The schema is introduced by Aristotle specifically for locomotive action, and its extension to perceptual, phantastic, and cognitive motion is analogical rather than strictly isomorphic. At the perceptual level, for instance, the "moved mover" (the sense faculty) does not mediate between an unmoved originator and a moved body in quite the way *orexis* does at the locomotive level; rather, the actualization of the sense faculty *is* the actualization of the organ's capacity — they are the same event under different descriptions. The kinetic vocabulary is accordingly applied by analogy rather than by direct transfer. Where this matters for the analysis, the analogical character of the extension is noted explicitly.

## 5. Relative Unmoved Originators

In its absolute sense, the term "unmoved mover" belongs to the Prime Mover of *Metaphysics* XII, 1072a23-26 and *Physics* VIII, 256a-260a. However, Aristotle employs the three-factor schema with *relative* unmoved movers. In the *De Anima* III.10 passage quoted above, the "realizable good" is identified as the unmoved originator *within the practical syllogism*, not as the cosmological Prime Mover. At each level of motion, something functions *as* the unmoved originator relative to that particular motion-event, without being the cosmological Unmoved Mover. In the case of perception, the sensible object functions as the unmoved originator relative to the perceptual motion: the object acts on the sense faculty without being reciprocally acted upon *in that respect* (*qua* sensible). One reason for adopting this schema is that it is scale-invariant: the same structure applies at the cosmic level (Prime Mover → celestial spheres → sublunary motion) and at the psychological level (sensible object → sense faculty → perceiver). The three factors are formally identical; only the domain changes. This is not accidental; it reflects Aristotle's conviction that psychic events are a species of natural motion, subject to the same structural principles as any other kinesis the natural world exhibits.

## 6. The Actuality-First Architecture and the Iterated Chain

The actuality-first architecture and the three-factor schema interlock at a precise point: the completed actuality at each node is *what serves as the relative unmoved originator of the next motion*. $A_0$ (motion and time as first actuality) is what lets $A_1$ be what it is — the ontological ground upon which the preconditions for perception can obtain. $A_1$ (the sensible object actually exercising its sensible power, together with the sense-organ in its first actuality) is the unmoved originator of the perceptual motion $A_1$→$A_2$. $A_2$ (the completed perceptual act together with the dual residual trace it deposits — resonant *aisthēma* and resonant *orexis*) is the unmoved originator of the phantastic motion $M_2$→$A_3$. $A_3$ (the *phantasma* proper, crystallized by *phantasia* at the central organ) is the unmoved originator of the cognitive motions $M_3$→$A_4$. $A_4$ (the completed cognitive actuality — the universal grasped, the past recalled, the possibility evaluated, the belief held) is, when its content discloses a realizable good, the unmoved originator of the orectic motion through which appetitive action proceeds. Each completed actuality is both a terminus that *names* the motion preceding it (*Physics* V.5, 229b25) and an unmoved source that *initiates* the motion following it. Motion takes its name from its end; the end, once achieved, becomes the beginning of what comes next.

The chain of motion is accordingly not a sequence of *different kinds* of events but the *same motion-structure* iterated at increasing levels of complexity. At each stage, the moved mover is both patient and agent — the hinge that converts one motion into the next. The sense faculty is moved by the object and moves the organism. *Phantasia* is moved by the residual trace and moves the psycho-somatic state at the central organ. The rational cognitive apparatus is moved by the *phantasma* and moves the soul toward its determinate cognitive completion. Appetite is moved by the *phantasma*-mediated realizable good and moves the body. Between each pair of hinges stands a completed actuality: the stable node from which the preceding motion receives its name and the succeeding motion receives its impetus. Time measures the passage from node to node — the before-and-after of each motion — and is itself intelligible only through the actualities that bound it.

The generalization that licenses this iterated architecture is that each transition from one actuality to the next is itself a motion — a further actualization of a capacity that was latent in the preceding stage. $A_0$ → $A_1$ is the actualization of the world's natural beings (made possible by motion and time); $A_1$ → $A_2$ is the actualization of perception (made possible by the sensible object and sense-organ); $A_2$ → $A_3$ is the actualization of the *phantasma* (made possible by the residual trace of perception); $A_3$ → $A_4$ is the actualization of the completed cognitive actuality (made possible by the *phantasma* as object of thought); $A_4$ → subsequent stages is the actualization of appetitive action (made possible by the completed cognitive actuality that discloses a realizable good). Each transition is a motion in the *energeia ateles* sense of *Physics* III.1, 201a10-14; each terminus is a completed actuality that becomes the unmoved originator of the next. The chain's primitive unit — actuality grounding the motion that produces the next actuality — iterates at every stage, and its specification at $A_0$ is what licenses everything downstream.

## 7. Burke on Entelechy

Kenneth Burke's reading of Aristotle's entelechy provides an interpretive resource for understanding how the chain's iterated architecture licenses itself. Burke observes that the entelechy introduces "another kind of priority, namely the 'principle' involved in a given form" — a priority that runs opposite to temporal sequence. A stage that follows another in time may be prior in form and substantiality: "man is 'prior' to boy because man has already attained its complete form whereas boy has not" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 261). The distinction Burke draws — between *temporal priority* and *priority in form and substantiality* — is crucial for the chain structure. Each actuality in the chain stands in both relations to its neighbors. $A_0$ is temporally prior to $A_1$ (motion and time must be in act before sensible objects and sense-organs can actually exist), but $A_1$ is formally prior to the specific perceptual motions it will ground, just as the completed form of man is formally prior to the boy who is becoming a man. The chain is, accordingly, not a linear sequence of self-sufficient stages but a nested architecture in which each actuality licenses and is licensed by others in a reciprocal relation of temporal and formal priority. This reciprocal structure is what makes the chain genuinely iterated rather than merely sequential: each node is simultaneously the terminus of what precedes it and the principle of what follows it, and each node's intelligibility depends on both relations at once.

## 8. Citation and Nomenclature Conventions

The chapter observes the following conventions. Aristotelian citations are given in Bekker notation — book, chapter, and page-column-line reference — rather than by page number in any particular modern edition. Thus *De Anima* II.12, 424a17-24 rather than "*De Anima*, p. 18." Bekker notation fixes the location in Aristotle's text independently of edition and translation, and its consistent use reflects the chapter's interpretive commitment to locating claims in the original text rather than in any derivative apparatus. Secondary scholarship is cited by author, short title, and page number (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 261), following standard academic conventions for works whose authorship and edition are proprietary rather than canonical.

Greek terms are transliterated in italics when deployed as technical vocabulary (*kinēsis*, *energeia*, *dunamis*, *entelecheia*, *aisthēsis*, *phantasia*, *phantasma*, *chronos*, *nun*, *poiesis*, *pathesis*, *pros ti*, *ousia*, *pathos*, *pathē*, *logos*, *doxa*, *orexis*, *epithumia*, *thumos*, *boulēsis*, *hexis*, *alloiōsis*, *hylē*, *eidos*). Where the Greek is provided directly alongside the transliteration, it appears in the original script (κίνησις, ἐνέργεια, etc.). Coined terms introduced by this dissertation — *resonant kinēsis*, *resonant aisthēma*, *resonant orexis* — appear in bold on first introduction and in italicized form thereafter.

Chain notation uses subscripted capitals for actuality nodes ($A_0$, $A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$, $A_4$) and subscripted capitals with arrows for motions ($A_0$→$A_1$, $M_1$→$A_2$, $M_2$→$A_3$, $M_3$→$A_4$, etc.). Where the context makes clear that a transition between two actualities is meant, the arrow notation is used; where the motion itself requires separate designation from its terminus, the motion is named as $M_n$→$A_{n+1}$.

## 9. Interpretive Scope and Flagging Practice

The chapter's primary source is Aristotle, read across the treatises enumerated in §2 above. Secondary scholarship — Dorothea Frede, Victor Caston, Kevin White, Martha Nussbaum, Debra Hawhee, Ned O'Gorman, Jose Gonzalez, Daniel Gross, John Bowin, Christina Papachristou, James Gibson, Jacob von Uexküll — is deployed where it illuminates the Aristotelian argument under development. Secondary sources are treated as commentary on Aristotle rather than as independent philosophical positions; where a secondary source advances a claim that goes beyond what Aristotle himself argues, the claim is marked as such and engaged on its own terms.

Heidegger, Rickert, and Kenneth Burke in his phenomenological-rhetorical register are deferred to *Chapter X: The Motion-Inducing Soul — Rhetoric, Attunement, and Actualization (Heidegger, Rickert, Burke)*. The deferral is motivated by the interpretive scope of the present chapter: the actualization chain is developed here primarily as an Aristotelian architecture, and the Heideggerian, Burkean, and Rickertian resources that radicalize or reinterpret that architecture belong to the subsequent chapter's treatment. Closing footnotes at the end of each section within this chapter flag the forthcoming engagement without developing it. Burke's reading of Aristotle's entelechy (*A Grammar of Motives*, p. 261) is an exception: it is deployed in the present chapter at §7 above because it bears directly on the chain's actuality-first architecture and does so in a register continuous with Aristotle's own. Where Burke's rhetorical theory of motivated action is at stake — the *Rhetoric of Motives* and *Grammar of Motives* taken together as an account of how motion-inducing appeals work rhetorically — the treatment belongs to the forthcoming chapter.

The chapter observes a *flagging practice* for interpretive readings that go beyond Aristotle's explicit statements. Where the chapter advances a reading Aristotle himself does not articulate — the ontological incompleteness of time without a counting soul, the dual-trace thesis distinguishing resonant *aisthēma* from resonant *orexis*, the orthogonal committal dimension of *doxa*, the gatekeeping condition for doxastic commitment, the "taking-as" distinction between *phantasia* as aspectual presentation and *doxa* as propositional ratification — the reading is marked as interpretive rather than exegetical. Flagging serves two functions: it preserves the distinction between what Aristotle argues and what this chapter proposes on Aristotle's behalf, and it invites engagement with alternative readings that the textual evidence could equally license. The chapter's interpretive moves are defended on the grounds that they cohere with Aristotle's broader commitments and illuminate structural features of the chain that the texts themselves presuppose but do not fully articulate. Where the chapter's reading is controversial, the controversy is acknowledged and the reading's textual warrants specified.

## 10. Chapter Map

The chapter proceeds as follows. $A_0$ (*Motion and Time as First Actuality*) establishes the ontological ground of the chain: motion as *energeia ateles*, time as the countable number of motion, and the motion-time parallel that licenses the iterated chain structure. $A_1$→$A_2$ (*The Actualization of Perception*) develops the preconditions for perception, the hylomorphic reception of sensible form, the unity of mover and moved as a single *energeia* under two *logoi*, perception as enmattered preservative *alloiōsis*, the persisting residual motion (*resonant kinēsis*), and the dual-trace thesis that specifies the internal structure of that residue. $M_2$→$A_3$ (*The Phantastic Motion and the Phantasma Proper*) traces the secondary actualization through which the residual trace is taken up by *phantasia* and crystallized at the central organ into the *phantasma* proper, with its inheritance of the dual-trace structure. $A_3$'s subsequent sections analyse the cognitive engagements of the *phantasma* at $M_3$→$A_4$ — intellection addressing the image as bearing a universal, memory addressing it as likeness of the past, the synthetic mode of deliberative and speculative *phantasia* addressing multiple *phantasmata* as composable into unified objects of thought, and the orthogonal committal dimension of *doxa* ratifying the aspectual presentation as propositional truth-claim. The treatment of emotion and appetitive action — how the completed cognitive actuality, when it discloses evaluatively complex content, flips the latent resonant *orexis* into the determinate higher-order *pathē* that mobilize *orexis* into appetitive form, and how that mobilized *orexis* moves the body to action — follows the cognitive analysis. The chapter closes with a synthesis that situates the chain's architectural contribution within the dissertation's wider rhetorical project and prepares the transition to Chapter X's engagement with Heidegger, Rickert, and Burke.
