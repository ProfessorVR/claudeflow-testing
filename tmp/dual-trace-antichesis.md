# The Dual Trace: Resonant Motion, Resonant Affect, and the Mechanism of Antichesis in Aristotle's Ontology of Motion and Time

## 1. Kinēsis as Ontological Ground

Any serious investigation into the architecture of Aristotle's psychology must begin with the recognition that *kinēsis*—motion, change, process—is not merely one category among many but rather the ontological ground upon which the entire edifice of soul, perception, affect, and time is erected. Motion in the Aristotelian framework is not reducible to mere spatial displacement; it encompasses qualitative alteration, quantitative growth and diminution, and the coming-into-being and passing-away of substances. Thus, to speak of kinēsis is to speak of the most fundamental modality through which beings disclose themselves in the world. As Heidegger emphasizes in his reading of Aristotle, the investigation is not simply about those beings determined by φύσις but rather "about φύσις itself, about the being of these beings" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 207)[1]. This distinction is crucial: kinēsis is not an accidental property that attaches to beings from the outside; it is the very manner in which beings are present, the mode of their being-there in the world. Accordingly, when we approach the soul's operations—perception, imagination, desire, judgment—we must understand them as fundamentally kinetic phenomena, as modalities of motion that unfold within the living being's encounter with its environment. Burke's dramatistic approach foregrounds the question of what motivates action, and his analysis of substance reveals a paradox at the heart of any grounding concept: "when we confront a simplicity we must forthwith ask ourselves what complexities are subsumed beneath it" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 126)[2]. Indeed, the apparent simplicity of kinēsis as a concept conceals an extraordinary complexity; it is at once the condition for the soul's receptivity to form, the mechanism by which phantasmata are generated, and the temporal substrate through which experience acquires duration and sequence. Heidegger's interpretive work further clarifies this point by situating kinēsis among the fundamental categories that make ontological inquiry possible. Specifically, Heidegger notes that "characters such as κίνησις, τόπος, χρόνος, the ἀρχαί" constitute the horizon within which beings become intelligible, and that "change as a mode of being of being-there itself" is the proper framing for understanding motion's ontological significance (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 214). Hence, kinēsis is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be inhabited—an ontological ground that makes possible the entire chain of operations from sensation to deliberation, from affect to action. The implications of this grounding are far-reaching. If kinēsis is the mode of being of being-there itself, then every subsequent operation of the soul—perception, imagination, desire, memory—must be understood as a particular modulation or determination of motion. The soul does not stand apart from motion and then engage with it; the soul *is* a principle of motion, and its faculties are differentiated modes of kinetic activity. This insight establishes the framework within which the concepts of resonant motion, resonant affect, and antichesis can be developed, for these concepts name the specific ways in which the kinetic ground of the soul gives rise to the dual traces—kinetic and affective—that structure human experience. Burke's insistence that reduction to simplicity demands attention to the complexities subsumed beneath it reminds us that the unity of kinēsis conceals a rich internal differentiation, one that the subsequent sections of this analysis will endeavor to articulate (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 127). 

## 2. The T-Stage Chain and Taking-As

The concept of the T-Stage Chain names the sequential yet interpenetrating stages through which a living being moves from initial sensory contact with its environment to fully articulated judgment and action. In Aristotle's psychology, this chain proceeds through aisthēsis (perception), phantasia (imagination), orexis (desire), and ultimately praxis or phygē (action or avoidance). Each stage is not a discrete, self-contained operation but rather a transformation of the kinetic impulse initiated in sensation—a taking-up and re-articulation of the motion that the sensible object has set in train. Specifically, phantasia occupies a pivotal position in this chain, for it is the faculty that transforms raw perceptual motion into something that can be taken *as* something, thereby enabling the transition from mere sensation to meaningful apprehension. As Aristotle argues in *De Anima*, "Sensitive imagination, as we have said, is found in all animals, deliberative imagination only in those that are calculative: for whether this or that shall be enacted is already a task requiring calculation; and there must be a single standard to measure by, for that is pursued which is greater" (Aristotle, *De Anima*, pp. 51–52)[3]. This passage reveals the structural differentiation within phantasia itself: sensitive imagination operates at the level of immediate perceptual taking-as, while deliberative imagination introduces a comparative, calculative dimension that allows the organism to weigh alternatives against a unified standard. The operation of "taking-as" is thus the fundamental mechanism by which the T-Stage Chain advances from one stage to the next. In perception, the soul takes the sensible form without the matter; in phantasia, the residual motion left by perception is taken as an image or phantasma that can be manipulated independently of the original sensory stimulus. Subsequently, this phantasma is taken as desirable or aversive by orexis, which in turn motivates pursuit or avoidance. The chain is not linear in any simple sense; rather, it is recursive and self-modifying, as the affective coloring introduced at the level of orexis feeds back into phantasia, altering the very images through which the world is apprehended. Von Uexküll's analysis of the functional cycle (*Funktionskreis*) in animal perception offers a parallel framework for understanding this recursive structure. 121), thus illustrating that perception is never merely passive reception but an active projection of meaning-signs onto the encountered world. Similarly, in Aristotle's account, the soul does not passively receive forms but actively constitutes them through the kinetic operations of phantasia. When the phantasma is taken as representing a state of affairs, it gives rise to doxa; when doxa is taken as bearing on the organism's well-being, it gives rise to pathos (affect). Thus, the T-Stage Chain is simultaneously a chain of kinetic transformations and a chain of evaluative determinations, each stage presupposing the one before it while adding a new dimension of meaning. Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle's ontological vocabulary reinforces this point: ὁρισμός, the delimiting definition, "is not a way of apprehending through sharp determination, but rather the specific character of ορισμός ultimately arises from the fact that the being itself is determined in its being as circumscribed by the πέρας" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 25–26)[4]. Taking-as, in other words, is not an imposition of categories from without but a disclosure of the being's own limits and determinations from within. 

## 3. Dioxis and Phygē: The Pursuit/Avoidance Structure

At the heart of the T-Stage Chain lies a fundamental bifurcation: every encounter with the world resolves, at the level of orexis, into either pursuit (*diōxis*) or avoidance (*phygē*). This binary structure is not an arbitrary classification imposed upon experience but rather the most basic orientation of the ensouled being toward its environment. The organism does not first perceive and then decide whether to pursue or avoid; rather, the very structure of perception is already inflected by the pursuit/avoidance orientation, since phantasia presents the world not as neutral data but as a field of affordances and threats. Indeed, James Gibson's ecological approach to perception provides a compelling modern articulation of this principle. Gibson's insistence on the organism-environment reciprocity, his understanding that perception is fundamentally oriented toward action, and his treatment of affordances as properties of the environment relative to the animal all resonate with the Aristotelian structure of diōxis and phygē. Gibson observes that "the 'permanent objects' of the world, which are of so much concern to psychologists and philosophers, are actually only objects that persist for a very long time" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 37)[5], thereby dissolving the static object-world in favor of a dynamic, event-structured environment in which the organism is always already engaged. This dynamic engagement is precisely what the pursuit/avoidance structure describes. For Aristotle, diōxis and phygē are not merely behavioral outcomes but ontological orientations of the soul; they are the ways in which being-there is moved by its encounter with the world. The structure is already present at the level of sensitive phantasia, where the animal perceives something as pleasant or painful and is immediately moved toward or away from it. At the level of deliberative phantasia, the structure becomes more complex, as the organism must calculate which of several possible objects of pursuit is "greater"—that is, more desirable relative to a single standard of measurement (Aristotle, *De Anima*, pp. 51–52). Aristotle's *Rhetoric* further elaborates this structure by examining how contrary states—such as health and illness, pity and indignation—are essentially paired possibilities. As Aristotle notes, "if it is possible for one of a pair of contraries to be or happen, then it is possible for the other: e.g. if a man can be cured, he can also fall ill; for any two contraries are equally possible, in so far as they are contraries" (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, pp. 68–69)[6]. This principle of contraries is foundational for the pursuit/avoidance structure, since every object of pursuit is simultaneously a potential object of avoidance, and every state of well-being implies the possibility of its opposite. Gibson's ecological framework  the environment is structured not by neutral physical properties but by what it affords the organism—what it offers for action, whether beneficial or harmful. The ambient optic array, as Gibson theorizes it, is structured by "the layout of surfaces, the pigmentation of surfaces, and the shadowing of surfaces" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 109)[7], and this structuring is always relative to the perceiving organism's capacities for pursuit and avoidance. Von Uexküll's concept of the *Umwelt* provides yet another parallel: the organism's perceptual world is constituted by the "perception marks" and "effect marks" that it projects onto its environment, such that "the sensation 'blue' becomes the 'blueness' of the sky, the sensation 'green' becomes the 'greenness' of the lawn" (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 52–53). Hence, the pursuit/avoidance structure is not imposed upon a pre-given world but is constitutive of the world as experienced. 

## 4. Resonant Motion: The Kinetic Trace

When perception ceases—when the sensible object is no longer present to the sense organs—the motion initiated by the encounter does not simply vanish. Rather, it persists as what we may call *resonant motion*: a lingering kinetic trace within the soul that continues to reverberate after its original cause has departed. This concept, designated *apoleipomenē kinēsis* (remaining motion) in the Aristotelian corpus, is the mechanism by which phantasmata are generated and sustained. The phantasma is not a static image stored in some mental repository; it is an ongoing motion, a kinetic residue that retains the formal structure of the original perceptual encounter while being detached from the material presence of the sensible object. Thus, resonant motion is the bridge between aisthēsis and phantasia, the process by which the perceptual present extends itself into the experiential past and the anticipated future. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's ontology of motion provides essential context for understanding this kinetic trace. Specifically, the concept of ἐνέργεια—actuality, being-at-work—is central to grasping how motion persists. As Heidegger notes in his discussion of κινητόν, the mobile being is characterized by ἐνέργεια—the being-at-work of what is capable of motion (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 282). The residual motion of phantasia is thus not a diminished or degraded form of perception but a genuine mode of being-at-work, an actuality in its own right. The phantasma is the ἐνέργεια of the kinetic trace, the mode in which the soul continues to be moved by what it has encountered. Similarly, Aristotle's account of the discriminating power of the soul illuminates how resonant motion is structured temporally. The discriminating faculty, Aristotle argues, operates "qua undivided one, and active in a single moment of time, while qua divisible it twice over uses the same dot at one and the same time" (Aristotle, *De Anima*, p. 40)[8]. This remarkable  the soul's kinetic activity possesses a dual aspect: it is at once unified and divided, operating in a single temporal instant while simultaneously articulating difference within that instant. Resonant motion, accordingly, is not a simple repetition of the original perceptual motion but a complex, internally differentiated kinetic event. The kinetic trace thus constitutes the temporal substrate of experience. Without resonant motion, there could be no continuity between perceptual moments, no basis for memory, anticipation, or deliberation. The phantasma, as the product of resonant motion, is what allows the organism to navigate a world that is not merely present but temporally extended—a world of sequences, durations, and expectations. Gibson's observation that we should "talk about changes, events, and sequences of events but not about time as such" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 37)[9] is instructive here, for it reminds us that the temporal dimension of experience is not an abstract container but is constituted by the very kinetic processes that unfold within the organism-environment system. 

## 5. Resonant Affect: The Affective Trace

If resonant motion names the kinetic trace left by perception, then *resonant affect* names the affective trace—the lingering emotional coloring that accompanies and inflects the phantasma. In Aristotle's account, pathos (affect, emotion) is not an independent faculty but a modulation of kinēsis: affect *is* a particular kind of motion in the soul, one that is inseparable from the phantasmatic content that occasions it. When we are moved by pity, for instance, the motion is not merely cognitive but somatic and evaluative; the entire being-there of the organism is reoriented in relation to its world. As Aristotle explains in the *Rhetoric*, "Most piteous of all is it when, in such times of trial, the victims are persons of noble character, for their suffering is undeserved and it is set before our eyes" (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, pp. 60–61)[10]. Here, the affective trace is explicitly linked to the visual and imaginative presentation of suffering: what is "set before our eyes" is not merely a perceptual datum but a phantasmatic construction that resonates with evaluative and emotional significance. Thomas Rickert's concept of ambient rhetoric provides a contemporary framework for understanding how affective traces operate within the broader environment of meaning. Rickert argues that rhetoric is not simply a matter of persuasive speech or deliberate communication but is always already embedded in an ambient environment that "cradles, without forcing, particular affective responses or that fosters an attunement" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric: The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, p. 172)[11]. This notion of attunement resonates profoundly with the Aristotelian concept of pathos as a mode of being-moved: the affective trace is not a discrete mental event but a pervasive orientation of the organism within its environment, an attunement that shapes how subsequent phantasmata are generated and interpreted. Accordingly, resonant affect is not merely a consequence of perception but a constitutive condition of future perception; the affective trace left by one encounter inflects the phantasmatic horizon within which subsequent encounters are apprehended. The relationship between resonant affect and the pursuit/avoidance structure is also essential. Since pathos depends upon orexis (desire), and orexis depends upon phantasia, the affective trace is always already oriented toward diōxis or phygē. The lingering emotional coloring of a phantasma is precisely what makes it an object of pursuit or avoidance in future encounters. Similarly, Aristotle notes in the *Rhetoric* that pity and indignation are "directly opposed" yet structurally linked: both arise from the perception of what is happening to others, and both presuppose the phantasmatic capacity to imagine oneself in the other's situation (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, pp. 60–61). Thus, the affective trace is inherently bifurcated, always potentially resolving into contrary orientations—a structural feature that anticipates the mechanism of antichesis to be discussed in the following section. 

## 6. Antichesis: The Unitary Mechanism of Internal Resonance

The concept of *antichesis* names the unitary mechanism by which resonant motion and resonant affect are simultaneously generated and held in dynamic tension within the soul. Antichesis, literally a "counter-placement" or "opposition," describes the internal differentiation that occurs when the kinetic trace of perception is split into its kinetic and affective dimensions—dimensions that are distinguishable in analysis yet inseparable in actuality. This mechanism is rooted in Aristotle's broader ontological commitment to the explanatory power of contraries. As noted in the *Rhetoric*, contraries are "equally possible, in so far as they are contraries" (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, pp. 68–69)[12]; hence, the internal structure of the phantasma is always organized around opposing poles—pleasure and pain, pursuit and avoidance, presence and absence. Antichesis is the name for this structural opposition as it operates within the kinetic life of the soul. The mechanism of antichesis can be understood through the Aristotelian concept of the discriminating faculty. In *De Anima*, Aristotle describes a faculty that is simultaneously one and two: unified in its act of discrimination yet divided in the content it discriminates. The faculty, "qua undivided one, and active in a single moment of time, while qua divisible it twice over uses the same dot at one and the same time" (Aristotle, *De Anima*, p. 40)[13], embodies the very structure of antichesis. The "same dot" used twice at the same time is the kinetic moment in which the dual trace—kinetic and affective—is produced. The phantasma is thus not a simple residue but a complex, internally counter-placed structure in which the formal trace of the perceived object (resonant motion) and the evaluative trace of the organism's response (resonant affect) coexist in dynamic opposition. Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle's ontological vocabulary further illuminates this mechanism. The concept of the ὑποκείμενον, as Heidegger reads it, is not the static "substance" of metaphysical tradition but rather "that which becomes visible in assertion" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 226–227)[14]. The ὑποκείμενον, in this reading, is the ground upon which antichesis operates: it is the substrate that can be "convert[ed]" from ὑποκείμενον into non-ὑποκείμενον and vice versa, a process of counter-placement that mirrors the internal bifurcation of the phantasma into its kinetic and affective traces. Hence, antichesis is not a secondary operation performed upon a pre-given phantasma; it is the very mechanism by which the phantasma comes into being as a dual trace. Without antichesis, there would be no differentiation between the what and the how of experience—between what is perceived and how it is felt. The unitary mechanism of internal resonance ensures that every phantasma is simultaneously a kinetic event and an affective event, a representation and an evaluation, a trace of the world and a trace of the soul's response to the world. 

## 7. The Dual Trace and Temporal Navigation

The dual trace—resonant motion and resonant affect, generated through the mechanism of antichesis—constitutes the fundamental structure by which the ensouled being navigates temporal experience. Time, for Aristotle, is not an independent container within which events occur but is itself dependent upon kinēsis: time is the number of motion with respect to before and after. This structural relationship means that the dual trace is not merely situated *in* time but is constitutive *of* time as experienced. The kinetic trace provides the formal continuity that allows the organism to recognize sequences and durations, while the affective trace provides the evaluative gradient that distinguishes significant from insignificant moments, marking certain temporal nodes as worthy of pursuit or avoidance. Together, these traces enable the organism to orient itself within a temporal horizon that extends both backward (through memory) and forward (through anticipation and deliberation). Burke's analysis of the relationship between individual action and larger historical or dialectical processes offers a suggestive analogy for understanding temporal navigation through the dual trace. 288–289). While the metaphysical framework differs dramatically, the structural insight is relevant: the individual organism's affective responses—its "blind passions"—serve as the medium through which broader patterns of temporal coherence are articulated. The dual trace, in other words, is not merely a private, subjective phenomenon but a mode of engagement with the larger temporal order of the world. Similarly, Aristotle's account of deliberative phantasia—which requires that "there must be a single standard to measure by, for that is pursued which is greater" (Aristotle, *De Anima*, pp. 51–52)[15]—reveals that temporal navigation is fundamentally comparative: the organism must weigh present phantasmata against past traces and future projections, using the dual trace as its instrument of measurement. Heidegger's treatment of κίνησις, τόπος, and χρόνος as co-primordial categories further clarifies this temporal architecture. By insisting that these characters constitute the horizon of ontological inquiry and are not merely empirical observations about the physical world, Heidegger positions the dual trace as an ontological structure rather than a psychological mechanism (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 214). The organism's capacity to navigate time is thus not an accidental feature added to its perceptual capacities but an essential dimension of its being-there. Von Uexküll's concept of the *Umwelt* reinforces this point: the organism does not inhabit an objective, universal time but a species-specific temporal environment constituted by the rhythm of its perception-action cycles. The effectors, as von Uexküll explains, "impress their 'effect mark' on the objects that lie outside the subject" (von Uexküll, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 52–53), and these effect marks are themselves temporal phenomena—they alter the environment in ways that shape subsequent perceptual encounters. Accordingly, the dual trace is the mechanism by which the organism stitches together the discontinuous moments of its perceptual life into a continuous temporal fabric, enabling navigation through a world that is always in motion. 

## 8. Conclusion: Completing the Human Experience

The analysis developed across the preceding sections reveals that the dual trace—resonant motion and resonant affect, united through the mechanism of antichesis—is the fundamental structure by which the ensouled being completes its experience of the world. Without the kinetic trace, there would be no continuity, no persistence of form beyond the perceptual instant; without the affective trace, there would be no evaluative orientation, no basis for the pursuit and avoidance that structure meaningful engagement with the environment. Together, these traces constitute the phantasma not as a static image but as a dynamic, internally differentiated kinetic event that bridges perception and action, present and future, self and world. Gibson's ecological approach reminds us that perception is never a matter of receiving static inputs but of engaging with "changes, events, and sequences of events" within a structured ambient array (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 37). The dual trace is the organism's mode of holding these changes, events, and sequences together—of making them available for deliberation, memory, and anticipatory action. Burke's dramatistic insight, that every simplicity conceals complexities that must be brought to light, applies here with full force: the apparent unity of experience is in fact the product of a complex mechanism—antichesis—that simultaneously generates and holds in tension the kinetic and affective dimensions of the phantasma (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 126). The human capacity to navigate a temporally extended world, to weigh alternatives, to feel the pull of contrary possibilities—all of this is grounded in the dual trace and the mechanism that produces it. Aristotle's ontology of motion and time, read through the lens of antichesis, thus reveals the deep structure of human experience: not a sequence of isolated moments but a resonant, self-differentiating process in which the soul is continuously moved by the world and continuously moves the world in return. The pursuit/avoidance structure, articulated through sensitive and deliberative phantasia, is the living expression of this process—a process that, as Aristotle recognizes, makes possible not only perception and affect but the very temporality within which human life unfolds. ---
