## 1. Emotion is Motion: *Phantasia*, Appearance, and the Affective Architecture of Aristotelian Being-Moved

Aristotle's account of emotion (*pathos* "passion/emotion/affect/being-affected") is not the modern psychological category of inner feelings, nor is it an epiphenomenon sitting atop otherwise rational operations of the human psyche. Rather, it is a *movement* (*kinēsis* "motion/change") of the soul, a dynamic reorientation within the field of appearances that alters how the world shows up and, in so doing, alters how one is disposed to act within it. Emotion is not simply something that happens inside a subject; it is the soul's *being-moved* by what appears significant to it. Emotion is therefore inseparable from *phantasia* ("imagination/presentation/the making-present-of-what-is-not-now-given") — the representational, imagistic, quasi-perceptual capacity through which things "show up" in the first place (DA III.3-8). Indeed, the very word, *emotion*, already encodes the kinetic ontology that Aristotle attributes to *pathos*. According to the Oxford English Dictionary, *emotion* derives from Middle French *émotion* ("a moving, stirring, agitation," especially in the sense of social or political unrest) and ultimately from Latin *emovēre*, meaning "to move out, remove, agitate." Morphologically composed of e- ("out, forth") and *movēre* ("to move, set in motion"), the term literally designates a moving-out, a displacement or perturbation that carries the subject beyond a prior equilibrium. The modern English form follows this lineage closely; its internal structure — e- (out) + mot- (move) + -ion (denoting the act or process) — naming not a static inner feeling but the *event* of being-moved, the process through which the soul is set into motion in response to what appears significant. The word's deeper Indo-European root, *meue- ("to push, move, stir"), likewise links *emotion* to *motion*, *momentum*, and *motivate*, reinforcing that its semantic horizon is fundamentally dynamic. Thus even at the level of etymology, *emotion* mirrors the Aristotelian claim that *pathos* is a form of *kinēsis*: a being-moved of the soul toward or away from perceived goods and evils. To undergo emotion is therefore to be shifted or displaced by the force of appearance — already in motion, already reoriented. In this way, the etymology of *emotion* not only confirms but amplifies the ancient insight: emotion is the embodied signature of significance, the soul's kinetic exposure to what matters. **[H1]**

>
> The opening thesis you stake — that *pathos* ("passion/emotion") is *kinēsis* ("motion") of the soul, a being-moved by what appears significant rather than an inner feeling — receives its strongest philosophical defense in Heidegger's GA 18 §18c reading of *De Anima* A 1. The decisive passage:
>
> > "*Πάθος* (*pathos* "passion"), in relation to the *ζωὴ πρακτικὴ μετὰ λόγου* (*zōē praktikē meta logou* "life-as-acting-with-speech"), is thus a *being-taken* of being-there. Being-there is taken with that which is there in the world *from without*, but from without *in the sense of the world as the wherein of my being*.... Therefore, the πάθη (*pathē* "passions") are not 'psychic experiences,' are not 'in consciousness,' but a being-taken of human beings *in their full being-in-the-world*. That is expressed by the fact that the whole, the full occurrence-context, in which it is found in this happening, in being-taken, belongs to the πάθη (*pathē*). The so-called 'bodily states' of anxiety, joy, and so forth, are not symptoms, but also belong to the characteristic being of beings, of human beings." (GA 18, p. 133)
>
> Three points of structural amplification follow. *First*, your etymological move from *emovēre* (the moving-out) to the Greek *kinēsis* finds its ontological correlate in Heidegger's "being-taken" (*Genommenheit*): the *e-* of *emotion* is precisely the "from without" of being-taken, and the "*motion*" is the *kinēsis* of being-in-the-world *itself*, not the agitation of an interior. The user's reading and Heidegger's converge in refusing the inside/outside topology that the modern psychological category presupposes.
>
> *Second*, your formulation that emotion "is the soul's *being-moved* by what appears significant to it" is structurally identical to Heidegger's claim that the *πάθη* (*pathē*) are the *modes in which being-there is taken with what is there in the world*. The "what appears significant" of your formulation is what Heidegger calls the world-as-wherein-of-my-being: the field within which something can show up *as mattering*. Emotion is therefore not a response to an antecedently given world but the very mode in which the world is disclosed *as* mattering in the first place.
>
> *Third*, the σωτηρία / φθορά distinction Heidegger isolates at GA 18 p. 132 — that πάσχειν (*paschein* "being-affected/undergoing") has *two* senses, "destruction by an opposite" (*φθορά*, *phthora*) and "preservation by what is actual" (*σωτηρία*, *sōtēria*) — provides the ontological signature of your "being-moved" thesis. Emotion is *being-affected* in the σωτηρία sense: the affecting does not annihilate but *brings into determinate actuality* a being-disposed that was already a possibility of the soul. Your "kinetic exposure to what matters" is precisely the σωτηρία-mode of πάσχειν: a being-affected that constitutes rather than diminishes.

Aristotle repeatedly returns to the interdependence between *phantasia* ("imagination/presentation") and *pathos*. In *De Anima* he defines *phantasia* as "a movement resulting from actual perception" (429a1), a residual *pathos* that persists after the object has gone (ibid.). Because this internal movement lingers, recombines, and re-presents, it becomes the very condition for emotional response in the absence of immediate stimuli. Remembering, hoping, fearing: each is a case of the soul being-moved by a *phantasma* ("image/presentation/mental presentation") of what is not-present but which nevertheless appears *as if* present. Emotion is fundamentally structured by this imagistic mediation.

The primary definition of emotion in Aristotle's corpus appears not in *De Anima* or any of the traditionally categorized psychological treatises, but in the *Rhetoric*: "the emotions are all those feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements, and that are also attended by pain or pleasure—such are anger, pity, fear and the like, with their opposites'" (II.1, 1378a20-22). Though this definition appears within the context of rhetorical theory's practical applications, it carries substantial ontological weight; and, it is this definition that must anchor any systematic analysis of where emotion belongs in the causal chain from perception to animal movement. Two elements of this definition are rather poignant: emotions "so change men as to affect their judgements" (*kriseis* "judgments/decisions/position-takings"): emotions are judgment-modifying affections or states in that they are not merely felt disturbances but cognitive events. Or, at the very least, they are events with cognitive consequences that reshape the evaluative landscape within which the person deliberates and acts. Second, emotions are "attended by" pain or pleasure; the pleasure or pain that accompanies fear or anger or shame is just that, an accompaniment and not the emotion in its entirety. This second feature is decisive for, in my analysis, it is what distinguishes emotion from the basic affective valence, or what I call *resonant orexis*: the raw pleasure/pain, pursue/avoid complex that Aristotle locates at the level of perception itself, where "the soul makes a sort of affirmation or negation, and pursues or avoids" (*DA*, III.7, 431a8-10). This basic affective valence — the organism's pre-propositional orientation toward the pleasant and away from the painful (what Heidegger calls mood) — is co-present with perception from thee earliest moment of the actualization chain. **[H2]**

>
> *First*, the κρίσεις (*kriseis*) -affecting moment you isolate is given its structural meaning in Heidegger's διαφέρουσι πρὸς τὰς κρίσεις (*diapherousi pros tas kriseis*) reading. *Kriseis* here are not external judgments altered by emotion-from-without; they are *position-takings* of being-there *from out of* the changed disposition. The Greek phrase Heidegger emphasizes — *διαφέρουσι* — names a "differentiating-of-oneself-from-oneself," a *being-different-from-what-one-was* in respect of how one stands toward the matter. Your formulation that emotions "reshape the evaluative landscape within which the person deliberates and acts" maps directly: the differentiated κρίσις is the new evaluative landscape, articulated *from* the changed disposition rather than *added on top of* an unchanged one.
>
> *Second*, your distinction between emotion proper and the "attended by" pleasure-pain is structurally vindicated by Heidegger's third aspect — the *οἷς ἕπεται λύπη καὶ ἡδονή* (*hois hepetai lupē kai hēdonē*) clause. Heidegger insists that *ἕπεται* (*hepetai* "follows/is co-given") here does *not* mean "follows" temporally — pleasure and pain are not consequences that arrive after the emotion, but *co-given* with it as a "being-disposed-as-higher-or-lower." This is precisely the structural status you assign to the hedonic accompaniment as distinct from the emotion's full structure. The πάθος is the changed-disposition-with-its-position-taking; the λύπη/ἡδονή is the tonal-elevation/depression co-given with that change. Heidegger's reading of *Rhet*. B 1 thus directly grounds the architectural cut you draw between *resonant orexis* (basic valence) and the higher-order πάθη.
>
> The three Heideggerian aspects together provide the phenomenological framework on which your subsequent analysis can lean: every πάθος is a (1) change-in-disposition that (2) differentiates the subject's position-taking and is (3) co-given with hedonic tonality. Each of these maps to a structural moment your draft analyzes — the change to *kinēsis*, the position-taking to *doxa* (*doxa* "opinion/belief/yes-saying"), the hedonic co-givenness to *resonant orexis*.

Anger is not undifferentiated displeasure but "desire accompanied by pain for conspicuous revenge for a conspicuous slight" (*Rhetoric* II.2, 1378a30). Each emotion, in fact, in Aristotle's taxonomy, specifies a cognitive content, a conative orientation, and a hedonic accompaniment (the pain or pleasure that attends the whole complex). It is this tripartite determinacy that separates emotion from the basic affective valence, or resonant *orexis* ("desire/striving/reaching-toward"), of pursuit-and-avoidance.

Here I must note that the present analysis restricts itself to rational animals: those possessing *logos* ("speech/reason/discursive articulation") and, therefore, capable of *doxa* (opinion/belief) as it is the faculty that the *De Anima* insists is necessary for the production of genuine emotion in contrast to the mere entertaining of fearful images.

Aristotle establishes the ontological framework for understanding emotion when he argues that all affections of the soul are "enmattered accounts" (*DA*, I.1, 403a25-b19) that cannot be abstracted from their material instantiation. The dialectician, aristotleobserves, would define anger as "a desire for retaliation" or "a desire for returning pain for a given pain." The physicists, by contrast, would define it as "a boiling of the blood and hot stuff about the heart" (403a31-b1). However, neither account is sufficient without the other; the full definition requires both: anger is the *desire* for retaliation *realized in* the boiling of blood around the heart. The formal and material accounts are complementary rather than competing; indeed, Aristotle argues that anything less would fail to capture the entirety of the phenomenon. The implications presented here are considerable. if anger is constitutively composite — simultaneously a cognitive evaluation (the perceived slight), a conative orientation (the desire to retaliate), and a physiological alteration (the heating of blood) — then no analysis or explanation of emotion that reduces it to any one of these components will suffice or contain true explanatory value. Emotion is not merely judgment, though it includes judgment; it is not merely desire, though it includes desire; it is not merely a bodily change, though it is inseparable from bodily change. The enmattered account thus imposes a structural constraint on any placement of emotion in the actualization chain: emotion must be located at a point where cognitive, conative, and physiological processes converge, and it must be understood as something irreducible to any one of them.

As we have discussed the structural components of which any and all emotions must consist, we must now turn to the conditions required for emotion to be produced: "Further, when we think something to be fearful or threatening, emotion is immediately produced, and so too with what is encouraging; but when we merely imagine we remain as unaffected as persons who are looking at a painting of some dreadful or encouraging scene" (*DA* III.3, 427b21-24). While this passage is used by Aristotle to distinguish between *phantasia* and *doxa*, he does so on an affective basis. The bare *phantasma* — the image entertained at will (the fearful scenario merely imagined) — leaves the soul unaffected. This suggests that the higher-order, determinate emotional states (the *pathē* of the *Rhetoric*) are not produced by *phantasia* alone. What produces them is *doxa* — the involuntary belief that the object is genuinely fearful — and the production is immediate, admitting of no perceptible, temporal interval between the formation of the belief/opinion and the onset of emotion.

Accordingly, *doxa*, for rational animals, serves as not only a condition but also as a cognitive gate through which emotion must pass to enter the actualization chain. The involuntariness of *doxa* is what gives emotion its distinctive phenomenological character as something that befalls the agent rather than something the agent elects to undergo. One does not choose to be afraid; one finds oneself afraid becauzse one has involuntarily *taken something to be* threatening. **[T1]**

> **[T1] Heideggerian Tension**
>
> The "*doxa* (*doxa* "opinion/belief/yes-saying") as cognitive gate" thesis is your most distinctive structural commitment in this chapter, and it is the locus of the deepest divergence between your reading and Heidegger's GA 18 reading of the πάθη. The directional dependence is reversed.
>
> Heidegger's master claim at the close of his Chapter 3 analysis runs:
>
> > "Insofar as the πάθη (*pathē* "passions") are not merely an annex of psychical processes, but are rather *the ground out of which speaking arises, and which what is expressed grows back into*, the πάθη, for their part, are *the basic possibilities in which being-there itself is primarily oriented toward itself*, finds itself. The primary being-oriented, the illumination of its being-in-the-world is not a *knowing*, but rather a *finding-oneself*." (GA 18, p. 176)
>
> And on the relation of *δόξα* (*doxa* "opinion/belief/yes-saying") to the πάθη at GA 18, p. 94:
>
> > "*Φαντασία* (*phantasia* "presenting-itself, having-present") is *τὸ φαίνεσθαι* (*to phainesthai* "the self-showing"), the simple having-present of something. *δόξα* (*doxa*) is no longer a seeing but stands at the *end* of seeing; it is a *φάσις* (*phasis* "yes-saying, asserting"). But although it is a yes, it is still not a knowing.... A *πίστις* (*pistis* "trust, conviction"), a *φάσις* (*phasis*), accompanies *δόξα* (*doxa*) itself."
>
> What is at stake. Your account places *doxa* as the *cognitive gate* through which emotion must pass; in your architectural picture, the *phantasma* deposits a latent affective valence that requires *doxa*'s propositional ratification (its *taking-as-true*) to become a determinate higher-order πάθος. The directional dependence runs: *phantasia* → *doxa* → πάθος. On Heidegger's reading, the directional dependence runs in the reverse: πάθη → λόγος (and so → *doxa*). The πάθη are the *prior orientation* in which alone *doxa* can take its yes-saying position. Without the πάθη having already disclosed the world *as mattering*, there would be nothing for *doxa* to ratify. The *λόγος*-bearing yes-saying of *doxa* presupposes the disposition that the πάθη already constitute.
>
> Three considerations clarify the divergence and what is at stake.
>
> *First*, the directional question. Your text uses *phantasma* + latent valence + *doxa*-ratification → determinate πάθος as the architectural sequence. Heidegger has πάθος-as-disposition (already disclosed) + *phantasia*-as-bringing-the-look-into-presence + *doxa*-as-yes-saying-on-the-basis-of-the-disposition. The first treats *doxa* as a *generative gate* for the higher-order emotion; the second treats *doxa* as a *supervening yes-saying* that ratifies what the disposition has already disclosed.
>
> *Second*, the textual basis. Both readings can claim Aristotelian warrant. Your reading rests on *DA* III.3, 427b21-24 (the painting passage), where bare *phantasma* "leaves the soul unaffected" and *doxa* is required for the emotion to be produced. Heidegger's reading rests on *Rhetoric* B 1, 1378a 20 sq. (the constitutive aspects), where the πάθη are presented as already-disclosive of the world's mattering, and on the structural claim that *λόγος* arises from the πάθη. The two passages may not be in direct conflict — *DA* III.3 may concern the *propositional articulation* of higher-order emotion, while *Rhet*. B 1 concerns the *prior dispositional ground*. But your architectural diagram makes *doxa* causally upstream of higher-order emotion, while Heidegger's makes it phenomenologically downstream of πάθος-as-disposition.
>
> *Third*, what is at stake philosophically. Your reading preserves the architectural cleanness of the actualization chain: emotion enters at a determinate point (the *doxa*-gate) and is generated by determinate operations on the *phantasma*. Heidegger's reading preserves the phenomenological priority of disposition over assertion: the πάθη are the *medium* in which alone any *doxa*-ratification is intelligible as ratification *of something mattering*. The two are not necessarily exclusive — your *doxa*-gate may be accommodated within Heidegger's broader thesis if "the production of higher-order *πάθη* by *doxa*" is read as the *articulation* of a prior disposition rather than its *generation*. But as stated, your text presents *doxa* as the cause of higher-order emotion; this is the formulation that diverges from Heidegger's.
>
> *Recommendation for the chapter.* This tension is productive rather than destructive. The architectural cut you draw — basic *resonant orexis* present from A₁ onward, higher-order πάθη arising only after *doxa*-ratification — is philosophically defensible and may genuinely refine Heidegger's account by introducing a structural distinction GA 18 leaves implicit. But the divergence should be marked explicitly. A footnote (or a paragraph in the [forthcoming chapter] where the Aristotelian–Heideggerian integration occurs) acknowledging that on Heidegger's reading the directional dependence runs from πάθος-as-disposition to λόγος-bearing *doxa* — and clarifying why your architectural picture nonetheless places *doxa* upstream of higher-order πάθη without reducing the πάθη to *doxa*-products — would secure the cut and clarify its standing relative to GA 18.

The individual definitions for various emotions presented in the *Rhetoric* flesh out the composite structure Aristotle identifies in *De Anima* using anger as his representative example. Each definition, as previously articulated, specifies a cognitive content, a hedonic valence, and (in most cases) a conative component. For anger, the cognitive content is the perceived slight; the conative component is the desire for revenge; and, the hedonic component is pain. For Fear, the cognitive content is the anticipated evil; the conative component — though not explicitly stated — is the desire to avoid or flee the anticipated evil; and, the hedonic accompaniment is pain or disturbance. For pity, the cognitive content is complex: it involves not only (a) the perceived or apparent suffering of another, but also (b) the judgment of undeserved suffering and (c) the additional judgment that we ourselves or someone we care about are vulnerable to similar suffering and *soon*. The conative component is implicit as Aristotle does not name a desire in the definition; rather, as I read it, pity contains an aversive motivation: a wish that such an evil *not* happen (to the sufferer, to oneself, or to one's friends/family). Accordingly, the conative element could be the desire to avoid or avert harm; and/or, a desire to aide the sufferer. The hedonic accompaniment, just as with anger, is pain. Lastly, for shame, the cognitive content is the judgment of one's own disgrace; that some act, trait, circumstance, etc. (whether already done, happening in the now, or anticipated) will cause others to look upon or judge them unfavorably. The conative component, though not explicitly stated, is easier to parse given the contrastive definition of *shamelessness*. In light of this, the conative element of shame is likely one caring about their standing ("saving face," to use a colloquial idiom), and, therefore, the desire to avoid, conceal, or escape whatever threatens them. The shameless person has the same potential cognitive content available (they could recognize the same facts as discrediting) but they lack the motivational care or concern that would make those facts harmful. The hedonic accompaniment, then, is pain or disturbance. In each of the aforementioned emotions, we sees a structured whole containing all three components. Aristotle does not treat the emotions as simple hedonic states (pain/pleasure) differentiated only by intensity; rather, they are differentiated by the specific propositional content of the evaluation that structures them.

The causal chain presented by Aristotle in *Movement of Animals* gives us the clearest illustration of the relation between psychological states and action. To reiterate: sense-perception or thinking → imagination → desire → affections → organic parts → movement.

The location of "affections" between desire and the organic parts is philosophically significant and one of the leading justifications for my differentiation between affection as*pathos* and affection as emotion or *pathe*.

While Aristotle uses affection (*pathos*) interchangeably, for the sake of explanation I must separate the two cases: affection as *pathos* refers solely to the affective valence or resonant *orexis* (good/bad, pursue/avoid) that accompanies every act of perception, and affections as emotion (*pathē*) refers strictly to the determinate, higher-order emotions like anger, fear, pity, shame, etc.

The causal chain supplied in *Movement of Animals* suggests that the emotions have a somatic-preparatory role: they are what translate desire into bodily readiness *and this is further indicated by the enmattered account and bodily accompaniment*. The nature of this readiness is as follows: "the object we pursue or avoid in the field of action is, as has been explained, the origin of movement, and upon the thought and imagination of this there necessarily follows a heating or chilling. For what is painful we avoid, what is pleasing we pursue, and anything painful or pleasing is generally speaking accompanied by a chilling and heating" (701b33-7022). The physiological changes associated with emotion are not incidental accompaniments but the mechanism by which desire is realized in bodily movement. The emotions *are* the preparation of the organic parts, a *desire realized in* physiological change. This convergence between the enmattered account of *De Anima* and the causal chain of the *Movement of Animals* demonstrates emotion's genuine causal role in the perception-to-movement sequence. The "boiling of blood around the heart" that Aristotle identifies as the material component of anger is the same phenomenon that he identifies as the "affection" that prepares the organic parts for movement in *Movement of Animals*. Furthermore, the fact Aristotle gives emotions and desire their own step within the causal chain suggests that emotion includes desire without being reducible to desire alone. In each of the emotions discussed above, the desire-component is part of the formal structure of the emotion: to define what anger *is* requires mentioning what anger *wants*. Desire (*orexis*) is the moved mover of animal locomotion (*DA* III.10, 433b10-18) — the faculty that, moved by the apparent good, moves the animal. If emotion were simply identical with desire, there would be no reason to distinguish them in the causal chain, and the placement of "affections" as a separate stage would be redundant. The ontological status of emotion as separate from distinct from desire consists precisely in its composite character: emotion is desire *together with* physiological change *structured by* cognitive content. The desire for retaliation is a component of anger, but anger is the desire for retaliation *as experienced in the boiling of blood*, in response to a perceived slight, directed at the one who has delivered it. It is this composite — note the desire alone — that prepares the organic parts for movement.

As we have already discussed, in emotion formation the role *doxa* plays in emotion reveals that emotion is not a raw feeling or mere physiological disturbance; rather, it is a state of the soul that necessarily involves, as a constitutive condition, a truth-committal cognitive act. The person who fears does not merely *feel* afraid; the person who fears *takes something to be* fearful, and it is this *taking-as* that produces the fear. Indeed, Aristotle's formulation makes the *doxastic* element the proximate cause of the emotional state, with the hedonic and physiological components following as consequences of the truth-commitment rather than preceding it. However, to reiterate, Aristotle's *doxa* is not a deliberate formation of a considered judgment arrived at through careful reasoning; it is the involuntary taking of something as being the case, a cognitive event that befalls the rational agent rather than one the agent deliberately performs. The speed with which emotion follows *doxa* — "immediately" — suggests that the *doxastic* component of emotion is closer to perceptual recognition than to discursive inference. When the rational agent "perceiving by sense that the beacon is fire, it recognizes in virtue of the general faculty of sense that it signifies an enemy, because it sees it moving" (*DA* III.7, 431b5-6), the *doxa* "this signals danger" may form almost instantaneously, and fear may follow without any perceptible interval.

In light of analyze emotion as Aristotle present it across *De Anima*, *Rhetoric*, and *Movement of Animals*, we can surmise the perception-to-movement chain of a rational animal confronting an emotionally significant object. Sense-perception (or thinking) delivers the *resonant aisthēma* (formal content) together with the *resonant orexis* (basic affective valence): the object is registered as pleasant or painful, and the soul's assertoric pursuit/avoidance orientation is engaged. This constitutes the first moment of the chain. The *phantasma* that results from perception carries this affective charge as a dispositional property — the image of the perceived object retains its valence — but the charge is latent rather than mobilizing. At this stage, the soul "remains unaffected" in the higher-order sense: there is no fear, no anger, no pity, only the basic orientation of pursuit or avoidance. The transition to genuine emotion occurs when *doxa* forms on the basis of the *phantasma*. The rational agent *takes the object to be* genuinely fearful, offensive, pitiable, or shameful and "emotion is immediately produced." The *doxa* transforms the *phantasma's* latent affective valence into a determinate emotional state: the undifferentiated "this is bad" becomes the specific "this is a destructive future evil" (fear), or "this is an undeserved slight" (anger). Each emotion specifies a particular evaluative structure, a particular conative orientation, and a particular pattern of physiological preparation. The emotion then does genuine causal work. Its physiological component — the heating, cooling, trembling, blushing, etc. associated with the emotions — prepares the organic parts for the specific kind of movement that the emotion's conative component requires. The boiling of blood in anger prepares the body for aggressive action; the cooling of blood in fear prepares the body for flight or freezing. In this way, emotion is the hinge between the cognitive evaluation (*doxa*) and the bodily movement (*kinesis*): it translates judgment into somatic readiness. The resulting desire (*orexis*) — the "moved mover" — is thus not a separate faculty that operates independently of emotion but is, in cases of emotional action, the conative component of the emotion itself. The desire for retaliation that constitutes part of anger is the *orexis* that moves the angry agent toward aggressive action. The desire to flee that constitutes part of fear is the *orexis* that moves the fearful agent toward flight. Emotion, on my reading, is the actualization of the *orektikon* capacity into a specific form of desire directed at a specific object under a specific evaluative description.

However, this structure alone does not fully encompass the role emotion plays in animal movement as Aristotle presents it. The *Rhetoric*'s definition of emotion as "judgement-modifying" entails a feedback loop: the emotion produced by *doxa* alters the conditions under which subsequent *doxai* are formed. Aristotle specifies this mechanism in *De Insomniis*: when the soul is in the grip of emotion, the "controlling sense" — the corrective faculty that normally intercepts misleading *phantasmata* before they can generate *doxa* — is "disabled" (460b3-16). *Phantasmata* that would, under normal conditions, be corrected by the rational faculty are instead taken-as true, generating further *doxai* and thereby further emotions. The result is a self-reinforcing loop: an initial *doxa* produces an initial emotion; the emotion impairs the corrective faculty; subsequent *phantasmata* pass through the gat into *doxa* unopposed; the new *doxai* produce further emotions; and so on. This recursive structure explains a range of phenomena that Aristotle recognizes: the escalation of anger through sustained rumination on the perceived slight; the intensification of fear through repeated imagining of the anticipated evil; the susceptibility of the dream, the ill, and the passionately agitated to *phantasmatic* deception. It also explains the rhetor's strategy in *Rhetoric*: by first establishing an emotional attunement in the audience, the rhetor weakens their corrective faculties and thereby makes them more susceptible to the *phantasmata* that vivid language (*energeia* "actuality/being-at-work/activity") presents to their imaginations. The loop is not a defect of the system but an intrinsic feature of the relationship between emotion and *doxa* in rational animals.

A critical question we must now ask is whether emotion is a necessary intermediate for every action or whether it is variably present depending on the character of the object and the nature of the desire involved. My reading of the texts suggests the later. Consider the drinking example Aristotle gives us in *Movement of Animals*: "For when a man is actually using perception or imagination or thought in to that for the sake of which, what he desires he does at once. For the actualizing of desire si a substitute for inquiry or thinking. I want to drink, says appetite; this is drink, says sense or imagination or thought: straightaway I drink. In this way living creatures are impelled to move and to act, and desire is the last cause of movement, and desire arises through perception or through imagination and thought. And things that desire to move and act sometimes from appetite or impulse and sometimes from wish" (701a29-b1). Here, the chain runs from appetite to action, mediated by a *phantasma* that identifies the present object as drink. No *doxa* intervenes; no emotion is mentioned; the desire is simple appetite responding to a sensory presentation. The action is, so to speak, emotionally transparent — it passes through the system without engaging the higher-order evaluative structures that produce fear, anger, or pity. By contrast, fear involves emotion as a necessary intermediate. The person who *takes the object to be* fearful does not merely desire to flee; the person *fears*, and it is the fear — the composite of cognitive evaluation, conative orientation, and physiological preparation — that specifies the form of the ensuing desire and action.

Similarly, the angry person does not merely desire to retaliate; the person is *angry*, and the anger provides the evaluative structure (the perceived slight), the hedonic coloring (the pain), and the somatic preparation (the heating of the blood) that together constitute the proximate cause of the aggressive action.

The presence of emotion, therefore, seems to be variable and tracks the distinction between simple appetitive actions and evaluatively complex actions. Where the object is straightforwardly pleasant or painful and the desire is basic appetite or aversion, emotion in the higher-order sense is not required; basic affective valence, *resonant orexis*, suffices to direct the organism toward or away from the object. Where the object engages the evaluative categories of the *Rhetoric*, emotion is a necessary intermediate that specifies the particular form of desire, the particular pattern of somatic preparation, and the particular character of the resulting action. This distinction corresponds, at least approximately, to the distinction between actions mediated by *phantasia* alone and actions mediate by *doxa*: since higher-order emotion requires *doxa*, and since *doxa* requires *logos*, emotion enters the chain specifically at the point where the rational faculties engage with the *phantasmatic* content. In the drinking case, the appetitive faculty responds to *phantasia* without *logos*; in the fear case, the rational faculty forms a *doxa* on the basis of the *phantasma*, and emotion follows.

Emotion follows not judgment (*krisis* "judgment/decision") but appearance-laden belief: e.g. that *seems* dangerous. In this sense, emotion isn't of the outcome of rational deliberation but a pre-rational movement of the soul in response to a *phantasmatic* presentation. Emotion responds to *how things appear*, not to how reason later adjudicates them. It operates, as Heidegger would later say, at the level of *Stimmung* (German: "mood, attunement") or mood: the atmospheric attunement that precedes and conditions intelligibility itself (BCAP 13-18). Aristotle confirms the internal motional character of basic affective valence and emotion when he writes that "being pained or pleased, or thinking, are movements (each of them a being-moved), and the movement is originated by the soul" (*DA* 408b5-7). Pain and pleasure are not passive accompaniments but dynamic modifications of the soul's orientation towards perceived goods and evils. They indicate how the soul is already oriented *toward* or *away* from something perceived as good or evil. The soul, in short, *moves itself* — and what we call emotion is the form this self-movement takes when shaped by particular appearances. This is precisely Aristotle's point in *Movement of Animals*: animals and humans move "for the sake of something," and all such movement arises from intellect, imagination, appetite, and desire (701a16-24), all of these are reducible to desire (*orexis*); and desire, in turn, depends on *phantasia*'s presentational content. As Nussbaum notes in her commentary, *phantasia* is indispensable because it supplies the *material* for practical reasoning and action. Emotion, therefore, is not merely a cause of later motion: emotion is a motion, the soul's kinetic alignment toward a world disclosed as mattering in a particular way.

Because *phantasia* generates appearances beyond the immediately present, Aristotle explicitly states that memory and anticipation rely on its operations. In *De Memoria* he writes that we remember when the "movement caused by the object is preserved internally" (450a20–25). To remember grief is to feel grief again; to anticipate pleasure is to feel pleasure now in relation to a not-yet. When Aristotle writes in *Rhetoric* I.xi that "both the man who remembers and the man who hopes will be attended by an imagination of what he remembers or hopes" (1370a), he articulates the temporal depth of emotional life. Emotion is not anchored to the present; it reaches into past and future by means of *phantasmatic* appearance. Fear — "a pain or disturbance due to imagining some destructive or painful evil in the future" (1382a22-23) — is paradigmatic. The feared event does not exist; the *phantasma* does. And the soul moves as though the feared thing were present. Thus, *phantasia* is not a decorative adjunct to emotion; it is the condition of possibility for the soul being-moved by absences, possibilities, appearances, and counterfactuals.

If *phantasia* supplies the appearance, *doxa* is the moment of assent: the *taking-as*. Aristotle insists that emotion follows from this involuntary assent rather than from the later activity of rational judgment. *Doxa* is therefore the hinge between appearance and affective motion. This explains several aspects of how *phantasia* leads to emotion:

- False beliefs or opinions generate real emotions: The sound mistaken for a rattlesnake produces real fear.
- Art produces emotion: One can respond fearfully or sorrowfully to *phantasmatic* representations despite knowing they are unreal.
- Emotion can contradict judgment: Knowing the rope is not a snake does not neutralize the startle. Even knowing that the rope is not a snake after the fact does not necessarily neutralize the instinctual fear felt because emotion is keyed to *appearance*, not to rational correction.

Aristotle's examples consistently demonstrate that emotion is "appearance-laden" cognition. Emotion warps the field of appearance, which in turn warps judgment. *Phantasia* thus conditions both the genesis of emotion and emotion's feedback effects on cognition.

Aristotle's rhetorical theory makes explicit the practical, world-shaping power of emotion. To persuade is not primarily to offer proofs but to reshape the listener's *phantasmatic* field. The rhetor must supply images — concrete, narrative, affectively charged — that allow the audience to see what the rhetor wants them to see. It is this imagistic seeing that grounds the subsequent formation of *doxa*, the ensuing movement of emotion, and the eventual act of judgment. The rhetorical cascade follows the actualization chain precisely: new *phantasma* → new *doxa* → new emotion → new orientation → new action. Hence Aristotle's insistence that fear, pity, and anger each have characteristic appearances. These definitions reveal that *pathos* is not merely a feeling but a *way of world-disclosure* — a way things appear when moved in a certain direction.

This is where the deep Heideggerian resonance becomes visible. Heidegger calls rhetoric "the hermeneutic of Dasein itself," the discipline in which "the self-elaboration of Dasein is expressly executed" (Basic Concepts (GA 18); Heidegger, *Heidegger and Rhetoric* ed. Gross p. 1). *Doxa* and Emotion–as *pathos*, as being-moved–was already the site where Aristotle located the fundamental dynamics through which human beings find themselves oriented in a world. Heidegger radicalizes this insight by reading *pathos* as the primordial attunement–or mood (*Stimmung*) –that constitutes the clearing in which beings can appear at all. Thus, the Aristotelian apparatus of *phantasia*, *doxa*, and *pathos* is not merely psychological: it sketches the pre-cognitive, affective, atmospheric structure of being-in-the-world itself. Because emotion is a self-motion of the soul toward or away from what appears good or bad, every emotion entails desire, orientation, and action-tendency. Emotions are therefore *teleological*: they set the direction of motion toward a perceived end. Emotion is not a barrier to action but the very medium in which purposive action takes shape. *Phantasia* is the bridge here. *Aisthēsis* (*aisthēsis* "perception/sense-perception") presents neutral stimuli; *phantasia* renders them as *something* — dangerous, insulting, pleasurable, enviable — and in doing so makes them significant. Once something is significant, the soul moves; and, when the soul moves, the body follows. **[H4]**

For Aristotle, emotion is the kinetic, affective, evaluative dimension of how the world appears to us. It is grounded in *phantasia*, shaped through involuntary belief, felt as pleasure or pain, and expressed as motion toward or away from what the soul takes to be significant. Emotion is not an add-on to rationality, nor a disruption of cognition, but the way the soul's self-movement discloses significance at all. In this sense, Aristotle's theory anticipates later phenomenological accounts—Heidegger's most explicitly—that interpret emotion as a mode of attunement, a way the world already "matters" before deliberation begins. Emotion, in its full Aristotelian sense, is nothing less than the *affective architecture of being-in-the-world*.

Aristotle argues that *phantasia* is "held to be a movement" (Aristotle, "On the Soul" 428b 11), and by further describing phantasia as an "affection" (pathos) he connects the movement produced by phantasia with that produced by affect or pathos. As he defines them, "[t]he emotions are all those feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements, and that are also attended by pain or pleasure. Such are anger, pity, fear and the like, with their opposites" ("Rhetoric" 1378a 21-23). "For all living things," as he states in *Movement of Animals*, "both move and are moved for the sake of something, so that this is the limit of all their movement-that for the sake of which" (6.13-16). As he continues, "the living creature is moved by intellect, imagination, purpose, wish, and appetite" all of which, barring intellect, are reducible to desire (6. 16-24). Accordingly, Aristotle posits that we must "admit to the full that being pained or pleased, or thinking are movements (each of them a being moved), and that the movement is originated by the soul" ("On the Soul" 408b 5-7). If Aristotle is correct and pleasure is the sensation of a particular emotion, and if "imagination" is a weakened sensation, then both the man who remembers and the man who hopes will be attended by an imagination of what he remembers or hopes" (Rhet. 1370a; I.xi.6; 117; my emphasis).

A crucial implication in this notion is the temporal range available to phantasia; for to remember is to bring images of past events, objects, persons, and scenes before the eyes in the present, while to hope or fear is to project potential images into the future based off the present situation.

When attempting to evoke rational desires, what Aristotle defines as those things "they desire to see and possess" on the basis of the listener being persuaded that such things are pleasurable (qtd. in Hawhee, 145; my emphasis), the strength of the emotional appeal depends on the rhetor's ability to engage his audience's phantasmatic capacity. Indeed, it is the capacity of phantasia to encompass and incorporate phantasmata of the past, present, and future within a discourse which generates the driving pathetic force that works to shape opinion and thereby lead to judgment.

The very possibility to evoke an affectual response in others depends on the phantasmatic capacity of the mind. This is most clearly illustrated in Aristotle's definition of fear in the *Rhetoric*: "[f]ear may be defined as a pain or disturbance due to *imagining* some destructive or painful evil in the future" (1382a 22-23; my emphasis). This further clarify how phantasmatic capacity operates to form an opinion in my example with the rattlesnake: our fear of the snake upon hearing the rattle is generated from imagining a future where we are bit. Additionally, it illustrates how discourse can activate phantasia to generate a specific emotional affect. The effect of this emotional appeal, therefore, is entirely dependent on the ability of the speaker or author to activate the phantasmatic capacity in their audience: for "we [can] act in accordance with desire, as in the case of moral weakness" (Aristotle, "On the Soul" 433a 2-3) "action is prompted by desire" (Soul 433a) and desire can be prompted by action. Or, as he states elsewhere, "And on this account thinking that one ought to go and going are virtually simultaneous, unless there be something to hinder action." "[t]he organic parts suitably prepared by the affections, these again by desire, and desire by imagination" ("Movement of Animals" 702a 15-21). Phantasia, therefore, is fundamentally involved in every action. It, as O'Gorman concludes, "forms the objects of belief and desire, even gives birth to belief and desire ... and provides the images that are to be objects of deliberative desire, ethical choice, and political judgment" (O'Gorman 34).

## Emotion: The Form of Desire Under Evaluative Disclosure

Aristotle's account of *pathos* is not the modern psychological category of inner feelings, nor is it an epiphenomenon sitting atop the rational operations of the soul. *Pathos*, on Aristotle's account, is a *movement* (κίνησις, *kinēsis* "motion/change") of the soul, a dynamic reorientation within the field of appearances that alters how the world shows up and, in so doing, alters how one is disposed to act within it. The etymology of the English term preserves what the Greek asserts: *emotion* derives through Middle French *émotion* from Latin *emovēre*—"to move out, to set in motion"—and its morphological structure (*e-* "out" + *mot-* "move" + *-ion* "the act of") names not an inner feeling but the *event* of being-moved, the soul's kinetic exposure to what matters. To undergo an emotion is, accordingly, to be displaced by the force of an appearance taken as significant—already in motion, already reoriented. What the English word encodes at the level of etymology, Aristotle articulates at the level of ontology: *pathos* is a species of κίνησις (*kinēsis* "motion"), a being-moved of the soul toward or away from perceived goods and evils.

### The Primary Definition

The primary definition of emotion in Aristotle's corpus appears not in *De Anima* but in the *Rhetoric*: "the emotions are all those feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements, and that are also attended by pain or pleasure—such as anger, pity, fear and the like, with their opposites" (*Rhetoric* II.1, 1378a20–22). Though the passage is developed in the service of rhetorical theory, its ontological weight is considerable, and the definition must anchor any systematic analysis of where emotion belongs in the causal chain from perception to animal movement. Two features of the definition are decisive. First, emotions "so change men as to affect their judgements" (κρίσεις, *kriseis* "judgments/position-takings")—they are not merely felt disturbances but judgment-modifying states, cognitive events, or at minimum events with cognitive consequences that reshape the evaluative landscape within which the person deliberates and acts. Second, emotions are *attended by* pain or pleasure; the hedonic accompaniment is precisely an accompaniment, not the emotion in its entirety.

This second feature is the hinge upon which the entire downstream analysis turns. It is what distinguishes emotion from the basic affective valence present from the first moment of perception onward—the raw pleasant-or-painful, pursue-or-avoid orientation that Aristotle locates at the level of perception itself, where "to perceive then is like bare asserting or thinking; but when the object is pleasant or painful, the soul makes a sort of affirmation or negation, and pursues or avoids the object" (*DA* III.7, 431a8–10). This basic valence I name *resonant orexis*: the organism's pre-propositional orientation toward the pleasant and away from the painful, the "why it matters" that is co-constitutive with perception and inherited as a dispositional property by the *phantasma* that perception leaves behind. It is paired, in the *aisthēma* that perception deposits, with what I name the *resonant aisthēma* proper: the formal-epistemic content of the residual motion, the "what it is" preserved in the image after the object has withdrawn. The *resonant orexis* is dispositional rather than mobilizing: it colors the perceptual field without yet specifying what kind of affective mobilization is underway.

Emotion, by contrast, is determinate. Anger, on Aristotle's account, is not undifferentiated displeasure but "desire accompanied by pain for conspicuous revenge for a conspicuous slight" (*Rhetoric* II.2, 1378a30–31). Fear is not generic aversion but "a pain or disturbance due to imagining some destructive or painful evil in the future" (II.5, 1382a21–22). Pity is not simple sorrow but "a feeling of pain at an apparent evil, destructive or painful, which befalls one who does not deserve it, and which we might expect to befall ourselves or some friend of ours" (II.8, 1385b13–16). Shame is "pain or disturbance in regard to bad things, whether present, past, or future, which seem likely to involve us in discredit" (II.6, 1383b15–17). Each emotion, in Aristotle's taxonomy, specifies three components: a cognitive content (for anger, the perceived slight; for fear, the anticipated destructive evil; for pity, the undeserved suffering; for shame, the anticipated discredit), a conative orientation (the desire to retaliate; the desire to flee; the desire to relieve or avert; the desire to conceal or avoid), and a hedonic accompaniment (pain, in each of these cases, though pleasure is admitted for anger insofar as "the thoughts dwell upon the act of vengeance"). The cases demonstrate a single thesis: emotions are not differentiated from one another by hedonic intensity but by the specific propositional content of the evaluation that structures them. It is this tripartite determinacy—cognitive, conative, hedonic—that separates emotion from the *resonant orexis* of pursuit-and-avoidance.

To mark this distinction throughout the analysis, I use *pathos* (the lowercase, singular form) for the basic affective valence of *resonant orexis* that is co-constitutive with perception from A₁ onward, and *pathē* (or "higher-order emotion") for the determinate emotional states—anger, fear, pity, shame, and their kin—that arise only after the operations upon the *phantasma* that will shortly be specified. Aristotle himself does not enforce this terminological distinction; *pathos* and *paschein* do double duty across the corpus. The distinction is nevertheless textually warranted by the contrast between *DA* III.7, 431a8–10 (the basic valence inherent to perception) and *Rhetoric* II.1, 1378a20–22 (the higher-order states that modify *kriseis*), and it is structurally load-bearing: the chain's architecture depends upon what is present from A₁ as dispositional ground being differentiated from what arises at A₃ as mobilized emotion. **[T2]**

### The Enmattered-Accounts Argument

Aristotle establishes the ontological framework for understanding emotion in the opening chapter of *De Anima*, where he argues that the affections of the soul are "enmattered accounts" (λόγοι ἔνυλοι, *logoi enuloi* "enmattered accounts/material-formal articulations") (*DA* I.1, 403a25–b19). The methodological lesson is developed through anger as the representative case. The dialectician, Aristotle observes, would define anger as "a desire for retaliation" or "a desire for returning pain for a given pain." The natural philosopher, by contrast, would define it as "a boiling of the blood and hot stuff about the heart" (403a31–b1). Neither account is sufficient on its own. The dialectician's formal account omits the matter in which the desire is realized and accordingly fails to capture the causal role that the physiological alteration plays in the emotion's operation. The natural philosopher's material account omits the form that makes the physiological alteration *this* emotion rather than some other and accordingly fails to distinguish anger from fever. The full definition, Aristotle insists, requires both: anger is the desire for retaliation *realized in* the boiling of blood around the heart. The formal and material accounts are complementary rather than competing, and the natural philosopher's task is precisely to combine them.

The implications are considerable. If anger is constitutively composite—simultaneously a cognitive evaluation (the perceived slight), a conative orientation (the desire to retaliate), and a physiological alteration (the heating of the blood)—then no analysis that reduces emotion to any one of these components can carry genuine explanatory value. Emotion is not merely judgment, though it includes judgment; it is not merely desire, though it includes desire; it is not merely a bodily change, though it is inseparable from bodily change. The enmattered-accounts argument thus imposes a structural constraint upon the placement of emotion in the actualization chain: emotion must be located at a point where cognitive, conative, and physiological processes converge, and it must be understood as something irreducible to any one of them. What the *Rhetoric* catalogues as the particular structure of each emotion, *De Anima* I.1 grounds as a methodological principle: emotion is composite in its essence. **[H3]**

### The Production Condition: *Doxa* as Gate

The enmattered account specifies what emotion *is*; it does not specify *when* emotion arises. The production condition is supplied by the passage at *DA* III.3, 427b21–24, which distinguishes *phantasia* from *doxa* on an affective basis: "when we think something to be fearful or threatening, emotion is immediately produced, and so too with what is encouraging; but when we merely imagine we remain as unaffected (ἀπαθῶς ἔχομεν, *apathōs echomen* "we have ourselves un-affectedly/we are without πάθος") as persons who are looking at a painting of some dreadful or encouraging scene." The passage does double duty. It establishes that the bare *phantasma*—the image entertained at will, the scenario merely imagined—leaves the soul unaffected in the higher-order sense: there is *resonant orexis*, the dispositional orientation of pursuit or avoidance that the *phantasma* inherits from perception, but there is no fear, no anger, no pity. It establishes, correlatively, that what produces the higher-order *pathē* is *doxa*: the involuntary belief that the object is genuinely fearful, the truth-commitment through which the *phantasma*'s taking-as becomes the soul's taking-as-true. And it establishes that the production is immediate, admitting of no perceptible interval between the formation of the *doxa* and the onset of emotion (εὐθὺς πάσχομεν, *euthus paschomen* "immediately we are affected/at once we undergo πάθος").

*Doxa*, accordingly, serves as a cognitive gate through which emotion must pass to enter the actualization chain. The involuntariness of *doxa* (*DA* III.3, 427b17–21) is precisely what gives emotion its distinctive phenomenological character as something that befalls the agent rather than something the agent elects to undergo. One does not choose to be afraid; one finds oneself afraid because one has involuntarily *taken something to be* threatening. The taking-as that the *phantasma* accomplishes, ratified as taking-as-true by *doxa*, converts the *phantasma*'s latent *pathos* into the determinate *pathē* that mobilizes the soul. The undifferentiated "this is bad" of *resonant orexis* becomes the specific "this is a destructive future evil" of fear, or the specific "this is an undeserved slight" of anger. Each emotion specifies a particular evaluative structure, a particular conative orientation, and a particular pattern of physiological preparation, and the specification is accomplished not by *phantasia* alone but by the *doxa* that ratifies what the *phantasma* has taken the object to be.

### Somatic Preparation and the Causal Chain

The *Movement of Animals* supplies the clearest statement of emotion's causal function: "the organic parts are suitably prepared by the affections, these again by desire, and desire by imagination" (*MA* 702a17–19). The placement of the affections (πάθη, *pathē* "passions") between desire and the organic parts is philosophically significant. It locates emotion precisely at the point where desire is translated into bodily readiness—where the conative orientation that desire supplies acquires the somatic configuration that will carry it into action. The physiological changes associated with the emotions are accordingly not incidental accompaniments but the mechanism through which desire is realized in bodily movement. Aristotle is explicit: "the object we pursue or avoid in the field of action is...the origin of movement, and upon the thought and imagination of this there necessarily follows a heating or chilling. For what is painful we avoid, what is pleasing we pursue, and anything painful or pleasing is generally speaking accompanied by a chilling and heating" (*MA* 701b33–702a2). The emotions *are* the preparation of the organic parts—a desire realized in physiological change, pattern-matched to the specific action that the emotion's cognitive content discloses as to-be-pursued or to-be-avoided.

The convergence between the enmattered account of *DA* I.1 and the causal articulation of *MA* 702a15–21 confirms emotion's genuine causal role in the perception-to-movement sequence. The "boiling of blood and hot stuff about the heart" that anger is, on the natural philosopher's definition, is the same phenomenon that the *MA* names as the affection that prepares the organic parts for aggressive action. The formal account (desire for retaliation) and the material account (boiling of blood) specify what a single phenomenon is under two *logoi*; the *MA* specifies what that phenomenon does within the causal chain that issues in movement. Thus the boiling of blood in anger prepares the body for aggression; the cooling of blood in fear prepares it for flight or freezing (*MA* 701b16–32); the trembling and cardiac acceleration that attend the imagining of something fearful configure the organic parts for the specific locomotor act that the emotion's conative component requires.

That Aristotle gives emotions and desire separate moments within the causal chain suggests, furthermore, that emotion includes desire without being reducible to desire alone. In each of the cases catalogued above, the desire-component is part of the formal structure of the emotion: to define what anger *is* requires naming what anger *wants*. Desire (*orexis*) is the moved mover of animal locomotion (*DA* III.10, 433b10–18)—the faculty that, moved by the apparent good, moves the animal. If emotion were simply identical with desire, the chain's differentiation of "affections" from "desire" would be redundant. The ontological status of emotion as distinct from desire consists precisely in its composite character: emotion is desire *together with* physiological change *structured by* cognitive content. The desire for retaliation is a component of anger, but anger is the desire for retaliation as experienced in the boiling of blood, in response to a perceived slight, directed at the one who has delivered it. It is this composite—not the desire alone—that prepares the organic parts for movement. Within the architecture of the actualization chain, this places the somatic-preparatory function of emotion *internal* to M₃ → M₄: the *MA*'s placement of affections between desire and the organic parts names a functionally distinguishable moment within the *orektikon* motion rather than a separate macro-stage in the A₀–A₄ skeleton. What the chain requires at M₃ → M₄ is the conversion of *orexis* into the specific form of desire that the emotion's cognitive and conative structure dictates, and it is the affections—the physiological component of the emotion-desire composite—that accomplish this conversion by configuring the body for the determinate action the emotion calls for.

### Variable Presence

Emotion is not a universal stage of the actualization chain but is variably present, entering the sequence only when the content of *doxa* engages the evaluative categories that structure higher-order *pathē*. Three cases must be distinguished. The first is simple appetitive action, in which desire responds to *phantasia* without doxastic mediation. Aristotle supplies the canonical example in the *Movement of Animals*: "I want to drink, says appetite; this is drink, says sense or imagination: straightaway I drink" (*MA* 701a32–33). Here the chain runs from appetite through a *phantasma* that identifies the present object as drink, and the action follows without any engagement of *doxa* or higher-order emotion. The *resonant orexis* inherited from perception suffices to orient desire toward the pleasant object; no evaluatively complex *doxa* intervenes, and no fear, anger, or pity mediates the image and the act.

The second case is habitual-rational action, in which *doxa* is operative but higher-order emotion is by-passed. The practical syllogism that Aristotle develops at *MA* 701a7–25 proceeds from a universal premise—"this kind of man is an enemy," "all sweet things are to be tasted"—conjoined with a particular perception or *phantasma* ("this kind of man," "this is sweet") to issue in action. The universal premise is itself a settled *doxa* held as a *hexis*, and the syllogism can fire from the settled belief without the evaluative complexity that higher-order emotion requires. Long-habituated actions, in particular, may pass through the doxastic gate so rapidly and with such settled content that no emotional mobilization intervenes between the particular judgment and the act. The animal acts from a committed belief, but the belief's content does not disclose an evaluatively complex object that would engage the categories of the *Rhetoric*'s *pathē*.

The third case is evaluatively complex action, in which *doxa* discloses something as fearful, offensive, pitiable, or shameful and the corresponding *pathē* is produced immediately. Here the person who takes the object to be fearful does not merely desire to flee; the person *fears*, and it is the fear—the composite of cognitive evaluation, conative orientation, and physiological preparation—that specifies the form of the ensuing desire and action. Similarly the angry person does not merely desire to retaliate; the person is *angry*, and the anger provides the evaluative structure (the perceived slight), the hedonic coloring (the pain, and the concomitant pleasure of anticipated vengeance), and the somatic preparation (the heating of the blood) that together constitute the proximate cause of the aggressive action.

The three cases correspond, approximately, to a progression along the axis of evaluative complexity. Where the object is straightforwardly pleasant or painful and the desire is basic appetite or aversion, *resonant orexis* suffices and emotion in the higher-order sense is not required. Where the object is processed through settled *doxa* but does not engage the evaluative categories of fear, anger, pity, or shame, *doxa* mediates without emotional mobilization. Where the object engages those categories, higher-order *pathē* is a necessary intermediate that specifies the particular form of desire, the particular pattern of somatic preparation, and the particular character of the resulting action. It is the third case that the chain's architecture must most carefully accommodate, for it is here that the full composite structure—cognitive, conative, physiological—performs its work.

### The Feedback Loop

The *Rhetoric*'s definition of emotion as judgment-affecting (*Rhetoric* II.1, 1378a20–22) entails a structural feature that the linear chain cannot fully capture: a feedback loop through which emotion, once produced, alters the conditions under which subsequent *doxai* are formed. Aristotle specifies the mechanism in the *De Insomniis*. "We are easily deceived respecting the operations of sense-perception when we are excited by emotions," he observes, "and different persons according to their different emotions; for example, the coward when excited by fear, the amorous person by the object of his desire; so that, with but little resemblance to go upon, the former thinks he sees his foes approaching, the latter, that he sees the object of his desire; and the more deeply one is under the influence of the emotion, the less similarity is required to give rise to these impressions" (*De Insomniis* 460b3–11). The controlling sense—the corrective faculty that in ordinary waking cognition intercepts misleading *phantasmata* before they can generate *doxa*—is impaired under the sway of active emotion, and *phantasmata* that would normally be corrected are instead taken as true, generating further *doxai* and thereby further emotions.

The result is a self-reinforcing loop. An initial *doxa* produces an initial emotion; the emotion impairs the corrective faculty; subsequent *phantasmata* pass through the gate into *doxa* unopposed; the new *doxai* produce further emotions; and the process continues until some external intervention, some exhaustion of the somatic preparation, or some recovery of the corrective faculty breaks the recursion. The structure explains a range of phenomena that Aristotle recognizes: the escalation of anger through sustained rumination on the perceived slight; the intensification of fear through repeated imagining of the anticipated evil; the susceptibility of the dreamer, the ill, and the passionately agitated to phantasmatic deception (*De Insomniis* 460b3–16; 461b3–8). It also explains the rhetor's strategy in the *Rhetoric*: by first establishing an emotional attunement in the audience—pity for the client, indignation toward the opponent, fear of the projected consequence—the rhetor weakens the audience's corrective faculties and thereby makes them more susceptible to the *phantasmata* that vivid language (ἐνέργεια, *energeia* "actuality/being-at-work/activity") presents to their imaginations. The loop is not a defect of the psychological system but an intrinsic feature of the relationship between emotion and *doxa* in rational animals.

It should be noted that the feedback loop, as developed here, is an interpretive reconstruction rather than a doctrine Aristotle states in these terms. The *De Anima* passage at 427b21–24 establishes that *doxa* produces emotion immediately; the *De Insomniis* passage at 460b3–16 establishes that emotional states permit *phantasmata* to be taken as true when the controlling faculty is impaired; the *Rhetoric* passage at 1378a20–22 establishes that emotions modify *kriseis*. That these passages describe aspects of a single recursive mechanism is a plausible but not indisputable interpretive claim; each passage addresses, strictly speaking, a different phenomenon. The synthesis is, however, philosophically productive, and the linear A₀ → A₄ notation of the chain cannot fully represent it. A complete topological representation would require a return path from the emotion node at A₃ back to the gatekeeping condition within M₂ → M₃.

### Emotion's Function in the Chain

What the chain requires at M₃ → M₄, under evaluatively complex conditions, is the conversion of *orexis* into a determinate form of desire directed at a specific object under a specific evaluative description. Emotion is precisely this conversion. Without it, the chain would have a generic moved mover (desire, set in motion by the apparent good) but no mechanism to specify *which* form of desire corresponds to *which* disclosure of the apparent good. The desire to flee is not the desire to retaliate; the desire to relieve is not the desire to conceal. Each determinate conative orientation is individuated by the cognitive structure of the emotion that specifies it, and each draws upon a determinate pattern of somatic preparation—the cooling of fear, the heating of anger, the sympathetic pain of pity—that configures the body for the action the emotion calls for.

Emotion, then, does not precede desire as a separate stage within the chain; emotion is the form in which desire is actualized when *doxa* has disclosed evaluatively complex content. Fear is not something that happens before the desire to flee; fear *is* the desire to flee insofar as it is structured by the cognitive evaluation of an anticipated destructive evil and experienced in the somatic alteration of chilled blood. Anger is not something that happens before the desire to retaliate; anger *is* the desire to retaliate insofar as it is structured by the perception of an unjustified slight and experienced in the somatic alteration of heated blood. The *Rhetoric*'s definitions confirm this composite structure: each emotion is defined as a desire of a certain kind, attended by pain or pleasure, directed at a certain kind of object, under a certain evaluative description. The three-factor schema of *DA* III.10 is preserved—*orexis* remains the moved mover, the realizable good remains the unmoved originator, the animal via its bodily instrument remains that which is moved—but the moved mover acquires, in the evaluatively complex case, an internal complexity that the schema's original formulation leaves unspecified. Emotion mobilizes this internal complexity. It is the actualization of the *orektikon* capacity into a specific form of desire directed at a specific object under a specific evaluative description, the form in which the soul's being-moved becomes the body's being-moved, and the mechanism through which the world's disclosure as mattering in a certain way becomes the animal's motion in the corresponding direction.

### *Phantasia* in Every Action

The foregoing account places *phantasia* at the centre of every moment of the chain, and its centrality deserves explicit statement in closing. Aristotle defines *phantasia* as "a movement resulting from actual perception" (*DA* III.3, 429a1–2), a residual motion that persists after the object has withdrawn and, by persisting, recombining, and re-presenting, supplies the condition for emotional response in the absence of immediate stimuli. Remembering, hoping, fearing, pitying: each is a case of the soul being-moved by a *phantasma* of what is not-present but which nevertheless appears *as if* present. "Both the man who remembers and the man who hopes," Aristotle observes, "will be attended by an imagination of what he remembers or hopes" (*Rhetoric* I.11, 1370a28–30). The temporal depth of emotional life is accordingly supplied by *phantasia*: the feared evil does not yet exist, yet the *phantasma* of it does, and the soul moves as though the feared thing were present. Fear—"a pain or disturbance due to imagining some destructive or painful evil in the future" (*Rhetoric* II.5, 1382a21–22)—is paradigmatic, for it names the emotion whose object is definitionally an anticipated *phantasma* rather than a present sensible.

The centrality of *phantasia* extends beyond the temporal horizon of emotion to the very possibility of practical reasoning and rhetorical persuasion. Nussbaum observes that *phantasia* is indispensable because it supplies the images upon which deliberative *phantasia* operates when it synthesizes alternatives into unrealized possibilities and measures them against a single standard.[^20] Hawhee demonstrates, correlatively, that rhetorical persuasion depends on the rhetor's ability to activate the audience's phantasmatic capacity—to supply images concrete, narrative, and affectively charged enough to allow the audience to see what the rhetor wants them to see (Hawhee, 145). The imagistic seeing that rhetoric induces grounds the subsequent formation of *doxa*, the ensuing movement of emotion, and the eventual act of judgment. The rhetorical cascade follows the actualization chain precisely: new *phantasma* → new *doxa* → new emotion → new orientation → new action.

[^20]: Nussbaum, *The Fragility of Goodness*, on *phantasia*'s role as the material supplier of practical reasoning; see also Nussbaum's commentary on the *De Motu Animalium*.

O'Gorman captures the synthesis: *phantasia* "forms the objects of belief and desire, even gives birth to belief and desire…and provides the images that are the objects of deliberative desire, ethical choice, and political judgment" (O'Gorman, 34). Every action, on Aristotle's account, is mediated by *phantasia*-supplied appearance—whether the immediate *phantasma* that identifies the present object as drink, or the settled *phantasma* that informs the universal premise of a practical syllogism, or the projected *phantasma* that deliberative reasoning synthesizes into an anticipated possibility, or the vivid *phantasma* that rhetorical ἐνέργεια (*energeia* "actuality/being-at-work") induces in the audience. Emotion enters the chain wherever such an appearance discloses evaluatively complex content that *doxa* ratifies as true, and it enters as the form desire takes under that disclosure. What *phantasia* presents, *doxa* commits to; what *doxa* commits to, emotion mobilizes; what emotion mobilizes, action completes. Emotion is not, accordingly, an add-on to rationality, nor a disruption of cognition, but the way the soul's self-movement discloses significance at the precise point where significance must become motion. **[H5]**

> **[H5] Heideggerian Expansion**
>
> The closing claim — that *phantasia* is at the centre of every moment of the chain — finds its strongest philosophical defense in Heidegger's GA 18 §18c reading of *De Anima* A 4, 408b 11 sqq.:
>
> > "If, however, even *νοεῖν* (*noein* "thinking/intellection") [the thorough deliberating of a matter, when I do not have it perceptually present] is something like *φαντασία* (*phantasia*) or cannot be without *φαντασία*, then thinking too cannot be without participation in the context of the entire life of a human being.... *Φαντασία* (*phantasia*) is the ground for *νοεῖν* (*noein*). Insofar as *νόησις* (*noēsis* "the activity of intellection") is the highest possibility for the being of human beings, the entire being of human beings is determined so that it must be apprehended as the *bodily being-in-the-world* of human beings." (GA 18, p. 134)
>
> The convergence with your claim is structurally complete. *Three points of amplification.*
>
> *First*, your "*phantasia* at the centre of every moment of the chain" is, on Heidegger's reading, not just an architectural observation but the philosophical key that recovers Aristotle from the Cartesian tradition. If φαντασία grounds even νόησις — the highest cognitive activity — then the full reach of human being is bodily-disclosive through and through, and there is no level of cognition that can be analyzed apart from its φαντασία-grounded bodily condition. Your formulation "the way the soul's self-movement discloses significance at the precise point where significance must become motion" is the existential-analytic name for what Heidegger's φαντασία-as-ground-of-νοεῖν thesis ontologically establishes.
>
> *Second*, the rhetorical cascade you specify — *phantasma* → *doxa* → emotion → orientation → action — is consistent with Heidegger's reading of the πάθη as ground of λόγος when the chain is read in the directionality your text supplies (from *phantasia* outward toward action). Heidegger's directionality (πάθη ground λόγος, hence ground *doxa*) does not contradict yours; it concerns the *prior question* of *what makes the cascade possible at all*. The cascade you describe presupposes that *phantasia* can disclose something as evaluatively complex; this presupposition is what Heidegger's reading of πάθη-as-disposition supplies. Your cascade describes how the actualization unfolds; Heidegger's analysis describes the dispositional ground from which any such unfolding is intelligible. The two analyses are layered, not opposed.
>
> *Third*, Nussbaum's claim that φαντασία "supplies the *material* for practical reasoning" — which you cite — is exactly aligned with Heidegger's reading. Heidegger insists that φαντασία is not "imagination" in the modern psychological sense but *τὸ ποιεῖν παρόν* (*to poiein paron* "the making-present"), the bodily-disclosive holding-in-presence of what is not now sensorily given. As "the bodily ground of νοεῖν," φαντασία is the structural condition under which there can be *materials* of practical reasoning at all — whether the immediate *phantasma* of "this is drink," the settled *phantasma* of the practical syllogism's universal premise, or the vivid *phantasma* of rhetorical *ἐνέργεια*. Your closing synthesis — "what *phantasia* presents, *doxa* commits to; what *doxa* commits to, emotion mobilizes; what emotion mobilizes, action completes" — is, on Heidegger's reading, the architectural specification of how the bodily disclosure that φαντασία already accomplishes runs through the determinate stages of the actualization chain.
>
> The deepening offered here is one of *philosophical foundation*: GA 18 supplies the ontological reading of φαντασία on which your architectural specification depends, and the convergence of your account with Heidegger's at this final juncture is, philosophically, the convergence of an actualization-chain analysis with the existential-phenomenological analysis of bodily disclosure that the chain presupposes.
