Orienting Thesis of §17
§17 is the ontological pivot of Chapter 3 of the First Part. Having shown in §15–16 that ἦθος (ēthos, “character”)
and πάθος (pathos, “being-affected”) function as πίστεις (pisteis, “means of credence”) in Aristotle’s Rhetoric,
Heidegger now argues that the proper grasp of πάθος (pathos) requires a prior grasp of ἕξις (hexis, “having,”
“composure,” “being-composed-as-to”). His thesis: ἕξις (hexis) is not a “habit” in the ordinary moralistic
sense, but a fundamental concept of being—the determination of the genuineness of being-there in a moment
of being-composed as to something (p. 119). The four senses of ἔχειν (echein, “to have”) in Metaphysics Δ 23 all
converge on a single ontological structure: being after a definite being-possibility, or its negation. Ἀρετή (aretē,
“virtue,” “excellence”) is then articulated as ἕξις προαιρετική (hexis proairetikē, “a being-composed in the abil‐
ity-to-resolve-oneself”), structured as μεσότης (mesotēs, “mean”) and oriented toward καιρός (kairos, “the
right moment”). The result is an analysis of human being-there as temporally cultivated composure—a 1924
anticipation of the temporality and historicality of Being and Time.

§17a. Ἔχειν (echein) and Ἕξις (hexis)
pp. 116–119

The Methodological Opening
Heidegger transitions from §16 by carrying forward a decisive insight: πάθος (pathos) “already has within itself
the relation to ἕξις (hexis)” (p. 116). The two concepts “lend themselves to being characterized by Aristotle as
fundamental concepts of being.” Πάθος (pathos) is indicated as a being-concept since πάσχειν (paschein, “to un‐
dergo”)—in contrast to ποιεῖν (poiein, “to do”)—represents a basic aspect of the analysis of κίνησις (kinēsis,
“motion”), of being in the sense of being-moved. Ἕξις (hexis) refers back to ἔχειν (echein, “to have”)—and Ar‐
istotle himself recognizes ἔχειν (echein) as a mode of being, as is shown by the fact that it appears among the
ten categories.
“Ἔχειν (echein) is the being-structure to be set forth in the two salient phenomena, πάθος (pathos) and
ἕξις (hexis).” (p. 116)

The Four Senses of Ἔχειν (echein) in Metaphysics Δ 23
Aristotle says, by way of introduction, that τὸ ἔχειν λέγεται πολλαχῶς (to echein legetai pollachōs, “‘having’ is
said in many ways”)—but the meanings are “not an arbitrary jumble” (p. 116): they relate to a basic meaning
that becomes visible by working through the individual senses.
1. Ἄγειν κατὰ τὴν αὑτοῦ φύσιν ἢ κατὰ τὴν αὑτοῦ
ὁρμήν
(agein kata tēn hautou physin ē kata tēn hautou hormēn, “leading according to one’s own nature or impulse”)

“τὸ ἄγειν κατὰ τὴν αὑτοῦ φύσιν ἢ κατὰ τὴν αὑτοῦ ὁρμήν, διὸ λέγεται πυρετός τε ἔχειν τὸν ἄνθρωπον καὶ οἱ
τύραννοι τὰς πόλεις καὶ τὴν ἐσθῆτα οἱ ἀμπεχόμενοι” (Met. Δ23, 1023a 8)

Translation: “leading according to its own fully determined possibility of being-there—φύσις (physis,
‘nature’)—or according to the impetus that is lying within the beings in question as such.” Heidegger’s
examples (Aristotle’s): “fever has the person [sickness has attacked him ‘whom it has’ or ‘whom it has seized’];
the tyrants have the towns [they rule over them]; the ones who are dressed have clothes (ὄν).” This first sense is
ἔχειν (echein) in the sense of ἄγειν (agein, “leading,” “directing”)—directing according to φύσις (physis) or
ὁρμή (hormē, “impulse,” “impetus”).
2. Ἐν ᾧ ἂν ὑπάρχῃ ὡς δεκτικῷ
(en hō an hyparchē hōs dektikō, “in which something is present as in that which is receptive”)

“ἐν ᾧ ἂν ὑπάρχῃ ὡς δεκτικῷ, οἷον ὁ χαλκὸς ἔχει τὸ εἶδος τοῦ ἀνδριάντος καὶ τὴν νόσον τὸ σῶμα” (Met. Δ23,
1023a 11)

Translation: “The metal has the look of a statue [has the look, is a statue]. The body has the illness [it is sick].”
The more precise determination of this sense of having is being: in itself something is present, “for which
being-present the being in question itself has the readiness (δεκτικόν, dektikon)” (p. 117). The metal is determ‐
ined as metal on account of the δεκτικόν (dektikon, “the receptive,” “the able-to-take-in”); it is determined in
its being such that it can become a statue.
Heidegger now makes a crucial conceptual disambiguation:
“The metal is determined as ὕλη (hylē, ‘matter’). In this context, ὕλη (hylē) does not mean an indeterm‐
inate ‘material,’ but rather a positive character of a mode of being-there. Being in preparedness for … is a
positive determination of a being. Having means nothing other than being the wherein of a beingpresent of something out of preparedness.” (p. 117)

This is one of the most ontologically significant passages in §17: ὕλη (hylē) is not “stuff” awaiting form—it is
being-in-preparedness, an active mode of being-there. [Note: this positive sense of ὕλη (hylē) will return, in
transformed shape, in §18d, where ὕλη (hylē) of the πάθη [pathē] is identified with σῶμα (sōma, “body,” corpor‐
eality).]

Translation: “Pillars hold, have the weight lying upon them, and, as the poets say, Atlas holds the vault of
heaven”: having in the sense of holding, and indeed as κωλύειν (kōlyein, “holding off”)—“holding off another
being, hindering it from being as it would like to be according to its own being, ‘according to its genuine
ὁρμή (hormē, ‘impulse’).’” The ὁρμή (hormē) of the weight is to fall downward; the vault of heaven “has the
tendency to fall down upon the earth.” Having in this sense of holding is the holding off of another from its
determinate being-possibility, which lies in its ὁρμή (hormē), is the συνέχον (synechon, “holding-together”).
Συνεχές (syneches), “continuum,” “constancy,” is “a basic aspect of the being of things moved” (Physics, Book
5).

The Common Ontological Structure
“These four kinds of ἔχειν (echein) always mark beings with the being-character of being after a definite
being-possibility, or its negation, which, in the case of negation, is the same as that of holding off some‐
thing from being genuinely as it would like to be.” (p. 118)

It is no accident, Heidegger notes, that Aristotle says in conclusion: καὶ τὸ ἕν τινι δὲ εἶναι ὁμοτρόπως λέγεται καὶ
ἑπομένως τῷ ἔχειν (kai to en tini de einai homotropōs legetai kai hepomenōs tō echein, Met. Δ23, 1023a 21)
—“Having is said in the same way as being-in-something.” Ἑπομένως (hepomenōs, “following along”): “this
meaning of being-in-something is already co-given with having; the character of having and being had has
that of being-in-something” (p. 118).

It is therefore justifiable, Heidegger argues, that ἔχειν (echein) appears among the categories alongside κεῖσθαι
(keisthai, “being-positioned”)—and that ἕξις (hexis) is to be elucidated as a mode of being-there. Aristotle in

Translation: “Ἕξις (hexis) is the ἐνέργεια (energeia, ‘being-at-work,’ ‘actuality’), the genuine there, the beingpresent of the having and of what is had” (p. 118). The there is related to having—having as the having of what
is having and of what is had. Within this being-context, ἕξις (hexis) means the genuine being-present of
having as such.
The Μεταξύ (metaxy, “the between”)
“ὅταν γὰρ τὸ μὲν ποιῇ τὸ δὲ ποιῆται, ἔστι ποίησις μεταξύ: οὕτω καὶ τοῦ ἔχοντος ἐσθῆτα καὶ τῆς ἐχομένης
ἐσθῆτος ἐστι μεταξὺ ἕξις” (Met. Δ20, 1022b 5)

Translation: “When the one does something, the other is done; thus the doing as such is the μεταξύ (metaxy),
the between. There is also a between in the case of having clothes on, having-on on the one side, the clothes
that are put on on the other side.” The having-on as such is the ἕξις (hexis). This having is something ultimate:
nothing more can be had on its part—Aristotle observes that we cannot say that the having of clothes leads to
a new being-determination, “but rather simply the there, the being-present.” The clothes “are what consti‐
tutes the genuine there of the clothes,” in being put on, when they reach their τέλος (telos, “end,” “comple‐
tion”).

Ἕξις (hexis) as Διάθεσις (diathesis) and Τάξις (taxis): Metaphysics Δ 19
Aristotle further characterizes ἕξις (hexis) as διάθεσις καθ᾽ ἣν ἢ εὖ ἢ κακῶς διάκειται (diathesis kath’ hēn ē eu ē
kakōs diakeitai, Met. Δ20, 1022b 10)—“an allotment according to which something is well or badly disposed.”
Διάθεσις in Chapter 19 is having as a τάξις (taxis, “allotment,” “ordering”): “an allotment that has the
character of θέσις (thesis, ‘positing’)”—a posited allotment, “not a merely accidental being-thrown-together,
but a being-posited.” Διακεῖσθαι (diakeisthai) is related to μεταβάλλειν (metaballein, “changing-over”),
“which happens through the πάθη (pathē)” (p. 119).
“The ἕξις (hexis) as διάθεσις (diathesis), as τάξις (taxis), springs from προαίρεσις (proairesis, ‘resolution,’
‘choice’): the proper finding-oneself in the being-allotted of the moment.” (p. 119)

The Culminating Definition of §17a
“Ἕξις (hexis) is the determination of the genuineness of being-there in a moment of being-composed as to
something: the various ἕξεις (hexeis) as the various modes of being able to be composed. Ἕξις (hexis) is, in
an entirely fundamental way, the being-determination of genuine being, here in relation to human
πρᾶξις (praxis, ‘action’). Πρᾶξις (praxis) is characterized through ἀρετή (aretē), and ἀρετή (aretē) is
characterized as ἕξις προαιρετική (hexis proairetikē). Πρᾶξις (praxis), as the how of being-in-the-world,
appears here as the being-context that we can also designate in another sense as existence.” (p. 119)

The orientation toward καιρός (kairos, “the right moment”) emerges directly:
“Being-composed is not something optional and indeterminate, for in ἕξις (hexis) lies the primary ori‐
entation toward the καιρός (kairos): ‘I am there, come what may!’ This being-there, being-on-the-alert
in one’s situation, in relation to its matter, characterizes ἕξις (hexis).” (p. 119)

The Hermeneutic Function of Ἕξις (hexis)
Heidegger summarizes: ἕξις (hexis) plays a fundamental role in the Aristotelian analysis of being but also
becomes important for another basic determination—Aristotle discusses στέρησις (sterēsis, “privation”) in op‐
position to ἕξις (hexis) and in detail with κίνησις (kinēsis). The task is to understand the πάθη (pathē) as those
determinations that “characterize the audience in a definite situation” (p. 120). The audience is in a definite
situation over against the speaker; this situation becomes co-determinative of how the audience understands.
The πάθη (pathē) are topics insofar as they are co-decisive for the manner and mode of λέγειν (legein, “speak‐
ing”); the λόγος (logos) has its basis in the πάθη (pathē) themselves.
“To take hold of what is meant by πάθη (pathē) in the right way, we are choosing a roundabout path
toward ἕξις (hexis), by way of a general hermeneutic guide.” (p. 120)

Three Questions for Every Πάθος (pathos): Rhetoric Β 1, 1378a 24
Aristotle, Heidegger notes, “considers the affectus in three respects” (p. 121):
1. Πῶς διακείμενοι εἰσίν (pōs diakeimenoi eisin): “How do we find ourselves genuinely? What sort is our beingin-the-world, when we are in a rage, when we are in fear, when we feel pity?”
2. Ποίαις (poiais): “About what do we get angry, lose composure?”
3. Ἐπὶ ποίοις (epi poiois): “In relation to whom, in encountering which sort of human beings, are we there in
this way?”
The three questions disclose “the basic structure of the πάθη (pathē)”: the orientation toward being-with-oneanother of being-there as being-in-the-world (p. 121). [This threefold structure is the proto-form of the
threefold structure of attunement (Stimmung) in Being and Time §29: how one finds oneself, what one is
attuned to, with respect to whom.]

Ἕξις (hexis) and Ἀρετή (aretē): Four Basic Aspects of Being-There
Heidegger now turns to Nicomachean Ethics Book 2, Chapters 1–5: “only that which is most important for
demonstrating ἕξις (hexis) to you, and at the same time, for clarifying a basic concept of the Nicomachean
Ethics, the μεσότης (mesotēs)” (p. 121). The crucial preliminary disambiguation:
“Μεσότης (mesotēs) is not some kind of ‘mediocrity,’ not a determination of human actions in which it
would amount to mediocrity, not a so-called ‘bourgeois morality,’ not a principle of ‘ranking values’;
but rather a basic relation to ἕξις (hexis), and so to the being-there of human beings, to πρᾶξις
(praxis), and so to the καιρός (kairos). The Nicomachean Ethics is altogether different from the ethics of
a mediocre averageness and from the conventional.” (p. 121)

From the connection between ἕξις (hexis) and ἀρετή (aretē), “four basic aspects of being-there result” (pp. 121–
122):
1. Πρᾶξις (praxis) is concern. “Πρᾶξις (praxis), ‘action,’ concern is in itself the concern of the being-there
which is concerned. In being involved in the world, in dealing with it, in occupying oneself with other
human beings, being-there itself, which is involved in this way, is concerned with itself, with its being.
Being-there as concern is care about itself, for the most part inexplicitly. This basic phenomenon is hidden
in the concept of ἕξις (hexis)—ἕξις as the having of something, the mode of being in having, the mode of
being-positioned in relation to what is had.” [proto-Sorge]
2. Particularity. “In ἕξις (hexis), being-there will show itself more precisely in its particularity. The being of
human beings, human being as being-there, is particular, at the moment: ἕξις (hexis) is a being-composed
of being-there, oriented toward the moment.” [proto-Jemeinigkeit]
3. Being-oriented toward the moment within “more or less.” “This being-composed, this being-oriented
toward the moment, is the sort of possibility that has seized being-there itself on the basis of its particular
situation. In an average way and for the most part, being-there stays within the degrees of ‘more or less,’
too much and too little.”
4. Self-cultivation in being-there itself. “On the basis of this three-fold basic structure, it is also evident that
ἕξις (hexis) is a basic determination of being-there itself, that the γένεσις (genesis) of this ἕξις (hexis), the
manner and mode in which being-there itself comes to a being-composed in relation to itself, can have
the opportunity and the type of its cultivation only, again, in being-there itself. Being-there must, for it‐
self, take the opportunity to cultivate this being-composed as a possibility.”

Ἀρετή (aretē) as Mode of Being-There, Not Property
“Ἀρετή (aretē) as ἕξις (hexis) is not a property, not a possession brought to being-there from without,
but is rather a mode of being-there itself. We are encountering once again, as always, the peculiar
category of the how. Ἀρετή (aretē) is a how of being-there, not as a fixed property, but rather as the how
of being-there determined by its being, characterized by temporality, by the stretching across time. For
this reason, ἀρετή (aretē) is and comes to be δι᾽ ἔθους (di’ ethous), ‘through habit’.” (p. 122; cit. NE Β 1,
1103a 25)

The possibilities of being-composed are graspable “only by way of undergoing various situations involving
risk” (p. 122). The opportunity of cultivating this how of being-there is called for “only by way of not
retreating from life’s possibilities and risks.”

The Circularity Problem and the Τέχνη Analogy
Heidegger acknowledges the difficulty: “what, in general, does it mean to become just through acting justly?
Of course, I must already be just to act justly” (p. 123). Aristotle resolves this in NE Β 3 by recourse to relations
of a different sort, in τέχνη (technē, “craft,” “skill”): in τέχνη (technē), it depends upon the γιγνόμενα (gignom‐
ena, “things that come to be”) behaving in the right manner. Shoemaking depends upon the shoe, the τέλος
(telos), the ἔργον (ergon, “work,” “product”), behaving in the right manner.
The decisive disanalogy:
“The being of human beings is determined in its ἔργον (ergon) as πρᾶξις (praxis). This has the τέλος (te‐
los) in itself, comes to its end through itself. For this reason, the basic conditions governing the ἔργον
(ergon) of human beings are entirely different from those in the case of a τέχνη (technē). For this πρᾶξις
(praxis), it depends on how the one acting, as such, behaves toward himself.” (p. 123)

Ζωὴ Πρακτικὴ Μετὰ Λόγου (zōē praktikē meta logou) and the Four-fold Encountering of the World
“In itself, ἕξις (hexis) is related to the ζωὴ πρακτικὴ μετὰ λόγου (zōē praktikē meta logou, ‘practical life

and λυπηρόν (lypēron, ‘the painful’). Our being-in-the-world is always characterized by this
disposition-of-being-elevated and being-burdened, specifically in a way that we find ourselves within
the degrees of a bad mood or an elevated mood. Ἕξις (hexis) is the determinate being-composed within
this way of being. In this way, ἀρετή (aretē) is determined in its being-character.” (p. 125)

Excursus: Ἕξις (hexis) as Δύναμις (dynamis) — Heidegger’s Three-Layered Claim
The compressed sentence at p. 125 in which Heidegger writes that “ἕξις (hexis) also has the further meaning
that it is the same as the δύναμις (dynamis) of any being at all” — and that, “here, ἕξις (hexis) has the definite
orientation toward the being of human beings” — performs a tightly compressed three-layered ontological
move that warrants explicit unpacking. The three layers are not optional embellishments; they are loadbearing for everything that follows in §17b–γ and §18.
Layer 1: Ἕξις (hexis) is a being-determination of anything that is at all
The first layer is a claim of universal ontological scope. Ἕξις (hexis) is not a moralist’s concept restricted to
ethics; it is a structure of being as such. This is why it appears among the categories (Met. Δ20, 1022b 4) and
why Heidegger has spent §17a working through the four senses of ἔχειν (echein) in Metaphysics Δ23: metals,
towns, basins, the vault of heaven all have in determinate ways. Ἕξις (hexis) names the settled, structural way
in which any being “is after a definite being-possibility, or its negation” (p. 118).
Layer 2: Aristotle’s own equation — Ἕξις (hexis) is the same as Δύναμις (dynamis)
The second, decisive layer is that this universal ontological structure coincides with what Aristotle calls
δύναμις (dynamis). The equation is not Heidegger’s editorial gloss; it is Aristotle’s own. In Metaphysics Δ20,
1022b 10 (which Heidegger has been working through immediately preceding this passage), Aristotle explicitly
identifies one sense of ἕξις (hexis) with διάθεσις καθ᾽ ἣν ἢ εὖ ἢ κακῶς διάκειται (diathesis kath’ hēn ē eu ē kakōs
diakeitai) — “a disposition according to which something is well or badly disposed (διάκειται, diakeitai).” A
διάθεσις (diathesis) is a settled way of being-able-to-be-affected: precisely a δύναμις (dynamis). And in Meta‐
physics Δ12, 1019a 15 ff. — the locus classicus for the senses of δύναμις (dynamis) — Aristotle distinguishes the
principal senses that converge with ἕξις (hexis):
• δύναμις τοῦ ποιεῖν (dynamis tou poiein) — the being-able-to-do (active power);
• δύναμις τοῦ παθεῖν (dynamis tou pathein) — the being-able-to-undergo (passive power);
• the fitness or aptitude of a thing to do or undergo well — a normative δύναμις.
Three structural features of δύναμις (dynamis) make this Aristotelian equation indispensable for Heidegger’s
argument:
1. Standing capacity, not occurrent state. A δύναμις is what a being has at its disposal even when not cur‐
rently exercising it. The courageous person is courageous when asleep; the metal is in preparedness for be‐
coming a statue even before it is cast. Δύναμις is settled being-able-to, not unrealized possibility waiting to

§17. Ἕξις — Heidegger, GA 18

happen. This is why ἕξις (hexis) can be the ἐνέργεια (energeia, “being-at-work,” Met. Δ20, 1022b 4) of hav‐
ing: the “being-there” of the having itself, even before any further act.
2. Bivalence: receptivity and activity together. Δύναμις is both power-to-do and power-to-undergo — the
same settled capacity is exercised in πρᾶξις (praxis) and is liable to be struck or displaced in πάθος (pathos).
This bivalence is structurally essential: a purely active “habit” could not be brought-out-of-composure; a
δύναμις-sense ἕξις (hexis) precisely can. Without this Aristotelian equation, ἕξις (hexis) and πάθος (pathos)
would have to come from different sources; with it, they are two faces of one structural capacity.
3. Preparedness toward a determinate range. Δύναμις is never bare possibility — it is always δύναμις τοῦ
τοιοῦδε (dynamis tou toioude), “capacity for such-and-such.” The metal’s δύναμις to become a statue (the
§17a example on p. 117) is its δεκτικόν (dektikon) — its being-in-preparedness for that determinate εἶδος (ei‐
dos, “look”). Δύναμις and δεκτικόν are two angles on the same ontological positivity that Heidegger
insisted on at p. 117 (the ὕλη [hylē] is “not an indeterminate ‘material’ but a positive character of a mode of
being-there”).
Layer 3: The narrowing — “here, ἕξις has the definite orientation toward the being of human beings”
The third layer narrows the universal δύναμις-structure to the specific case Heidegger and Aristotle are pursu‐
ing. Ἕξις qua δύναμις is universally ontological; it applies to metals, plants, animals, anything that “has” a
settled capacity. But the analysis of πρᾶξις (praxis) and ἀρετή (aretē) requires that this universal structure be
taken with reference to the determinate kind of being the human being is — namely, ζωὴ πρακτικὴ μετὰ λόγου
(zōē praktikē meta logou). The narrowing adds three specifically human-ontological features:
• The δύναμις in question is one that can be cultivated through ἔθος (ethos, “habit”) — unlike, say, the
metal’s preparedness, which is given by φύσις (physis, “nature”) and not subject to ethical γένεσις (genesis).
• The δύναμις is structured by λόγος (logos) — speech, articulation, deliberation — which is why ἀρετή
(aretē) will turn out to be ἕξις προαιρετική (hexis proairetikē): a δύναμις borne specifically by προαίρεσις
(proairesis, “resolution”).
• The δύναμις is bound to καιρός (kairos, “the right moment”) — it is a δύναμις for seizing the situation, not
a δύναμις for producing a fixed result. This is why, ten pages later (p. 126), Heidegger glosses the μέσον
(meson) of ἀρετή (aretē) as a δύναμις in the sense of κριτική (kritikē) — the “ability-to-separate” that
αἴσθησις (aisthēsis) itself displays at De Anima Β 11, 424a 4.
Why the Three-Layered Claim Carries the Whole §17 → §18 Transition
The δύναμις-equation is not a marginal aside — it is the load-bearing concept that licenses the formula a few
lines later (p. 125):
“Ἕξις (hexis) is nothing other than a how of πάθος (pathos), being-out-of-composure, in relation to
being-composed-as-to…” (p. 125)

This sentence only works if ἕξις (hexis) is read as a δύναμις (dynamis) in the bivalent Aristotelian sense. Because
δύναμις is intrinsically both active power and passive power, ἕξις (hexis) and πάθος (pathos) can be co-originary
structures of the same human being-in-the-world: the same settled capacity that is exercised as πρᾶξις (praxis,
the ἐνέργεια of having) is what is struck, displaced, or brought out of composure in πάθος (pathos, the πάσχειν of
having). Heidegger’s entire interpretation of human being-there as the dialectic of composure and being-

Aristotle seeks to sharpen the being-determination of ἀρετή (aretē) by taking it as μεσότης (mesotēs)—by

Πρᾶξις vs. Τέχνη: The How vs. the Result
“In the case of an action—in the narrow sense in which it is opposed to ποίησις (poiēsis)—it does not,

Πρᾶξις Demands Constant Re-appropriation
Aristotle sharply distinguishes ἀρετή (aretē) and action from τέχνη (technē) in NE Β 3, although he initially

knowing. It requires in itself a fully determinate duration.” By contrast, πράττειν (prattein), “action,” as well

take up a definite material, but rather cultivate the how of dealing itself.
“The how is only appropriated in such a way that the human being enables himself to be composed at
each moment; not routine but holding-oneself-open, δύναμις (dynamis) in the μεσότης (mesotēs). All of
human living cannot be there constantly. The possibilities that a human existence has at its disposal are
not constantly there within the stretching of being-there; it loses itself. The possibility deteriorates,
and being-there requires ever new and constantly repeated appropriation.” (p. 128)

moment as a whole. Therefore, acting seldom occurs on the basis of the μεσότης (mesotēs) and in the μεσότης

The Closing Formulation: Historicality
“The whole question of self-habituating must be seen from the look of the possibility on which

Synthetic Reading of §17
Architectural Position
§17 is the systematic hinge of Chapter 3. §13–14 established πίστις (pistis, “credence”) and the rhetorical
analytic; §15–16 located ἦθος (ēthos) and πάθος (pathos) as πίστεις; §17 now grounds πάθος (pathos) ontologically
by working through its co-originary structure, ἕξις (hexis). §18 will then return to πάθος (pathos) with the
“hermeneutic clue” that §17 has provided. The key formula linking the two: “Ἕξις (hexis) is nothing other
than a how of πάθος (pathos)” (p. 125).

Six Theses Heidegger Establishes
1. Ἕξις (hexis) is a fundamental concept of being, derived from ἔχειν (echein) as a category. The four senses of
ἔχειν (echein) (Met. Δ23) all express being after a definite being-possibility. (§17a)
2. Ὕλη (hylē) is not indeterminate “material” but a positive mode of being-there: being-in-preparedness
(δεκτικόν, dektikon). (§17a)
3. Ἕξις (hexis) is the determination of the genuineness of being-there in a moment of being-composed; it
carries the orientation toward καιρός (kairos) and is intrinsically related to its negation in πάθος (pathos).
(§17a)
4. Ἀρετή (aretē) is not a property but a mode of being-there, characterized by temporality, with its γένεσις
(genesis) only in being-there itself—not deliverable by τέχνη (technē) or παραγγελία (paraggelia). (§17b–α)
5. Μεσότης (mesotēs) is not mediocrity but the structural relation πρὸς ἡμᾶς (pros hēmas); for human being

6. The always of being-there is the frequently of repetition; ἀρετή (aretē) demands acting-anew at each mo‐

Connections to Being and Time (1927)
• Ἕξις → Befindlichkeit (BT §29): “the determination of the genuineness of being-there in a moment of
being-composed” (p. 119) is the proto-formulation of attunement.
• The four basic aspects → Sorge / Jemeinigkeit (BT §9, §41): being-there as concern hidden in the concept
of having; particularity at the moment.
• Καιρός → Augenblick (BT §68): “in an operation, the moment is destroyed” (p. 128) is the 1924 form of the
contrast between authentic moment-of-vision and inauthentic now.
• The frequently of repetition → Wiederholung / Geschichtlichkeit (BT §74, §72–77): the closing formula‐
tion on historicality (p. 129) is a direct anticipation.
• Πρᾶξις as existence (p. 119): explicit equivalence; Existenz as “the how of being-in-the-world.”

Two Tensions Worth Flagging
1. Circularity in the genesis of ἀρετή (aretē): how does the initial ἕξις (hexis) become possible if ἕξις (hexis)
requires prior ἕξις (hexis)? Aristotle’s τέχνη-analogy is acknowledged as inadequate by Heidegger, but the
circle is left unresolved.
2. Repetition vs. routine: Heidegger insists that genesis is “acting anew in every moment,” not the building
of routine—yet Aristotle’s ἐθίζειν (ethizein) and the γραμματικός (grammatikos) example seem to involve
precisely the kind of routine-building Heidegger rejects. The tension between Heidegger’s existential
reading and Aristotle’s practical pedagogy persists.
