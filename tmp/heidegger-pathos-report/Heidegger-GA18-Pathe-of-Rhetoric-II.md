# Heidegger, GA 18

## Anger, Fear, Shame, and the Higher-Order Πάθη of *Rhetoric* II

### A Detailed Analysis of *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* (Marburg 1924)

*Source: Martin Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* (GA 18), trans. Robert D. Metcalf and Mark B. Tanzer (Indiana University Press, 2009). Primary sections under interpretation: §16 (pp. 109–115), §17 (pp. 116–128), §18 (pp. 129–139), §20 (pp. 162–166), §21 (pp. 167–176). Page numbers refer to the published English edition.*

## 1. The Problem and the Stakes

Heidegger's 1924 Marburg lecture course returns to Aristotle's *Rhetoric* and *Nicomachean Ethics* with a single, polemical aim: to wrest the analysis of the πάθη — anger, fear, shame, pity, indignation, the family of higher-order emotions Aristotle catalogues in *Rhetoric* B 2–11 — out of the philosophical-psychological tradition that has settled around them. That tradition, beginning with Stoicism and consolidating through Augustine, Aquinas, Descartes, and the modern "psychology of the affects," construes a πάθος as a "psychic experience" in an "inner life," symptomatically registered in a "body" understood as something other than the soul. What the Cartesian inheritance bequeaths is a topology of inside-and-outside in which an emotion is an inner event with outer signs. Heidegger's central thesis in §18 is that this picture *begins by losing the phenomenon*. "The πάθη are not 'psychic experiences,' are not 'in consciousness,' but a being-taken of human beings *in their full being-in-the-world*" (p. 133). Anger is not a feeling that occurs alongside facial flushing; fear is not a private mood that "comes over" a self-standing subject. Each is a determinate mode in which Dasein finds itself disclosed to its world, takes a position in the situation, and moves toward speaking.

The lectures position the πάθη at three intersecting points in the architectonic of human being-there. First, they are *πίστεις* — modes of trust, of being-convinced — through which the rhetorical situation is structured (§16). Second, they are *fundamental concepts of being*, co-original with ἕξις, articulating the basic ontological structure of beings whose way of being is *zōē praktikē meta logou* (§§17–18). Third, they are the *ground of λόγος itself*: it is on the basis of being-affected that speaking arises, and into which what is spoken sinks back when the conversation ends (§21g, p. 176). This last claim, which Heidegger states with unmistakable emphasis at the close of Chapter 3 of the First Part, is the conclusion toward which the entire analysis builds. The higher-order emotions — those we call anger, fear, shame, pity, indignation — are not appendages to a discourse already underway. They are the soil out of which discourse grows, and the rhetorical art is the cultivation of that soil.

The analysis that follows takes up the πάθη through five concentric framings, each of which Heidegger develops at a different scale. *First*, πάθος as πίστις: the rhetorical reading of the emotions as constitutive moments of the speech-situation (§16). *Second*, the four general meanings of πάθος drawn from *Metaphysics* Δ 21 and the architecture of ἕξις/πάθος as fundamental concepts of being (§§17–18). *Third*, πάθος as full bodily being-taken — the *λόγοι ἔνυλοι* thesis from *De Anima* A 1 (§18c–d). *Fourth*, ἡδονή and λύπη as ontological-affective ground tones (§20). *Fifth*, the master case of fear (φόβος, *Rhetoric* B 5) as the demonstration that the πάθη disclose the futural character of being-there and bring it to speech (§21). Throughout, the question is the same: how does Heidegger let the higher-order emotions show themselves *as they are* — not as inner episodes but as the modes in which a finite, embodied, speaking being finds itself in the moment, in the situation, with others, before what is coming?

## 2. The Πάθη as Πίστεις: The Rhetorical Frame (§16)

### 2.1 Theoretical and Practical Negotiating

§16 opens at the seam between two modes of *legein*. Up to this point, Heidegger has been treating *doxa* as the ground of dialectical inquiry — the *protasis*-*problēma* structure through which propositions are put forward and disputed in theoretical investigation. He now turns to a second mode of *legein* whose object is "not an identical fact of the matter that would be transmitted within a science" but "something that differs according to circumstances of being-there, change in mood, change of view" (p. 110). This is the world of practical deliberation: the assembly, the law-court, the situation in which speaking always takes its bearings from the variable disposition of those who hear and the variable comportment of the one who speaks. Here, "alongside the premise, something else must be placed in view — calculation must be carried out as to the mood of those spoken to, the situation at each moment of things, the manner and mode in which one stands toward the matter" (p. 110). The shift is decisive: the πάθη enter not as supplementary considerations but as *constitutive moments* of the discursive situation. Without them, there is no *λέγειν* to analyze.

Aristotle's three constitutive aspects, as Heidegger reads them through *Rhetoric* B 1, are: (1) ἦθος, the comportment of the speaker; (2) πάθος, the disposition of the hearers; (3) the matter itself (p. 110). These are not three "appeals" in the modern rhetorical sense — they are three determinations of the rhetorical situation in its full ontological structure. Each is a *πίστις* — a mode of trust, a mode in which the rhetorical situation is *held together* and the speech can do what it does. The πάθη take their place in this triad as the dispositional ground of the audience: the manner in which the hearer has already been delivered over to the matter under deliberation before any specific argument has been made, and the manner in which this delivery shifts, changes, opens or closes possibilities of position-taking, in the course of the speech itself.

### 2.2 The Three Meanings of Πάθος

Heidegger isolates *three* basic senses of πάθος, against which the rhetorical analysis must be set (p. 113):

> "We can list three basic meanings of this expression, and accordingly three concrete contexts that it designates: (1) the *average, immediate* meaning is that of 'variable condition'; (2) a *specifically ontological* meaning, which is important for the understanding of κίνησις: πάθος in connection with πάσχειν, what one most often translates as 'suffering'; (3) a *resulting* meaning: variable condition in relation to a definite concrete context, variable condition within a definite being-region of life: 'passion.' Πάθος in this last sense is the topic of the *Rhetoric* and the *Poetics*." (p. 113)

This triple sense is not a matter of nominal ambiguity. Each meaning indexes a distinct stratum of the phenomenon, and Heidegger moves through them with care. The "variable condition" is the ordinary use of the word — a quality that something has, can lose, can have differently. The "ontological" sense, treated more fully in §18b through the four meanings of *Metaphysics* Δ 21, identifies πάθος with the very structure of *alterability* that belongs to a being capable of having something happen to it; this is the sense Aristotle will need for the analysis of κίνησις in the *Physics*. The third sense — "passion" in the rhetorical-poetic sense — is the one most familiar to us under the heading "emotion": the determinate, articulated, situationally complete dispositions Aristotle catalogues in *Rhetoric* B 2–11. These three meanings are not parallel but stratified: the rhetorical sense (3) presupposes the ontological sense (2), which in turn presupposes the average sense (1). Anger as a "passion" is intelligible only on the basis of anger as a determinate motion (κίνησις) of a living being, which in turn presupposes the more general structure of alterability that being-living involves.

### 2.3 The Three Things "in the Soul": Πάθη, Δυνάμεις, Ἕξεις

Heidegger now draws on *Nicomachean Ethics* B 4, 1105b 19 sqq., where Aristotle introduces three things that "come to be in the soul" (*ginomena en tē psychē*): πάθη, δυνάμεις, and ἕξεις. The three are not parallel categories but three ontological registers of *the same being-in-the-world*. Πάθη are, on Aristotle's own statement that Heidegger underlines, "*the sort of thing with which* ἡδονή and λύπη are co-given" — the family that includes "ἐπιθυμία, ὀργή, φόβος, θάρσος" and so on (p. 113, citing *NE* B 4, 1105b 21 sqq.). Δυνάμεις are the *being-determinations of living things that Aristotle too characterizes as φύσει ὄν*: the being-possibilities of factical being-there to be enraged, sad, happy, hating, and so on (p. 114). Ἕξεις are "the manner and mode in which we are in such a πάθος" — the πῶς ἔχομεν πρὸς τὰ πάθη, "how we carry ourselves," "what composure we have," with such a πάθος (p. 114).

Heidegger's reading of this triad is structurally decisive. The πάθη are *modes of being itself*; the δυνάμεις are the *being-possibilities* corresponding to those modes; the ἕξεις are the *settled manner of comportment* in relation to those possibilities. The three are not stratified hierarchically (lowest to highest, or material to formal); they articulate three different aspects of the same phenomenon. Anger as πάθος is the determinate being-taken in this moment; anger as δύναμις is the standing capacity to be brought into anger; ἕξις is the cultivated manner — magnanimity, irritability, equanimity — in which this human being characteristically is or is not brought into anger by what is encountered. The πάθη and δυνάμεις are *γινόμενα ἐν τῇ ψυχῇ*, "things that come to be in the soul," but the soul here is not a private inner space — it is *psychē* in Aristotle's sense, the *ousia* of the living being, the way of being of a being that is *zōē praktikē meta logou*.

### 2.4 The Constitutive Aspects (Rhet. B 1)

The *Rhetoric*'s first determination of the πάθη names three constitutive aspects (p. 115):

> "(1) μεταβάλλοντες: something along the way with respect to which 'a change comes for us,' through which 'we change' from one disposition to another. (2) Combined with this change, *διαφέρουσι πρὸς τὰς κρίσεις*, 'we differentiate ourselves' from ourselves before the change in that which is the hearer's task: 'to take a position,' 'to form a view.' (3) *οἷς ἕπεται λύπη καὶ ἡδονή*, not 'following,' but rather 'co-given' in combination with the πάθη is a 'being-disposed-as-higher-or-lower' of the being-there in question." (p. 115)

The three moments — change, position-taking, co-givenness with pleasure-pain — articulate a single phenomenon from three angles. *Metaballontes*: the πάθος is essentially a *change* — being-there does not stand outside it as an observer who notes the change; being-there *is* what changes, and in changing comes to differ from itself. *Diapherousi pros tas kriseis*: the change is not a mere alteration but a re-positioning vis-à-vis the matter to be decided; what was just now seen as not-very-bad now appears unbearable, what was just now seen as friend now appears as enemy. *Hois hepetai lupē kai hēdonē*: this re-positioning is not affectively neutral — every πάθος is co-given with a tonality of finding-oneself "elevated" or "depressed," "higher" or "lower." Heidegger insists that *hepetai* here does not mean "follows" temporally — pleasure or pain is not a consequence that arrives after the πάθος. It is *co-given*, simultaneous, constitutive: the πάθος *is* a way of being elevated or depressed in the situation. This is Heidegger's first formal statement of what *Being and Time* will name *Befindlichkeit*: a finding-oneself that is, in its very structure, a tonality of mattering.

The conclusion of §16 sounds, in 1924, the keynote that the entire subsequent analysis will elaborate. "*How what is designated as πάθος defines being-in-the-world in a fundamental sense*, and how it comes into consideration as such a basic determination of being-in-the-world with the cultivation of κρίσις, of 'taking-a-position,' of 'deciding' a critical question. By showing this fundamental role of πάθη in κρίνειν as such, we will also gain the possibility of seeing the basis of λόγος itself more concretely" (p. 114). The πάθη are not appendages to discourse, nor accessories to judgment; they are the determinations of being-in-the-world out of which both speaking and judging arise.

## 3. The Four General Meanings of Πάθος (§18b, *Met.* Δ 21)

Heidegger's structural analysis of πάθος as a *fundamental concept of being* is built from *Metaphysics* Δ 21, where Aristotle gives four general meanings (p. 131):

1. **Πάθος as alterability-as-such.** "*Πάθος λέγεται ἕνα μὲν τρόπον ποιότης καθ' ἣν ἀλλοιοῦσθαι ἐνδέχεται*" — being-constituted regarding which something underlies alteration. White-black, sweet-bitter, heavy-light: these are determinations that a being can have in such a way that they can also be reversed. Πάθος in this broadest sense is "a determination of beings with the character of becoming-otherwise" (p. 131). To be a πάθος in this sense is to *have* the structure of being-able-to-be-otherwise — the structural openness without which neither change nor disposition is intelligible.

2. **Πάθος as the energeiai of these alterations.** "*Ἔτι δὲ τούτων ἐνέργειαι καὶ ἀλλοιώσεις ἤδη*" — the actual occurrings of such alteration. Not just the structural possibility (sense 1), but the actuality of *something happening* in the constitution of such a being. *Energeia* here means "the being-there of such a shifting occurring-to-one" (p. 131). This is πάθος *as* the actual befalling, the actual coming-to-pass of the alteration.

3. **Πάθος as the harmful, the painful — the βλαβεραί ἀλλοιώσεις καὶ κινήσεις.** Now the determination narrows. "Πάθος as the occurring-to-one that has the character of the unpleasant, the βλαβερόν" (p. 131). Harm is closely related to λύπη: "my attunement to this occurring affects me," it produces a "becoming-otherwise in the sense of becoming-depressed" (pp. 131–132). The third meaning refines the second: not just any occurrence, but the occurrence that tones the living being *down*.

4. **Πάθος as the magnitude of the harmful.** "*Ἔτι τὰ μεγέθη τῶν συμφορῶν καὶ λυπηρῶν πάθη λέγεται*" — πάθος as the "size" or "measure" of what occurs to me in a harmful way: "that is a blow to me" (p. 132). This fourth meaning narrows still further: not just the harmful occurrence, but the magnitude of harm — the gravity of the calamity that struck.

The progression is not arbitrary. It moves from the *most general ontological structure* (alterability) through the *actual occurrence* of alteration (energeia of alteration), to the *qualitative determination* of that occurrence as harmful, to the *magnitude* of the harm. Heidegger's phenomenological summary collects the four under a single thesis: "From these four meanings, the *genuine relatedness of πάθος becomes visible*; it is related to the *being of living things*, which is characterized by a thus-finding-oneself-again-and-again" (p. 132). The four senses, taken together, articulate πάθος as the structure of *being-occurrable-to* that belongs to the way of being of a finite living being. To live, in the Aristotelian sense Heidegger recovers, is to be ontologically structured as *that to which something can happen* — and to which, in its concrete factical existence, *this and that, again and again, does* happen.

A further specification follows. Πάθος includes within itself *μεταβολή*, *κίνησις*, *ἀλλοίωσις* — the basic motions of *Physics*. The occurring-to-one has the character of *φθορά*, "passing-away," but also of *σωτηρία*, "preserving." Heidegger underlines a remarkable Aristotelian observation: "*οὐκ ἔστιν ἁπλοῦν οὐδὲ τὸ πάσχειν*, ἀλλὰ τὸ μὲν φθορά τις ὑπὸ τοῦ ἐναντίου, τὸ δὲ σωτηρία μᾶλλον τοῦ δυνάμει ὄντος ὑπὸ τοῦ ἐντελεχείᾳ ὄντος" (p. 132, *De an.* B 5, 417b 2 sqq.) — there is not just *one* kind of πάσχειν: one is destruction by an opposite, the other is preservation by the actuality of what one was potentially. This is the philosophical key Heidegger drives home: *not all being-affected is loss*. Hearing a sound is a being-affected by which my hearing is *brought to its full being*, not destroyed. Learning is a being-affected by which I come into possession of what I am potentially. Πάθος, in its widest ontological reach, includes the structure by which a living being is *brought into its proper actuality* through its encounter with the world. To this Heidegger appends the historical observation, of evident interest to him: "Hegel took the phenomenon of *σώζειν* from Aristotle in the expression *Aufhebung* (sublation)" (p. 132).

The four meanings, then, do not yield a "definition" of πάθος as a univocal concept. They circumscribe a *being-region*: the region of beings whose way of being involves alterability, who undergo actual alterations, who can be tuned-down by harmful occurrences, and to whom such occurrences come with measurable gravity. This is the region of *living* beings — and, more specifically, of human living beings whose way of being is *zōē praktikē meta logou*, life-as-acting-with-speech. The higher-order emotions — anger, fear, shame — are πάθη in *all four* senses simultaneously: they are determinate alterabilities, actual occurrings, possible toning-downs, and they come with a magnitude that strikes the one to whom they happen.

## 4. Πάθος as the Bodily Being-Taken of Being-There (§18c–d, *De Anima* A 1)

### 4.1 The Anti-Cartesian Thesis

§18c is the philosophical center of the entire pathos-analysis. Heidegger here lays out the thesis that distinguishes his reading from every "psychological" treatment of the emotions. "Πάθος, in relation to the *ζωὴ πρακτικὴ μετὰ λόγου*, is thus a *being-taken* of being-there. Being-there is taken with that which is there in the world *from without*, but from without *in the sense of the world as the wherein of my being*. The possibilities and ways of its being-taken follow from being-there itself. Thus, this being-taken of being-there as being-in-its-world does not involve anything like what we could designate as the 'spiritual,' which invites the conception of πάθος as affect. Instead, it is always a being-taken of beings as living things as such" (p. 133).

Three commitments are packed into this passage. First, πάθος is a *being-taken*. To be in a πάθος is not to undergo something inside a self-standing inner subject but to be *delivered over*, *gripped by*, *taken into* something that comes from out of the world. Second, this "from without" is not an outside set against an inside; it is *the world as the wherein of my being*. Heidegger's language is exact: the world is not outside Dasein, it is the "wherein" — the medium, the dimension, the *in-which* — of being-there. Third, the structure of this being-taken is not "spiritual" (in the inner-subject sense): it is a being-taken of *living beings as such*, in their full bodily existence.

Heidegger underlines this with one of the most striking philological observations in the lectures, drawn from *De Anima* A 4, 408b 11 sqq.: "*To say that the soul gets angry is the same as to say that the soul builds a house. It would be better to say not that the soul has pity or learns or believes, but that the human being does these things τῇ ψυχῇ — by means of soul*" (p. 133). The soul is not the *agent* of the πάθη; the human being, in its full being-in-the-world, is the agent — and the soul *names the way in which the human being is taken*. Soul is here conceived "as ousia, insofar as the being-taken of beings as living things is expressed in the πάθη" (p. 133). This is the Aristotelian hylomorphic position recovered: psychē is not an inner thing alongside the body but the form of the living body, the structure that articulates the body's bodily being as the being of a living thing.

The methodological consequence Heidegger draws is unmistakable: "Therefore, the πάθη are not 'psychic experiences,' are not 'in consciousness,' but a being-taken of human beings *in their full being-in-the-world*. That is expressed by the fact that the whole, the full occurrence-context, in which it is found in this happening, in being-taken, belongs to the πάθη. The so-called 'bodily states' of anxiety, joy, and so forth, are not symptoms, but also belong to the characteristic being of beings, of human beings" (p. 133). The bodily moments — the boiling of blood in anger, the shudder in fear, the hot flush in shame — are *not symptoms* of an inner state. They *are* the πάθος itself, in its proper bodily way of being. To speak of "outer signs" of an "inner emotion" is to have already lost what the πάθος *is*.

### 4.2 Πάντα μετὰ σώματος: The Aporia of *De Anima* A 1

Heidegger reaches behind §18c to the founding aporia of *De Anima* Book A, Chapter 1. Aristotle there asks "whether they [the πάθη] are all common to the [ἀνθρώπῳ; here κοινά means all human beings], or if there are πάθη that are cut off from the soul" (p. 133). The aporia is: are the πάθη entirely a matter of the human being-in-its-full-being, or are some of them so deeply interior that they belong to the soul without bodily participation? Aristotle's answer is decisive for Heidegger: "*Most people* (invocation of the average experience of human beings) believe that the body somehow takes part in all having-courage, and so on, and in all perceiving in general. The expression *aisthanesthai* — 'having-courage toward,' 'being-inclined,' and so on — is not used in the narrow sense of perception, but as awareness in the sense of *having-there of something that is around me*. To most, even *νοεῖν* seems to be the sort of thing in which the body does not take part. *If, however, even νοεῖν* (the thorough deliberating of a matter, when I do not have it perceptually present) is something like a φαντασία or cannot be without φαντασία, then thinking too cannot be without participation in the context of the entire life of a human being" (pp. 133–134).

This passage has decisive consequences for the analysis of the higher-order emotions. The reasoning runs: even *thinking*, the most apparently disembodied of human capacities, requires φαντασία — making-present, the holding-there of what is not now sensorily given. And φαντασία, as the bodily mode of presenting-to-oneself, is *meta sōmatos*, "with the body." Therefore even thinking is *bodily*. *A fortiori*, every πάθος — anger, fear, shame, pity — is bodily through and through. It is not that the body "accompanies" anger; anger *is* a determinate occurrence of the bodily being-taken in which the human being is delivered over to the situation as humiliated, threatened, or insulted. Heidegger states the consequence in the strongest terms: "Insofar as νόησις is the highest possibility for the being of human beings, the entire being of human beings is determined so that it must be apprehended as the *bodily being-in-the-world* of human beings" (p. 134). And the methodological corollary: "Only in *phenomenology* has the division between 'psychic' and 'bodily' begun to be dissolved.... *The primary being-there-function of bodiliness secures the ground for the full being of human beings*. The beginnings of the entire tradition's erroneous orientation toward the biological (Descartes's *res cogitans* — *res extensa*) is also found in Aristotle" (p. 134).

The remark cuts in two directions at once. It is a polemic against Descartes — and against the entire post-Cartesian psychology of the affects — but it also notes that the "erroneous orientation" begins in Aristotle himself. Heidegger's point is not that Aristotle is anti-Cartesian *avant la lettre*; it is that Aristotle has not yet *completed* the move, that the philosophical task is to draw out what is implicit in *De Anima* A 1 against what is misleading in the same text (and what was disastrously made into a system in modernity).

### 4.3 The Double Consideration: Eidos and Hule

§18d turns to the question that is for Heidegger the methodological crux: how should the πάθη be defined? Aristotle gives two candidate definitions of anger and asks which is the "real" one (p. 137, drawing on *De an.* A 1, 403a 26 sqq.). The *dialektikos* — the rhetorician who deals with anger as a phenomenon of soul-in-the-world — defines anger as *ὄρεξις ἀντιλυπήσεως*: an aiming-after pay-back, a "being-after pay-back, being-toward-others" (p. 137). The *phusikos* defines it as *ζέσις τοῦ περὶ καρδίαν αἵματος καὶ θερμοῦ*: "a determinate boiling up of blood in the heart and of the temperature" (p. 137). Both are *λόγοι*: both are determinate ways of *addressing* anger and bringing it to language. The first yields the *eidos* of anger; the second yields the *hulē*, the "stuff," the corporeal medium.

Heidegger insists that the answer is not "either-or" but rather a determinate *together*. "The first *λόγος* yields the genuine eidos; the second is the hulē. But as determination of the being of human beings in the world, it is necessarily co-determined by being a *ζέσις*, a 'boiling up' of the blood" (p. 137). This is the formula by which Heidegger names the πάθη *λόγοι ἔνυλοι* — "enmattered λόγοι," meaning-articulations whose meaning is inseparable from their bodily occurrence. "*The eidos of fearing has the primary relatedness to a finding-oneself of the body*. Both eidos and hule accounts are *λόγοι ἔνυλοι* — 'material' in an entirely different sense" (p. 139).

The thesis matters because it determines how the higher-order emotions are to be understood. Anger is not "the desire for retaliation plus, contingently, a hot flush": it is the bodily being-toward-others as having-been-slighted, in which the eidetic structure (aim-at-pay-back-for-an-undeserved-slight) is *already* the form of a bodily occurrence. The shaking and the blood-rush are not symptoms of a hidden judgment; they are part of the way the judgment is *bodily there*. Conversely, the bodily occurrence is not a brute physiological event happening to a soul that judges separately; it is the bodily form of *just this* judgment — the slight, the perpetrator, the wish for retaliation — articulated in flesh.

It is this double-articulation that distinguishes the *real phusikos* from the ancient physiologists who treated the πάθη as merely material processes. The genuine philosopher of nature — the *protos philosophos* — must take both *λόγοι* together. "The hierarchy [of investigators] runs: technites, doctor, phusikos, mathematikos, *protos philosophos*, corresponding to increasingly fundamental modes of questioning" (p. 138). Only the *protos philosophos* sees the phenomenon as it is: bodily-formed, eidos-articulated, world-engaged, being-occurred-to. The higher-order emotions are not material processes (the doctor's domain), nor are they immaterial *ideai* (the dialektikos in isolation); they are structural moments of the way a finite living being whose way of being is *zōē praktikē meta logou* finds itself in its world.

### 4.4 Fear in the Face of the Nothing

A philosophically explosive remark anticipates §21. "For the Greeks, fear as anxiety is co-constitutive of the manner and mode of grasping what is and what is not. Thus Aristotle views the phenomenon of fear so broadly that he also becomes attentive to the fact that there is *fear even if nothing is there* that would be the direct inducement of a fear — *fear in the face of the nothing*" (p. 129). The remark, uttered in 1924 — five years before "What Is Metaphysics?" and three before *Being and Time* — already names the figure of *Angst* that will define Heidegger's mature analytic of disposition. What will be philosophically completed in *Being and Time* §40 is here already glimpsed in Aristotle: an emotion need not have a definite this-and-such as its object to be fully real and structurally significant. The "what" before which one is afraid can be *nothing* in particular — and that very indeterminacy can disclose the world more originarily than any determinate object could. The reading here is *Aristotle through Augustine through Luther* — a thread Heidegger explicitly traces: "*initium sapientiae timor Domini*" (Augustine, cited at p. 175). Fear in the face of the nothing is not a privation of fear; it is the most originary form of fear, the form that brings being-there's being into question as such.

## 5. Ἡδονή and Λύπη as Ontological-Affective Ground Tones (§20)

§20 turns to the relation of πάθος to ἡδονή and λύπη. Heidegger's reading is sharp against the standard translations: *ἡδονή* is *not* "pleasure" understood as a subjective psychological state, and *λύπη* is *not* "pain" understood as a localizable bodily sensation. They are, on his reading, ontological-affective determinations of being-in-the-world — modes in which living beings are present *in the moment*, oriented toward what concerns them, reaching toward what completes them or recoiling from what diminishes them.

The key claim is given as a structural ontological thesis: "*ἡδονή is nothing other than a fundamental determination of being-in-the-world*" (p. 163). All living beings *πάντα γὰρ φύσει ἔχει τι θεῖον* — "by nature have something divine in them" (p. 163, *NE* K 2, 1153b 29 sqq.) — pursue *ἡδονή* because it is "*the genuine being-character of living beings as such*: the tendency toward being-there-completed." Pleasure is here neither a sensation nor a goal external to action; it is *the way a living being is directly present to itself in the actuality of its proper being*. Heidegger reaches the strongest formulation: *ἡδονή* "can be directly identified with *ζῆν*, 'living'" (p. 165).

Two further structural specifications matter for the higher-order emotions. *First*, ἡδονή is *ἐν τῷ νῦν* — "in the moment" — and is no κίνησις in the sense of a process spanning time but a determination of the *presentness* of being-there as such (p. 164). It is complete at each moment, like seeing or hearing, not a span. The thesis has a temporal corollary: pleasure and pain are not in time as a determinate stretch of duration; they constitute the very presentness in which time is articulated for being-there. *Second*, ἡδονή is *co-given* (*hepetai*) with every mode of being-there — "with every πάθος, but equally with every perceiving, every thinking, considering, with θεωρία" (p. 166). There is no mode of being-there from which pleasure-or-pain is absent: every act of perceiving, judging, contemplating, suffering is tonally colored by ἡδονή or λύπη.

The implication for the analysis of the higher-order emotions is direct. Each πάθος of *Rhetoric* B — anger, fear, shame, pity, indignation, envy, kindness, friendship — carries its tonal signature: anger is co-given with pain at a slight, but Aristotle insists also with a peculiar *hēdonē* of the imagined revenge; fear is dominated by *lupē* but laced with the *elpis* of escape (which itself carries *hēdonē*); shame is the determinate pain of having-fallen-short before an audience that matters. The tonal structure is not added on top of an emotional content; it *is* the emotional content insofar as that content is a way of finding-oneself-in-the-world. The dual possibilities of *hairesis* (going-toward, seizing) and *phugē* (fleeing) — which Heidegger names "the basic motivations of being-there" (p. 166) — are precisely the dispositional poles of every πάθος. Each higher-order emotion can be charted on the *hairesis*-*phugē* axis: anger is toward, fear is away-from, kindness is toward, indignation is against. The πάθη of *Rhetoric* II are differentiated determinations of these basic dispositional axes, each modulated by its specific *eidos* (slight, threat, undeserved-fortune-of-others) and its specific bodily form.

## 6. The Master Case: Φόβος (§21, *Rhetoric* B 5)

### 6.1 Why Fear is the Test Case

Heidegger devotes more sustained attention to fear than to any other πάθος in the lectures. Two reasons drive this attention. First, fear is the πάθος in which the ontological structure of being-toward-the-future is most clearly visible: what one fears is not yet there but is *coming toward me*, and this *coming-toward-me* discloses the futural character of being-there itself. Second, fear shows in the most concentrated form the connection between πάθος, *πίστις*, and λόγος: the one who is brought into fear is precisely the one who *runs to confer*, becomes "ready to deliberate" (*Rhet.* B 5, 1383a 6), and so becomes the one whom rhetoric can address. Fear is thus the exemplary case for the master thesis that the πάθη are the ground of λόγος.

Heidegger announces the dual orientation at the start of §21 (p. 167): φόβος as πάθος — being-afraid as a fully determinate concretion of being-out-of-composure; and φόβος as πίστις — clue to how being-afraid is a basic determination of the being-there of the hearer who co-speaks in deliberation. The two together demonstrate the structural integration of the πάθη-analysis with the rhetorical analysis: φόβος is not first a psychological state and then, on top of that, rhetorically useful; it is, in its essential structure, *already* a mode of being-there that *brings to deliberation*. The rhetorical use does not modify a pre-given fear; it cultivates the disposition of fear in the mode that opens the hearer to discourse.

### 6.2 The Definition: φόβος = λύπη τις ἢ ταραχὴ ἐκ φαντασίας μέλλοντος κακοῦ φθαρτικοῦ ἢ λυπηροῦ

Aristotle's formal definition (*Rhet.* B 5, 1382a 21) is unpacked by Heidegger phrase by phrase (p. 168):

- *λύπη τις*: distress, "a being-toned-down," a "feeling-recoil," "a flight," "a finding-oneself that recoils from itself."
- *ταραχή*: confusion, "being-led-by-another," "being-through-another." In fear I am not in possession of myself but *led* by what comes toward me.
- *ἐκ φαντασίας*: "from something that shows itself." The fear arises not from perception (αἴσθησις) of what is present but from φαντασία — the making-present of what is not yet there. *Φαντασία* in the original sense is *τὸ φαίνεσθαι*, "the self-showing": something shows itself to me without being there in the manner of αἴσθησις, "a being-led-by-another on the basis of the seeing-before-oneself of a being that is not yet there."
- *μέλλοντος*: "not yet there," in the sense of "standing-before, coming-toward-me." This is the *futural* moment.
- *κακοῦ φθαρτικοῦ ἢ λυπηροῦ*: an evil that *can do damage to me*, "something harmful to me."

Heidegger's reformulation crystallizes the four moments into a single definition: "*A disposition that is set before an approaching possibility that pertains to me, comes toward me, and as such announces itself, specifically through the announcement*" (p. 168, italics in original).

Three things are decisive. First, the disposition is "set before" — *praeposita* — to use the German Latinate idiom that lies behind Heidegger's *Vorgesetztheit*: it stands in front of the possibility, it does not precede it as a separate state to which the possibility is added. Second, the possibility "pertains to me" — it must be *μοι*, mine, mattering to me — without this bearing on me, there is no fear. Third, the possibility "announces itself" — it becomes manifest *through the announcement*, *through* the σημεῖα that signal its approach.

### 6.3 The Three Moments of the Threatening: Possibility, Powerfulness, Proximity

Heidegger isolates the three structural moments of the *φοβερόν*, the threatening (pp. 169–170):

**(1) Possibility/indeterminacy.** "*φαίνεται*: it must 'show itself' as thus and so, and yet as not genuinely there. The fearsome is characterized by *possibility, being-possibility*, but in the sense of the *indeterminate*. The aspect of indeterminacy enhances, in particular the possibility, that it *can* with regard to the possible disposition of fearing. *The indeterminate alone enhances the threateningness*" (p. 170). What is fully determinate, certain, and inevitable does not threaten; it crushes. The threatening must be *possible* — could happen, may happen, is on the way — and its threateningness is *enhanced* by indeterminacy. The vague rumor of war is more threatening than the announced day of battle.

**(2) Powerfulness (δύναμιν ἔχειν μεγάλην).** "That which comes toward me with the character of '*powerfulness*,' in opposition to which I find myself in a definite weakness, can do me harm. It is constitutive of threat" (p. 170). The threatening must have, relative to me, a power against which I am weak. A wasp threatens me but does not threaten an elephant; the same wave threatens the swimmer but not the cliff. *Powerfulness* is *constitutive*, not contingent: there is no fearsome that is not *more powerful than I*.

**(3) Proximity (πλησιασμός).** "That which is thus powerful in the possibility of being-able-to-arrive, 'being brought into proximity,' *πλησιασμός*, turns the threat into 'danger.' Something threatening in the greater distance is not danger. *Threat becomes danger when it draws nearer to me*" (p. 170). Proximity converts threat into *Gefahr* — threat that is now-pressing-upon-me. Death is threat; death-three-weeks-away is danger.

The three moments are not parallel but cumulative: a fearsome-thing must be *possible*, *more powerful than I*, and *drawing near*. Where any of the three is absent, fear in its determinate form does not arise. Heidegger insists, however, on a phenomenological principle that disrupts any merely object-side account: "*There is no parallel between the frightening and being-afraid*. Not all aspects of the φοβερόν are contained in fearing, and vice versa.... Fear is, precisely, a definite finding-oneself, *a behaving with respect to oneself in being-threatened*" (p. 170). The structure of the threatening articulates one side of a phenomenon whose other side is the disposition; neither side is reducible to the other, and the phenomenon is *the structural fit between them*.

### 6.4 The Σημεῖα: Announcement and the Cultivation of Indeterminacy

The threatening does not arrive as a bare presence; it *announces itself* through *σημεῖα*. Aristotle names three: *ἔχθρα*, *ὀργή* — "hostile attitude," "rage"; *ἀδικία* — "unjust attitude"; *ἀρετὴ ὑβριζομένη* — "seriousness that ridicules is provoked." Heidegger's interpretation is illuminating: the σημεῖα *take over* the function of the threatening, "they themselves become frightening" (p. 171). The reference-direction shifts: in seeing the σημεῖα, I find myself already in the proximity of the threat; the announcement *is* the proximity.

What is decisive is that the σημεῖα work *not by determining* the threat but by *enhancing its indeterminacy*. "We will have to consider how these σημεῖα are precisely σημεῖα by virtue of the fact that they cultivate this possibility *in its very indeterminacy*" (p. 170). When I see rage in another's face I do not know *exactly* what they will do; I know something coming-toward-me-might-do-something-harmful, and the σημεῖον intensifies this by leaving the determination open. This is one of Heidegger's most penetrating phenomenological observations: *the threatening becomes more threatening as its determination becomes more open*. The "reserved, the ironic, the slick" — *πρᾶοι, εἴρωνες, πανοῦργοι* — are the *most* frightening because *one does not know where one stands with them* (p. 173). "[One does not know whether everything is all right with them, or whether they only act as though it is.] With them, it is not manifest whether a threat is near [whether they are planning something], so it is never clear that, in fact, no danger is present" (p. 173). Heidegger's language is exact: the *indeterminacy* of proximity, not its determinacy, constitutes the highest threateningness.

### 6.5 The Φοβεροί: Frightening Human Beings (§21d)

Aristotle enumerates *nine characters* of frightening human beings (*Rhet.* B 5, 1382b 4–18). Heidegger reads them as *φοβερά seen according to the orientation of being-with-one-another* — the catalogue is a phenomenology of the social structure of threat (pp. 171–173):

1. **Those to whom I am compromised** — those who know what I have done — possibility of betrayal.
2. **The powerful** — δύναμιν ἔχοντες — characterized by the dual "can" of *προαίρεσις* (they have the power *and* the possibility of resolving to use it).
3. **Those injured or believing themselves injured** — they are out to avenge.
4. **Those who have themselves injured another** — they fear retaliation and may strike preemptively.
5. **Competitors in the same matter** — capability carries threat.
6. **Those who threaten anyone who can obtain more than they** — the powerful are vulnerable to the more-powerful.
7. **Those superior who have already ruined us** — they may complete the ruin.
8. **Those weaker than us, out to ruin** — they may strike from desperation.
9. **The reserved, the ironic, the slick** — most frightening through *indeterminacy of proximity*.

The catalogue is not a list of types but a phenomenology of the *social ontology of threat*. All nine are characterized by *προαίρεσις* — they are beings capable of *resolving thus and so* — and so are *frightening-as-φοβεροί* in the specific sense that what makes them threatening is not a brute force but a *power-plus-resolution*. The φοβεροί are "*the φοβερά seen according to this orientation of being-with-one-another*" within the *πόλις* (p. 173). Heidegger's brief catalogue here is one of the few sustained phenomenologies of the social-political dimension of fear in his early work, and is in some ways unique to GA 18 — it has no direct analogue in *Being and Time*, where the analytic of *Angst* moves rapidly past the social-political determinations of fear.

### 6.6 The Genuine Φοβερόν: Unavoidability and Receding Hope (§21e)

The genuinely frightening, Heidegger says, is "*the unavoidable*, not in an absolute sense, but *only for me*" (p. 173). Two further specifications constitute genuineness:

- The unavoidability "is there with the opponent" — it is *for me with another* — "the possibility of unavoidability is not absolute, but is there with another who is hostile to me. Insofar as unavoidability is not absolute, but is there with another, and the other possesses *προαίρεσις*, it is characterized as threat."
- "What is threatening to the highest degree must, in a certain sense, hold out *the prospect of remaining absent*. The frightening becomes more genuine, *the more the prospect of help disappears*. Where there is no help, and yet it is still expected by the one threatened, the prospect of it remaining absent must still persist" (p. 173).

This second moment is decisive and counterintuitive. *Genuine fear requires hope*. If *ἐλπίς* — the hope of being saved, *ἐλπὶς σωτηρίας* — is *fully* extinguished, the result is not fear but despair, resignation, or numb acceptance. If hope is *robust*, the result is at most concern. *Fear* arises precisely in the structural configuration where hope is *receding*, where the prospect of help is *diminishing-but-not-yet-vanished*. This is the constitutive *τἀραχή* of fear: the *opposedness* of *οἴεσθαι* (believing oneself in danger) and *ἐλπίς* (hoping nonetheless to escape). Heidegger formulates it directly: "*The disquiet is nothing other than the opposedness of οἴεσθαι and ἐλπίς*: believing oneself to be lost, and hoping nonetheless. The possibility of salvation must be held fast, and in the expectant holding fast of the possibility of not-being-annihilated, the peculiar 'recoiling' from that which threatens me operates — λύπη as φυγή. The possibility of being-saved — in short, of *being*, is there, but nonetheless I recoil from being. That is the basic sense of *ταραχή*. Being-there does not depart from itself, but rather holds fast in hope of the possibility of salvation" (p. 174).

The two basic moments of *ταραχή* are *δίωξις* and *φυγή* — pursuit and flight — both, Heidegger underlines, "*basic determinations of the genuine being-moved of being-there*" (p. 174). Fear is not a static state but a *being-pulled-in-two-directions*, a structural tearing in which Dasein is simultaneously *recoiling-from* the threat and *holding-fast-to* the hope of escape. The unity of these two opposed motions *is* the πάθος.

### 6.7 Οἴεσθαι: The Dispositional Ground

The structural condition of fear, beyond the object-side analysis of the φοβερά, is the *οἴεσθαι* of the one afraid. "*Being-afraid must be characterized as an οἴεσθαι*. The one who is brought into fear must once 'believe' that the definite thing that threatens, threatens *him*, and further, that what is threatening proceeds *from this definite human being, and that he threatens him now*" (p. 174). Without this dispositional uptake — this *believing-it-to-be-the-case-for-me* — fear cannot arise.

Heidegger draws out the negative cases. "Those for whom it goes swimmingly, who regard themselves as safe from every threat, those who have riches, physical strength, followers and influence at their disposal. These are never brought into fear; with them there arises a distinctive disdain, arrogance, and impudence" (p. 174, *Rhet.* B 5, 1382b 35 sqq.). And another negative case: "Those who have a different *οἴεσθαι*: those who believe that nothing more can happen to them since they have already been through everything. The 'hardened' stand outside the possibility of fear" (p. 174). These two negative cases — the arrogant and the hardened — are not failures of perception (they may see the threat clearly) but failures of *appropriating* the threat into *belief about themselves*. "This type of appropriating genuine being-there into one's beliefs about it must be shifted; it must come into a definite *οἴεσθαι*, so that what is frightening in general can come into my proximity" (p. 174).

Two consequences follow. First, the rhetorician who would cultivate fear in the audience cannot rely on simply describing threats — he must *shift the disposition* of the hearer, *bring them into an οἴεσθαι* in which the threat-in-general can become threat-for-me. Aristotle's catalogue in *Rhetoric* II is the technical guide for *just this work*. Second, the structural truth disclosed by the negative cases is that *fear is a mode of self-relation, not merely a relation to an object*: it is a *finding-oneself-as-vulnerable-to-this*. Where vulnerability is denied or has dissolved, fear is impossible. This is the structural truth that lies behind the Augustinian thesis Heidegger cites at p. 175: "*initium sapientiae timor Domini*" — fear is the beginning of wisdom because it is the form in which Dasein *finds itself as the kind of being whose being is at issue*.

### 6.8 Fear as Πίστις, Courage as Ἕξις, the Πάθη as Ground of Λόγος

The conclusion Heidegger draws from the φόβος-analysis ties the three master claims of the entire pathos-analysis together. *First*, fear *brings to speaking*. "Insofar as *οἴεσθαι* and *ἐλπίς* bring human beings into this disquiet, they become ready to *deliberate*. Human beings who are brought into fear run to one another to confer, to get counsel. If I allow people to be brought into fear, if I make out political events as dangerous, I thereby make people ready for, and inclined toward, conferring. I make them into those who contribute to the realization of an intended decision; I do this for the purpose of their becoming themselves *πίστις*" (p. 175). Fear is not merely *an* affect that rhetoric exploits; it is the *condition of the possibility* of certain forms of deliberative speaking. To bring an audience to fear is *to bring it to discourse*.

*Second*, courage (*ἀνδρεία*) is the *ἕξις of fear* — the settled manner of being-afraid in the right way. "*Fear has, as a determinate πάθος, the possibility of a ἕξις. Such a possibility is courage. ... Fear is the condition of the possibility of courage*" (p. 175). The thesis is sharply counter-Stoic: courage is not the *absence* of fear but the cultivated form of fearing-rightly. "Whoever is not afraid vis-à-vis persuading himself not to be afraid (which is the case most of the time), does not yet get around to making a decision in the right sense, and being courageous. It is a question of *taking hold of* courage. It is a question of *being afraid in the right manner*, and thereby coming to resoluteness" (p. 175). Courage *presupposes* fear: only the one who has felt fear in the proper structural form can have the cultivated disposition that holds firm in its presence. The fearless are not courageous; they are without the existential field in which courage could be cultivated.

*Third*, and this is the macro-conclusion of the entire Chapter 3 of the First Part, the πάθη are the *ground of λόγος*. "Insofar as the πάθη are not merely an annex of psychical processes, but are rather *the ground out of which speaking arises, and which what is expressed grows back into*, the πάθη, for their part, are *the basic possibilities in which being-there itself is primarily oriented toward itself*, finds itself. The primary being-oriented, the illumination of its being-in-the-world is not a *knowing*, but rather a *finding-oneself*" (p. 176, italics in the original). This is the central thesis of GA 18 on the πάθη, and it should be heard with full weight: the higher-order emotions are not psychical processes that accompany or color a discourse already underway. They are the *soil* out of which discourse springs, and *back into which it sinks* when the situation closes. Speaking arises *because* being-there finds itself in the modes of fear, anger, shame, pity, kindness — and what is spoken is *taken up* by the πάθη again as the situation re-stabilizes around the new κρίσις. "Only within the thus characterized finding-oneself and being-in-the-world is it possible to speak about things, insofar as they are stripped of the look they have in immediate relations" (p. 176). The order of philosophical priority is reversed: *λόγος* is not the master of *πάθος*; *πάθος* is the antecedent ground of *λόγος*.

The systematic remark Heidegger appends is sharp: "Only if one sees being-there in this way can one set the πάθη back in place. Only from this standpoint can one understand what was a strain for the Greeks, who were to a certain degree in love with *λόγος*: to work their way toward a concreteness, from out of the field of discussion and idle chatter. Only thus can we understand that it is false when one holds Greece in general to be a fantastical place, as if things just fell into the lap of these distinguished men" (p. 176). The Greek achievement, for Heidegger, was the discovery — against the temptation of mere speech-making — that genuine speaking arises from and returns to the πάθη as the modes of bodily being-in-the-world, and that the rhetorical-philosophical analysis of the πάθη is therefore the propaedeutic to all genuine *λόγος*.

## 7. The Anatomy of a Higher-Order Emotion: The Generic Heideggerian Schema

Synthesizing across §§16, 18, 20, and 21, a generic schema for any higher-order πάθος in Heidegger's reading can now be set out. Each of the πάθη Aristotle treats in *Rhetoric* II — anger, calm, friendship, enmity, fear, confidence, shame, kindness, pity, indignation, envy, emulation — is a determinate concretion of the same five-fold ontological structure.

**(1) The disposition (πῶς ἔχοντες).** Each πάθος is a determinate manner of *finding-oneself-disposed*. It is not an inner state that the human being inspects; it is the way the human being *is taken* in this moment, in this situation. Anger is finding-oneself-as-having-been-slighted-by-a-particular-person-who-could-have-acted-otherwise; shame is finding-oneself-as-having-fallen-short-before-an-audience-that-matters; pity is finding-oneself-as-confronted-with-undeserved-suffering-of-one-with-whom-I-could-imagine-myself.

**(2) The toward-which (ποίοις) and toward-whom (ἐπὶ ποίοις).** Each πάθος has a determinate intentional structure: it is *about* this and *with respect to* these. The toward-which articulates the world-side of the disposition (what is at issue); the toward-whom articulates its social-ontological side (the persons to whom and before whom it is articulated). Anger is *with respect to* the slight, *toward* the slighter; pity is *with respect to* undeserved harm, *toward* the one suffering. The two together articulate the *aspectual* disclosure that the πάθος accomplishes — the way the situation is given *as* this-and-that, *for me*.

**(3) The bodily form (the *λόγος ἔνυλος*).** Each πάθος has a determinate bodily articulation that is not symptomatic but constitutive. The hot rush of anger, the cold of fear, the hot flush of shame, the welling of the eyes in pity — these are *part of* the way the πάθος *is* in the world. To strip a πάθος of its bodily form is to lose the phenomenon. The bodily form is *λόγος-articulated* — it has the *eidos* of the slight, the threat, the fall — but it is *λόγος-ἔνυλος*, "enmattered," meaning-bodily-bodied-forth.

**(4) The futural-temporal moment.** Each πάθος is structured by a determinate temporality. Fear is the futural-coming-toward; anger is the present-recoil-from-having-been-slighted-with-a-prospective-pay-back; shame is the present-recoil-from-having-publicly-fallen-short with an indeterminate future of being-thus-marked; pity has a present-confrontation-with another's present-suffering with a backward look at the events that produced it. Each πάθος articulates being-there in its three temporal ecstases — having-been, present, projected-futural — but with a *characteristic profile* that distinguishes one πάθος from another.

**(5) The disclosive function: bringing to κρίσις, bringing to λόγος.** Each πάθος shifts the hearer toward *taking-a-position*. Each opens or closes specific possibilities of *κρίσις* — view-construction, decision. Each cultivates a disposition that, at its limit, *brings to speech*: anger brings the slighted to confront, fear brings the threatened to confer, shame brings the fallen-short to defend or reform, pity brings the witness to alleviate. The cultivation of the πάθος is the cultivation of the *bringing-to-discourse*, and rhetoric, on Heidegger's reading, is the systematic art of just this cultivation.

These five moments together articulate the generic structure of the higher-order πάθος. Each Aristotelian emotion in *Rhetoric* II is a specific filling-out of the five — a determinate concretion in which the disposition, the world-side and social-side intentionality, the bodily form, the temporality, and the discursive function are *just so*, and not otherwise. The catalogue of *Rhetoric* B 2–11 is not a list of psychological types; it is a phenomenology of the determinate ways in which a finite, embodied, speaking being whose way of being is *zōē praktikē meta logou* finds itself disposed before the matters that concern it, in the *πόλις* with others who matter.

## 8. The Πάθη and the Ἕξεις: The Cultivation Question (§17)

The five-fold schema would remain merely descriptive if it were not embedded in the structural relation Heidegger establishes between πάθος and ἕξις in §17. *Hexis* — "having" in the sense of a settled comportment — is not a categorial supplement to πάθος; it is the structural counterpart through which πάθος becomes, for the human being, *cultivated*. "*Hexis is nothing other than a how of πάθος, being-out-of-composure, in relation to being-composed-as-to*" (p. 125). The two are co-original: *to be a being capable of πάθος is to be a being whose πάθη can be settled into ἕξεις*.

This structural co-originality has decisive consequences for the higher-order emotions. *First*, each πάθος has a *characteristic ἕξις-correlate*: courage as the ἕξις of fear, magnanimity as the ἕξις of anger, modesty as the ἕξις of shame, philanthropic disposition as the ἕξις of pity. The ἕξις is not the *absence* of the πάθος but its *cultivated form* — the manner in which the human being has come to "have" the πάθος in the right way, at the right moment, in relation to the right things, with the right intensity. *Aretē* is precisely this: *ἕξις προαιρετική, ἐν μεσότητι οὖσα τῇ πρὸς ἡμᾶς, ὡρισμένη λόγῳ καὶ ὡς ἂν ὁ φρόνιμος ὁρίσειεν* (p. 130, *NE* B 6, 1106b 36 sq.). Virtue is the cultivated ἕξις in which the human being is composed before the πάθη in the manner that the *φρόνιμος* — the one who can look around in the moment — would determine.

*Second*, the cultivation of ἕξις is itself a temporal-historical structure. *Aretē comes to be di' ethous*, "through habit," but Heidegger insists this is not mechanical repetition. "Repetition does not mean the bringing-into-play of a settled completedness, but rather *acting anew in every moment on the basis of the corresponding resolution*. Cultivating ἕξις never depends on an operation, a routine. *In an operation, the moment is destroyed*" (p. 128). The temporal character of *aretē* is "the *frequently of repetition*" (p. 129) — the structure by which being-there *acts anew* in each moment, drawing on the cultivated capacity, but not allowing the cultivation to displace the moment. This is decisive for the πάθη: the courageous person is not the person who has *eliminated* fear or has reduced fear-response to a habit; the courageous person is the one who, *in this moment, before this threat*, fears in the manner that just-this-situation calls for.

*Third*, the cultivation of *aretē* is *meson pros hēmas* — "mean in relation to us." There is no absolute mean for the cultivation of the πάθη; the right disposition before threat is not a fixed quantity but a structural fitting-to-the-situation in which the *φρόνιμος* sees what is *μέσον* now. "*For our being, characterized by particularity, no unique and absolute norm can be given*" (p. 126). This is one of Heidegger's strongest formulations of the existential particularity of being-there: the higher-order emotions cannot be cultivated by following a rule, because the right cultivation is always the right cultivation *for this being-there in this moment in this situation*.

*Fourth*, the relation of ἕξις to *kairos* — the moment, the critical opportunity — defines the cultivated form of every πάθος. "In ἕξις lies the primary orientation toward the kairos: 'I am there, come what may!'" (p. 119). The cultivated πάθος is the πάθος that finds itself in the *kairos* — that is *open to* the determining moment and able to compose itself toward what the moment demands. This is, for Heidegger, the structural meaning of *aretē* as *ἕξις προαιρετική*: a settled capacity whose actualization is always a fresh resolution-in-the-moment.

The architecture is now complete. The πάθη are *modes of being-taken*; the δυνάμεις are the *standing capacities for those modes*; the ἕξεις are the *cultivated manners of being-in-the-modes*; *aretē* is the *cultivated manner that fits the kairos*. Each Aristotelian higher-order emotion has its place in this architecture: as a determinate πάθος (anger, fear, shame), as a δύναμις (capacity to be brought into anger, fear, shame), as a possible ἕξις (irascibility, cowardice, shamelessness — or magnanimity, courage, modesty), and as a possible *aretē* (the cultivated form of having the πάθος in the manner the *φρόνιμος* would determine).

## 9. Synthesis: The Five Theses

Drawing the threads of the analysis together, Heidegger's reading of the higher-order πάθη of *Rhetoric* II in GA 18 issues in five master theses, each of which redirects the philosophical analysis of the emotions away from the post-Cartesian psychological framework.

**Thesis I: The πάθη are not psychic experiences but bodily modes of being-in-the-world.** "*The πάθη are not 'psychic experiences,' are not 'in consciousness,' but a being-taken of human beings in their full being-in-the-world*" (p. 133). To analyze a higher-order emotion is not to describe an inner state but to articulate a determinate manner in which a finite, embodied, speaking being finds itself in the situation, with others, before what concerns it.

**Thesis II: The πάθη are *λόγοι ἔνυλοι* — bodily-articulated meanings.** The eidetic structure of a πάθος (the "look" that anger or fear has) is inseparable from its bodily articulation. The dialectician's definition (anger as orexis-of-pay-back) and the natural philosopher's definition (anger as boiling of blood at the heart) are both *λόγοι*, and the *protos philosophos* must take both together. The bodily moments are *constitutive*, not symptomatic.

**Thesis III: The πάθη are co-original with the ἕξεις.** Πάθος and ἕξις are fundamental concepts of being, articulating the being-structure of beings whose way of being is *zōē praktikē meta logou*. Each πάθος has its possible ἕξις-correlate (courage as the ἕξις of fear, magnanimity as the ἕξις of anger), and *aretē* is the cultivated ἕξις that fits the *kairos*.

**Thesis IV: The πάθη are temporally articulated, ecstatic, and disclosive.** Each higher-order emotion has a characteristic temporal profile (fear is futural, anger is present-with-prospective-payback, pity is present-with-retrospect, shame is present-fall-with-indeterminate-future). The πάθη articulate being-there's three temporal ecstases — having-been, present, projected-futural — in determinate concretions, and they *disclose* the world in tonal aspects (as threatening, as slighting, as worthy of pity, as shame-inducing) that *λόγος* presupposes.

**Thesis V: The πάθη are the ground of λόγος.** "*The πάθη are the ground out of which speaking arises, and which what is expressed grows back into*; they are *the basic possibilities in which being-there itself is primarily oriented toward itself*, finds itself" (p. 176). Speaking is not a master operation that draws on the πάθη as resources; speaking *arises out of* the πάθη and *returns to* them. The rhetorical art is the cultivation of the dispositional ground that brings being-there to discourse, and Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II is therefore not a manual of persuasion-techniques but a phenomenology of the disclosive structures through which being-there finds itself in the *πόλις* with others.

These five theses, taken together, constitute Heidegger's recovery of the Aristotelian analysis of the higher-order emotions from the philosophical-psychological tradition. The πάθη are not psychological phenomena to be added to a prior account of cognition; they are the *prior* — they are the modes in which being-there has its world disclosed to it, and on the basis of which both cognition and speaking become possible. To analyze anger, fear, shame, pity, indignation, kindness — the family of higher-order emotions Aristotle catalogues in *Rhetoric* II — is, on Heidegger's reading, to undertake the *phenomenology of disclosedness* itself: the inquiry into how a finite, embodied, speaking being finds itself in the world before any explicit knowing or saying, and *on the basis of which* knowing and saying become possible at all.

Heidegger's reading throughout works *through* the Aristotelian text rather than around it. The interpretation does not impose existential categories on a foreign material; it draws out, in the language of phenomenology, the structural features that Aristotle's own *λόγος* circumscribes. The 1924 lectures are accordingly best read as a sustained *recovery* — of the Aristotelian phenomenon-analysis from the post-Aristotelian philosophical-psychological tradition that has, in Heidegger's view, settled around it without seeing it.
