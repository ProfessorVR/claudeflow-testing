Martin Heidegger, Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy

GA 18 · Marburg Lectures, Summer Semester 1924 · trans. Metcalf & Tanzer (Indiana UP, 2009)

§15. Δόξα.

(Nicomachean Ethics Ζ10 and Γ4)
Book pp. 93–108 · PDF pp. 108–123

Orienting Thesis of §15

§15 is the systematic threshold of Chapter 3 of the First Part. Heidegger has just completed the analysis of πίστις (pistis, “credence,” “trust”) as the basic phenomenon disclosed by the Rhetoric; he now turns to δόξα (doxa, translated by Aristotle’s English tradition as “opinion”) as the ontological matrix within which πίστις (pistis) operates. His decisive move is to refuse the standard epistemological reading of δόξα as defective knowing. Instead, δόξα (doxa) names the average, everyday discoveredness (Entdecktheit) of being-with-one-another in the world—the way being-there initially has its world there in an average way. Δόξα (doxa) is “to have a view” (eine Ansicht haben), “ἔνδοξον (endoxon, ‘reputable,’ ‘held-in-view’)”: a positive ontological structure of being-in-the-world, prior to and grounding both rhetorical persuasion and theoretical science. It is, decisively, “the basis from which science in general arises” (p. 99) and “the characteristic trust in that which shows itself initially” (p. 103). Heidegger thus reads δόξα (doxa) not against ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē) but as that which underlies it—a 1924 anticipation of the doctrine of everydayness (Alltäglichkeit) and das Man (“the they”) in Being and Time.

§15a. Demarcation of Δόξα (doxa) in Contrast with Ζήτησις (zētēsis), Ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē), and Φαντασία (phantasia)

pp. 93–94 · NE Ζ 10, 1142b 11 ff.

The Opening Move: Δόξα (doxa) as Φάσις (phasis), Not Ζήτησις (zētēsis)

Heidegger anchors his analysis in Nicomachean Ethics Ζ Chapter 10, where Aristotle distinguishes δόξα (doxa) from three closely-related phenomena. The opening contrast is with ζήτησις (zētēsis, “seeking,” “investigating”):

“According to Aristotle, δόξα (doxa) is οὐ ζήτησις (ou zētēsis), ‘not a seeking,’ but rather φάσις τις ἤδη (phasis tis ēdē): ‘I have a view already.’ I do not seek first; I am not, at first, on the way to the ascertaining of the structure of a matter, but I am situated thus and so toward the matter. Φάσις (phasis): a certain λέγειν (legein), a certain yes-saying to that of which I have a view.” (p. 93; cit. NE Ζ 10, 1142b 14)

The decisive structural feature: δόξα (doxa) is characterized by its being a yes-saying and not an investigating, a reflecting, a coming-first-to-a-view. It stands at the end of seeing, not at its beginning. Φάσις (phasis) names this character: δόξα (doxa) asserts, it holds a view—as already-in-place—not as a result of inquiry but as the antecedent ground from which inquiry could even begin.

Δόξα (doxa) vs. Ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē): The Question of Ὀρθότης (orthotēs) and Possible Falsity

The second contrast is with ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē, “scientific knowing,” “knowing information”). Aristotle (NE Ζ 10, 1142b 11) draws the distinction through ὀρθότης (orthotēs, “direction,” “correctness,” “being-directed”):

“If I know definite information about something, it belongs to the sense of this knowing that what is known cannot be ‘false,’ cannot be ψευδές (pseudes), since in that case it would not be ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē). Δόξα (doxa) must possess ὀρθότης (orthotēs), to which belongs ‘direction’ toward, ‘being-directed’ toward ἀλήθεια (alētheia). Having-a-view is thus only a view; it could also be otherwise. In itself, δόξα (doxa) is true and false. It could be thus, and could also be otherwise. Being-directed toward ἀλήθεια (alētheia) is constitutive of δόξα (doxa), and therefore the possibility of ψεῦδος (pseudos) belongs to it.” (pp. 93–94)

Heidegger underlines that “Plato (Theaetetus, Sophist, Philebus) could not yet see that this ‘it could be otherwise’ also belongs to the view itself” (p. 94). The crucial Aristotelian advance: in δόξα (doxa), I do not absolutely claim that “it is so,” but instead that it could be otherwise; we suppose within a certain φάσις (phasis). The δυνατόν—ἀδύνατον (dynaton—adynaton, “possible—impossible”) structure is intrinsic to δόξα (doxa) itself. Ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē) is structurally incapable of falsity; δόξα (doxa) is structurally capable of both.

Ἐνδεχόμενον (endechomenon): The Region of “Beings That Can Also Be Otherwise”

“Ἔνδοξον (endoxon) is the manner of being-oriented in which one is oriented toward beings that can also be otherwise. There is the possibility of the view being revised. With respect to beings that always are how and what they are, with regard to ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē), there is no revision. On the other hand, revisability belongs to δόξα (doxa) in itself; it is assumed of itself.” (p. 94)

This is the first ontological characterization of δόξα (doxa) proper:

“Δόξα (doxa) is the mode in which the world of being-with-one-another is there. The possibility of a speaking-against-one-another in being-with-one-another is thereby brought about. That one has this view and another has that view, since beings can be otherwise, is the basic possibility of being-against-one-another. Δόξα (doxa) is the mode in which we have living there in its everydayness.” (p. 94)

The Programmatic Conclusion: Δόξα (doxa) as the Mode in Which Living Knows from Out of Itself

“Living knows from out of itself, not in the way of science, theoretically; that is only a distinct possibility. Δόξα (doxa) is the mode in which living knows from out of itself. The aim of λόγοι ῥητορικοί (logoi rhētorikoi), the cultivation of πιστεύειν (pisteuein, ‘trusting,’ ‘crediting’), is nothing other than the cultivation of a δόξα (doxa), of the right view of a matter. Therefore, πιστεύειν (pisteuein) belongs to δόξα (doxa); a certain πίστις (pistis), a φάσις (phasis), accompanies δόξα (doxa) itself. Therefore, animals have no δόξα (doxa) since they have no λόγος (logos); a φάσις (phasis) is impossible for them. The there for such a being is different.” (p. 94)

Δόξα (doxa) vs. Φαντασία (phantasia): The Third Demarcation

Heidegger anticipates the third Aristotelian distinction (which he will treat more fully in §15c): φαντασία (phantasia, “making-present,” “presenting-itself”) is “a determinate ‘making-present’ of something that can also be true or false, but in a different sense than δόξα (doxa).” Aristotle treats this difference in De Anima Γ 3 (cit. Γ 3, 428a 18 ff.). For now, the key point: “Φαντασία (phantasia) is simple having-present. Therefore, animals possess φαντασία too, while δόξα (doxa) is only there where λόγος (logos) is” (p. 96).

§15b. Making-Present the Context for the Treatment of Δόξα (doxa)

pp. 94–95

The Hermeneutic Re-Anchoring

Heidegger interrupts the schematic demarcation to re-anchor the analysis in its larger context: human being-there is being-in-a-world, “more precisely as being-with-one-another, the having-with-one-another of the world, in which one is” (p. 94). This having-with-one-another is a dealing with the world as concern—a dealing characterized by depending on being itself in this being-in-the-world. Εὐδαιμονία (eudaimonia, “flourishing,” “genuine being-well”) “is taken into concern.”

“Being-with-one-another in the mode of speaking-with-one-another possesses the basic determination of speaking-with-one-another; λόγος (logos) is a basic phenomenon of κοινωνία (koinōnia, ‘community,’ ‘being-in-common’). Λόγος (logos) has the basic function of making-manifest that within which living as being-in-a-world maintains itself: δηλοῦν (dēloun). Being-in-the-world is a way of being that has uncovered the world; being-in is oriented, the in-which is uncovered.” (p. 94)

The Programmatic Statement: Conceptuality as a Basic Possibility

“Ultimately, we want to set forth the phenomenon of being-in in order to understand, on this basis, conceptuality as a basic possibility. Being-in-the-world is the basic character of being-there with regard to its discoveredness: the world as that with which living deals within a certain familiarity in a concernful, speaking way. This familiarity regarding the world, and dealing and living in it, is borne by speaking as the peculiar exhibiting of that to which one is oriented. At the same time, this familiarity is the mode in which views and orientations are cultivated. Views are cultivated, renewed, established, hardened in speaking.” (p. 95)

The decisive methodological claim: speaking-with-one-another is “the clue to the uncovering of the basic phenomenon of the discoveredness of being-there itself as being-in-a-world.” And this is why rhetoric is taken “as a concrete guide insofar as it is nothing other than the interpretation of being-there with regard to the basic possibility of speaking-with-one-another” (p. 95).

Three Πίστεις (pisteis) Analogous to the Structure of Speaking

Heidegger reminds the reader of the three πίστεις (pisteis) Aristotle distinguishes, “analogous to the structure of speaking itself” (Rhet. Α 1, 1355b 26):

  1. Speaking about something — περὶ τινος δηλοῦν, δεικνύναι (peri tinos dēloun, deiknynai);
  2. Speaking to someone — πρός τινα (pros tina); he who is spoken to is the ἀκούων (akouōn), the listener (πάθος);
  3. Speaking-being itself — λέγων (legōn) (ἦθος).

In all of these, “what is spoken about shows itself, with the basic determination that it could be otherwise; from time to time it differs.” The being that is under discussion in everydayness is not the ἀεὶ ὄν (aei on, “always-being”), but rather the ἐνδεχόμενον καὶ ἄλλως ἔχειν (endechomenon kai allōs echein, “that which can simultaneously be more or less what it precisely is”) (p. 95).

The Καιρός (kairos)-Anchor

“Aristotle defines the τέλος (telos) of πρᾶξις (praxis) as a τέλος κατὰ τὸν καιρόν (telos kata ton kairon). One sees ‘in the moment’ how, where, when, to whom, in the fixing of dealings according to being. A characteristic of concern appears in view of the καιρός (kairos)—it always vacillates; it does not stand fast.” (p. 95; cit. NE Γ 1, 1110a 13)

And the conclusion of §15b, which sets up §15c:

“Regarding these beings, it is valid to state definite views, to cultivate others, to bring being-there into δόξα (doxa), to carry an ἔνδοξον (endoxon) about the world forward. Thus we come up against the basic mode in which the world of such concern is possessed: δόξα (doxa), translated as ‘having a view about something,’ ‘about that, I am of the view,’ ‘I am for …’ Aristotle dealt with this basic phenomenon of δόξα (doxa) quite often and in various ways. It is the phenomenon that is widely discussed in the late Platonic dialogues, above all in the Theaetetus, Philebus, and Sophist.” (p. 95)

Heidegger states a self-evident hermeneutical rule: “these phenomena discussed by Plato can be approached through Aristotle, where they are extended further and become clearer than they are in Plato. The perspective is opened up for that which Plato did not see, a perspective that has a fundamental significance for a basic part of Platonic philosophy” (p. 95). This is the methodological principle that grounds Heidegger’s entire reading procedure: read Plato through Aristotle, because Aristotle articulates explicitly what Plato could only see implicitly.

§15c. Repetition and Continuation of the Demarcation: Δόξα (doxa) and Being-Resolved (Προαίρεσις)

pp. 95–101 · NE Ζ 10 (recap) and Γ 4

The Three Aspects Recapitulated

Heidegger characterizes δόξα (doxa) more schematically “without going into a genuine interpretation of the section in question” (p. 95). Three aspects of δόξα (doxa) stand out as demarcated in opposition to three related phenomena:

1. Δόξα (doxa) vs. Βουλεύεσθαι (bouleuesthai) / Ζήτησις (zētēsis)

Βουλεύεσθαι (bouleuesthai, “deliberating”): “a being-after a definite view that I want to achieve. Through deliberating, I want primarily to come to the τέλος (telos) of a δόξα (doxa); it is not a yes. I only want to appropriate a definite view regarding a set of facts, whereas δόξα (doxa) is no longer a seeing, but stands at the end of seeing; it is a φάσις (phasis). But although it is a yes, it is still not a knowing” (p. 96; cit. NE Ζ 10, 1142a 31; 1142b 13).

2. Δόξα (doxa) vs. Ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē): The Region of Ἀεί vs. Ἐνδεχόμενον

“The basic presupposition for the possibility of knowing is a presupposition regarding the being which is known—that it is always the same as it is, that it cannot change. Something that is not ἀεί (aei) can change. In ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē), I do not need to have the matter actually there. Regarding the ἐνδεχόμενον (endechomenon), there is no ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē), but only δόξα (doxa). Still, δόξα (doxa) is akin to ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē) since it is a yes-saying, a φάσις (phasis). It is determined in opposition to ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē) through the ὀρθότης (orthotēs). In δόξα (doxa), I do not have the being itself, but rather an orientation in relation to it, which is directed to the ἀληθές (alēthes).” (p. 96; cit. NE Ζ 10, 1142b 10)

The view has the tendency to intend the being unconcealed in itself—“but it is found in δόξα (doxa) itself that it is only a conception that, as conception, is likely false. With δόξα (doxa), the matter can indeed be false—it is not an absolute claim. In the being of being-a-view is found the concession that it can be this way or that way; it can also be otherwise. Δόξα (doxa) with its ὀρθότης (orthotēs) is thereby distinguished from” ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē) (p. 96).

3. Δόξα (doxa) vs. Φαντασία (phantasia): The Distinction in De Anima Γ 3

Aristotle treats the difference in De Anima Γ 3, 428a 1 ff. Φαντασία (phantasia) can also be false, but in a different sense than δόξα (doxa). The decisive distinction:

“Since φαντασία (phantasia) can also be false, it is related to δόξα (doxa). But how can it be false? Because its turning out to be in fact true or false is added to it, while in δόξα (doxa) the being-able-to-be-true-or-false is already contained in the formation of the view. Φαντασία (phantasia) is simple having-present. Therefore, animals possess φαντασία (phantasia) too, while δόξα (doxa) is only there where λόγος (logos) is.” (p. 96)

Aristotle’s formula at De an. Γ 3, 428a 18 ff. (which Heidegger underlines): “πάσῃ μὲν δόξῃ ἀκολουθεῖ πίστις, πίστει δὲ λόγος: τῶν δὲ θηρίων ἐνίοις φαντασία μὲν ὑπάρχει, λόγος δ᾽ οὔ” (pasē men doxē akolouthei pistis, pistei de logos: tōn de thēriōn eniois phantasia men hyparchei, logos d’ ou)—“To every δόξα (doxa) πίστις (pistis) follows; to πίστις (pistis) follows λόγος (logos); but in some animals φαντασία (phantasia) is present, but not λόγος (logos).” This is the textual locus on which the entire 1924 reading of the Rhetoric rests: δόξα → πίστις → λόγος, three structurally linked moments of human (not animal) being-in-the-world.

4. The Decisive Distinction: Δόξα (doxa) vs. Προαίρεσις (proairesis)

Aristotle draws this distinction at NE Γ 4. “At first glance, this juxtaposition of δόξα (doxa) and προαίρεσις (proairesis, ‘resolution,’ ‘being-resolved’) is surprising. One does not immediately see that being-resolved about something and having-a-view about something have anything to do with each other. It should be noted that προαίρεσις (proairesis) had been interpreted by previous philosophy, by Plato, as a certain kind of δόξα (doxa). Therefore, there must be certain aspects of the phenomenon of προαίρεσις (proairesis) that allow this interpretation” (p. 97).

“This becomes clear when we translate δόξα (doxa) correctly: ‘I am for maintaining the matter is thus and so.’ But being-for-something can, then, also mean: I am for a matter being done thus and so. But that I am resolved that a matter be done thus and so is προαίρεσις (proairesis).” (p. 97)

The Four-fold Demarcation of Προαίρεσις (proairesis)

Aristotle demarcates προαίρεσις (proairesis) itself in four directions (NE Γ 4, 1111b 10 ff.):

  1. Vs. ἐπιθυμία (epithymia, “being-inclined,” “having-an-impulse”): being-impulsive about something;
  2. Vs. θυμός (thymos, “being-in-excitement,” “being-aroused”): having-passion for …;
  3. Vs. βούλησις (boulēsis, “wish”): wishing-for-something;
  4. Vs. δόξα (doxa).

The first three are characterized only briefly “for the purpose of understanding προαίρεσις (proairesis) since προαίρεσις (proairesis) is, indeed, a determination of ἀρετή (aretē). Ἀρετή (aretē) as disposal over a being-possibility is further explicated as ἕξις (hexis), a ‘having-alongside-oneself’ of a definite condition and so; ἕξις προαιρετική (hexis proairetikē), ‘possibility of being-resolved to…,’ being able to thus resolve oneself in a definite moment” (p. 97; cit. NE Β 6, 1106b 36).

Why the Four-fold Grouping?

Heidegger pauses for a methodological reflection: “Why is προαίρεσις (proairesis) generally set in opposition to the four above phenomena? This question must be posed in every interpretation of an Aristotelian analysis because it clarifies the basic character of the phenomenon. Grouping together such phenomena that possess in themselves such concrete aspects depends on their having a definite kinship by virtue of these aspects.” (p. 98)

“The five phenomena are fully characterized as being-after something, with the character of having-in-advance, so that what one is after is there in advance in a particular way—προαίρεσις (proairesis). The toward-which is there from the outset. This being-after something with the character of the in-advance is found in ἐπιθυμία (epithymia) exactly as it is in θυμός (thymos). It is fully explicit in the case of wish. A being-after something in the direction of the ἀληθές (alēthes) is also found in δόξα (doxa). To opine that the matter is thus and so lies in the view itself. This being-after something—something that I do not yet generally possess, but which already occupies me nevertheless—is the phenomenon that motivates bringing these various phenomena together with προαίρεσις (proairesis).” (p. 98)

Why Προαίρεσις (proairesis) is Not the Same as the First Three

Aristotle (NE Γ 4, 1111b 12): “οὐ γὰρ κοινὸν ἡ προαίρεσις καὶ τῶν ἀλόγων, ἐπιθυμία δὲ καὶ θυμός”—“There is no προαίρεσις (proairesis), no being-resolved, for living things that do not speak. Speaking, deliberating, belong to προαίρεσις (proairesis). Only a resolution that passes through deliberation is a genuine resolution.” In NE Β 2 (cit. 1139b 5), resolution is characterized as ὄρεξις διανοητική (orexis dianoētikē, “a striving determined by thorough consideration”)—a being-after that is determined by thorough deliberation, by διαβούλευσις (diaboulēsis).

The example: “He who lacks self-control acts ἐπιθυμῶν (epithymōn) such that he goes off after the matter. But this going-off is not a resolute acting. The self-controlled acts resolutely, but he does not need to be the ἐπιθυμῶν. Ἐπιθυμία (epithymia) and θυμός (thymos) go after a ἡδύ (hēdy, ‘pleasant’) and a λυπηρόν (lypēron, ‘painful’) that disposition supports and tones down. Προαίρεσις (proairesis) goes after the πρακτόν (prakton), that which is decisive for a concern in the moment, that which comes into question for it. That is what the resolution brings together. Orientation toward the right moment belongs to προαίρεσις. Προαίρεσις is not a so-called act; it is a genuine possibility of being in the moment” (p. 98).

The Three Ways Προαίρεσις (proairesis) is Distinguished from Βούλησις (boulēsis)

Aristotle distinguishes προαίρεσις (proairesis) from βούλησις (boulēsis) (NE Γ 4, 1111b 19 ff.):

  • Προαίρεσις (proairesis) never goes after something that is impossible. “If someone wanted to say that he is resolved to an impossibility, we would say that he is foolish.”
  • Βούλησις (boulēsis) can be directed at something impossible. “Wishing… can be directed at something that is impossible.” Aristotle’s example (1111b 22): we wish “that the actor, or such and such a person, that takes part in a competition, receive the prize.” That is possible but is not under our control.
  • Προαίρεσις (proairesis) always goes after something that is under our control. “Προαίρεσις (proairesis) leads to the ἔσχατον (eschaton, ‘the extreme,’ ‘the final point’), to the point that I grasp, that I genuinely institute through action.” (p. 99)

The Seven Differences Between Δόξα (doxa) and Προαίρεσις (proairesis)

Aristotle now “brings forward seven differences between προαίρεσις (proairesis) and δόξα (doxa)” (p. 99). Heidegger lists them numerically:

#Difference
1 Toward-which. Προαίρεσις (proairesis) is directed toward beings with regard to which I can accomplish something—the ἀρχὴ πράξεως (archē praxeōs) must be up to me; toward a συμφέρον (sumpheron, “the conducive”), “something that comes into question to concern, as dealt with in such a way that I can take it in hand.” Δόξα (doxa) is not only directed toward συμφέροντα (sumpheronta), “that which can be changed, but also toward that which is ἀεί (aei).” (NE Γ 4, 1111b 31)
2 Genuine direction. Δόξα (doxa) goes after the ἀληθές (alēthes) and the ψευδές (pseudes); δόξα depends upon grasping it in its being: that which a view is about. Προαίρεσις (proairesis) depends upon how it should be done, what should happen with it, that which is posited in a resolution—a πρακτὸν ἀγαθόν (prakton agathon, “a doable good”).
3 Disinterestedness of δόξα. “Whoever has a view is not otherwise determined in their ἦθος (ēthos) by this having-a-view. Having-a-view about a definite matter is not an ‘ethical’ determination; it does not pertain to changing the general comportment of human beings according to their being. Alternatively, the manner and mode in which I am resolved, what stands in προαίρεσις (proairesis), is decisive for my being, for the manner and mode in which I am, for my ἦθος (ēthos). Thus δόξα points to a certain indifference with relation to being. Having-a-view presupposes a certain disinterestedness with regard to that which the view is about. This is important for the Greek conception of science.” (NE Γ 4, 1112a 1)
4 The being-character of the toward-which. In one case the ἀληθές (alēthes) and in the other case the πρακτόν (prakton): having-a-view as comporting itself points to a definite seeing, to the manner and mode in which beings are there, in which it deals with beings as beings. Προαίρεσις (proairesis) is directed at the “seizing” and “renouncing” of a matter; δόξα is directed at ἀληθεύειν (alētheuein, “disclosing-truthfully”). (NE Γ 4, 1112a 3)
5 Genuineness. Δόξα depends on ὀρθότης (orthotēs), by its approaching the ἀληθές, the being as it is. Προαίρεσις, on the other hand, is not concerned with setting forth beings in their being. It is decisive for προαίρεσις that it be suitably reckoned. It does not depend on setting forth all of the being-aspects of a concrete situation, on theoretically describing them; but instead προαίρεσις depends on deliberating correctly, on keeping an eye on what comes into consideration for the πρακτόν (prakton). Indeed, that is also an ἀληθεύειν, but one that is essentially distinct from δόξα. Its correctness is oriented to the πρακτόν, while the correctness of δόξα is oriented to the ἀληθές. (NE Γ 4, 1112a 5)
6 Standing-toward-knowing. Δόξα is related to what one does not know precisely, to beings that are still concealed. The point is that δόξα is directed at “what we do not yet genuinely know”; προαίρεσις is directed at “what we know most of all” in the sense of cognition, what we have clearly reckoned about, what corresponds to the circumstances on which it depends. (NE Γ 4, 1112a 7)
7 Reckoning vs. resolving. “One can very well have the best view and yet come to, or be resolved to, a κακόν (kakon, ‘bad,’ ‘evil’). Δόξα and προαίρεσις are distinguished in themselves. In the best case, being able to comport oneself about something and being able to be resolved in the right way are not conflated.” (NE Γ 4, 1112a 8)

The Convergence Point

“Δόξα (doxa) and προαίρεσις (proairesis) approach each other precisely when one takes δόξα in the narrow meaning of being-able-to-be-otherwise, the ἐνδεχόμενον ἄλλως (endechomenon allōs), insofar as it is a συμφέρον (sumpheron). I can have a definite view about a matter in light of its conduciveness; I can be for maintaining that it is better than the others.” (p. 101)

§15d. The Character of Δόξα (doxa) as the Orientedness of Average Being-with-One-Another-in-the-World

pp. 101–103

The Decisive Existential Determination

This is the structurally most important subsection of §15—and the most explicit anticipation of Being and Time’s analytic of everydayness. Heidegger now “gathers the entire analysis together and orients it, with regard to its content, to the question that genuinely interests us: the peculiar phenomenon of being-oriented in the world, how human being-there initially has its world there in an average way, how orientedness is in the having-there of the world” (p. 101).

“Δόξα (doxa) is the genuine discoveredness of being-with-one-another-in-the-world. The world is there for us as what-is-with-one-another in discoveredness, insofar as we live in a δόξα (doxa) means having it with others. That others also have it belongs to opinion.” (p. 101)

The Range: Δόξα (doxa) Reaches Πάντα (panta, “all things”)

“The next thing to notice is that the realm of δόξα (doxa) is πάντα (panta). Even in everydayness, being-oriented in the world is not only directed to πρακτά (prakta); discoveredness does not only exist with regard to πρακτά. I do not only know information about my concrete task, about what I have to do in my immediate surroundings. Instead, I also have a definite view of the way that the world and nature are, of that in which there are no πρακτά—of the moon, of the stars, of what is ἀεί (aei) for the Greeks. Δόξα (doxa) reaches out to the entire world; for the πρακτόν (prakton) with which I deal is not a determinate realm of beings, but that with which I have to do as beings, that which is itself in the world, in the being of nature.” (p. 101)

Being-For (Φάσις) as Going-Along With What Initially Shows Itself

“The manner and mode in which this world is possessed as uncovered to a certain degree is this being-for, maintaining that it is thus. In this being-for as the character of δόξα (doxa), lies the determination of going-along with the way that the world initially shows itself, the moment of trust in the immediate aspect. Nothing other than this is Thales’ opinion, that ὕδωρ (hydōr, ‘water’) is the πρῶτον (prōton), that the genuine ἀρχή (archē, ‘origin,’ ‘principle’) of being is ‘water.’ Such a determination is intelligible on the basis of the prevalence of a thoroughgoing trust in that which initially shows itself. That which initially shows itself is taken as what the world initially is, according to Thales.” (p. 102)

The Authority of the Anonymous: Who Has Δόξα (doxa)

This is the passage in §15 that most directly prefigures das Man:

“The one who possesses δόξα (doxa) belongs necessarily to the determination of δόξα. With an ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē), it does not matter who has it. For a valid proposition, it does not matter who I am; that contributes nothing to the elucidation, to the being-true, of what is known. By contrast, the one having the view is, as such, co-decisive for δόξα. Who has it is of great importance. The matter in itself cannot speak purely for itself. It is concealed; I have a view of it. In δόξα, the matter itself does not only speak for itself to the extent that it is uncovered, but it also speaks for he who has the view, for whom the φάσις (phasis), the yes of δόξα holds. Accordingly, the stability of a δόξα is not exclusively grounded in the state of affairs that it conveys, but in him who has the δόξα.” (p. 102)

Authority, Stubbornness, and the Indefinite “One” (das Man in nuce)

“In this structure of δόξα (doxa), lies the possibility of its reaching a characteristic authority and stubbornness. One repeats the opinions to others. Repeating does not depend on investigating what is said. What is said is not decisive, but rather that it is he who said it. Behind the authority of the view stand other people, who are peculiarly indefinite, whom one cannot get a hold of—one has the view. This is a characteristic authority, stubbornness, and a force that is found in δόξα itself.” (p. 102)

The German man—the impersonal “one” that Being and Time §27 will thematize as das Man (“the they”)—is here, in 1924, already named as the indefinite, ungraspable authority that grounds δόξα (doxa).

The Two-fold Function of Δόξα (doxa): Average and Anchoring

“Δόξα (doxa) is the genuine orientedness of being-with-one-another-in-the-world, that is, of average being-with-one-another. Average: the task of investigating the world is not posited. In δόξα, and on its basis, one has to do with the world in the way that one lives in the world in an everyday manner and has to do with things. One does not have to investigate everything with regard to its concrete content; what others say about it is what one thinks about it.” (p. 102)

Δόξα (doxa) as Basis, Source, and Motive of Discoursing-with-One-Another

“Thus δόξα (doxa) is simultaneously set forth as the basis and the motive of discoursing-with-one-another, of negotiating-with-one-another. For although δόξα possesses a kind of stability, that about which one has a view can indeed always still be discussed. That is, its sense is to leave a discussion open. Λόγος (logos), negotiating something, is constantly latent; in δόξα, bringing-to-language is constantly on the alert. Δόξα is precisely that from which speaking-with-one-another arises, by which it is motivated; and, at the same time, it is also that with which it negotiates. Thus δόξα is the basis, source, and motive for discoursing-with-one-another, in such a way that what is yielded by negotiating itself has the character of a δόξα and therewith takes over the very function of being-with-one-another in the world. Δόξα has the authority and guidance of being-with-one-another in the world.” (p. 102)

The Decisive Programmatic Statement: Δόξα (doxa) as Basis of Ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē)

“I have emphasized that the region of being of δόξα (doxa) is not limited to that which can also be otherwise; it is also the basis for the mode of grasping beings that we designate as ἐπιστήμη (epistēmē), as θεωρεῖν (theōrein). Even beings with which I negotiate, not in the sense of concern but in the sense of setting forth facts in the way that they are, are initially there in a δόξα (doxa). From there, Aristotle consciously refers back to the history of philosophy. He initially reviews every fundamental problem with regard to the way one thought about it, on the basis of the positive understanding of the fact that the matter must have somehow come into view in such a δόξα. Indeed, δόξα (doxa) is the characteristic trust in that which shows itself initially. And that which shows itself initially is the basis of the investigation of the matter itself.” (p. 103)

§15e. Δόξα (doxa) as the Basis of Theoretical Negotiating

§15e–α. Pre-given (Πρότασις) and Project (Πρόβλημα) as From-Which and About-Which of Theoretical Negotiating

pp. 103–106 · Topics Α 4 and Α 10–11

The Methodological Bridge from Practice to Theory

Heidegger turns to the Topics in order to show “that δόξα (doxa) also controls theoretical λέγειν (legein), λόγος in the sense of the ‘treatment’ of something, of theoretical explication and thorough consideration as opposed to practical negotiating, such as a trial—‘treating’ in the sense of διαλέγεσθαι (dialegesthai), the way that one speaks about a matter—the aim of accomplishing something by its means has to be abandoned; λέγειν in itself is that which is now of concern” (p. 103). Topics Α 4 (101b 12 ff.):

“ἔστι δ᾽ ἀριθμῷ ἴσα καὶ τὰ αὐτά, ἐξ ὧν τε οἱ λόγοι καὶ περὶ ὧν οἱ συλλογισμοί. γίνονται μὲν γὰρ οἱ λόγοι ἐκ τῶν προτάσεων: περὶ ὧν δὲ οἱ συλλογισμοί, τὰ προβλήματά ἐστι.”
“The wherefrom, on the basis of which there is discourse, and that about which there is discourse, are numerically equal and the same. That about which there is discourse are the προβλήματα (problēmata), and that on the basis of which discourse is, the πρότασις (protasis).” (p. 103)

Aristotle’s claim is that both phenomena—πρότασις (protasis, “pre-given,” “premise,” “what is set forth in advance”) and πρόβλημα (problēma, “projection,” “what is thrown-forward,” “problem”)—arise from δόξα (doxa): they are ἔνδοξα, “expressings that stand in some sort of connection with δόξα, that come out of δόξα and maintain themselves in relation to it” (p. 103).

Πρότασις (protasis): “What is Given in Advance”

The two phenomena are distinguished by the τρόπος (tropos, “manner and mode”) (Top. Α 4, 101b 29). Πρότασις (protasis): “to put beforehand,” “what is given in advance.” Heidegger’s example: “ἆρά γε τὸ ζῷον πεζὸν δίπουν ὁρισμός ἐστιν ἀνθρώπου;”—“It is, indeed, well expressed: the human being is a bipedal living thing, the definition of the human being! Of course, living thing is the genus of the human being!” (Top. Α 4, 101b 30). Ἆρά γε: “You also hold the opinion that this and that are thus, which we want to take as a ground!” (p. 104).

Πρόβλημα (problēma): “Projection”

For πρόβλημα, the question is: “πότερον τὸ ζῷον πεζὸν δίπουν ὁρισμός ἐστιν ἀνθρώπου ἢ οὔ;”—“Is the determination, the human being is a bipedal living thing, the definition of the human being? [Question:] Is it or is it not?” (Top. Α 4, 101b 32). “Πρόβλημα requires a fully determinate, clear definition. Θέσις (thesis) is a characteristic πρόβλημα” (p. 104; cit. Top. Α 11, 104b 19).

The Structural Synthesis: How the Possibilities of Δόξα Themselves Make Λόγος Possible

“Now it is a matter of understanding how the individual possibilities within which the world is negotiated arise from this δόξα. This sense of δόξα is apt for making a discussion possible. That over which the view has authority is such that it still allows there to be discourse about it. The possibility of negotiating-with-one-another is implicit in δόξα. Κοινωνία (koinōnia, ‘community’) is fulfilled in this way. All coming-to-an-understanding in being-with-one-another is a coming-to-an-understanding on a particular basis of being-trusted with regard to something, a basis for discussion that is itself not discussed. This intimate trust is that from out of which and into which speaking occurs insofar as the result of negotiation itself again has the character of ἔνδοξον (endoxon).” (p. 104)

Πρότασις (protasis) as Ἀρχή (archē)

That from out of which the discourse occurs is not explicitly there—but to the extent that it is explicit, it yields the phenomenon of πρότασις (protasis), “pre-giving” of that from out of which one speaks but which does not come to language in discussion. “This ‘from out of which the discourse occurs’ is designated in a theoretically fixed way as ἀρχή (archē), insofar as it deals with a fully precise speaking in the sense of theoretical exhibiting and proving, where the phenomenon of speaking-to-one-another is indeed there but not explicitly. The treatment too is directed to an addressee—the context of συλλογισμός (syllogismos). Here, πρότασις is designated as ἀρχή. The principles that are presupposed, and from which the proof proceeds, are a fully determinate case of the original context that is spoken on the basis of something familiar” (p. 105).

Πρότασις Διαλεκτική: Ἐρώτησις Ἔνδοξος

Aristotle deals with this in Topics Book 1, Chapter 10 (cit. 104a 8): ἔστι δὲ πρότασις διαλεκτικὴ ἐρώτησις ἔνδοξος. The πρότασις διαλεκτική is characterized as ἐρώτησις ἔνδοξος (erōtēsis endoxos): “a questioning that maintains itself in the vicinity of that which a fixed view is about”—μὴ παράδοξος (mē paradoxos, “not against the general opinion”) (p. 105). Ἐπώτησις ἔνδοξος: “a questioning that maintains itself in what general opinion is about.” Questions that are introduced with ἆρά γε (“it is indeed true”), request an agreement. Ἐρώτησις is ἀποκρίσεως αἴτησις, “the ‘requesting an answer.’” In the πρότασις, “one is requested to agree with what is said, a concession in the sense that one then stands on common ground for further discussion. This αἴτησις ἀποκρίσεως, as the advance laying of the ground on which further discussion is to proceed, is directed toward what appears to be in a certain way ‘to everyone, to most, to the informed’” (p. 105; cit. Top. Α 10, 104a 9).

Πρόβλημα (problēma) as Θεώρημα (theōrēma): “Something to be Examined”

The πρόβλημα, by contrast, is not concerned with giving something in advance in the sense of a ground. Rather, Aristotle designates the πρόβλημα as θεώρημα (theōrēma), “something to be examined,” “that which is to be the object of speaking” (p. 106; cit. Top. Α 11, 104b 1). The question-character of the πρότασις is such that it requests agreement, while θεώρημα means something whose investigation is to be carried out in negotiations. It is something that is συντείνον ἢ πρὸς αἵρεσιν καὶ φυγήν (synteinon ē pros hairesin kai phygēn), “that is subject to be grasped, that I either resolve to or renounce,” or πρὸς ἀλήθειαν καὶ γνῶσιν (pros alētheian kai gnōsin), “that something is uncovered and brought to knowledge” (Top. Α 11, 104b 2).

Πρόβλημα as Ἔνδοξον: The Debatable

“The relation of πρόβλημα (problēma) to ἔνδοξον (endoxon): it is something opened up in such a way that no agreement between them occurs. ‘περὶ οὗ ἢ οὐδετέρως δοξάζουσιν ἢ ἐναντίως οἱ πολλοὶ τοῖς σοφοῖς ἢ οἱ σοφοὶ τοῖς πολλοῖς ἢ ἑκάτεροι αὐτοὶ ἑαυτοῖς,’ ‘with regard to them, there is no aspect from which one came to a determinate view; according to its character, it is open in a way that is debatable, or it is such that with regard to them the many think otherwise than those who understand, or with regard to them they are in disagreement with each other.’ What is to be investigated has the character of the debatable.” (p. 106; cit. Top. Α 11, 104b 3)

Θέσις (thesis): The Special Πρόβλημα

A particular form of the πρόβλημα is the θέσις (thesis): “Not every πρόβλημα is θέσις, but every θέσις is πρόβλημα.” Θέσις δέ ἐστιν ὑπόληψις παράδοξος τῶν γνωρίμων τινὸς κατὰ φιλοσοφίαν, “a taking-for [ὑπόληψις is another word for δόξα], the παράδοξος—next to, outside of, stands next to δόξα.” Such a ὑπόληψις is “not an arbitrary particular case that anyone would have thought up, for it belongs to δόξα as constitutive of the possessor. Θέσις is a δόξα such that ‘the one who has it belongs to those who are entrusted with φιλοσοφία (philosophia),’ in the sphere of those who deal with the genuine consideration of beings.” Heidegger’s examples: Antisthenes’ thesis that there is no contradicting; Heraclitus’ saying that everything is in motion; Melissus’ saying that ἕν τὸ ὄν (hen to on, “being is one”) (p. 106; cit. Top. Α 11, 104b 19, 104b 21).

§15e–β. Inability-to-Get-Through (Ἀπορία) as the Topic of Theoretical Negotiating

pp. 107–108 · Metaphysics Β 1

The Bridge from Topic to Aporia

Heidegger now turns to Metaphysics Β 1 to show that the topic of διαλέγεσθαι (dialegesthai) is essentially limited and indicated: the topic must have an ἀπορία (aporia) in itself (p. 107). The bridge passage:

“Aristotle characterizes discourse, the topic of rhetoric, as τὰ ἤδη βουλεύεσθαι εἰωθότα (ta ēdē bouleuesthai eiōthota): that which is treated in scientific discussion as τὰ λόγου δεόμενα (ta logou deomena, ‘those things needing speech’), λόγος meant in the sense of διαλέγεσθαι. That which is dealt with in διαλέγεσθαι is such as to require (δέομαι, deomai) that speaking which has no further aim, which does not follow from the natural function of practical speaking. Here, λόγος is separated from πρακτόν; λόγος has become πρᾶξις (praxis). Here, λόγος appears as negotiating in its pure function, as the exhibiting of that about which there is negotiation, with regard to how and what it is.” (p. 107)

Λόγου Δεόμενον (logou deomenon): The Topic Must Require Speech

Discussion proceeds with regard to what requires λόγος, and therefore what is not clear without qualification, what is not brought to intelligibility in another way, what cannot be imparted in another way. “A λόγου δεόμενον is not simply the matter of a ‘reprimand’ (κολάσεως, kolaseōs) or of a ‘direct, simple perceiving.’” Aristotle’s examples (Top. Α 11, 105a 4): “Those that have difficulties, whether honoring the gods or loving their parents, need to be reprimanded”; as we would say, they need a smack upside the head. “Here, it would not make sense to undertake the writing of a treatise about ‘whether the snow is white or not’; here, it is simply a matter of opening one’s eyes” (p. 107; cit. Top. Α 11, 105a 7).

“It is becoming clear, now, that a basic requirement of conversation is that one agree on the topic of conversation, whether the topic allows its concrete sense to be discussed, or whether the topic does not lie outside of all discussion. However, not just anything that requires a particular grounding, that is not settled by a reprimand or by direct perception, is a λόγου δεόμενον.” (p. 107)

The Inner Logic of Aporia: Aristotle’s Vocabulary

Aristotle offers an extensive treatment of ἀπορία (aporia) in Book 3, Chapter 1 of the Metaphysics. “Ἀπορία (aporia) makes its appearance in the context of λέγειν, of independent λόγος; not in a πρᾶξις, but rather that through which λέγειν itself is πρᾶξις” (p. 108). Aristotle’s vocabulary cluster:

  • ἀπορεῖν (aporein) — “not getting through,” Met. Β 1, 995a 25;
  • εὐπορεῖν (euporein) — “getting-through-well,” Met. Β 1, 995a 27;
  • διαπορεῖν (diaporein) — “running through, course of” aporia, Met. Β 1, 995a 28;
  • προαπορεῖν (proaporein) — “forging in advance,” Met. Β 1, 995a 29.

Πορεῖν (porein) meant “running,” “going,” in the sense of λέγειν in a discursive mode, λέγειν in the function of ἀποφαίνεσθαι (apophainesthai, “making-manifest”). Ἀπορεῖν: “to not get through” in this being-in-progress, in running-through, in the course of this exhibiting. The α-privative shows that one must πορεῖν (porein) in general, that one maintains oneself in an exhibiting. The τέλος is εὐπορεῖν, getting-through-well. Ἀπορία is not itself a τέλος, but is at the service of a determinate getting-through; it is always the on-the-way to …, with regard to which one initially does not get through. The function of ἀπορεῖν is δηλοῦν (dēloun) in the mode in which one exhibits “knots” in the πρᾶγμα (p. 108; cit. Met. Β 1, 995b 2).

The Decisive Definition: Aporia is Cultivation of Inquiry

“Ἀπορεῖν is fulfilled in the way that one calls upon the prevalent opinions about a matter. Prevalent opinions should be thoroughly considered to see how far the matter is shown in them. Ἀπορία has the positive sense of disclosing the matter in advance according to determinate characteristics. Only when I have gone through a preliminary inability-to-get-through, specifically exhibiting where I have gotten through, do I genuinely possess the τέλος (telos) of the investigation. I can then decide, at the end of the investigation, whether or not I found what I was seeking. Ἀπορία has the sense of the cultivation of an interrogatory stance of scientific research. The cultivation of the interrogatory stance means nothing other than fixing the matter that is spoken about according to basic determinations, leading the questioning in definite directions.” (p. 108; cit. Met. Β 1, 995a 33)

The classic examples of this method in Aristotle: Physics Book 1, Chapters 2–9, where Aristotle goes through the aporias of beings with regard to their being-in-motion; the corresponding aporias of being qua being in Metaphysics Book 2 [actually Β], Chapters 1–6. “The basic presupposition for this is that one gains an orientation from a definite fundamental experience of the matter. Only, then, if I already have the matter in a legitimate way, can I venture to approach aporias. This is not a seizing of arbitrary difficulties and contradictions. Ἀπορία is the way to cultivate actual questioning with the possible aim of εὐπορεῖν” (p. 108).

Synthetic Reading of §15

Architectural Position

§15 is the systematic foundation of Heidegger’s 1924 Aristotle-interpretation. §13–14 had set up πίστις (pistis) as the basic phenomenon disclosed by the Rhetoric; §15 now grounds πίστις (pistis) ontologically by working through δόξα (doxa) as its matrix. From §15 onward, §16 will deploy ἦθος (ēthos) and πάθος (pathos) as πίστεις resting on the δόξα-foundation; §17 will isolate ἕξις (hexis) as the being-structure of composure within this δόξα-world; §18 will return to πάθος (pathos) as bodily being-taken-by-the-δόξα-world. The whole architecture of Chapter 3 rests on §15’s discovery that δόξα (doxa) names the average everydayness of being-with-one-another.

Six Theses Heidegger Establishes

  1. Δόξα (doxa) is φάσις (phasis), not ζήτησις (zētēsis): a yes-saying standing at the end of seeing, not a seeking. (§15a)
  2. Δόξα (doxa) is the mode of being of the ἐνδεχόμενον (endechomenon): of beings that can-also-be-otherwise. Ὀρθότης (orthotēs) and possible ψεῦδος (pseudos) are intrinsic; revisability belongs to δόξα in itself. (§15a)
  3. Animals have no δόξα because they have no λόγος; the chain δόξα → πίστις → λόγος (De an. Γ 3, 428a 18) defines specifically human discoveredness. (§15a, §15c)
  4. Δόξα (doxa) is not the same as προαίρεσις (proairesis): the seven differences (NE Γ 4) hinge on toward-which (ἀληθές vs. πρακτόν), the disinterestedness of δόξα with regard to ἦθος, and the indifference of δόξα-who for theoretical truth. (§15c)
  5. Δόξα (doxa) is the genuine discoveredness of average being-with-one-another-in-the-world: it has authority and stubbornness via the indefinite “one” (man); reaches πάντα; is the basis, source, and motive of all discoursing; and is the basis from which ἐπιστήμη arises. (§15d)
  6. Πρότασις (protasis) and πρόβλημα (problēma) both arise from δόξα: theoretical negotiating itself rests on ἔνδοξον. The topic of theoretical διαλέγεσθαι must be λόγου δεόμενον and must contain an ἀπορία in itself—not seizing arbitrary difficulties but cultivating the interrogatory stance of scientific research. (§15e)

Connections to Being and Time (1927)

  • Δόξα → das Man / Alltäglichkeit (BT §27, §35–38): the indefinite “one has the view” (p. 102), the authority and stubbornness of repeating-without-investigating, the “average being-with-one-another” (p. 102) directly anticipate “the they” (das Man) and idle-talk (Gerede).
  • The chain δόξα → πίστις → λόγος → Rede / Verstehen / Befindlichkeit (BT §28–34): the existentiale of discourse (Rede) and its degenerate mode in idle talk presuppose the same disclosedness-structure that 1924 reads in δόξα.
  • Going-along with what initially shows itself → falling (Verfallen) (BT §38): “trust in the immediate aspect” (p. 102), with Thales’ ὕδωρ as exemplary case, prefigures the structure of Verfallen as absorption in the world of concern.
  • Δόξα as the basis of ἐπιστήμη → the existential foundation of science (BT §69b): the doctrine that science arises from, and remains indebted to, average pre-theoretical disclosedness is articulated more clearly in 1924 than in BT itself.
  • Ἀπορία → the question of being: aporia as “cultivation of the interrogatory stance of scientific research” (p. 108) is the methodological self-description that BT§1–8 will perform on the τὸ ὂν ᾗ ὄν question itself.

Three Tensions Worth Flagging

  1. The disinterestedness of δόξα in NE Γ 4 vs. the existential weight of δόξα in §15d. Difference 3 of the seven (p. 100) says δόξα “does not pertain to changing the general comportment of human beings according to their being.” Yet §15d insists that δόξα is the discoveredness in which average being-with-one-another has its being. The tension can be resolved by distinguishing the narrow NE-sense (δόξα as theoretical view, ethically indifferent) from the wide ontological sense Heidegger develops (δόξα as the comportmental medium of everydayness)—but Heidegger does not always thematize the distinction.
  2. Δόξα reaches πάντα (p. 101) yet is structurally bound to the ἐνδεχόμενον (p. 94). If δόξα reaches even the ἀεί ὄν (the moon, the stars), then the binding to revisability seems to weaken. Heidegger handles this by saying that science in general “arises from δόξα as basis” (p. 99) without yet being δόξα; but the precise demarcation between (a) δόξα as starting-point that science transcends and (b) δόξα as ineradicable matrix that science cannot escape is left implicit.
  3. The relation of δόξα’s “authority” (p. 102) to the philosopher’s θέσις παράδοξος (p. 106). The philosopher’s θέσις stands “next to” or “outside” δόξα (παράδοξος)—yet it remains a kind of δόξα (ὑπόληψις) belonging to one entrusted with φιλοσοφία. Whether genuine philosophy can ever fully escape δόξα, or whether it remains, even in its highest reaches, δόξα of a special kind—this question, which becomes urgent in Being and Time’s methodological reflections, is opened but not resolved here.
