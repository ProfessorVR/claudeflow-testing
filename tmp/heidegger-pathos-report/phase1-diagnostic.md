## A. Linguistic-Stylistic Profile

### A.1 Sentence-level architecture

The draft's prose is *periodic*: sentences extend through multiple subordinate clauses, accumulating qualifications before resolving into a thetic conclusion. Mean sentence length is in the 35–60-word range; the common form is a short declarative head followed by a long appositive or relative-clause expansion. Two diagnostic examples:

> "It is not simply something that happens inside a subject; it is the soul's *being-moved* by what appears significant to it." (1.1, p. 1) — short, two-clause periodic structure with semicolon hinge.

> "Emotion is therefore inseparable from *phantasia*—the representational, imagistic, quasi-perceptual capacity through which things 'show up' in the first place (DA III.3-8)." (1.1, p. 1) — em-dash inserts a definitional gloss; parenthetical citation closes the sentence.

> "Indeed, the very word, *emotion*, already encodes the kinetic ontology that Aristotle and Heidegger attribute to *pathos*." (1.1, p. 1) — short, climactic; uses "already" as a structural marker.

Sentences open frequently with discourse-organizing transitions (*However*, *Indeed*, *Accordingly*, *Hence*, *Lastly*, *By contrast*, *Furthermore*, *Thus even at the level of etymology*). The author favors *resumptive openings* — "As we have already discussed," "In light of analyzing emotion as Aristotle presents it," "With our terminological reckoning complete" — that mark phase-transitions within the argument.

### A.2 Paragraph pacing

Paragraphs run long (typically 8–18 sentences, 200–500 words). The dominant paragraph form is a **thesis-then-warrant** structure: an initial assertion in one or two sentences, followed by extended Aristotelian textual support, an explicit gloss, and a concluding-sentence return to the thesis. Example: section 1.4, p. 4, paragraph beginning "Accordingly, *doxa*, for rational animals, serves as not only a condition but also as a cognitive gate…" — six sentences develop the gate-thesis with quoted Aristotle and a closing restatement.

A subset of paragraphs are *summative-recapitulatory*: they collect prior moves and re-state the consolidated position. The closing paragraph of 1.6 ("Emotion, in its full Aristotelian sense, is nothing less than the *affective architecture of being-in-the-world*.") and the closing paragraph of 1.7 use this form. The recapitulation closes the section's argumentative arc.

### A.3 Italicization conventions

Three distinct uses, consistently maintained:

1. **Transliterated Greek** (always italicized): *pathos*, *pathē*, *kinēsis*, *phantasia*, *phantasma*, *aisthēsis*, *orexis*, *doxa*, *aisthēma*, *kriseis*, *paschein*, *energeia*, *epithumia*. The transliteration is the primary form in running text.
2. **Coined / technical terms** (italicized): *resonant orexis*, *resonant aisthēma*, *pathos simpliciter*, *articulational concretion*, *takes the object to be*, *takes-as*. The italicization signals "this is a defined term."
3. **Word-as-word and concept-emphasis**: *emotion* (when discussing the lexeme), *motion*, *moved mover*, *not undifferentiated displeasure but 'desire accompanied by pain for conspicuous revenge for a conspicuous slight'*. The user uses italics for emphasis in the manner of analytic philosophy — never bold for emphasis.

Italics are **not** used for direct quotations (which receive double quotation marks instead) and **not** for non-Greek titles other than work titles.

### A.4 Greek-formatting convention

The integration brief prescribes "Greek script + italicized transliteration in parentheses + translation in quotation marks." The draft's *actual* practice is the **inverse**: italicized transliteration appears in running text, with Greek script + translation supplied in parentheses on first occurrence (then dropped on subsequent occurrences). Two diagnostic examples:

> "Aristotelian *pathos* (πάθος 'affect'), *pathē* (πάθη 'passions/emotions')…" (1.1 → 1.2, p. 1–2)

> "*aisthēsis* ('perception')" (1.6, p. 8) — here the Greek script is omitted on a later occurrence

> "the soul makes a sort of affirmation or negation, and pursues or avoids" (DA III.7, 431a8-10) (1.2, p. 3) — a quoted translation; no Greek transliterated alongside.

The convention as practiced is therefore: *italicized transliteration* in running prose; (Greek script "translation") on first occurrence; (Greek script) or the transliteration alone on subsequent occurrences. The integration brief's prescription is roughly correct in *components* but reverses the order. **Phase 2/3 should match the draft's practice, not the brief's nominal description.**

### A.5 Citation conventions

- **Inline format**: italicized work title + book/section in roman + Bekker, no comma between work and section, comma before Bekker. Examples: "(*DA* III.3, 427b21-24)", "(*Rhetoric* II.1, 13789a21-22)" *[sic — typo flagged]*, "(*Movement of Animals* 701b33-7022)" *[sic — second typo flagged]*. Some occurrences drop the work-title italics in informal-shorthand: "(DA III.3-8)" (1.1, p. 1).
- **Translator references**: appended after a quotation in parens, often as "(Loeb)" or by translator name where relevant.
- **Footnotes** are reserved for: full quoted Aristotelian definitions (anger, fear, pity, shame at footnotes 11–14); etymological / OED references; Greek-text clarifications; minor cross-references.
- **Cross-references** to Heidegger appear inline in the form "Heidegger" (no further citation when the immediate context fixes the source) or as quoted page references "(133)" — **not** as full GA-volume citations. The draft is informal about Heidegger citation: page numbers only, occasionally bare.
- The draft contains **two visible Bekker typos**: "13789a21-22" should read "1378a21-22" (1.2, p. 2); "701b33-7022" should read "701b33-702a3" or similar (1.5, p. 5). And **multiple `(CITE)` placeholders** in footnotes 6, 7, and 17 — three placeholders signaling the author's awareness that citations are pending.

### A.6 Em-dash, semicolon, parenthetical patterns

- **Em-dashes** (heavy use): definitional inserts, appositives, list-elaborations, and chain-projections. Diagnostic examples:
  - "*phantasia*—the representational, imagistic, quasi-perceptual capacity through which things 'show up' in the first place" (1.1, p. 1) — definitional insert
  - "what I call *resonant orexis*: the raw pleasure/pain, pursue/avoid complex…" (1.2, p. 3) — coined-term apposition with colon
  - "the four general meanings—alterability, energeia of alteration, harmful occurrence, magnitude—" *[paraphrased pattern]*
  - "The rhetorical cascade follows the actualization chain precisely: new *phantasma* → new *doxa* → new emotion → new orientation → new action." (1.7, p. 11) — em-dashes / colons + arrow chain
- **Semicolons** (moderate use): for clause-coordination where the two clauses are conceptually parallel or contrastive. "Anger is not something that happens before the desire to retaliate; anger *is* the desire to retaliate as it is structured by the perception of an unjustified slight…" (1.7, p. 10).
- **Parentheticals** (heavy use): Greek-script glosses; Bekker citations; etymology breakdowns ("e- ('out, forth') + *movēre* ('to move, set in motion')"); translator references; brief examples ("(such as anger, fear and the like, with their opposites)").

### A.7 Authorial voice and stance

The author writes in the first person, but sparingly and only at structural junctures. Diagnostic instances:

- "When it comes to Aristotle's account of emotion, **I am in agreement with Heidegger**" (1.1, p. 1) — opening alignment
- "what **I have coined** as *pathos simpliciter*" (1.1, p. 1) — terminological credit
- "what **I call** *resonant orexis*" (1.2, p. 3) — coined-term flag
- "**I can now posit** my overall pathetic thesis" (1.2, p. 3) — thesis-introduction
- "**in my analysis**, it is what distinguishes emotion from the basic affective valence" (1.2, p. 3) — analytical framing
- "**as I read it**, pity contains an aversive motivation" (1.4, p. 5) — interpretive framing
- "**The reasoning presented here**" (1.3, p. 4) — argumentative stance

The "I" appears at the moments when the author either (i) coins a term, (ii) takes an interpretive position, or (iii) introduces a thesis. Otherwise the prose is impersonal-academic.

### A.8 Term-introduction vs. recapitulation

A consistent two-step pattern:

- **First occurrence**: full form with parenthetical gloss. "*pathos simpliciter* or *resonant orexis*"; "*phantasia*—the representational, imagistic, quasi-perceptual capacity…"; "*doxa* (opinion/belief)".
- **Subsequent occurrences**: bare italicized form. "*resonant orexis*"; "*phantasia*"; "*doxa*".

The pattern holds within sections; cross-section recapitulations occasionally re-introduce the gloss for a major term (e.g., *resonant orexis* is re-glossed in section 1.6 after its 1.2 introduction).

### A.9 Marginalia and editorial state

The draft contains several **author-marked uncertainties** that bear on stylistic and substantive integrations:

- **Yellow highlights** (in-line word/phrase markings signaling pending revision): "what Heidegger calls mood" (1.2, p. 3); "conative orientation" (1.2, p. 3 — sidebar note questions whether "conative" is the right word); "BCAP 13-18" (1.6, p. 8 — incomplete citation); "Except for intellect" (1.6, p. 8 — uncertain commitment); "(CITE)" markers (1.2 fn. 6, fn. 7; 1.7 fn. 17); "befalls" (1.4, p. 4 — possibly considering revision); "and this is further indicated by the enmattered account and bodily accompaniment" (1.5, p. 5); "or what Heidegger calls in *Being and Time* the 'existential-hermeneutical 'as'' (159)" / "or what Heidegger calls the 'apophantical 'as'' (159)" (1.2, p. 3 — uncertain about whether to include the Heideggerian comparison); "mobilizing" (1.6, p. 6); "or contain true explanatory value" (1.3, p. 4); "*pathē*" (1.5, fn. 15).
- **Orange/peach sidebar boxes** (longer marginal annotations): the *phantasia*-*pathos* interdependence note (1.3, p. 4); the rational-animal restriction note (1.3, p. 4); "Not sure if 'conative' is the correct word. Perhaps adopt something from Heidegger to replace it." (1.3, p. 4); the enmattered-account structural-constraint statement (1.3, p. 4).

These markings are diagnostic of where the author is *open to additions*, *aware of citation gaps*, or *contemplating Heideggerian terminology imports*. Phase 2 should treat them as priority candidates for integration where they intersect with Theses A and B.

## B. Structural Map

The draft is a single chapter section ("1. Emotion: The Form of Desire Under Evaluative Disclosure") with seven sub-sections.

### 1.1 — *Emotion is Motion*: The Affective Architecture of Aristotelian Being-Moved (*Paschein*)

*Pages 1–2.* **Claim advanced**: emotion is *kinēsis* of soul — being-moved, dynamic reorientation in the field of appearances — not psychic experience or epiphenomenal sign of inner state. Opens with explicit alignment with Heidegger's GA 18 p. 133 thesis (πάθη as "being-taken of human beings in their full being-in-the-world"). Develops the kinetic-ontology reading via the *Rhet*. → *DA* line and reinforces it etymologically through "emotion" ← *emovēre* ← *meue*-. Closes with a thesis-announcement: *pathos simpliciter* / *resonant orexis* (basic affective valence, perceptual level) and *pathē* (higher-order rhetorical emotions, *doxa*-articulated level) are "two articulational concretions of a single ontological structure."

**Load-bearing element**: the closing thesis-announcement — Thesis B in the integration brief.

**Transition out**: the closing sentence sets up section 1.2 by promising terminological reckoning before further analysis.

### 1.2 — *Pathos* and *Pathē*: Two Articulational Concretions

*Pages 2–3.* **Claim advanced**: the four-fold of *Met*. Δ 21 articulates pathos at successively narrower levels of concreteness; the *Rhet*. II.1, 1378a20-22 definition (emotions "so change men as to affect their judgements" + "attended by pain or pleasure") anchors the rhetorical-emotional sense. Distinguishes *resonant orexis* (basic valence, *DA* III.7, 431a8-10) from full *pathē* (cognitive content + hedonic tonality + conative orientation). Reproduces Heidegger's gloss on *Rhet*. B 1's three constitutive aspects (*metaballontes*, *diapherousi pros tas kriseis*, *hois hepetai lupē kai hēdonē*). Closes with the **"overall *pathetic* thesis"**: single ontological structure of *pathos* invariant across (a) basic *resonant orexis* at perceptual level and (b) higher-order *pathē* at *doxa*-articulated level; difference is *not categorical but articulational*; minimal articulation = recognition without proposition (with highlighted reference to Heidegger's "existential-hermeneutical 'as'" SZ 159); maximal articulation = propositional evaluation (highlighted reference to Heidegger's "apophantical 'as'" SZ 159).

**Load-bearing element**: the "overall *pathetic* thesis" — the most explicit and complete formulation of Thesis B in the draft.

**Implicit/compressed**: the four-sense check at the basic-valence level is asserted but not performed; the three-aspect check at the basic-valence level is asserted but not performed.

### 1.3 — Enmattered-Accounts

*Page 4.* **Claim advanced**: emotion is constitutively composite — cognitive + (hedonic) + conative + physiological — and irreducible to any one component. *DA* I.1, 403a25-b19 (the dialectician/physicist contrast for anger; *logoi enuloi*) is the central Aristotelian anchor. Argues that "no analysis or explanation of emotion that reduces it to any one of these components will suffice [or contain true explanatory value]" and that the enmattered account "imposes a structural constraint on any placement of emotion in the actualization chain."

**Load-bearing element**: the irreducibility claim — supports Thesis B by establishing that the higher-order *pathē* are composite-structured (i.e., the maximal-articulation form involves cognitive content that basic valence lacks).

**Implicit/compressed**: the section does not extend the *logoi enuloi* analysis to basic *resonant orexis* — the enmattered-account thesis is implicitly applied only to higher-order *pathē*, leaving open whether basic valence is also enmattered (it is, on Aristotle's own reading, since perception-with-valence is *energeia* of an embodied perceiving-desiring composite).

**Marginalia**: three orange-box sidebar notes (phantasia-pathos interdependence; rational-animal restriction; "conative" word-choice question); two yellow highlights ("or contain true explanatory value"; the structural-constraint sentence).

### 1.4 — The Articulational Concretion: From *Doxa* to *Pathē*

*Pages 4–5.* **Claim advanced**: *doxa* is the cognitive gate through which *phantasma* must pass for higher-order *pathē* to be produced. Anchored in *DA* III.3, 427b21-24 (the painting passage — *doxazein* immediately produces emotion; mere *phantasia* leaves the soul *apathōs*). Specifies *doxa* as **involuntary**: it *befalls* the agent rather than being elected. *Doxa* is "closer to perceptual recognition than to discursive inference" — fast, immediate, taking-as. Section closes with individual emotion definitions (anger, fear, pity, shame) showing each has cognitive content + hedonic valence + conative orientation; these constitute "a structured whole."

**Load-bearing element**: this section *is* the doxa-gate thesis (Thesis A). The most explicit formulation: "*doxa*, for rational animals, serves as not only a condition but also as a cognitive gate through which emotion must pass to enter the actualization chain."

**Implicit/compressed**: the *concretional* vs. *generative* distinction is implicit but not named. Verbs like "produces" ("the *taking-as* that produces the fear") and "transforms" can be read in either register; the synthesis would prefer "articulates" or "concretizes."

### 1.5 — Somatic Preparation: Emotion in the Causal Chain

*Pages 5–6.* **Claim advanced**: the *MA* causal chain (sense-perception/thinking → imagination → desire → affections → organic parts → movement) places emotions ("affections") between desire and organic parts; emotions are *desire realized in physiological change*. Establishes emotion's distinctive ontological role: composite of cognitive + hedonic + conative + somatic, irreducible to desire alone.

**Load-bearing element**: the somatic-preparatory function as definitive of the higher-order emotion's place in the actualization chain.

**Implicit/compressed**: the parallel question — how *resonant orexis* prepares for action without higher-order somatic articulation — is not addressed; the basic-valence case is bracketed in this section.

**Marginalia**: yellow highlight on "and this is further indicated by the enmattered account and bodily accompaniment" (signaling the author may revise this connective phrasing).

### 1.6 — Variable Presence: *Resonant Orexis* and Articulationally Developed *Pathē*

*Pages 6–9.* **Claim advanced**: emotion's presence in the actualization chain is *variable* — the chain runs through emotion when the object engages evaluatively complex categories, and runs around emotion (basic appetite + recognition) when it does not. *MA* 701a25-b1 (the drinking case) is the central textual phenomenon. Develops the appearance-laden cognition reading: emotion is "way of world-disclosure," a self-motion of the soul shaped by particular appearances. Engages Heidegger's *Stimmung*-reading directly: "Heidegger calls rhetoric 'the hermeneutic of Dasein itself'… the discipline in which 'the self-elaboration of Dasein is expressly executed' (Basic Concepts (GA 18); Hedegger and Rhetoric ed. Gross p. 1)." Closes with a synthesis paragraph: emotion is "the kinetic, affective, evaluative dimension of how the world appears to us"; "the *affective architecture of being-in-the-world*."

**Load-bearing element**: the variable-presence thesis (operationalizing Thesis B); the convergence with Heideggerian *Stimmung* / mood.

**Implicit/compressed**: the synthesis's three observations on *MA* 701a25-b1 (energeia of orexis as substitute for inquiry/thinking; the "or…or…" disjunction explicitly including *aisthēsis* alone; "what we do without reflection, we do quickly" as normal for rational animals) are not surfaced individually. Case 2 (habitual-rational action with *doxa* but without higher-order *pathē*; *MA* 701a7-25) is not developed.

**Marginalia**: yellow highlights on "(CITE)" (footnote in body), "BCAP 13-18", "Except for intellect", "(CITE)" (footnote 17), "mobilizing".

### 1.7 — The Feedback Loop: Synchronic Chain, Diachronic Saturation

*Pages 9–11.* **Claim advanced**: *De Insomniis* 460b3-11 establishes that emotional excitement biases perception — the controlling sense (corrective faculty) is impaired; *phantasmata* pass into *doxa* unopposed; new *doxai* produce new emotions. Self-reinforcing loop. Caveat: the loop is "an interpretive reconstruction rather than a doctrine Aristotle states in these terms." Specifies the A₀ → A₄ chain notation. Notes that "a complete topological representation would require a return path from the emotion node at A₃ back to the gatekeeping condition within M₂ → M₃." Two sub-paragraphs ("Emotion's Function in the Chain" and "Phantasia in Every Action") synthesize emotion's role: *orexis* is the moved mover, but in evaluatively complex cases, emotion *is* the form *orexis* takes when *doxa* discloses evaluatively complex content.

**Load-bearing element**: the feedback-loop topology — the section that intersects most directly with Part V of the synthesis.

**Implicit/compressed**: the synchronic/diachronic distinction is gestured at in the section title but not articulated as a structural distinction in the body. The two distinct *De Insomniis* mechanisms (Stimmung-saturation of basic valence vs. corrective-faculty impairment) are conflated under "self-reinforcing loop." Geworfenheit / *hexis* are not introduced as the appropriate Heideggerian / Aristotelian vocabulary for diachronic dispositional persistence.

### B.1 — Where transitions occur

Section transitions are smooth and use the resumptive-opening pattern: 1.1 → 1.2 ("However, before I can more fully analyze…"); 1.2 → 1.3 ("Aristotle establishes the ontological framework for understanding emotion when…"); 1.3 → 1.4 ("As we have discussed the structural components…"); 1.4 → 1.5 ("The causal chain presented by Aristotle in *Movement of Animals* gives us…"); 1.5 → 1.6 ("In light of analyzing emotion as Aristotle presents it across…"); 1.6 → 1.7 ("The *Rhetoric*'s definition of emotion as judgment-affecting (Rhetoric II.1, 1378a20-22) entails a structural feature…").

The seam most relevant for thesis-strengthening integrations is **1.2 → 1.3**, where the "overall *pathetic* thesis" is announced and then immediately moved away from toward enmattered-accounts. A bridge passage that performs the four-sense check and three-aspect check at the basic-valence level (synthesis Part I §§1.3–1.4) would naturally land here.

The seam most relevant for the synchronic/diachronic clarification is **inside 1.7** — the existing "complete topological representation" gesture is the natural insertion point for the synthesis's Part V resolution.

### C.1 Thesis A — *Doxa* as Cognitive Gate

#### C.1.a Where explicitly stated

1. **Section 1.4, p. 4** (most explicit):

   > "The involuntariness of *doxa* is what gives emotion its distinctive phenomenological character as something that *befalls* the agent rather than something the agent elects to undergo. 4. **Section 1.6, p. 6** (variable-presence operationalization):

### C.2 Thesis B — *Resonant Orexis* and Higher-Order *Pathē* as Two Articulational Hows of a Single Ontological Structure

#### C.2.a Where explicitly stated

1. **Section 1.1 closing, p. 2** (thesis announcement):
   > "*pathos* designates the basic affective valence—the *pathos simpliciter* or *resonant orexis* of pursuit and avoidance—while *pathē* designates the determinate higher-order emotions of the *Rhetoric*; both are treated as two articulational concretions of a single ontological structure."

2. **Section 1.2, p. 3** (the "overall *pathetic* thesis" — fullest formulation):
   > "There is a single ontological structure of *pathos*—articulated by the four senses of the *Metaphysics* and the three constitutive aspects of the *Rhetoric*—that is invariant across (a) basic *resonant orexis* at the perceptual level and (b) the higher-order *pathē* at the *doxa*-articulated level. The difference between the two is not categorical but articulational: (a) realizes the structure at minimal cognitive articulation (recognition without proposition or what Heidegger calls in *Being and Time* the 'existential-hermeneutical 'as'' (159)); (b) realizes it [at] maximal cognitive articulation (propositional evaluation under the categories of the *Rhetoric* or what Heidegger calls the 'apophantical 'as'' (159))."

3. **Section 1.6, pp. 6–7** (variable-presence operationalization):
   > "The presence of emotion, therefore, seems to be variable and tracks the distinction between simple appetitive actions and evaluatively complex actions. Where the object is straightforwardly pleasant or painful and the desire is basic appetite or aversion, emotion in the higher-order sense is not required; basic affective valence, *resonant orexis*, suffices to direct the organism toward or away from the object.
