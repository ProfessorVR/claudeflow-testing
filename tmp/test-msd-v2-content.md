# Aristotle, Motion, Time, and the Faculties of Temporal Awareness: A Dissertation Section

## 1. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding

Aristotle's philosophical enterprise, far from offering a merely static taxonomy of beings, unfolds as a sustained inquiry into the dynamic structures that make beings intelligible in their appearing and their becoming. To articulate Aristotle's understanding of being is, accordingly, to reckon with his insistence that substance—οὐσία—operates as the basic concept around which all other determinations of existence orbit. As Heidegger's reading of Aristotle demonstrates, the concept of οὐσία cannot be confined to a single register; it carries with it a constitutive ambiguity that is itself generative for philosophical thought (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 2–7). Indeed, Aristotle himself acknowledges that being admits of multiple senses, and that substance must be understood not only as that which is predicable but also as that which underlies all predication. As Aristotle argues, "each thing then and its essence are one and the same in no merely accident" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 80–81), a formulation that binds the identity of a thing to its essential self-disclosure. Within the broader rhetorical tradition, this Aristotelian commitment to the multivalence of being has been read as opening a space for the ambient, world-disclosing dimensions of rhetoric; specifically, it invites the user to consider how being is not merely a logical posit but a lived, situated phenomenon that calls upon the full range of human attunement (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric: The Attunements of Rhetorical Being*, pp. 53–54). Thus, Aristotle's understanding of being serves as the ground from which motion, time, and perception derive their philosophical significance. ## 2. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding: Being and Becoming

The question of what it means for a being to be, and to be in a determinate way, runs through the entirety of Aristotle's metaphysical project; it is here that the relationship between potentiality and actuality acquires its deepest resonance. Aristotle's treatment of the indeterminate—that which exists potentially and not yet actually—reveals a philosophical sensitivity to the way in which beings hover at the threshold of disclosure and concealment. As Aristotle writes, those who confuse the potential with the actual "seem, then, to be speaking of the indeterminate, and, while fancying themselves to be speaking of being, they are speaking about non-being; for that which exists potentially and not actually is the indeterminate" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 42–43). This distinction between potentiality and actuality is critical for understanding how Aristotle conceives of motion, for motion is precisely the actualization of that which exists in potency. The Heideggerian reading of Aristotle extends this insight by arguing that kinesis, or motion, should not be understood as a mere physical displacement but rather as a fundamental mode of being itself; "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Hence, the articulation of Aristotle's understanding of being necessarily leads us toward a confrontation with the phenomenon of motion—the passage from potency to act that is constitutive of the very structure of becoming. ## 3. Aristotle on Motion

Motion, for Aristotle, is not reducible to local change or mere physical displacement; it is, rather, the ontological process whereby beings come to be what they are, transitioning from potency to fulfillment. The *Movement of Animals* makes explicit that the inquiry into motion requires us to identify an ultimate originary principle; Aristotle states that "the origin of other motions is that which moves itself, and that the origin of this is the immovable, and that the prime mover must of necessity be immovable" (Aristotle, *Movement of Animals*, pp. 1–4). Similarly, Heidegger's reading of Aristotle underscores the affective and dispositional character of motion within the soul; as the *Heidegger and Rhetoric* volume observes, "Passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Motion, thus understood, encompasses not only physical locomotion but also the movements of desire, aversion, and deliberation that characterize living beings; Heidegger highlights that the basic dispositions of the soul—δίωξις and φυγή, going-toward and shrinking-back—are themselves fundamental modes of being-moved (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 205–206). Accordingly, Aristotle's account of motion provides the indispensable ontological backdrop against which time, perception, and the faculties of the soul must be situated. ## 4. Aristotle on Time

Time, in Aristotle's philosophy, is inseparable from the perception of motion; it is through the discernment of before and after in change that the soul apprehends temporal succession. As Bowin observes, Aristotle holds that "we measure time by counting units of time (e.g., days) which are determinate because they are marked out by motions that are determinate in time (e.g., celestial motions)" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). This account reveals that time is not an independent substance subsisting apart from beings in motion but is, rather, something that emerges through the soul's discriminating activity upon change. Indeed, the measurement of time depends on a kind of counting—one that Aristotle regards as the most exact form of measurement because it relies upon absolutely indivisible units rather than units that are merely indivisible in relation to perception (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). Aristotle's metaphysical analyses further confirm that the beautiful can be found in motionless things, yet the understanding of time demands that we attend to that which is in motion and in conduct; as Aristotle himself notes, "the good and the beautiful are different (for the former always implies conduct as its subject, while the beautiful is found also in motionless things)" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). Time, then, belongs properly to the domain of conduct and change, and its apprehension requires a soul capable of registering motion as such. ## 5. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The interdependence of motion and time in Aristotle's thought constitutes one of the most profound aspects of his natural philosophy; it is a relationship that resists any simple reduction of one term to the other. We perceive time only insofar as we perceive motion, and yet time itself is that by which motion is measured and rendered intelligible. Bowin's analysis clarifies that the determinacy of temporal units is parasitic upon the determinacy of certain paradigmatic motions—specifically, celestial motions—which serve as the standard against which all other durations are assessed (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). This reciprocal relationship means that time is neither a mere abstraction nor a brute physical fact; it is, rather, a relational structure disclosed through the soul's engagement with the world of change. The Heideggerian tradition deepens this insight by reading Aristotle's kinesis as a fundamental way of being rather than a secondary attribute of beings, thus revealing that the experience of time is always already embedded in the existential structures of Dasein (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Subsequently, what might appear as a purely cosmological thesis—the dependence of time on celestial motion—turns out to have profound implications for the philosophy of mind, insofar as it requires a perceiver capable of discriminating the before and after. Aristotle's metaphysical framework supports this conclusion by insisting that being itself admits of dual forms—fulfillment and matter—thus ensuring that time, as the measure of passage from potency to act, is woven into the very fabric of being (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). ## 6. Aisthēsis and Temporal Awareness

The perception of time, or aisthēsis in its temporal dimension, raises the question of how the soul's immediate sensory contact with the world gives rise to an awareness of succession and duration. How does the perceiving soul register not merely the presence of a quality but the passage from one moment to another? Burke's rhetorical analysis, though primarily concerned with the persuasive dimensions of language, offers an instructive parallel; he draws attention to the way in which temporal and logical priority become intertwined in human understanding, noting the "puns of logical and temporal priority whereby the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). This  the perception of time is never a bare sensory datum; it is always already inflected by the interpretive and narrative structures through which beings make sense of their experience. The naming of objects in the field of moral and political life further illustrates the entanglement of perception and judgment; Burke identifies the "question-begging appellative" as a device that designates objects through evaluative categories, thereby shaping perception at its source (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 107–108). The soul's awareness of temporal passage, thus understood, is not a passive registration but an active discrimination that draws upon the full resources of language, judgment, and rhetorical attunement. Aisthēsis, then, provides the raw material for temporal awareness, yet it must be supplemented by further faculties if the unity of temporal experience is to be secured. ## 7. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

If aisthēsis supplies the sensory basis for the perception of change, phantasia—the faculty of imagination—serves to unify and preserve these discrete sensory moments into a coherent temporal experience. Phantasia, as O'Gorman elucidates in his reading of Aristotle's *De Anima*, "is intimately connected to the sense of sight, preserving visual perceptions in a sort of after-image, and is itself a sort of psychē-sight, the source of mental images or visual impressions" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric: Lexis, Appearance, and the Epideictic Function of Discourse*, p. 4). This preservation of perceptual content beyond the moment of immediate sensation is precisely what enables the soul to apprehend temporal duration, for without such retention the before and after could never be held together in a single act of awareness. Furthermore, phantasia does not mechanically reproduce sense data; as O'Gorman notes, "there is no need to assume any precise correspondence between a phantasma and that which it is a phantasma of" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric: Lexis, Appearance, and the Epideictic Function of Discourse*, p. 4). This productive looseness in the relationship between phantasma and its object opens the space for creative synthesis—the soul does not merely replay the past but actively constructs a unified temporal field. Burke's analysis of the temporizing of essence further supports this point, insofar (White 1985) how the logical identity of a thing can be narrativized into a story of origins and becomings (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Thus, phantasia functions as the bridge between raw sensory impression and the higher-order cognition of temporal succession, enabling the user to inhabit a world structured by before, now, and after. ## 8. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle is distinctive in its refusal to treat Aristotelian concepts as relics of a bygone metaphysics; instead, Heidegger reads Aristotle as a thinker whose insights into being, motion, and the soul remain vital for contemporary phenomenology. The concept of ὁρισμός, or definition, is for Heidegger not merely a logical tool but a mode of being-in-the-world; (Hawhee 2011), "ὁρισμός is not a way of apprehending through sharp determination, but rather the specific character of ὁρισμός ultimately arises from the fact that the being itself is determined in its being as circumscribed by the πέρας" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 25–26). This reading transforms the notion of definition from an epistemological operation into an ontological event—the self-delimitation of a being within the horizon of its world. Similarly, Heidegger's reading of the passions reveals that they are not static psychological states but dynamic ways of being-moved that constitute the very disclosedness of Dasein; passions are both motions and causes of motions, altering the way in which beings are encountered and judged (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Aristotle's metaphysical insistence that the prime mover must be immovable (Aristotle, *Movement of Animals*, pp. 1–4) is thus reinterpreted by Heidegger as pointing toward a fundamental structure of being itself—the way in which all motion presupposes a ground that is itself beyond motion. Hence, Heidegger's interpretation does not so much impose an alien framework upon Aristotle as it draws out latent possibilities within the Aristotelian text, possibilities that bear directly upon the questions of motion, time, and the ambient structures of rhetorical being that remain central to our national parks of philosophical inquiry. ---
