# A₀: Motion and Time as the First Actuality of the Perceptual Chain

The present section establishes motion and time as the first actuality — designated A₀ — upon which the entire chain of perceptual actualities depends. If perception is to be understood as a form of discriminating contact between rational soul and sensible world, then the ontological ground of that contact must be laid prior to any account of sense-organs, sensible objects, or the discriminating faculty itself. That ground is κίνησις, understood as the fulfilment of the potential qua potential, and χρόνος, understood as the number of motion in respect of before and after. Together, motion and time constitute the condition without which no sensible quality could exercise its power upon a medium, no medium could transmit that exercise to a sense-organ, and no sense-organ could receive the form without the matter. This section accordingly proceeds through three moments internal to *De Anima* III.2–3, reading each moment as evidence that Aristotle's account of perceptual discrimination presupposes the prior actuality of motion and time as its architectonic foundation. The first moment concerns the temporal unity required for discrimination; the second concerns the ontological structure of the discriminating faculty as numerically one yet divided in being; and the third concerns the definitional claim that the soul's two distinctive peculiarities are local movement and cognition. In each case, the argument demonstrates that what is at stake in discrimination cannot be rendered intelligible apart from the first actuality of motion and time — that is, apart from A₀. ---

## 1. "The Discriminating Power and the Time of Its Exercise Must Be One and Undivided" (*DA* III.2–3, 427a1-ff)

When Aristotle asserts that what discriminates the good and the bad does so at one and the same moment, and that the discriminating power and the time of its exercise must therefore be one and undivided, he secures a thesis whose implications extend well beyond the immediate context of perceptual judgment. The thesis is this: if discrimination involves the simultaneous awareness of difference — if the soul asserts both that the good and the bad are present now, and that they differ now — then the discriminating power cannot itself be temporally divided without ceasing to be a power of discrimination at all. This claim, however, is intelligible only against the background of Aristotle's account of time as the number of motion in respect of before and after, for the very notion of a "now" in which both objects are present presupposes a determinate relationship between motion and its temporal measure. Indeed, the "now" that is at issue in the discriminating act is not a bare instant abstracted from all process; it is, rather, the now of a continuous motion whose temporal character makes discrimination possible by holding the discriminating power and its objects together within a single, undivided present. To appreciate the force of this requirement, we must first attend to the structure of time as Aristotle articulates it in the *Physics*. Aristotle maintains that in so far as the now "is in succession, it is different (which is just what its being now was supposed to mean), but its substratum is the same; for motion, as was said, goes with magnitude, and time, as we maintain, with motion" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 60). The now, then, is not a static point but a moving boundary whose identity is constituted by the motion it measures; it is always the same substratum considered under different determinations, just as the moving body is the same body at different positions along a magnitude. This account is essential to the argument of *De Anima* III.2–3, because the discriminating power's unity with the time of its exercise depends upon the now being a single, undivided moment in which both objects — the good and the bad, the sweet and the bitter — are simultaneously present to the soul. Were the now itself divided, the soul could not assert both that the objects are present and that they differ, for it would have to assert the presence of one object in one now and the presence of the other in a different now, and accordingly it would not be discriminating between them at all but merely registering them in temporal succession. The warrant for this inference is grounded in the broader metaphysical principle that movement requires the prior actuality of that from which and that into which the moving thing moves. Aristotle observes in the *Metaphysics* that "if there is movement, and something moved, and everything is moved out of something and into something, it follows that that which is moved must first be in that out of which it is to be moved, and then not be in it, and move into the other and come to be in it, and that the contradictory statements are not true at the same time, as our opponents assert they are" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 129). The denial of simultaneous contradictories is thus a condition of the intelligibility of motion itself, and by extension it is a condition of the intelligibility of time, since time is the number of motion. But the discriminating act, too, depends on the non-contradiction of the now: the soul holds both objects before it at the same time precisely because the now is undivided, and it asserts their difference precisely because the now, though undivided, admits of the differential structure that Aristotle calls "before and after." Hence the discriminating power and the time of its exercise are bound together in a unity that is neither merely logical nor merely psychological but ontological — a unity grounded in the first actuality of motion and time. Furthermore, we may observe that this ontological grounding has implications for the rhetorical and persuasive dimensions of discriminative judgment. As Kenneth Burke argues, there is a sense in which the moment of discrimination is always also a moment of identification and division, for the soul that asserts both that the good and the bad are present and that they differ is performing a cognitive act that is structurally analogous to the rhetorical act of establishing what is fitting or proper. Burke notes that "the classic criterion of fitness (decorum, to prepon), while referring to a purely technical adaptation of artistic means to artistic end, can be invoked against a work which violates dominant criteria of social propriety" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, p. 149). The adaptation of means to end that Burke describes under the heading of decorum is, in its formal structure, precisely the adaptation of discriminating power to discriminated object that Aristotle analyzes in *De Anima* III.2–3; in both cases, the act of discrimination presupposes a unity of the discriminating agent with the moment of its exercise, a unity that cannot be temporally divided without destroying the fitness of the act itself. Thus, the rhetorical dimension of discrimination is parasitic upon the ontological dimension: without the first actuality of motion and time, there is no undivided now in which the soul can hold together the objects it discriminates, and accordingly there is no discrimination, no fitness, and no decorum. It is perhaps worth emphasizing, moreover, that the temporal undividedness required for discrimination is not a merely passive feature of the now but an active condition that the soul must sustain in its very exercise of the discriminating power. The soul does not simply find itself in an undivided now; it holds the now together by virtue of its capacity for simultaneous awareness. This capacity, however, is itself grounded in the prior actuality of motion and time, for it is only because motion is continuous and time measures motion continuously that the now can be undivided in the relevant sense. As Aristotle observes in the *Physics*, "every magnitude is divisible into magnitudes — for we have shown that it is impossible for anything continuous to be composed of indivisible parts, and every magnitude is continuous" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 80). The continuity of magnitude grounds the continuity of motion, and the continuity of motion grounds the continuity of time, and the continuity of time grounds the undividedness of the now — and it is this undividedness that, in turn, grounds the unity of the discriminating power with the time of its exercise. The chain of dependencies is thus: magnitude → motion → time → the undivided now → the unity of discrimination. The first actuality of the chain, A₀, is motion and time; everything else — including the discriminating power analyzed in *De Anima* III.2–3 — is downstream. Indeed, the claim that the discriminating power must be temporally undivided is, in the final analysis, a claim about the priority of actuality over potentiality. The discriminating power, considered as a capacity, is merely potential; it becomes actual only in the exercise of discrimination, and that exercise is possible only within the undivided now that motion and time provide. Thus the first actuality of motion and time is the condition for the second actuality of discrimination, and the architectonic structure of the perceptual chain is secured at its foundation. As Aristotle insists, "the principles of eternal things must be always most true; for they are not merely sometimes true, nor is there any cause of their being, but they themselves are the cause of the being of other things, so that as each thing is in respect of being, so is it in respect of truth" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 21). Motion and time, as the eternal and always-actual ground of perceptual discrimination, are accordingly the first principles of the perceptual chain — not merely its temporal preconditions but its ontological causes. ## 2. "What Discriminates, Though Both Numerically One and Indivisible, Is at the Same Time Divided in Its Being" (*DA* III.2–3, 427a1-ff)

The second moment of the argument concerns the internal structure of the discriminating faculty. Aristotle claims that what discriminates, though both numerically one and indivisible, is at the same time divided in its being. This formula — one in substrate, different in being — is the same formula that Aristotle deploys for perception and appetite at *De Anima* III.7 (431a13–14), and it recurs throughout his natural philosophy wherever a single subject must bear contrary determinations without being destroyed by them. The formula is, in effect, an application of the distinction between substrate and being that Aristotle develops in the *Physics* and the *Metaphysics*, and its deployment in the context of perceptual discrimination reveals the extent to which the discriminating faculty depends upon the prior actuality of motion for its very intelligibility. Consider the parallel with motion. In *Physics* IV.11, Aristotle argues that the now is "in succession" different, but its "substratum is the same; for motion, as was said, goes with magnitude, and time, as we maintain, with motion" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 60). The now is thus numerically one (it is always the same substratum, the same moving boundary) but divided in its being (it is always different in succession, marking a different position along the magnitude). This is precisely the structure that Aristotle attributes to the discriminating faculty: the faculty is numerically one and indivisible — it is a single point or a single capacity — but it is divided in its being insofar as it takes on the forms of the different objects it discriminates. The analogy between the now and the discriminating faculty is therefore not merely illustrative but constitutive: the discriminating faculty is structured like the now because both are instances of the more general ontological principle that a single substrate can bear contrary determinations without contradiction, provided that the contrariety belongs to being rather than to substrate. This principle is, however, ultimately grounded in Aristotle's account of motion as the actuality of the potential qua potential. For it is motion that provides the paradigmatic case of a single substrate bearing contrary determinations: the moving body is at one moment white and at another moment black, at one moment here and at another moment there, and yet it remains numerically one throughout the process of change. As Aristotle observes in the *Metaphysics*, "the same thing can be both, even at the same time if it is a universal (e.g. man can be both white and black), and if it is an individual it can still be both; for the same man can be, though not at the same time, white and black" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 122–124). The qualification "not at the same time" is significant, for it shows that the non-simultaneous bearing of contraries is a feature of motion considered in its temporal extension; but the discriminating faculty, as we have seen, must bear its contraries at the same time, in the undivided now. How is this possible? The answer lies in the distinction between substrate and being: the faculty is not simultaneously white and black in the same respect, but simultaneously determined by whiteness and blackness in its being while remaining numerically one in its substrate. The faculty is, as it were, the point at which two contrary motions converge — the motion of the white toward the perceiver and the motion of the black toward the perceiver — and it holds them together in a single act of discrimination by being undivided in substrate but divided in being. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle illuminates this convergence of contrary determinations within a single ontological site. In his lectures on the basic concepts of Aristotelian philosophy, Heidegger observes that "the being-context about which I say, 'the being is in movement,' is determined by the category of the πρός τι, the 'being-in-relation-to-an-other,' namely, the moving thing characterized by ποίησις, the moved thing by πάθησις" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 232). The discriminating faculty, in its relation to its objects, occupies precisely this πρός τι structure: it is the site of πάθησις, the undergoing that corresponds to the ποίησις of the sensible object's exercise of its sensible power. But the faculty can undergo contrary affections simultaneously only because it is, in its substrate, one and undivided — a single point of reception that is nonetheless differentiated in its being by the contrary forms it receives. Thus the formula "one in substrate, different in being" is not merely a logical formula but an ontological one, grounded in the structure of motion as the actuality of the potential qua potential. Similarly, the formula has implications for the way in which motion and time function as the first actuality of the perceptual chain. If the discriminating faculty is divided in its being, then its being is a complex structure — a structure that includes within itself the differentiation of contrary forms. But this differentiation is possible only because motion has already actualized the sensible qualities that the faculty receives: the white must be actually white, the black actually black, before the faculty can be divided in its being by their presence. The prior actuality of motion — the actuality by which sensible qualities come to be actual in the sensible world — is thus the condition for the faculty's being divided in its being. And since this actuality is temporal (since motion goes with time, and time with motion), the prior actuality of time is likewise a condition for the faculty's complex being. Thus A₀ — motion and time as the first actuality — is the ground upon which the discriminating faculty's internal differentiation is built. Furthermore, the distinction between substrate and being that Aristotle applies to the discriminating faculty is itself an instance of the broader metaphysical distinction between substance and its determinations. As Aristotle argues in the *Physics*, "substance is a single genus of being, so that the principles can differ only as prior and posterior, not in genus; for in a single genus there is always a single contrariety, all the other contrarieties in it being held to be reducible to one" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 11). The discriminating faculty, as a single substrate, is a kind of substance — not substance in the primary sense, but substance in the analogical sense of being the underlying subject of contrary determinations. Its unity is the unity of a single genus, and its differentiation is the differentiation of prior and posterior within that genus. The "single contrariety" to which all other contrarieties are reducible is, in the context of perceptual discrimination, the contrariety between the sensible qualities that the faculty discriminates — and this contrariety is itself a product of motion, since it is motion that brings contraries into being and sustains them in their actuality. Heidegger reinforces this reading when he notes that there are four types of movement corresponding to four categories: "Οὐσία corresponds to the mode of movement of γένεσις and φθορά, for τόπος there is φορά, for ποσόν there is φθίσις, for ποιόν there is ἀλλοίωσις" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 234). The discriminating faculty is divided in its being precisely because ἀλλοίωσις — qualitative alteration — has already occurred in the sensible world, producing the contrary qualities that the faculty must simultaneously receive. Without the prior actuality of this motion, the faculty would have nothing to discriminate, and its complex being — one in substrate, different in being — would collapse into undifferentiated potentiality. Moreover, the formula "one in substrate, different in being" recalls and deepens the relationship between the φάντασμα as memory-image and temporal awareness. As Aristotle maintains, the soul never thinks without a φάντασμα; the image functions simultaneously as an object of contemplation in itself and as a likeness (εἰκών) of the thing remembered. This dual-aspect structure mirrors the dual-aspect structure of the discriminating faculty: just as the faculty is one in substrate but divided in its being, so the φάντασμα is one image but divided into a present object of thought and a past object of memory. Heidegger's analysis of temporality in *Being and Time* develops this dual-aspect structure into a full account of Dasein's temporal constitution, for the having-been (Gewesenheit) of ecstatic temporality is precisely the sense in which what is present retains within itself the trace of what is past. As Heidegger observes, Dasein is "essentially not a Being-present-at-hand; and its 'spatiality' cannot signify anything like occurrence at a position in 'world-space,' nor can it signify Being-ready-to-hand at some place" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 139). The discriminating faculty's being, similarly, is not a present-at-hand occurrence but a temporal structure in which the past and the present converge — a convergence made possible by the prior actuality of motion and time. Thus the cross-textual bridge between Aristotle's φάντασμα and Heidegger's account of temporality confirms that the first actuality of motion and time is the condition for the discriminating faculty's complex internal structure. ## 3. "The Soul Is Characterized by Two Distinctive Peculiarities: Local Movement and the Cognitive Activities of Thinking, Understanding, and Perceiving" (*DA* III.3, 427a1)

Aristotle's definitional claim that the soul is characterized by two distinctive peculiarities — local movement and the cognitive activities of thinking, understanding, and perceiving — bears directly on the relationship between the soul and motion at A₀. For if the soul's two defining characteristics are motion and cognition, then motion is not merely an external condition that the soul encounters in the world; it is constitutive of the soul's very being. The soul is essentially motile, and its cognitive activities — thinking, understanding, perceiving — are modes of its motility. This claim, however, does not entail that the soul is itself in motion in the way that a body is in motion; rather, it entails that the soul's relationship to motion is internal and definitional, such that the soul's cognitive capacities cannot be understood apart from the prior actuality of motion in the natural world. Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle's concept of κίνησις is instructive here. In his lectures on the basic concepts of Aristotelian philosophy, Heidegger argues that being-in-movement is not a property added to a being from outside but a determination of the being's very being-there. Heidegger observes that "if a being in its being-there is characterized by ποίησις, there is a being there with it" that stands in the relation of πάθησις, and that accordingly "there are two modes of being-present in relation to movement: ποίησις and πάθησις" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 232–235). The soul, as the form of a living body, is characterized by both modes: it is the site of ποίησις insofar as it initiates local movement in the body, and it is the site of πάθησις insofar as it receives the forms of sensible objects in perception. The two distinctive peculiarities of the soul — local movement and cognition — thus correspond to the two modes of being-present in relation to movement, and the soul's being is accordingly constituted by its relationship to motion at the most fundamental level. This ontological constitution has implications for the way in which the soul relates to the world of sensible objects. If the soul is essentially characterized by local movement, then its cognitive contact with the world is not a disembodied act of contemplation but an engaged, motile encounter with things that are themselves in motion. 15–17) applies not only to the natural bodies that surround the soul but to the ensouled body itself, whose nature is to move and to perceive. The soul's local movement — its capacity to initiate locomotion in the body — is the first and most evident manifestation of its relationship to κίνησις; but its cognitive activities are no less dependent on motion, for perception requires the transmission of sensible forms through a medium, and this transmission is itself a species of motion (specifically, ἀλλοίωσις, qualitative alteration). Thus the soul's two distinctive peculiarities are not merely juxtaposed but internally connected: local movement and cognition are both modes of the soul's essential relationship to the first actuality of motion. Furthermore, this essential relationship illuminates the sense in which the soul's cognitive activities are activities — that is, actualities rather than mere potentialities. Aristotle's distinction between first actuality and second actuality is pertinent here: the soul's cognitive capacities, considered as first actualities, are dispositions or states of readiness that can be exercised in the act of perception or thought; but their exercise — the second actuality — presupposes the prior actuality of motion in the natural world. The soul cannot perceive unless sensible objects are exercising their sensible powers, and sensible objects cannot exercise their sensible powers unless motion has brought them into actuality. As Aristotle argues, "the principles of eternal things must be always most true; for they are not merely sometimes true, nor is there any cause of their being, but they themselves are the cause of the being of other things" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 21). Motion, as the eternal actuality that underlies all coming-to-be and passing-away, is the cause of the being of sensible qualities, and hence the cause of the being of the soul's cognitive contact with those qualities. The soul's two distinctive peculiarities are thus grounded in A₀. The rhetorical implications of this grounding are significant. Kenneth Burke, in his analysis of the relationship between motion and action, suggests that wherever there is meaning, there is persuasion, and that the very structure of motivated action presupposes a kind of ontological ground that cannot be reduced to mere physical movement. Rickert, extending this analysis, observes that the "I-situation" retains "a sense of I-ness and hence can provide an entryway for more intensely thinking the dissolution of any traditional notion of the subject" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 118–119). The soul's relationship to motion at A₀ is, in a sense, the ontological analogue of this I-situation: it is the site at which the distinction between motion and cognition — between the physical and the psychological — is both established and dissolved, for the soul's two distinctive peculiarities are internally connected through their common dependence on the first actuality of motion. The dissolution of the traditional subject-object distinction that Rickert describes in the context of ambient rhetoric has its Aristotelian precedent in the soul's essential motility, which ensures that cognition is never a disembodied act but always an engaged, temporal, motile encounter with the world. Indeed, Heidegger's analysis of Being-in-the-world in *Being and Time* develops this insight in a manner that is directly relevant to the present argument. Heidegger argues that Dasein's Being is fundamentally characterized by its involvement with a world of equipment and signification, and that this involvement cannot be understood in terms of mere spatial occurrence. As Heidegger observes, "when we have to do with anything, the mere seeing of the Things which are closest to us bears in itself the structure of interpretation, and in so primordial a manner that just to grasp something free, as it were, of the 'as', requires a certain readjustment" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 191). The prior actuality of motion — A₀ — is the condition for this engagement, for without motion there would be no world of sensible objects with which the soul (or Dasein) could be involved. Thus Heidegger's phenomenological analysis of Being-in-the-world confirms the Aristotelian thesis that motion and time are the first actuality of the perceptual chain. Moreover, the connection between Aristotle's two distinctive peculiarities and Heidegger's temporal ecstases illuminates the deeper structure of A₀. Heidegger's analysis of Zeitlichkeit as the primordial horizon for the understanding of Being develops this suggestion into a full-blown ontological account: temporality is not a property that the soul (or Dasein) possesses but the horizon within which its being unfolds. The analogy between Aristotle's now — "in succession different, but its substratum the same" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 60) — and Heidegger's ecstatic temporality is thus more than a historical coincidence; it is an indication that both thinkers recognized motion and time as the first actuality upon which all subsequent actualities — including the soul's cognitive activities — depend. Burke's observation that we should not "expect to find a flat antithesis between 'body' and 'spirit'" but rather that "'body' (in even its 'lowliest' forms) [should] be treated as a way into 'spirit'" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, p. 203) similarly speaks to the continuity between the soul's motile and cognitive dimensions. The soul's local movement is not opposed to its cognitive activities but is the bodily way into those activities; motion is the first actuality through which the soul enters into cognitive contact with the world. Thus the definitional claim of *De Anima* III.3, 427a1 — that the soul is characterized by two distinctive peculiarities — is ultimately a claim about the architectonic priority of A₀: motion and time are the ground upon which the soul's cognitive activities are built, and without that ground, the perceptual chain cannot begin. ## Conclusion

The foregoing analysis has demonstrated that motion and time, understood as A₀ — the first actuality of the perceptual chain — are the ontological condition for everything that follows in Aristotle's account of perceptual discrimination. The first moment of the argument showed that the discriminating power and the time of its exercise must be one and undivided, and that this unity presupposes the continuity of motion and the undividedness of the now. The second moment showed that the discriminating faculty is numerically one and indivisible but divided in its being, and that this complex internal structure mirrors the structure of the now itself, which is the same in substrate but different in succession. The third moment showed that the soul's two distinctive peculiarities — local movement and cognition — are internally connected through their common dependence on the first actuality of motion, and that this dependence is confirmed by Heidegger's phenomenological analysis of Being-in-the-world and Burke's rhetorical analysis of the continuity between body and spirit. In each case, the argument has proceeded by showing that the structures analyzed in *De Anima* III.2–3 are unintelligible apart from the prior actuality of motion and time — that without A₀, the perceptual chain cannot be grounded, the discriminating faculty cannot function, and the soul's cognitive activities cannot be actualized. The section thus licenses everything downstream in the chain: A₁, the world containing rational souls, sensible objects exercising their sensible powers, and sense-organs holding their first actuality as capacities, is possible only because A₀ — motion and time as the first actuality — has already secured the ontological ground upon which it stands. # VALIDATION APPENDIX
