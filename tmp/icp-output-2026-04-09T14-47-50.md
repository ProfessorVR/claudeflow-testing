Aristotle's phantasia emerges as a fundamentally disclosive capacity that structures the soul's encounter with beings beyond the immediacy of sensory presence, operating as an intermediary mechanism between perception and intellectual cognition that renders the absent accessible to consciousness. Heidegger's interpretation captures this mediating function precisely when he characterizes Aristotelian thinking as "not an appeal to a brain process, but to φαντασία, the 'making-present-to-itself' of the world, in which what is made present is not actually there, but instead is, say, in memory" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 149), thus positioning phantasia not as a mere psychological mechanism but as the condition for the world's self-disclosure beyond perceptual immediacy. Indeed, Aristotle himself establishes phantasia's essential role in theoretical cognition when he insists that thinking "cannot do without 'images'" (*De Anima* 431b2), thereby indicating that even the highest intellectual operations require this making-present-to-itself of what is not immediately given in sensation. This recognition has subsequently informed contemporary scholarship, which acknowledges "Aristotelian phantasia as a crucial part of the meaning-making process" (Ballif, *Theorizing Histories of Rhetoric*, p. 4), suggesting that its function transcends mere reproduction of sensory content and instead participates in the soul's active structuring of intelligibility. The characterization of phantasia as "making-present-to-itself" thus reveals its properly disclosive function: it does not simply recall or reproduce absent objects but renders them accessible to consciousness in their absence, thereby establishing the soul's relationship to beings that exceed the here-and-now of perception. This mediating role positions phantasia as the condition for encountering the world beyond sense-experience, yet this very function introduces a fundamental tension with Aristotle's causal derivation of phantasia from actual sensation (*De Anima* III.3), for if phantasia operates autonomously in memory, deliberation, and theoretical reasoning, its strict dependence on prior aisthēsis becomes problematic—a tension between causal origin and functional independence that reverberates throughout *De Anima*'s account of the soul's higher capacities.

Heidegger's approach, developed most systematically in his 1924 Marburg lectures, reads Aristotle's psychological treatises through the lens of *alētheia* as unconcealment, transforming phantasia from a psychological faculty into an ontological structure of world-disclosure (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 112). Specifically, Heidegger treats phantasia's "making-present" (*phainesthai*) as a mode of bringing beings into presence rather than as a mechanism for processing sensory data, aligning it with his broader project of recovering pre-Cartesian modes of encountering beings (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 127). However, this phenomenological appropriation risks subordinating Aristotle's detailed causal and functional analyses—phantasia's derivation from actual sensation, its role in animal locomotion, its susceptibility to error—to a disclosive framework that may not capture the full range of its operations in *De Anima*. The tension between Heidegger's emphasis on disclosure and Aristotle's biological-psychological account becomes particularly acute when phantasia functions in contexts where disclosure seems secondary to practical or cognitive tasks: the *phantasmata* required for thought (*De Anima* III.7, 431a14-17), the dream-images that arise without external stimuli (*De Anima* III.3, 428b10-17), or the appearances that guide animal movement without conceptual content (*De Anima* III.10, 433b27-30). Consequently, while Heidegger's reading illuminates phantasia's ontological significance as a structure of presence and absence, it may underplay those aspects of Aristotle's account that resist assimilation to a disclosure-centered framework, particularly phantasia's embeddedness in the psychophysical architecture of perception and its functional role in domains where bringing-to-presence serves biological rather than ontological ends.

Heidegger's concept of *Erschlossenheit* (disclosedness) establishes the existential structure through which Dasein's Being-in-the-world becomes manifest, radically departing from epistemological frameworks that treat world-access as a cognitive achievement or faculty-based operation (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 172). The 'there' (*Da*) constitutes Dasein's essential character such that "if it lacks its 'there', it is not factically the entity which is essentially Dasein; indeed, it is not this entity at all" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 172)—a formulation that renders disclosedness not an attribute Dasein possesses but the ontological condition of its very Being. This existential structure emerges from Heidegger's appropriation of Aristotle, where "the being-there of the human being was explicated as being-in-the-world, in such a way that λόγος came forth as the basic character of this being, determined as being-in: the how of the there of discoveredness" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 254), positioning λόγος as the articulative dimension of Dasein's constitutive openness rather than a logical faculty superimposed upon mute intuition. The world's disclosive function extends beyond revealing equipment: it "not only frees the ready-to-hand as entities encountered within-the-world; it also frees Dasein, and the Others in their Dasein-with" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 161), establishing disclosedness as the condition for both entity-manifestation and the mutual encounter of Dasein with itself and others in shared world-space. Nevertheless, this ontological prioritization of disclosedness creates interpretive tension with Aristotle's psychophysical architecture, where λόγος functions within a biological framework of perception, phantasia, and practical reasoning—domains where disclosure may serve functional ends (locomotion, deliberation, memory) rather than constituting the primary ontological phenomenon (Aristotle, *De Anima*, III.3, 428a1-5; III.10, 433b27-30). The question remains whether Heidegger's transformation of λόγος from a psychological capacity into the existential structure of Being-in-the-world adequately accounts for those passages where Aristotle treats discursive thought as causally dependent upon prior sensory and imaginative operations, suggesting that manifestness might emerge from psychic processes rather than grounding them (Aristotle, *De Anima*, III.7, 431a14-17).

Both phantasia and Erschlossenheit function as pre-predicative structures of disclosure that operate prior to explicit judgment, enabling the manifestation of beings before propositional articulation, yet they achieve this foundational role through fundamentally divergent architectures that expose rather than resolve the tension between Aristotelian psychophysics and Heideggerian existential ontology. In Aristotelian thinking, phantasia operates as 'making-present-to-itself' of the world where 'what is made present is not actually there,' functioning independently of immediate sensory presence while remaining causally tethered to prior perception (Aristotle, *De Anima*, III.3, 428a1-5; Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 165). This capacity to make-present what is absent operates before explicit cognitive acts, positioning phantasia as a mediating faculty between sensation and thought, where the image (φάντασμα) enables νοῦς to think universals through particular representations (Aristotle, *De Anima*, III.7, 431a14-17; III.8, 432a3-10). In contrast, Heidegger's Erschlossenheit as the structure of being-in constitutes the condition for any particular encounter with beings, where "the being-there of the human being was explicated as being-in-the-world, in such a way that λόγος came forth as the basic character of this being, determined as being-in: the how of the there of discoveredness" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 254). Heidegger further identifies uncovering where 'there is for uncovering no possibility of dissimulating, of mis-leading,' suggesting a primordial mode of disclosure prior to propositional truth or falsity that operates at the level of Dasein's existential constitution rather than as a psychological faculty (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 56-57, 265-266). This pre-predicative character positions both as foundational structures enabling subsequent cognitive and linguistic articulation, but whereas phantasia remains embedded within the functional architecture of the soul's perceptual and locomotive capacities—serving biological ends of survival, memory, and practical deliberation (Aristotle, *De Anima*, III.10, 433b27-30; *De Memoria*, 449b30-450a7)—Erschlossenheit claims ontological priority as the very structure through which world and self are co-originally disclosed, making Dasein's being-in rather than a psychic operation the ground of all manifestness (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 78-79, 161-162). The interpretive challenge lies precisely in this asymmetry: Aristotle's phantasia achieves its pre-predicative status by mediating between lower (sensation) and higher (intellection) psychic functions, whereas Heidegger's Erschlossenheit refuses such stratification, insisting that disclosure is not a capacity among others but the existential condition for any capacity whatsoever, thereby transforming rather than merely reinterpreting the Aristotelian framework (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 254-255, 280-281).

This structural parallel operates at the functional level; the ontological status of these structures differs significantly between Aristotle's and Heidegger's frameworks. For Aristotle, *phantasia* remains a psychic capacity (*dunamis*) belonging to the soul as the first actuality (*entelecheia*) of a natural body potentially having life, and its ontological status derives from its position within a hierarchical architecture of faculties where sensation provides the material basis from which imagination causally arises (Aristotle, *De Anima*, 414a12-14, 428a5-11). The *De Anima* defines the soul as "the first actuality of a natural body potentially having life" and establishes that "imagination is different from both perception and thought, though it is not found without sensation, and it is not possible to form beliefs without it," thereby situating *phantasia* within a causal-functional sequence where lower capacities ground higher ones (Aristotle, *De Anima*, 412a27-28, 427b14-16). Heidegger's *Erschlossenheit*, by contrast, does not name a faculty or capacity but rather the existential structure through which Dasein *is* its "there" (*Da*), a structure that cannot be derived from or grounded in anything more primordial because it constitutes the very condition of possibility for entities to show themselves as anything whatsoever (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 171-172, 226). Where Aristotle's ontology permits stratification—sensation is prior to imagination, imagination to intellection—Heidegger's existential analytic insists on the equiprimordiality (*Gleichursprünglichkeit*) of state-of-mind (*Befindlichkeit*), understanding (*Verstehen*), and discourse (*Rede*) as co-constitutive moments of disclosedness, refusing any hierarchical ordering that would make one existentiale the foundation for others (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 172-173, 203-204). The ontological divergence emerges most sharply in their respective treatments of the relationship between structure and temporality: Aristotle's *phantasia* operates within a soul whose temporal character consists in being the actuality of a body capable of undergoing change across time, whereas Heidegger's *Erschlossenheit* is temporality itself in its ecstatic unity, not something that occurs *in* time but the horizon within which anything like temporal succession or psychic process becomes intelligible (Aristotle, *De Anima*, 412a27-b6; Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 377-380, 401-402).

The divergence between Aristotle's faculty psychology and Heidegger's existential analytic crystallizes around fundamentally opposed ontological commitments regarding the status of psychic structures: Aristotle situates *phantasia* within a hylomorphic framework where it functions as a capacity (*dynamis*) belonging to the soul (*psyche*) as the form of a living substance, such that capacities are understood as properties that inhere in the substance itself (Aristotle, *De Anima*, 403a3-10, 414a4-14). This substance-ontological framework determines that "the being whose being constitutes the τί ἤν εἶναι is designated as ἕκαστον, 'the particular,' the τί ἤν εἶναι constitutes the particularity of beings," thereby anchoring capacities within a metaphysics where individual substances possess definitive properties as constituents of their essential nature (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 251). Heidegger's existential analytic, by contrast, transforms the locus of agency by rejecting this entire substance-metaphysical apparatus, refusing to treat Dasein's structures as present-at-hand properties that a subject possesses and instead analyzing them as constitutive of Dasein's way of Being (*Seinsweise*) itself (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 67-68, 114-115). The characterization of Dasein's "there" (*Da*) as essential to its being—not as a property inhering in a substance but as the very disclosure within which anything like properties or substances can first become manifest—stands in direct opposition to faculty psychology's treatment of capacities as attributes that belong to an already-constituted psychic substance (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 171-172, 220-224). Where Aristotle's *phantasia* remains ontologically dependent upon the soul as its substantial bearer, Heidegger's *Erschlossenheit* designates not a faculty possessed by Dasein but the disclosive openness that Dasein *is*, thereby dismantling the subject-property structure that underwrites the entire Aristotelian analysis of psychic capacities (Aristotle, *De Anima*, 429a10-13; Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 132-134, 162-163).

Despite their divergent ontological frameworks, both Aristotelian *phantasia* and Heideggerian *Erschlossenheit* exhibit a shared rhetorical-environmental dimension that positions the subject within disclosive contexts rather than in isolated cognitive space. Aristotle's treatment of *phantasia* in rhetorical contexts demonstrates this situational character, where the faculty operates not merely as internal image-production but as a mode of engagement with appearances structured by persuasive discourse; as O'Gorman observes, *phantasia* concerns "Lexis, Appearance, and the Epideictic Function of Discourse," indicating that imaginative presentation unfolds within the ambient conditions of rhetorical reception rather than in abstract mental operations (O'Gorman, "Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric," p. 1). Heidegger's appropriation of rhetorical language to describe concealment reveals a parallel environmental structure: "Thus, in his very first listing of the three modes of concealment, Heidegger attributes two of them directly to the language of rhetoric," suggesting that disclosure and concealment operate through situated, linguistically mediated contexts rather than through subject-independent cognitive mechanisms (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 143). This convergence points toward what contemporary interpretation recognizes as a fundamental but historically obscured dimension of human experience: "An important lesson here is that humans have always attended to the ambient, even if we are only now gaining self-reflexive access to that insight" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 23). The rhetorical dimension common to both frameworks reveals that disclosure is not a cognitive event occurring within a subject but a mode of world-encounter structured by ambient, persuasive, and situational contexts—a recognition that challenges the Cartesian assumption of cognition as occurring in detachment from environmental embeddedness (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, 1403b6-1404a39; Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 203-210).
