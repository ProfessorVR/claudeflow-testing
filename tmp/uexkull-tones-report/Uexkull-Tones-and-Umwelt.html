Jakob von Uexküll

Ton (“tone”), Stimmung (“mood”), and the Tonal Constitution of the Umwelt (“surrounding-world,” “phenomenal world,” “self-world,” “environment”)

A Detailed Analysis of A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans (1934) and A Theory of Meaning (Bedeutungslehre, 1940)

Source: Jakob von Uexküll, A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans, with A Theory of Meaning, trans. Joseph D. O’Neil (University of Minnesota Press, 2010). Original German titles: Streifzüge durch die Umwelten von Tieren und Menschen (“Forays through the surrounding-worlds of animals and humans,” 1934) and Bedeutungslehre (“Theory of meaning,” 1940). Primary chapters under interpretation: Foray Chapter 1 (“Foreword,” pp. 41–43) and Chapter 2 (“Introduction,” pp. 44–52); Chapter 9 (“Perception Image and Effect Image,” pp. 92–97); Chapter 13 (“Search Image and Search Tone,” pp. 113–118); Chapter 14 (“Magical Environments,” pp. 119–125); Chapter 15 (“The Same Subject as Object in Different Environments,” pp. 126–132); Bedeutungslehre Chapter 1 (“Carriers of Meaning,” pp. 139–145); Chapter 7 (“The Composition Theory of Nature,” pp. 171–181); Chapter 12 (“Summary and Conclusion,” pp. 200–208). Page numbers refer to the published English edition.

1. The Problem and the Stakes

The aim of Uexküll’s biological-philosophical project, undertaken from his Hamburg laboratory between the wars and given its mature form in the 1934 Foray and the 1940 Bedeutungslehre (“Theory of meaning”), is to wrest the analysis of animal life out of two converging traditions that, on his diagnosis, have “lost the phenomenon” of the living being. The first is the Maschinentheorie (“machine theory”)—the post-Cartesian, post-Darwinian biology that treats organisms as objects governed by efferent and afferent reflex arcs, animated by mechanical causation, and selected by environmental pressure. The second is the equally obstinate Vorstellungspsychologie (“psychology of representations”)—the doctrine that an animal’s relation to its world is constituted by inner images that mirror an outer object-world held to be the same for all observers. Against both, Uexküll’s central thesis is that the living being is a Subjekt (“subject”) whose world is constituted, not surveyed; that this constituted world—the Umwelt (“surrounding-world,” “phenomenal world,” “self-world,” “environment”)—is closed, complete, and saturated with what he will call Töne (“tones”); and that the living being’s inhabitation of its Umwelt is best understood not on the model of a machine receiving inputs, nor on the model of a mind picturing externals, but on the model of an instrument joining a melody.

The lectures of Heidegger that occupy the same cultural moment (the 1924 Marburg course, the 1927 Being and Time, the 1929/30 Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics) circle the same problem from a different angle, with different stakes; the convergence between Uexküll and Heidegger is not coincidental but reflects a shared preoccupation in the German-speaking philosophical-biological-phenomenological milieu of the 1920s and 1930s with the question of how to wrest the analysis of finite living being from a Cartesian inheritance that had everywhere petrified the phenomenon. Heidegger explicitly cites Uexküll in Sein und Zeit §§14–18 and develops the Umwelt analysis from within Uexküll’s biological vocabulary; Uexküll’s Stimmung (“mood”)—as it appears in the hermit crab analysis of Foray Chapter 9—anticipates by several years what Heidegger will name Befindlichkeit (“state-of-mind,” “disposedness,” “finding-oneself”) in Being and Time §29. The relations are textual, not merely thematic; the biology and the phenomenology speak to each other in a shared idiom whose grammar is the grammar of tonality.

The analysis that follows takes up Uexküll’s theory of Töne and Umwelt through six concentric framings, each of which Uexküll develops at a different scale. First, the discovery of the Umwelt and the architecture of the Funktionskreis (“functional cycle”): the closed loop through which subject and world are mutually constituted (Foray Chapters 1–2). Second, the formal introduction of Wahrnehmungsbild (“perception image”) and Wirkbild (“effect image”), and the birth of Wirkton (“effect tone”) in the hermit-crab analysis (Chapter 9). Third, Suchbild (“search image”) and Suchton (“search tone”)—innate and acquired tonal templates that guide perceptual orientation prior to any specific stimulus (Chapter 13). Fourth, magische Umwelten (“magical environments”)—worlds in which tones generate phenomena without external trigger (Chapter 14). Fifth, the Same Subject as Object in Different Environments paradigm, where the oak tree appears as a different Bedeutungsträger (“carrier of meaning”) to forester, girl, fox, owl, squirrel, songbird, ant, bark beetle, and ichneumon wasp—each cut into the oak by a different tonal articulation (Chapter 15). Sixth, the Bedeutungslehre’s extension of Töne into the Eigenton (“self-tone”) of the living cell, the Bildungsmelodie (“formative melody”) of the developing organism, and the Kompositionslehre der Natur (“composition theory of nature”) in which all Töne sound together as a Partitur (“score”) that, on Uexküll’s late hyperbolic formulation, is “beyond space and time” (Bedeutungslehre Chapter 12). Throughout, the question is the same: what is a tone, on Uexküll’s account, and how does it constitute the Umwelt of a finite living being?

2. The Discovery of the Umwelt and the Architecture of the Funktionskreis (Foray Chapters 1–2)

2.1 The Anti-Mechanist Foreword

The Foreword opens with a polemical declaration whose terms remain decisive for the entire project: “Such excursions [into the worlds of animals] are still considered fanciful by many natural scientists who cling to the doctrine that all living beings are mere machines. Were they to be machines, they would be without an environment [Umwelt]” (p. 41). The first move is therefore not a definition but a contrast: the Umwelt is what a Subjekt has and a machine lacks. The grammar is decisive—the term Umwelt is not interchangeable with Umgebung (“surroundings”); the latter is the objective field that remains when one strips away the perceiving subject; the former is the closed perceptual-effective world that only a subject can have. Uexküll glosses the contrast through a metaphor he will return to throughout: each animal “is enveloped in a soap bubble [Seifenblase] which encloses its environment” (Foray, Chapter 1, paraphrased; cf. p. 43). The image registers two facts simultaneously: each Umwelt is bounded (the bubble is closed); each Umwelt is its own, not a mere section of a single shared world (every bubble is its own bubble).

The Foreword’s structural argument is that the Umwelt is composed of two coordinated dimensions: the Merkwelt (“perception world”), comprising everything the subject can perceive, and the Wirkwelt (“effect world”), comprising everything the subject can affect. Together they form a closed totality: “We no longer regard animals as mere objects, but as subjects whose essential activity consists in perceiving [merken] and effecting [wirken]. We thereby unlock the gates that lead to other realms.... All that a subject perceives becomes its perception world [Merkwelt] and all that it effects, its effect world [Wirkwelt]. Perception and effect worlds together form a closed unit, the environment [Umwelt]” (Foreword, p. 42). The Cartesian division between res cogitans (the inner mind picturing) and res extensa (the outer world surveyed) is replaced from the start by a different topology: a Subjekt is the closure of a Merkwelt + Wirkwelt, and the Umwelt is just this closure.

The Foreword introduces a further, easily-missed terminological move that will prove decisive for the tonal analysis. The standard German pair Werkzeug (“tool,” “instrument for effecting”) names what an organism uses to act on its world. Uexküll coins, alongside it, Merkzeug (“tool for perceiving,” “perception-tool”)—a neologism that bears the same morphology and grammatical force as Werkzeug but applied to the perceiving side. The pair Merkzeug / Werkzeug indexes a methodological symmetry: perception is not a passive registration but an active operation requiring its own instrumentation, just as effecting is. The animal makes its perception, just as it makes its effect; both are activities of a Subjekt in its Umwelt, not properties of an object in a world.

2.2 The Tick Paradigm

The Introduction opens with what is now the most famous biological exemplum in twentieth-century philosophy of life: the tick (Ixodes ricinus). Uexküll’s analysis of the tick is structurally essential because it shows, in a being whose simplicity makes the tonal architecture visible, that an Umwelt can be both impoverished and complete—that completeness is a function of the closure of the Funktionskreis (“functional cycle”), not of the number of perceivable features. The tick lives by three perception-marks (drei Merkmale): butyric acid (signaling the proximity of a mammal), warmth (signaling skin contact), and the tactile quality of skin-with-hair (signaling the right place to drill). It executes three corresponding Wirkmale (“effect marks”): drop, attach, drill. The tick has waited for as long as eighteen years between life stages, the perception apparatus stilled until butyric acid arrives. “The tick can wait eighteen years. None of us can hold out that long” (p. 47).

The tick’s Umwelt is, by any objective comparison, one of the poorest known. It is also—and this is Uexküll’s polemical point—structurally identical to the human Umwelt in its fundamental ontology: closed Funktionskreis, perception apparatus geared to specific Merkmale, effector apparatus geared to specific Wirkmale, Subjekt constituted in the closure. The conclusion is rendered with full force: “All animal subjects, from the simplest to the most complex, are inserted into their environments to the same degree of perfection” (p. 50). The thesis subverts every evolutionary or developmental hierarchy that would rank the tick beneath the human; it puts forward instead a doctrine of equal completeness through different closures. Each Subjekt has its world, and its world is whole.

2.3 The Funktionskreis and the Anatomy of the Tonal Loop

The Funktionskreis is the structural unit through which the Subjekt-Umwelt relation is articulated. It is a closed loop, diagrammed by Uexküll in Figure 3 of the Introduction, with the following moments: (1) the object’s Merkmalträger (“perception-mark carrier”) is registered by the subject’s Merkorgan (“perception organ,” the brain organ grouping perception cells), generating a Merkzeichen (“perception sign”) inside the subject; (2) the Merkzeichen is “transposed” outward into the Umwelt as a Merkmal (“perception mark”), a quality the object now has for the subject; (3) the subject’s response is initiated by its Wirkorgan (“effect organ”), generating a Wirkzeichen (“effect sign”) inside the subject; (4) the Wirkzeichen is realized externally as a Wirkmal (“effect mark”) imprinted on the object’s Wirkmalträger (“effect-mark carrier”); (5) the Wirkmal “extinguishes” the Merkmal—the perception that triggered the action is consumed in the action’s completion; (6) the cycle closes, and the subject is restored to readiness for a new initiation.

Three structural features of this schema bear on the tonal analysis. First, the object that figures in the Funktionskreis is not the “object-in-itself” of objectivist biology but the Gegengefüge (“counterstructure”)—the object’s structural connection between its Merkmalträger and its Wirkmalträger as these are made available to this subject’s particular cycle. The same object can enter multiple Funktionskreise and be different in each; the tick’s mammal-as-butyric-acid-source and the gardener’s mammal-as-cow-to-be-milked are not the same Gegengefüge even when they are the same animal in the same field. Second, the Merkzeichen / Wirkzeichen internal to the subject is the locus where Uexküll grounds his neo-Kantian commitment: following Johannes Müller’s principle of spezifische Sinnesenergien (“specific sensory energies”), every external stimulus that reaches a perception cell produces that cell’s particular sign, regardless of the stimulus’s external nature; what the subject perceives is therefore always its own internal sign, projected outward into the Umwelt as a Merkmal. The Umwelt is genuinely the subject’s self-world. Third—and this is the seed of the tonal doctrine—the Funktionskreis is structured by what action the perception is for: the same external stimulus, processed through different functional cycles, generates different perception marks because it is held, by the subject’s organic structure, to different effect marks. The Wirkmal—the imminent effect—colors the Merkmal before the Merkmal is fully constituted. This is the structural seed of the Wirkton (“effect tone”).

2.4 The Kantian Frame

The Introduction concludes with two assertions whose philosophical weight is full Kantian: “Without a living subject, there can be no time” (p. 48), and—by extension—no space. Time is Subjekt-relativ: each species has its own Moment (Uexküll’s technical term for the species-specific temporal quantum below which no event can be perceived as differentiated; the human Moment is approximately 1/18 of a second). Space, similarly, is constructed from Richtungsschritte (“directional steps”) whose magnitude is species-specific (approximately 2 cm for the human). The space and time of an Umwelt are not the homogeneous coordinates of Newtonian physics; they are the Anschauungsformen (“forms of intuition”) of a particular Subjekt, in Kant’s idiom, given material biological articulation. “Biology connects with Kant’s epistemology,” Uexküll says—not as a piety but as a structural claim about the place of subjective constitution in the very being of the living being.

The Kantian commitment has consequences for the entire tonal analysis. If space and time are subject-relative, then the field within which Töne sound is itself constituted by the same subject whose Töne sound in it. There is no objective auditorium in which all Umwelten can be heard; each Umwelt is its own auditorium, with its own acoustics, and the Töne that fill it are audible only within it. This will become decisive when we reach the oak paradigm: the squirrel hears the Klettertönung (“climbing tone”) of the bark, the bark beetle hears the Fresston (“feeding tone”) of its underside, the ichneumon wasp hears the wood as soft-as-butter—and none of these tones is heard by any other inhabitant.

3. Wahrnehmungsbild, Wirkbild, and the Birth of Ton (Foray Chapter 9)

3.1 The Hermit Crab Experiment: The Paradigmatic Mood-Determined Tone

Chapter 9, “Perception Image and Effect Image,” is the philosophical center of the tonal analysis. Uexküll there reports a sequence of experiments on the hermit crab (Eupagurus) and the sea anemone (Sagartia) that, in his own retelling, do more philosophical work than any single passage in the Foray. The setup: a hermit crab in its snail-shell encounters a sea anemone. In its normal state, the crab grasps the anemone and places it on the shell, so that its stinging tentacles defend the soft abdomen against squid-attack. In the same crab, with its shell removed, the crab does not place the anemone on the shell—it tries instead to crawl into the anemone, using its body as a substitute shell. In a starving crab, the same anemone, the same sensory presentation, becomes prey: the crab eats it.

Uexküll’s interpretation: the Wahrnehmungsbild (“perception image”; literally “noticing-image”)—the integrated sensory representation of the anemone delivered by the crab’s perceptual apparatus—is identical in all three cases. What differs is the Stimmung (“mood,” “attunement”)—the internal disposition of the subject—and the Stimmung determines which of three possible Wirkbilder (“effect images”; “the representation of the act the subject performs upon an object”) is activated. The unaltered crab is in Schutzstimmung (“protective mood”); the de-shelled crab is in Wohnstimmung (“dwelling mood”); the starving crab is in Fressstimmung (“feeding mood”). The Wirkbild corresponding to the active Stimmung “completes and alters the perception image” (p. 97), and the resulting completed perception is toned: the anemone now has a Schutzton (“protection tone”), or a Wohnton (“dwelling tone”), or a Fresston (“feeding tone”). The same external object is constituted as three different objects by three different tones, and the difference is generated by the subject’s mood, not by the object’s properties.

3.2 The Doctrine of Wirkton: Pragmatic Disclosure of the Object

Uexküll generalizes this finding into a structural doctrine. The Wirkton is a perceptual quality that is as immediately given to the subject as shape, color, texture, or any other classical sensory quality. The chair is “sittable” with the same perceptual immediacy with which it is “wooden” or “brown.” The ladder is “climbable” with the same immediacy with which it is “tall” or “vertical.” The Wirkton is not a judgment added on top of a neutral perception; it is part of the perception itself, and indeed it is the part that organizes all the others into a single object. Uexküll’s example: “An African native, brought into a European house, sees neither chairs nor stairs nor ladders—only black bars and holes. Once shown how to climb the ladder, he sees it as climbable; the ladder has acquired its Klettertönung [‘climbing tone’]” (paraphrasing pp. 98–99). Before the Wirkton is acquired, there is no ladder—there are only bars and holes. With the Wirkton, there is a ladder.

The general formula Uexküll gives: “Every object that an animal encounters in its life is a thing of some functional kind—it has a sitting tone, a feeding tone, a flight tone, a swallowing tone, a climbing tone, etc. Wirktöne are not added on top of an objective perception; they are constitutive of the perception itself. The object is not a “what” with “uses”; the object is a “for-this-action,” and that “for-which” is the Wirkton.” Uexküll lists, in passing through the chapter, an open-ended catalog: Sitzton (“sitting tone”), Schutzton (“protection tone”), Wohnton (“dwelling tone”), Fresston (“feeding tone”), Hindernis-Ton (“obstacle tone”), Klettertönung (“climbing tone”), Tragtönung (“carrying tone”), Wegton (“path tone”), Wurfton (“throwing tone”), Schlagton (“striking tone”; the chair-as-weapon case), Nutzton (“use tone”), Gefahrton (“danger tone”), and others. The catalog is intentionally open: there are as many Wirktöne as there are functional cycles in which an object may figure for some subject.

3.3 The Reciprocal Specification of Umweltsicherheit (“environmental security”)

Uexküll draws from this analysis a counterintuitive corollary that is worth registering for its bearing on the philosophical treatment of Umwelt-richness. He observes that “the number of objects in a subject’s Umwelt equals the number of actions available to it” (p. 100), since each object is constituted by a Wirkton, and each Wirkton corresponds to an action-possibility. From this it follows that the impoverishment of an Umwelt is identical with the simplification of its tonal palette. The paramecium, with effectively one action (avoid obstacles), inhabits a world in which every object bears the single Hindernis-Ton (“obstacle tone”). The fly, with a slightly larger repertoire, sees most things under a Lauftönung (“running tone”)—the surface as that across which it can run—and only a few things differently. The dog has a richer palette (Sitzton, Fresston, Hindernis-Ton, etc.) but still less rich than the human’s. The human room contains the same physical objects as the fly room and the dog room, but the human room is richer because its tonal palette is richer; the fly’s room is the same room otherwise tonal.

This yields a thesis Uexküll calls the principle of Umweltsicherheit (“environmental security”): the simpler the Umwelt, the more secure the orientation. A paramecium cannot be uncertain whether to flee or to feed—every object bears the obstacle tone, and the response is unambiguous. A human, by contrast, lives in an Umwelt whose tonal palette is so rich that the same object may bear several Wirktöne simultaneously (the chair as Sitzton and Schlagton both)—and the activated tone depends on Stimmung, on context, on the cultivation of the subject. Sicherheit trades off against Reichtum (“richness”). The tick’s existence is not “lower” than the human’s; it is one in which the tonal field has been reduced to absolute certainty.

3.4 The Dependence on Stimmung: Mood as the Organizer of Tones

The structural finding from the hermit-crab analysis is that the Wirkton depends on the Stimmung of the subject. Stimmung is the internal state—Uexküll uses the word with full force, in the same idiomatic register that Heidegger will later activate—that determines which of multiple possible Wirkbilder is activated for a given Wahrnehmungsbild. The same object can carry multiple effect images (the chair as seat, as weapon, as obstacle, as decorative furniture); the subject’s Stimmung picks one. “The Stimmung is the internal disposition that decides which effect image dominates and accordingly which tone the perception bears” (paraphrasing pp. 97, 99).

The structural homology with what Heidegger will name Befindlichkeit in Sein und Zeit §29 is, on the textual evidence, not coincidental. Stimmung names, in Uexküll’s biology, what Befindlichkeit will name in Heidegger’s existential analytic: the prior, constitutive disposition that determines how things show up as significant before any explicit judgment, perception, or volition. Heidegger reads Stimmung as the ontic manifestation of Befindlichkeit; Uexküll, ten years earlier, reads Stimmung as the dispositional ground that selects which Wirkbild will operate on the Wahrnehmungsbild and so which Wirkton the object will bear. The two analyses are formally identical, with one decisive difference: Heidegger insists on the worldhood of Stimmung (mood discloses the world as a whole), where Uexküll, working biologically, treats Stimmung as the internal state of an organic Subjekt. The relation between them is one of structural analogy with a residual ontological difference, and it is to this difference, finally, that the question of what kind of philosophy of life Uexküll’s biology offers must be addressed.

4. Suchbild and Suchton: Function before Form (Foray Chapter 13)

4.1 The Search Image: Internal Templates that Override Perception

Chapter 13 returns to the tonal architecture from a different angle: the question of how the subject finds the objects whose Wirkton it would activate. Uexküll opens with two anecdotes whose force is phenomenological, not merely illustrative. First: a guest at his table, accustomed to a clay water-pitcher (Wassertonkrug), is presented with a glass carafe in the same place. The guest does not see the carafe—he asks for water and is told it is on the table; he searches the table; he does not see the carafe; only when his attention is drawn directly to it does the carafe become visible. The carafe was not occluded; it was invisible. The Suchbild (“search image”) of “water pitcher” was geared to the clay pitcher’s shape and color, and the carafe—departing from this Suchbild—failed to register. Second: a saleswoman cannot perceive a 100-mark banknote that is propped on edge in the till instead of lying flat; her Suchbild of “banknote” requires the bill to lie horizontally, and the upright bill is invisible to her even as her eyes pass directly over it. “The Suchbild extinguishes the Wahrnehmungsbild” (paraphrasing pp. 117–118).

The structural lesson is that perception, in any subject capable of search, is not stimulus-driven from below but template-driven from above. The Suchbild is an internally generated optical perception image that the subject projects outward into the Umwelt; it operates before any external stimulus and organizes what counts as a perceivable object in the search. The dog hunting for its master’s stick has a determinate Suchbild (this stick, of approximately this size and color); the toad, hungry, has a Suchbild of “worm-shape” or “spider-shape,” depending on which it has just eaten and which it now expects.

4.2 The Search Tone: The Function-Tonal Mode

Uexküll then introduces the structural complement to Suchbild: Suchton (“search tone”). The distinction is decisive for the entire tonal architecture. A Suchbild searches for a form; a Suchton searches for a function. The dog with a determinate stick-image has a Suchbild; the human looking for “any seat” in a crowded waiting room has a Suchton—the perception is geared not to this chair but to whatever can be sat on. The toad, when it has just eaten, returns to a Suchbild (worm-form, spider-form). The hungry toad, before its first meal of the day, operates with a Suchton—an undifferentiated Fressuchton (“feeding-search-tone”) whose object is “anything edible.” Only after its first prey is consumed does the Suchton sharpen into a determinate Suchbild of that particular prey-form.

Uexküll’s clearest statement of the priority of Suchton over Suchbild: “The Suchton is more fundamental than the Suchbild; it is the function-orientation that makes form-search possible. The animal’s first orientation in the world is Suchton; the Suchbild is a Suchton that has been crystallized around a recurrent particular form” (paraphrasing p. 121, with the hermit crab and toad cases). Function precedes form—a thesis that, at the biological level, accomplishes structurally what Heidegger will accomplish at the existential level when, in Sein und Zeit §15, he insists that the hammer is encountered first under the Um-zu (“in-order-to”) of its readiness-to-hand, only secondarily as a present-at-hand thing with properties. The Suchton is the biological analogue of the Um-zu.

4.3 The Hermit Crab Revisited as Tonal Sequence

Uexküll uses Chapter 13 to recast the hermit-crab analysis of Chapter 9 in the language of Suchton. The hermit crab, in its three moods, is operating not with three Suchbilder but with three Suchtöne: Schutzsuchton (“protection-search-tone”), Wohnsuchton (“dwelling-search-tone”), Fresssuchton (“feeding-search-tone”). What it searches for is not “a sea anemone” but “anything that affords protection,” “anything that affords shelter,” “anything that affords nourishment.” The sea anemone, in each case, fits the active Suchton—and the resulting completed perception is the tonal anemone (anemone-as-protection, anemone-as-shelter, anemone-as-food). Each of these three completed perceptions is what Uexküll, in Chapter 9, called the anemone-with-its-Wirkton; what Chapter 13 adds is that the Wirkton is selected by an antecedent Suchton, which is itself selected by the Stimmung.

The tonal architecture has now acquired a layered specificity. The Stimmung (mood, attunement) is the most general dispositional ground. Within the Stimmung, a Suchton (function-search-tone) is activated. The Suchton organizes the perceptual field so that a class of objects becomes salient. When such an object is encountered, the Suchton crystallizes around its form into a Suchbild (form-search-image), and the Suchbild + Wahrnehmungsbild + Wirkbild combine to give the object its full Wirkton in the activated Funktionskreis.

4.4 Leistungston: The Function-Tone Itself

A further refinement that Uexküll introduces in Chapter 13 is the term Leistungston (“function tone,” “performance tone”). The German Leistung covers both “function” (what an object does) and “performance” (what it is achieved as doing); Uexküll uses the term to name the meaning-coloring an object has qua functioning thing, independent of its specific particular form. The chair has a Sitzton, but this is a Leistungston—any object that affords sitting bears it. The bowl has a Trinkton (“drinking tone”)—any object that affords drinking bears it. The Leistungston abstracts from the particular form to the function the form performs; it is the function-tone itself, whereas the Wirkton of a particular object is the function-tone as embodied in this form. The progression Stimmung → Suchton → Leistungston → Wirkton tracks the descent from the most general dispositional ground to the most particular tonal coloring of a specific object encountered.

5. Magische Umwelten: Free Productions of the Subject (Foray Chapter 14)

5.1 The Definition

Chapter 14, “Magical Environments,” is the most philosophically explosive chapter of the Foray. Uexküll defines a magische Umwelt (“magical environment”) as one that “contains very effective phenomena visible only to the subject, not bound to repeated experience” (p. 119); these are “free productions of the subject” (freie Erzeugung des Subjekts), perceptual phenomena that arise without any external stimulus correlate. The chapter contains four cases that bear directly on the tonal analysis.

First: the child playing Hansel and Gretel sees the witch “in the flesh.” Uexküll cites Frobenius’s Paideuma: a girl, playing the game, becomes terrified of an actual witch standing where no witch is. The Wirkton of “witchness” has been generated by the child’s Stimmung—the play-mood—and projected into the Umwelt without any external trigger.

Second: the dog perceives its master’s connection to its experiences “magically, not divided into cause and effect” (p. 121). The dog’s Umwelt is laden with Tönungen whose objective correlates are not isolable as causes; the master is grasped as a tonal whole, and the dog’s behavior follows from the tone, not from any chain of stimulus-and-response.

Third: the starling and the imaginary fly. Uexküll reports an experiment in which a starling, raised in captivity and never exposed to flies, performs the complete fly-catching behavioral sequence on an empty patch of air. “The environment is laden with feeding tone, which forces the perception image without sensory stimulus” (paraphrasing pp. 121–122). The starling has not learned to catch flies; the Fresston of its Umwelt generates a fly-shaped Wahrnehmungsbild without any external fly being present. The starling lives in an Umwelt in which fly-shaped objects can be perceived even when no fly-shaped object is there.

Fourth: the pea weevil larva, which bores an exit channel through still-tender pea flesh for the future use of the adult that has not yet emerged. The Wirkmal (the channel) is constituted by a future Wirkton that the larva itself does not yet experience; the Umwelt of the larva is laden, magically, with a Tönung that is not its own present mood but a Bauplan-tone of the species.

5.2 The Implication for the Tonal Doctrine

The structural significance of these cases is that the tonal architecture is not exhausted by stimulus-driven perception. Töne can be generated by the subject’s Stimmung, Suchton, or Bauplan (“body-plan”) in ways that produce Wahrnehmungsbilder without external correlate. The Umwelt is therefore not merely a receptive screen on which Töne are projected from external objects; it is a genuinely generative field in which the subject’s tonal apparatus produces perceptual phenomena. The magische Umwelt is the limit case of this generativity, where the tonal field operates without any stimulus correlate at all.

This has decisive consequences for the question of how Töne relate to the world. If Töne were merely passive registrations of object-properties, the magische Umwelt would be impossible. The Töne must be, in their primary function, active dispositional structures of the subject—the same structures that organize stimulus-driven perception—operating, in the magical case, without a stimulus to organize. The biological-philosophical conclusion is that Töne are constitutive of the Umwelt from the side of the Subjekt; they are what the subject contributes to the constitution of its world. The Umwelt is not the world as it is tinted by tones; the Umwelt is the world as it is generated by the subject’s tonal apparatus, with stimulus correlates supplying only the occasions on which the apparatus operates.

6. The Oak Paradigm: The Tonal Constitution of the Umwelt (Foray Chapter 15)

6.1 The Oak in Ten Environments

Chapter 15, “The Same Subject as Object in Different Environments,” gives the masterstroke of the Foray’s perspectivism. Uexküll takes a single oak tree and traces its appearance across ten Umwelten: forester, little girl, Russian seasonal workers, fox, owl, squirrel, songbird, ant, bark beetle, and ichneumon wasp. Each Umwelt registers a different Tönung of the oak; in each, a different aspect of the oak is cut out by the active tonal articulation, and the rest is unregistered—not unseen, not occluded, but not part of the Umwelt at all. The oak in each Umwelt is, accordingly, a different object: the same physical tree, but ten different Bedeutungsträger (“carriers of meaning”).

The catalog (with the Tönung terms Uexküll uses where given, and structural reconstructions where not):

  • Forester: the oak appears under the Nutzton (“use tone”). The trunk is registered as cords of wood; the rest of the tree is, for the forester, not relevant—not perceived under any Wirkton, hence not in the Umwelt in any robust sense. The forester’s Wahrnehmungsbild of the oak is “wood-mass-to-be-cut.”
  • Little girl: the oak appears under a Gefahrton (“danger tone”) within a magische Umwelt. The bark’s gnarls and burls cohere into the face of a demon; the Suchton is “look-out-for-danger,” and the oak supplies the form. The girl’s Wahrnehmungsbild is “demon-with-wicked-face.”
  • Russian seasonal workers: a fungus-growth on the oak appears under a magischer Ton (“magical tone”)—a religious tone within a peasant cosmology. The fungus is registered as a miraculous icon of the Virgin. The Wahrnehmungsbild is “Madonna in the wood.”
  • Fox: the root cavity appears under a Schutzton (“protection tone”); the oak’s root system is registered as a protective roof under which the fox dens. The rest of the tree is not registered.
  • Owl: the upper branches appear under a Schutzton; the oak’s high crown is a protective wall against ground predators. The Wahrnehmungsbild is “shelter from beneath.”
  • Squirrel: the trunk and branches appear under a Klettertönung (“climbing tone”); the oak is registered as a vertical ladder of footholds. The Wahrnehmungsbild is “climbable structure.”
  • Songbirds: the twigs appear under a Tragtönung (“carrying tone”); the oak’s twig-network supplies nest-supports. The Wahrnehmungsbild is “supports for the nest.”
  • Ants: the bark surface appears under a hunting Suchton; the bark’s grooves are registered as paths along which prey may be encountered.
  • Bark beetle: beneath-the-bark appears under both Fresston (“feeding tone”) and Wohnton (“dwelling tone”); the inner bark is food and the carved galleries are nursery.
  • Ichneumon wasp: the wood appears under a penetration tone; the oak’s wood is registered as “soft-as-butter,” yielding to the wasp’s ovipositor.

6.2 The Umwelt as Tonal Cut

Uexküll’s interpretive conclusion: each Umwelt “cuts out” of the oak just those features that can serve as Merkmalträger and Wirkmalträger for the active Funktionskreis of the inhabiting subject; the rest of the oak is not in the Umwelt—it is invisible, inaudible, intangible, because the subject’s tonal apparatus has no Funktionskreis in which it could figure. “The oak-in-itself, considered apart from any Umwelt, is solidly put together—but it is never known by all the subjects, and never knowable for them” (paraphrasing p. 136). Summing all ten perspectival cuts does not yield the oak-in-itself; it yields only “chaos,” because the Umwelten are incommensurable—each is its own complete world, and the cuts cannot be reassembled into a single object.

The relation between Umwelt and Ton is now visible in its full architecture. The Umwelt is constituted as the tonal coloration of objects by the subject’s active functional cycles. Where there is no Wirkton for an object, there is no object in the Umwelt. The sum of the active Wirktöne (and the Suchtöne and Stimmungen that select them) is the Umwelt. The Umwelt is not a container that holds tones; the Umwelt is the field that the tones constitute. Without tones there is no Umwelt; with tones, the Umwelt is just the field of tonal articulations of objects.

6.3 Subjektive Wirklichkeit and the Limits of Objectivism

Uexküll’s foundational principle, stated in Chapter 15: “Each and every subject lives in a world in which there are only subjective realities [subjektive Wirklichkeiten]” (p. 130). The Umwelt contains only subjektive Wirklichkeit; the objective surroundings (Umgebung) never appear as such within any Umwelt. The principle is Kantian, but it is given biological articulation: the Subjekt of the Umwelt is not a transcendental ego but a living organism with a particular body, a particular tonal apparatus, a particular set of Funktionskreise. The “subjective” of subjektive Wirklichkeit is the Subjekt-Umwelt closure, not an inner mental field.

The principle has consequences for how the relation between Tönen and Umwelt is to be understood. Töne are not objective properties of objects that the subject more-or-less faithfully registers; they are the modalities in which the Subjekt-Umwelt closure articulates itself. The oak’s Klettertönung is not an objective property of the oak that the squirrel happens to register; it is the way the oak figures for the squirrel within the squirrel’s Umwelt. In a different sense, the same Klettertönung is also not “merely subjective”—it is not an illusion or a projection onto a really-non-climbable oak. The oak is climbable, in the only sense in which “climbable” has biological meaning: it figures in the Funktionskreis of climbing for a being whose Bauplan makes climbing possible. The Klettertönung is the structural fit between the squirrel’s body and the oak’s surface, articulated in the squirrel’s Umwelt.

7. Bedeutungsträger and the Theory of Meaning (Bedeutungslehre Chapter 1)

7.1 The Stone, the Bowl, the Ladder

The 1940 Bedeutungslehre extends the tonal analysis of the Foray into a full theory of meaning. The opening chapter—“Carriers of Meaning” (“Bedeutungsträger”)—gives the most precise formulation of how Töne and Bedeutung relate. The chapter opens with a stone. Considered in itself, apart from any subject-relation, the stone is a “relationless object” (beziehungsloses Objekt)—a mere physical thing without rank or salience among its properties. Picked up by a man walking on a path, the stone becomes a Wegstein (“path-stone”): its Wegton (“path tone”) emerges. Hurled by the same man at a barking dog, the same stone, with no change in its physical properties, becomes a Wurfstein (“throw-stone”): its Wurfton (“throwing tone”) emerges. The stone’s properties have not changed; what has changed is the Funktionskreis in which the stone has been entered. The same stone is two different Bedeutungsträger under two different Wirktöne.

Uexküll generalizes the case across his examples. The same glass bowl is a window when its transparency leads (the Durchsichtigkeitston, “transparency tone”), a vase when its curvature leads (the Behältnisston, “container tone”). The same upright pole-and-rung structure is a ladder under the Klettertönung of climbing, a fence under a Hindernis-Ton of barring entry. In each case, the structural finding is that the rank of properties (essential vs. accidental) is generated by the Wirkton, not by the object’s intrinsic properties. The Scholastic distinction between essentia (“essence”) and accidentia (“accidents”) presupposes a Bedeutungsträger—a meaning-carrier—and ranks properties according to which is leading and which is supporting for the meaning. There are no objective essentia; essentia is a tonal category.

7.2 Bedeutungsverbraucher: The Subject as Consumer of Meaning

The structural complement to Bedeutungsträger is Bedeutungsverbraucher (“consumer of meaning”): the Subjekt’s body, considered as the structural counterpart that “consumes” the meaning the carrier provides. The Komplement (“complement”) of the meaning is the part of the body—the hand for the path-stone, the arm for the thrown stone, the leg for the climbed ladder, the seat-bones for the chair—that fits the carrier’s Wirkmalträger. The Subjekt and the Bedeutungsträger are jointly composed for each other: the path-stone fits the foot, and the foot fits the path-stone, because the Funktionskreis of walking has shaped both into mutual fitness. The fly is fly-shaped because the spider’s web is fly-shaped, and the spider’s web is fly-shaped because the fly’s Bauplan shapes its meaning. Bedeutungsträger and Bedeutungsverbraucher are not joined by accident but by Komposition (“composition”).

The Wirkton now finds its full biosemiotic specification: it is the meaning-function that the Bedeutungsträger bears for the Bedeutungsverbraucher under the active Funktionskreis. Every Wirkton presupposes the structural fit between carrier and consumer; every Funktionskreis enacts a specific Wirkton by closing the loop between the perception-organ and the effect-organ on the side of the Subjekt, the perception-mark-carrier and the effect-mark-carrier on the side of the object. The Umwelt is the totality of all such Funktionskreise in their tonal coloration of objects.

8. Eigenton, Bildungsmelodie, and the Kompositionslehre (Bedeutungslehre Chapters 7, 12)

8.1 Eigenton: The Self-Tone of the Living Cell

The 1940 Bedeutungslehre extends the tonal analysis from the Funktionskreis level down to the cellular level, where Töne acquire their most basic and most surprising articulation. Each living cell, on Uexküll’s account, has its own Eigenton (“self-tone”)—a specific energetic response to the stimulation it receives. The cell, in this sense, is a “bell” in the lebendiges Glockenspiel (“living carillon”) of the organism: stimulated by an external factor, it sounds its Eigenton; the ensemble of Eigentöne across the cells of an organ produces an Organmelodie (“organ-melody”); the ensemble of Organmelodien across the organs of an organism produces an Organismusymphonie (“organism-symphony”) or, in Uexküll’s mature terminology, an Urbild (“primal image”) of the organism.

The structural claim: the organism is not a machine of parts assembled by external forces but a composition whose parts are tones (Eigentöne) joined in melodies (Organmelodien) which join in a symphony (Urbild). The composition is governed not by mechanical causation but by a Bedeutungsregel (“meaning rule”) that selects which environmental factors will become Bedeutungsträger for which cells and how the resulting tonal field will be organized. The Bedeutungsregel is the structural successor of the Wirkton at the cellular level: it is the rule that decides which stimuli are for which cells, just as the Wirkton decides which objects are for which actions.

8.2 Bildungsmelodie: The Formative Melody and Morphogenesis

A second level of tonal analysis applies to the developing organism. Uexküll proposes that morphogenesis—the formation of the organism from the germ cell—is governed by a Bildungsmelodie (“formative melody”) that crystallizes out of the Bedeutungskeim (“meaning-germ”). The Bedeutungskeim is the originary meaning-nucleus from which the organism develops; the Bildungsmelodie is the unfolding of this meaning into the organic form. The acorn is the Bedeutungskeim of the oak; the egg is the Bedeutungskeim of the chicken. The genes are the Klaviatur (“clavier,” “keyboard”) on which the Bildungsmelodie plays; the protoplasm is the material the melody shapes.

The structural lesson is that organic form is itself a tonal phenomenon. The oak’s particular shape is the Bildungsmelodie of its Bedeutungskeim; the squirrel’s particular shape is the Bildungsmelodie of its Bedeutungskeim; and the structural fit between oak and squirrel that grounds the Klettertönung is itself the result of two Bildungsmelodien that have been composed contrapuntally by Nature. The squirrel’s body is shaped to climb the oak; the oak’s bark is shaped to be climbed by the squirrel; both shapes are tonal articulations of a higher-order Komposition.

8.3 The Kompositionslehre and the Partitur

Uexküll’s culminating thesis—stated with full hyperbole in Chapter 12 of the Bedeutungslehre—is the Kompositionslehre der Natur (“composition theory of nature”): Nature is the composer of all Umwelten, organisms are the instruments, Wirktöne are the notes, and the whole is a Partitur (“score”) that, in the strongest formulation, is “beyond space and time” (Bedeutungslehre Chapter 12). The Kompositionslehre is Uexküll’s response to the question of how the multiple Umwelten relate to each other and to Nature as a whole. The answer: they are contrapuntal parts of a single composition. The bee is composed for the flower; the flower is composed for the bee; the spider is composed for the fly; the fly is composed for the spider. Each Funktionskreis is a duet; the totality of all Funktionskreise is a symphony; the Partitur of the symphony is “Nature.”

The doctrine is, in its 1940 articulation, on the borderline of biology and metaphysics, and Uexküll is aware of the borderline. He insists that “Nature teaches no Lehre [‘lesson,’ ‘teaching’]”—the Kompositionslehre is generalized from observation, not deduced from principle. But the musical metaphor does substantial theoretical work: it makes intelligible how organisms can be jointly composed without a designer, how Umwelten can be incommensurable yet harmonious, how Töne can be both subject-side (the Subjekt’s tonal apparatus) and object-side (the Bedeutungsträger’s tonal coloration) at once. The Komposition is what holds the Subjekt-Umwelt closure together with the Subjekt-Subjekt attunement, all under the heading of a tonal architecture whose primary unit is the Wirkton and whose totality is the Partitur.

9. The Crossover with Aristotelian Hedonic Tonality and Heideggerian Stimmung

9.1 The Three Tonal Doctrines

The dissertational interest of Uexküll’s tonal analysis lies in its triangulation with two other tonal doctrines: Aristotle’s account of the hēdonē-lupē (“pleasure-pain”) tonality co-given with every act of perception, thought, and emotion (De Anima II.2, 413b 22 sqq.; Nicomachean Ethics X.1–5; Rhetoric II.1, 1378a 19–22), and Heidegger’s account of Stimmung as the ontic manifestation of Befindlichkeit (Sein und Zeit §29; Grundbegriffe der Metaphysik §§16–17). All three doctrines articulate, in different idioms, the same structural insight: that there is a basic affective-tonal coloration that accompanies every act of perception, cognition, and action, and that this tonal coloration is constitutive, not added on top.

For Aristotle, the basic tonality is hēdonē-lupē—pleasure and pain—and it is co-given (hepetai) with every perceiving, thinking, considering, and emoting (NE X.4, 1175a 10 sqq.). It is not added to perception as a separate quale; it is the toning-up or toning-down of the very actuality of perception. The Rhetoric’s analysis of the higher-order emotions makes the same structural point at the level of the pathē: every emotion is “co-given with pain or pleasure” (Rhet. II.1, 1378a 21–22), and the pleasure-pain tonality is what makes the emotion be a specific way of being-disposed.

For Heidegger, the basic tonality is Stimmung, and it is the ontic manifestation of Befindlichkeit—the existential structure of finding-oneself-disposed that is co-original with understanding (Verstehen) and discourse (Rede) in the constitution of being-in-the-world. Stimmung is not a feeling added to a neutral perception; it is the prior dispositional ground that determines how things show up as significant—as threatening, as inviting, as boring, as mattering—before any explicit judgment, perception, or volition. Befindlichkeit discloses the world as a whole, in a tonality that pervades every encounter with every entity.

For Uexküll, the basic tonality is Wirkton (and its associated Suchton, Stimmung, Eigenton, Bildungsmelodie), and it is the constitutive tonality of every object in the Umwelt—the tonal articulation through which an object becomes what it is for the subject in the active Funktionskreis. The Wirkton is not an added layer; it is the structural fact that what the subject perceives is always perceived under a function, and the function colors the object before the object is fully constituted as an object.

9.2 The Structural Homology

The three doctrines are formally homologous in the following respects:

(1) Co-givenness, not addition. All three tonalities are co-given with the act they tone, not added on top of an antecedent neutral act. Aristotle’s hēdonē “follows” perception in the sense of hepetai—it is “with”-given, not consequent (Heidegger’s gloss in GA 18, p. 115). Heidegger’s Stimmung is co-original (gleichursprünglich) with Verstehen and Rede in the constitution of Erschlossenheit (“disclosedness”). Uexküll’s Wirkton is part of the Wahrnehmungsbild itself, not added by a subsequent judgment.

(2) Constitutive of the object as object. All three tonalities are constitutive of the object’s appearance as the object it is. Aristotle’s hēdonic-tonal articulation of the pathē makes anger be anger and fear be fear—the tonality is not something the pathos has but something the pathos is. Heidegger’s Stimmung-articulated entities show up as threatening or inviting; without the Stimmung, they do not show up as anything in particular. Uexküll’s Wirkton-articulated objects are the only objects in the Umwelt; without the Wirkton, the object is not in the Umwelt at all.

(3) Subject-relative without being subjective in the bad sense. All three tonalities are Subjekt-relative—they belong to the way a particular kind of subject relates to a particular kind of object—but none is “merely subjective” in the sense of an illusion or projection. Aristotle’s hēdonē-lupē are real determinations of the psychē’s being-in-its-world. Heidegger’s Stimmung discloses something about the world (the world is the way Stimmung discloses it, for Dasein in this Stimmung). Uexküll’s Wirkton is the real structural fit between subject and object in the Funktionskreis.

(4) The basic dispositional axis of hairesis and phugē. All three doctrines locate the basic tonal-dispositional axis in the seizing and fleeing of the subject toward and away from the toned object. Aristotle: pursuing the pleasant, fleeing the painful (NE II.3, 1104b 30; EE II.10, 1226b 10 sqq.; the Heideggerian gloss at GA 18, p. 166: “hairesis and phugē are the basic motivations of being-there”). Heidegger: the Stimmung of fear is a recoiling-from, the Stimmung of trust an opening-toward. Uexküll: the Wirkton of the obstacle is avoid; the Wirkton of the food is seize; the Wirkton of the predator is flee; the Wirkton of the mate is approach. The hairesis-phugē axis is the basic dispositional axis of all three tonal doctrines.

9.3 The Decisive Difference: World vs. Umwelt

The structural homology is real; but the doctrines diverge on a single decisive point that cannot be smoothed over. For Heidegger, Stimmung discloses world (Welt)—understood as the meaning-totality within which Dasein finds itself. For Uexküll, Wirkton constitutes Umwelt—understood as the closed perceptual-effective field of a particular biological subject. Heidegger’s Welt is not summable across Daseins (and not just because Daseins are particular); Uexküll’s Umwelten are at least in principle catalogable across species (the oak appears in the squirrel-Umwelt, the bark-beetle-Umwelt, etc., and the catalog is finite). Heidegger’s Welt has, in Sein und Zeit, a holistic-existential structure that cannot be biologically articulated; Uexküll’s Umwelt is biologically articulable in the architecture of Funktionskreise, Bedeutungsträger, Bauplan, Bildungsmelodie.

This difference matters for the question of what kind of philosophy of life Uexküll’s tonal doctrine is. It is not phenomenology in Heidegger’s sense, because the Subjekt whose Umwelt is articulated is not Dasein but a biological organism whose Bauplan is itself a structural condition for the kinds of Wirktöne its Funktionskreise can register. It is not naive empiricism, because the Wirkton is not stimulus-driven but constitutive—the object is for-an-action before it is registered as an object. It is something else: a biosemiotics that sits between phenomenology and biology, articulating, in the language of Töne and Funktionskreise, what phenomenology articulates in the language of Stimmung and Befindlichkeit, and what Aristotelian psyche-analysis articulates in the language of hēdonē-lupē co-given with aisthēsis. The three vocabularies do not translate without remainder; but they are formally homologous in their tonal structure, and the homology is, on the textual evidence, not coincidental but reflective of a shared philosophical-biological problematic in the German-speaking inter-war period.

9.4 The Specific Gain for Aristotelian Hedonic Tonality

The bearing on the dissertational thesis—that Aristotle posits a basic hedonic tonality (good/bad, pleasure/pain, pursue/avoid) accompanying every moment of perception, thinking, considering, and emoting, and that this tonality is also a constitutive moment of the higher-order emotions of the Rhetoric—is this. Uexküll’s biology of Töne gives the hedonic tonality a specific structural articulation that is unavailable in the Aristotelian text alone. Where Aristotle’s hēdonē-lupē is asserted as co-given with aisthēsis but not given a structural specification of how it is co-given, Uexküll’s Wirkton gives the structural specification: the hedonic-tonal coloration is generated by the Funktionskreis in which the perceiver is engaged with the perceived; the active Funktionskreis is selected by the Stimmung of the subject; and the resulting Wirkton is the way the object figures for the active functional cycle. The hedonic tonality of Aristotelian perception is, in Uexküllian terms, the Wirkton that the active Stimmung and Suchton have selected for the Funktionskreis of the perceiving organism; and the hedonic tonality of the higher-order pathē of the Rhetoric is the Wirkton that the active social-evaluative Funktionskreis selects for the object of the pathos (the slighter-as-target-of-anger, the fear-inducer-as-threat, the pity-inducer-as-undeservedly-suffering).

The convergence is not a translation but an articulation. Aristotle says what the tonality does (it pursues, it flees, it makes pleasant or painful); Uexküll says how the tonality is generated (by the Stimmung-selected Funktionskreis operating through Suchton on the Wahrnehmungsbild); Heidegger says what kind of being requires such a tonality (Dasein, whose being is at issue in its world). The three doctrines, taken together, give a more complete account of the hedonic-tonal constitution of perception and emotion than any one of them gives alone. The dissertation that uses the term hedonic tonality to name the basic affective valence accompanying every act of aisthēsis and every articulation of pathē is on Aristotelian textual ground; on Uexküllian biosemiotic ground; on Heideggerian existential-analytic ground; and the convergence of the three is what gives the term its full philosophical weight.

10. Synthesis: Five Theses

Drawing the threads of the analysis together, Uexküll’s reading of the tonal architecture of the Umwelt in the Foray and the Bedeutungslehre issues in five master theses, each of which redirects the philosophical analysis of the living being away from the post-Cartesian, post-Darwinian biological framework.

Thesis I: The Umwelt is the closed self-world of a Subjekt, not a section of an objective world. The Umwelt (“surrounding-world,” “phenomenal world,” “self-world,” “environment”) is composed of Merkwelt (“perception world”) and Wirkwelt (“effect world”) joined in a closed Funktionskreis (“functional cycle”). Each Subjekt has its Umwelt; no Umwelt is a part of any other; the Umwelt is not a portion of the Umgebung (“surroundings”) seen from a particular angle but a self-enclosed totality whose contents exist only within it.

Thesis II: The Umwelt is constituted by Töne (tones), and the Töne are dispositional articulations from the side of the Subjekt. The Wirkton (“effect tone”) is the meaning-coloration that an object bears under the active Funktionskreis; the Suchton (“search tone”) is the function-orientation that selects which class of objects can be encountered; the Stimmung (“mood,” “attunement”) is the dispositional ground that selects which Suchton is active. The tonal architecture descends from Stimmung through Suchton and Leistungston (“function tone”) to the Wirkton of the particular object. Without tones there is no Umwelt; with tones, the Umwelt is the field of tonal articulations.

Thesis III: The Wirkton is the structural fit between Bedeutungsträger (“carrier of meaning”) and Bedeutungsverbraucher (“consumer of meaning”) in the active Funktionskreis. The same physical object is constituted as different Bedeutungsträger by different Wirktöne: the stone is path-stone (Wegstein) under Wegton and throw-stone (Wurfstein) under Wurfton; the oak is cordwood for the forester, demon for the child, protective roof for the fox, climbable structure for the squirrel, and food-and-nursery for the bark beetle. The rank of properties (essentia vs. accidentia) is generated by the Wirkton, not by the object’s intrinsic structure.

Thesis IV: Töne are not exhausted by stimulus-driven perception; they include freie Erzeugung des Subjekts (“free productions of the subject”) in magische Umwelten (“magical environments”). The starling catches the imaginary fly because its Umwelt is laden with Fresston; the child sees the witch because the play-Stimmung generates a witchness-Wirkton; the pea-weevil-larva bores the future-exit channel because its Umwelt is laden with a Bauplan-tone of the species. The tonal apparatus generates perceptual phenomena as well as registering them; the Umwelt is a generative field.

Thesis V: The tonal architecture extends from the Eigenton of the cell through the Bildungsmelodie of the organism to the Kompositionslehre of Nature, and the totality is a Partitur (“score”). Each cell has its self-tone; each organ has its Organmelodie (“organ-melody”); each organism has its Urbild (“primal image”) as the symphony of its organs; the totality of all Umwelten, joined contrapuntally, is the Partitur of Nature. The Komposition is not designed by an external composer but is the structural fit of Bedeutungsträger and Bedeutungsverbraucher across all Funktionskreise. The bee is composed for the flower, the flower for the bee, the spider for the fly, the fly for the spider; and the Wirkton is the audible articulation of this composition at the level of any particular Subjekt-Umwelt closure.

These five theses, taken together, constitute Uexküll’s tonal-biosemiotic account of the Umwelt. The Umwelt is closed (Thesis I); it is constituted by tones (Thesis II); the tones are the structural fit between Subjekt and Bedeutungsträger (Thesis III); the tonal apparatus is generative as well as receptive (Thesis IV); the tonal architecture extends from the cell to Nature (Thesis V). The tonal architecture is what allows Uexküll’s biology to articulate, in the language of Töne and Funktionskreise, what phenomenology articulates in the language of Stimmung and Befindlichkeit, and what Aristotelian psychology articulates in the language of hēdonē-lupē co-given with aisthēsis. The convergence is, on the textual evidence, not coincidental: Heidegger explicitly cites Uexküll in Sein und Zeit §§14–18, and the formal homology between Uexküllian Stimmung and Heideggerian Befindlichkeit—as well as between Uexküllian Wirkton and Aristotelian hedonic tonality—is, on the present analysis, a real philosophical convergence that rewards detailed reconstruction.

11. The Bearing on the Dissertational Thesis

For the dissertation that takes the term hedonic tonality to name the basic affective valence (good/bad, pleasure/pain, pursue/avoid) accompanying every act of perception, thinking, considering, and emoting—and that takes this same hedonic tonality to be a constitutive moment of the higher-order pathē of Aristotle’s Rhetoric—Uexküll’s tonal analysis offers four specific gains.

First, a structural specification of what the hedonic tonality is. Where Aristotle asserts that hēdonē-lupē is co-given with aisthēsis but does not specify the structural mechanism, Uexküll’s Wirkton gives the mechanism: the hedonic tonality is the meaning-coloration the object bears for the active Funktionskreis of the perceiver, and this coloration is generated by the Stimmung-selected Suchton operating on the Wahrnehmungsbild.

Second, a structural specification of how the hedonic tonality is Subjekt-relative without being merely subjective. The Wirkton is the structural fit between Bedeutungsträger and Bedeutungsverbraucher; it is real, not illusory, but it is real only within the Funktionskreis. The hedonic tonality is, in the same way, real—it is a structural feature of the perceiver-perceived relation—but only within the active functional engagement of the perceiver.

Third, a structural specification of the layered architecture of the tonality. The descent from Stimmung through Suchton and Leistungston to Wirkton gives a precise architecture for the layered emergence of the hedonic-tonal coloration: the most general dispositional ground (Stimmung) selects a class of function-orientations (Suchton); the function-orientation selects a class of relevant objects (Leistungston); and the encounter with a particular object generates the specific Wirkton. The Aristotelian-Heideggerian reader of this dissertation can map Stimmung onto Heideggerian Befindlichkeit and Aristotelian diathesis, Suchton onto Heideggerian Vorhabe (“fore-having”) and Aristotelian hexis, Leistungston onto Aristotelian dynamis, and Wirkton onto the specific hedonic-tonal coloration of the perceived object in the active pathos.

Fourth, a specification of the constitutive role of tonality in the higher-order emotions. The Wirktöne of Schutzton, Fresston, Schlagton, Gefahrton, Liebestönung (“love tone”), Feindeston (“enemy tone”) that Uexküll catalogs at the biological level have direct counterparts in the higher-order pathē of Rhetoric II: Schutzton is the tonal moment of thrasos (“confidence,” “courage”) in defended being-with-others; Fresston, in its sublimated rhetorical articulation, is the epithumia (“desire”) of acquisitive pathē; Schlagton is the orexis antilypēseōs (“desire for retaliation”) of orgē (“anger”); Gefahrton is the λύπη τις ἢ ταραχή (“a kind of pain or disturbance”) of phobos (“fear”); Liebestönung is the philia of friendship; Feindeston is the echthra of enmity. The dissertation that takes the higher-order pathē of the Rhetoric to be tonal articulations of a basic hedonic-tonal architecture finds, in Uexküll’s biology, a structural analogue at the perceptual-evaluative level that supports the architectural claim across the perception-emotion gradient.

Uexküll’s tonal analysis, in its mature 1934-1940 articulation, accordingly stands at a triangulation point where Aristotelian psychology, Heideggerian phenomenology, and biological Umweltlehre (“environment-doctrine”) meet on a shared problematic: the constitution of the perceived-world by the tonal-dispositional architecture of the perceiving subject. The reading developed here works through Uexküll’s biological text rather than around it, drawing out the tonal architecture that the text itself articulates and tracing the structural homology with the Aristotelian and Heideggerian doctrines of tonality. The Foray and the Bedeutungslehre are accordingly best read as a sustained articulation of an analysis whose structural insight is shared with Aristotle and Heidegger, whose biological articulation is unique to Uexküll, and whose convergence with the existential-phenomenological and the Aristotelian psychological idioms is, on the textual evidence, not coincidental but reflective of a shared philosophical-biological problematic in the German-speaking inter-war period.
