# Aristotle, Motion, Time, and the Faculties of the Soul: Toward a Heideggerean Rereading

## 1. Articulate Aristotle's Understanding

To articulate Aristotle's understanding of being, motion, and the soul is to confront a network of concepts that resists reduction to any single philosophical register; indeed, the interrelation of *kinēsis*, *aisthēsis*, *phantasia*, and *logos* in Aristotle's thought forms a complex architecture wherein each term gains its meaning only in light of the others. As scholars working at the intersection of Heideggerian phenomenology and classical rhetoric have argued, Aristotle's account of passion and motion cannot be neatly separated from his account of judgment and discourse. Specifically, passion itself functions simultaneously as a motion and as a cause of further motions within the soul, constituting a capacity for alteration that directly impinges upon the domain of *logos*. As one commentator observes, "Passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Thus the passions are not mere psychological accompaniments to rational deliberation; they are ontologically significant movements that alter the very conditions under which judgment takes place. Heidegger's own reading of Aristotle's *pathē* treats them not  of knowledge but as *Befindlichkeiten*—dispositions or attunements through which Dasein finds itself already oriented in the world. This reinterpretation transforms our understanding of Aristotle's rhetoric, for it means that the rhetorical situation is not simply a context in which a speaker deploys arguments before a passive audience but a dynamic field of affective motions that shape the possibility of discourse itself. Similarly, Thomas Rickert's ambient rhetoric extends this trajectory by framing rhetorical engagement as a mode of dwelling that involves "active comportments toward the world, such as building, enacted less as the perpetuation of mastery and control than as a 'letting be' that spares, preserves, and cultivates human beings in the world" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 53–54). Hence, we must understand Aristotle's framework as one in which being, motion, and affective attunement are inseparable dimensions of the same ontological problematic—a problematic that Heidegger and subsequent rhetoricians have drawn upon to rethink the relationship between persuasion and existence. ## 2. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding

The task of articulating Aristotle's understanding is accordingly not merely exegetical but hermeneutical, for it requires us to attend to the ways in which his fundamental concepts have been received, transformed, and deepened by subsequent thinkers. The concept of *kinēsis*, for instance, should not be understood in merely mechanical terms as locomotion or physical displacement; instead, as Heidegger argues in his reading of Aristotle, *kinēsis* must be grasped as a fundamental way of being. Specifically, "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). This claim carries significant consequences for our understanding of both Aristotelian ontology and Aristotelian rhetoric, for it means that the movements of the soul—desire, aversion, perception, imagination—are not secondary phenomena supervening on a more basic static substance but constitute the very being of the living creature in its situatedness. What is at stake, then, is nothing less than the ontological status of human engagement with the world. Thus the concept of *ousia*, which Heidegger traces back through its customary and terminological meanings, emerges not as a fixed substrate but as a mode of being-there that is always already in motion. As Heidegger elaborates, *horismos* is not simply a way of apprehending through sharp determination; rather, "the specific character of ορισμός ultimately arises from the fact that the being itself is determined in its being as circumscribed by the πέρας" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 25–26). Accordingly, the very act of defining or delimiting a being is itself a response to the being's own self-showing, its own way of presenting itself within the horizons of *logos*. This insight is crucial for understanding how Aristotle's metaphysics and his rhetoric are deeply intertwined; the disclosure of beings through speech is itself a motion, a movement of the soul toward the world and back again. Rickert's notion that the world is not simply "gobbled up by rhetorical constructionism" but retains its own ontological thickness underscores the non-reductive character of this reading (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 309). ## 3. Aristotle on Motion

Aristotle's treatment of motion pervades his natural philosophy, his metaphysics, and his psychology; indeed, for Aristotle the question of motion is inseparable from the question of being itself. In the *Movement of Animals*, Aristotle establishes a foundational principle: "the origin of other motions is that which moves itself, and that the origin of this is the immovable, and that the prime mover must of necessity be immovable" (Aristotle, *Movement of Animals*, pp. 1–4). This principle structures the entire Aristotelian cosmos, from celestial bodies to the movements of animal limbs, and it grounds  all particular motions derive their intelligibility from an unmoved source that is itself pure actuality. Subsequently, in the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle extends this analysis to the domain of substance and cause, arguing that if there can be no spatial movement that does not conduce to the moving of a celestial body, then every substance that is immune from change and that has attained to the best must be considered an end; hence, there can be no other being apart from those already named (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 148–149). The implications for rhetoric and the philosophy of the soul are profound. If motion is ontologically primary—if *kinēsis* constitutes a fundamental way that Dasein is, as Heidegger insists (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27)—then the rhetorical situation is itself a field of motions: motions of desire and aversion, motions of perception and imagination, motions of speech and deliberation. As Heidegger further notes, disposition (*hēdonē*) carries within it the two fundamental possibilities of *diōxis* and *phygē*, "going-toward" the beneficial and "shrinking-back before it," and these are basic ways of being moved for *psychē*, for being-there (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 205–206). Accordingly, the rhetorical force of speech does not merely transmit information; it participates in the motions of the soul and thereby alters the affective and cognitive orientations of both speaker and audience. ## 4. Aristotle on Time

The question of time in Aristotle's philosophy is intimately connected to his treatment of motion, for time is defined in the *Physics* as the number of motion with respect to before and after. John Bowin's careful analysis of Aristotle's account reveals that we measure time by counting units—such as days—that are themselves determinate because they are marked out by motions that are determinate in time, specifically celestial motions (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). Counting is here identified as the most exact type of measurement, because the measure it involves is "absolutely indivisible, rather than 'indivisible in relation to perception'" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 8). This connection between time-measurement and perception is crucial, for it indicates that our awareness of time is not a purely intellectual operation but is rooted in the perceptual encounter with regular motions in the world. 18). Thus, temporal awareness involves both an immediate sensory dimension and a cognitive or discriminative dimension whereby we register the before and after of change. Aristotle himself, in the *Metaphysics*, distinguishes between being as actuality and being as potentiality, and this distinction maps onto the temporal structure of becoming: that which exists potentially is indeterminate and tends toward actualization through motion in time (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 42–43). Similarly, in the *Movement of Animals*, the prime mover's immovability serves as the condition for the intelligibility of temporal succession itself, since without an unmoved origin there could be no determinate ordering of before and after in the motions of natural beings (Aristotle, *Movement of Animals*, pp. 1–4). ## 5. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The relation between motion and time in Aristotle's philosophy is therefore not one of mere correlation but of mutual constitution; time is inconceivable without motion, and motion is inconceivable without temporal succession. As Bowin argues, Aristotle holds that we measure time by counting units that are marked out by determinate motions, which means that the apprehension of time presupposes the perception of change (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). Could it be, then, that the soul's capacity for temporal discrimination is itself a kind of motion—a movement of the perceptual faculty from one phase of experience to the next? This question points toward the deeper ontological significance of *kinēsis* in Aristotle's framework, for if motion is a fundamental way that Dasein is (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27), then temporal awareness is not an external measure imposed upon an inert world but an intrinsic feature of the being that perceives and acts. Hence, the passions—which are themselves motions and causes of motions—are also temporal phenomena; they unfold in time and they alter the experienced quality of temporal succession. Aristotle's  being has two forms, "not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157), further supports this reading, for (White 1985) the temporal unfolding of substance from potentiality to actuality is itself a mode of being. Accordingly, rhetorical discourse, which engages the passions and shapes the audience's temporal experience, operates within this same ontological domain; it is not merely a matter of constructing arguments but of attuning souls to particular rhythms and trajectories of motion across time. ## 6. Aisthēsis and Temporal Awareness

The faculty of *aisthēsis*—sense perception—stands at the threshold of temporal awareness in Aristotle's psychology, for it is through perception that the soul first encounters the successive phases of change that constitute experienced time. Kenneth Burke's analysis of rhetorical motivation offers a framework for understanding how perception and temporality are intertwined, specifically through his account of what he calls "the temporizing of essence," whereby "the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). This insight, though directed toward rhetorical strategy, illuminates the deeper philosophical point that essence and temporal becoming are convertible in Aristotle's framework; to perceive a thing is already to perceive it in its temporal unfolding, its emergence from potentiality to actuality. Thus *aisthēsis* is never a static apprehension of an isolated datum but a dynamic encounter with a world in motion. Burke  the rhetorical device of the "question-begging appellative" operates as a "fallacy of confusion" used "for the designation of objects belonging to the field of moral science" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 107–108). If we include the political dimension in this "moral" designation, we see how perceptual categories are always already laden with evaluative and temporal significance; what we perceive is shaped by how we are disposed toward it, and our dispositions themselves unfold over time. Specifically, the interplay between sensation and evaluation that Burke identifies in rhetoric mirrors the interplay between *aisthēsis* and *pathos* in Aristotle's psychology, where perception provides the raw material that the passions then organize into temporally extended patterns of approach and avoidance. ## 7. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

If *aisthēsis* provides the initial contact with temporal succession, it is *phantasia*—the faculty of imagination or appearance—that unifies disparate perceptual moments into a coherent temporal experience. As Ned O'Gorman argues, in *De Anima* phantasia is intimately connected to the sense of sight, "preserving visual perceptions in a sort of after-image, and is itself a sort of psychē-sight, the source of mental images or visual impressions" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). This preservative function is what allows the soul to retain past perceptions and to project future possibilities, thereby constituting the temporal horizon within which deliberation and action take place. Indeed, phantasia is not merely a reproductive faculty that copies sensation; it is a productive power that synthesizes perceptual data into images that exceed the present moment. 4). This lack of precise correspondence between the phantasma and its object is what gives phantasia its rhetorical power: it enables the speaker to place things "before the eyes" of an audience in ways that reshape their temporal experience of past, present, and future. 27–28). The phantasmatic image is not constrained to reproduce what was actually perceived; it can rearrange temporal order, compress or extend duration, and thereby create the rhetorical *kairos* that gives discourse its persuasive force. ## 8. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle represents perhaps the most consequential modern rereading of the Aristotelian corpus, for it transforms the concepts of *kinēsis*, *ousia*, and *logos* from categories of classical metaphysics into existential structures of Dasein. In his lectures on the basic concepts of Aristotelian philosophy, Heidegger argues that Democritus was "borne by the matter itself" and thus led to the discovery that beings must be apprehended not merely with regard to *hylē* but also with regard to their *schēma* (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 176–177). This image of being carried by the matter itself encapsulates Heidegger's broader hermeneutical principle: we do not impose concepts upon beings from the outside but allow the phenomena to guide our inquiry. Specifically, Heidegger reads Aristotle's treatment of being as fundamentally concerned with presence—*Anwesenheit*—the manner in which beings show themselves in the Greek understanding. Aristotle  the *Metaphysics* that "being has two forms—it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157), and Heidegger seizes upon this duality (Aristotle 2014) the Greek understanding of being oscillates between actuality and potentiality, between presence and the not-yet-present. Thus, motion emerges not as a deficient mode of being but as the very medium through which being discloses itself temporally. Rickert's ambient rhetoric extends this Heideggerian trajectory into the domain of rhetoric proper, suggesting that rhetorical attunement involves a "letting be" that cultivates human beings in the world rather than merely asserting mastery over it (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 53–54). Accordingly, we may conclude that Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle opens a space in which motion, time, perception, and imagination are understood not as discrete faculties or categories but as intertwined dimensions of Dasein's being-in-the-world—a being that is always already rhetorical in its fundamental orientation. ---
