# Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Perception: Toward a Rhetorical Ontology

## 1. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding

Aristotle's philosophical project, when read through the lens of contemporary rhetorical theory, discloses an understanding of being that is neither merely logical nor abstractly metaphysical but is instead oriented toward the lived, affective, and situated character of human existence. The concept of *ousia*, which Heidegger translates not as a static "substance" but as a mode of being-there, provides the initial entry point for grasping Aristotle's fundamental concerns. As Heidegger clarifies, "definitio is ορισμός, ορισμός is a λόγος, a 'self-expression' about being-there as being" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 25–26). This  Aristotelian categories are not inert classifications imposed upon the world but modes of disclosure through which beings show themselves. Thomas Rickert reinforces this insight by observing how dwelling entails not mastery but a "letting be" that cultivates world as already entailing human being (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 53–54). The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric*  *kinesis* in Aristotle should be understood not merely as physical displacement but as "a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Accordingly, articulating Aristotle's understanding requires attending to the dynamic, world-disclosing character of his basic concepts—concepts that resist reduction to static logical forms and instead disclose the temporally and affectively structured character of human situatedness in the world. ## 2. Articulating Aristotle's Understanding: Passion, Judgment, and Logos

To further articulate Aristotle's philosophical framework, one must attend to the role of the *pathē*—the passions or dispositions—in shaping both judgment and discourse. Far from being mere emotional disturbances that cloud rational deliberation, the passions for Aristotle constitute fundamental orientations through which human beings encounter and evaluate the world. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle, as documented by the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, insists that passion is itself "both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). This framing establishes the *pathē* as ontologically productive rather than epistemologically deficient. Heidegger  Aristotle's understanding of how passions alter judgments reveals the site where passions intrude upon *logos*—a term frequently rendered as "reason" by historians of philosophy, though its original sense encompasses articulated judgment and discourse more broadly (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Rickert's ambient rhetoric extends this insight by challenging the antifoundationalist position wherein "the world is gobbled up by rhetorical constructionism, making 'truth' or 'reality' a function of social agonism" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 309). Thus, on the Aristotelian-Heideggerian reading, passion and reason are not opposed poles but intertwined movements through which beings disclose their significance within the horizons of rhetorical being. ## 3. Aristotle on Motion

Motion, or *kinesis*, occupies a central position in Aristotle's natural philosophy, metaphysics, and psychology alike. In the *Movement of Animals*, Aristotle establishes the architectonic principle that "the origin of other motions is that which moves itself, and that the origin of this is the immovable, and that the prime mover must of necessity be immovable" (Aristotle, *Movement of Animals*, pp. 1–4). This hierarchical structure of motion—wherein self-movers derive their impetus from an unmoved origin—grounds Aristotle's account of animal locomotion, desire, and ultimately rhetorical action in a cosmological framework. Yet Heidegger's interpretation, as presented in *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, insists that *kinesis* cannot be confined to the physical sciences; instead, it designates a fundamental way that Dasein itself is (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Heidegger's reading of Aristotle further discloses how the basic movements of the soul—*diōxis* and *phygē*, or "going-toward" and "shrinking-back"—constitute not mere physiological reflexes but ontological dispositions through which being-there encounters what is beneficial or harmful (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 205–206). The *Metaphysics* extends these considerations into the domain of pure actuality, where Aristotle considers substances immune from change that have "attained to the best" and must be considered ends in themselves (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 148–149). Motion thus pervades Aristotle's thought as the medium through which potentiality achieves actualization at every ontological level. ## 4. Aristotle on Time

Time, for Aristotle, is inseparable from the perception and measurement of motion, yet it possesses its own distinctive structure that resists reduction to mere succession. John Bowin's careful  for Aristotle, "we measure time by counting units of time (e.g., days) which are determinate because they are marked out by motions that are determinate in time (e.g., celestial motions)" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). This formulation reveals the reciprocal dependence of time and motion: time is the measure of motion, yet determinate motions are required to render time measurable. Bowin  counting constitutes the most exact form of measurement because the unit it employs "is absolutely indivisible, rather than 'indivisible in relation to perception'" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle addresses the ontological status of indeterminacy itself, observing that those who speak of the indeterminate while fancying themselves to be speaking of being are in fact "speaking about non-being; for that which exists potentially and not actually is the indeterminate" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 42–43). Time, then, occupies a peculiar position within Aristotle's ontology: it is the number of motion with respect to before and after, yet its apprehension requires a perceiving soul capable of counting and discriminating temporal units. The *Movement of Animals* supplements this account by grounding temporal experience in the self-moving character of animate beings, whose pursuit and avoidance presuppose a temporal horizon within which action unfolds (Aristotle, *Movement of Animals*, pp. 1–4). ## 5. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The interdependence of motion and time constitutes one of the most intricate threads in Aristotle's natural philosophy, and its implications extend well beyond physics into the domains of perception, rhetoric, and ontology. Bowin's analysis illuminates how Aristotle's account of temporal measurement relies upon the regularity of celestial motions to provide the determinate units—days, months, years—by which we count and thereby cognize temporal passage (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 7–9). Yet the relation is not one of simple derivation; rather, motion and time are co-disclosing, each rendering the other intelligible. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* underscore this point by showing that Heidegger reads Aristotle's *kinesis* not as an objective physical process external to Dasein but as a way of being that constitutes Dasein's own temporal unfolding (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 26–27). Aristotle himself, in the *Metaphysics*, recognizes that spatial movement conduces to the ordering of celestial substances, and that every being immune from change has already "attained to the best" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 148–149). Hence, the relation between motion and time is not merely epistemological—a matter of how we happen to measure duration—but ontological, disclosing the very structure of beings in their temporal presencing. This intertwining of motion and time provides the ground upon which perception, imagination, and rhetorical action become possible. ## 6. Aisthēsis and Temporal Awareness

Perception, or *aisthēsis*, furnishes the initial contact with the temporal dimension of experience, providing the sensory foundation upon which more complex forms of temporal awareness are built. Kenneth Burke's rhetorical theory, though developed in a modern idiom, illuminates a structural parallel with Aristotle's account by demonstrating how the temporal dimension of rhetorical experience is embedded in the priority of origins. Burke observes that the logical idea of a thing's essence "can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). This insight—what Burke calls "the temporizing of essence"—reveals how perception is never merely passive reception but always already structured by narrative and temporal frameworks that organize sensory data into meaningful wholes. Indeed, sense perception for Aristotle involves a discriminating power that operates within time, and the unity of this discriminating act with its temporal exercise is essential to the coherence of perceptual experience. Burke's analysis of how rhetorical devices exploit the confusion between logical and temporal priority  temporal awareness is not a neutral backdrop for cognition but an active, rhetorically charged dimension of human engagement with the world (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Thus, *aisthēsis* and temporal awareness emerge as inseparable dimensions of the perceiving soul's encounter with the motions of beings. ## 7. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

If *aisthēsis* provides the initial apprehension of temporal passage, it is *phantasia*—the image-making or presentational capacity of the soul—that unifies disparate perceptual moments into a coherent temporal field. 4). This preservative and re-presentational function of phantasia enables the soul to hold together what has been perceived with what is anticipated, thereby constituting the specious present as a duration rather than a mere mathematical point. Bowin supplements this account by noting that *phantasia* has been implicated in special, common, and incidental perception alike, suggesting its pervasive role in the constitution of perceptual experience (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, pp. 17–19). Burke's rhetorical framework reinforces these considerations by showing how the temporizing of essence—the translation of logical priority into narrative sequence—depends upon the imaginative capacity to envision origins and ends, a capacity structurally analogous to Aristotelian *phantasia* (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Hence, *phantasia* operates as the faculty of temporal unification, gathering perceptions into the synthetic field within which rhetorical persuasion, deliberation, and epideictic display become possible. ## 8. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Heidegger's reading of Aristotle constitutes neither a mere historical reconstruction nor a neutral exegesis but a transformative retrieval that discloses the ontological dimensions latent within Aristotle's texts. In *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, Heidegger attends to the way in which Democritus was "borne by the matter itself," led to the discovery that beings must be apprehended not merely with regard to *hylē* but also with regard to their *schēma* (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 176–177). This attentiveness to matter and form as co-disclosing structures rather than separable abstractions exemplifies Heidegger's method of reading Aristotle against the grain of the scholastic tradition. The contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* document how Heidegger reinterprets Aristotle's passions as *Befindlichkeiten*—dispositions or attunements—rather than as static psychological states, thereby disclosing their ontological significance as ways in which Dasein finds itself in the world (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Aristotle's own *Metaphysics* provides the textual ground for this retrieval, particularly in its insistence that "being has two forms—it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 156–157). This duality of being as both *energeia* and *dynamis* furnishes Heidegger with the conceptual resources to think temporality, motion, and presence in a manner that exceeds the metaphysics of substance. Accordingly, Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle opens a pathway from ancient ontology to the existential analytic, demonstrating that the questions of motion, time, and perception are not relics of a superseded physics but enduring dimensions of the question of being itself. ---
