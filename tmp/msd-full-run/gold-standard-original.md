# Motion, Time, Perception, and Phantasia: Aristotle's Temporal Framework and Heidegger's Interpretation

## 1. Motion and Kinesis: Aristotle's Understanding of Motion as Change, Potentiality, and Actuality

At the foundations of Aristotle's natural philosophy lies the concept of *kinesis*---motion understood not merely as spatial displacement but as the broader phenomenon of change itself. In the *Physics*, Aristotle establishes that the things which exist by nature all share a decisive characteristic: "each of them has within itself a principle of motion and of stationariness (in respect of place, or of growth and decrease, or by way of alteration)" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 17). This formulation is crucial, for it immediately expands the semantic range of *kinesis* beyond mere locomotion to encompass qualitative alteration, quantitative growth and diminution, and substantial generation and corruption. Nature itself, Aristotle insists, "is a principle or cause of being moved and of being at rest in that to which it belongs primarily, in virtue of itself and not accidentally" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 17). Motion is thus not an external imposition upon beings but an intrinsic determination of natural entities---the very mark of their naturalness. The contrast with artifacts sharpens this point: a bed or a coat, considered as products of art, possesses no innate impulse to change; it is only insofar as such objects happen to be composed of earth or stone---natural materials---that they participate in the kinetic order at all.

The conceptual architecture supporting this account depends upon the paired categories of *dynamis* (potentiality) and *energeia* or *entelechia* (actuality). Aristotle's celebrated definition of motion as the actualization of what exists potentially, insofar as it is potential, resists reduction to any single category. A thing in motion is neither fully potential nor fully actual; it occupies a determinate ontological position between these poles. A house under construction is no longer merely bricks and timber in potentia, nor is it yet the completed dwelling in full actuality; the process of building is itself a mode of being, the being-at-work of what can be built precisely as buildable. Heidegger, in his 1924 lecture course, underscores precisely this point when he notes that "the concepts *dynamis*, *energeia*, *entelechia* are so worn out that one is not at all capable of seeing what was at stake in the fundamental meaning of these concepts" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 214). The task Heidegger identifies is one of recovery: we must work to inhabit the original philosophical situation in which these terms were forged, before centuries of scholastic and scientific usage obscured their ontological significance.

Aristotle's framework thereby establishes that becoming is not mere privation or negation of being. As he argues in Book I of the *Physics*, "a thing may come to be from what is not in a qualified sense, i.e. accidentally. For a thing comes to be from the privation, which in its own nature is something which is not---this not surviving as a constituent of the result" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 15--16). The failure to recognize this qualified sense of coming-to-be from what-is-not, Aristotle contends, led earlier thinkers astray, causing them to suppose that nothing comes to be or exists apart from what already is and thereby doing away with all becoming. Motion, then, is a genuine ontological phenomenon, irreducible to either static presence or mere absence; it is the mode of being proper to natural entities as they traverse the interval between potentiality and actuality.

## 2. Time and Chronos: Aristotle's Account of Time as the "Number of Motion"

Aristotle's treatment of time (*chronos*) in *Physics* IV is inseparable from his analysis of motion. His well-known formulation characterizes time as the number of motion with respect to the before and after---a formulation that carries several implications requiring careful unpacking. First, time is not an independent substance or container within which events occur; it is rather a structural feature of motion itself, disclosed through the act of counting or measuring. Second, the before and after that time numbers is originally given in motion: the spatial and qualitative ordering of a process provides the substrate upon which temporal sequence is articulated. Third, because time is constitutively related to numbering, it presupposes a soul (*psyche*) capable of performing the act of enumeration. Were there no soul to count, there could be motion but not, strictly speaking, time. This is a claim of extraordinary philosophical ambition, for it ties the very existence of temporal order to the presence of a perceiving, discriminating consciousness.

The "now" itself occupies a paradoxical position in Aristotle's account: it is both the boundary that divides past from future and the point of continuity that binds them together. Aristotle observes in the *De Anima* that "what thought thinks of and the time in which it thinks are in this case divisible only incidentally and not as such. For in them too there is something indivisible (though, it may be, not separable) which gives unity to the time and the whole of length; and this is found equally in every continuum whether temporal or spatial" (Aristotle, *On the Soul (De Anima)*, pp. 47--48). Time, as a continuous magnitude, is infinitely divisible, yet it is apprehended only through the discrete act of marking the now; this indivisible element that gives unity to the temporal continuum is thus not a component part but a structural principle---the very condition under which temporal extension becomes intelligible. The relationship between time and number also connects Aristotle's temporal analysis to his broader ontological framework: just as number is the measure of plurality, time is the measure of motion, and this measuring tracks the real structure of change, the actual transitions from potentiality to actuality that constitute the being of natural entities.

## 3. Motion-Time Relationship: How Motion and Time Are Co-Constitutive

Motion and time, on Aristotle's account, are not merely correlated but genuinely co-constitutive. We perceive time only when we perceive motion, and conversely, we perceive motion only within a temporal horizon. This co-constitution operates at multiple levels. At the most basic level, the before-and-after structure that time articulates is originally a feature of spatial magnitude through which motion proceeds: a body moving from one position to another traverses a spatial continuum, and the ordering of positions along this continuum provides the substrate for temporal ordering. Time thus inherits its continuity from the continuity of motion, which in turn inherits its continuity from the continuity of spatial magnitude. Yet the relationship is not simply one of derivation from space to motion to time; there is a reciprocal dependency. Motion is recognized as motion only insofar as it is temporally articulated, and temporal articulation is possible only through the perception of motion or change. When nothing changes in our experience---when we register no alteration whatsoever---time seems not to have passed.

This co-constitution has profound implications for the role of perception in the temporal framework. If time is the number of motion, and numbering requires a soul, then the entire motion-time complex is disclosed only within the perceptual field of a living being. The discriminating power of the soul---its capacity to distinguish the now from the now, the before from the after---is not an epiphenomenon supervening on an already temporal world; it is, rather, the very condition under which the temporal structure of motion becomes manifest. As Aristotle argues in the *De Anima*, the perceptual act that distinguishes different qualities operates within an undivided temporal moment, for "both the discriminating power and the time of its exercise must be one and undivided" (Aristotle, *On the Soul (De Anima)*, pp. 40--42). The unity of the perceiving moment is accordingly the condition for the differentiation of temporal succession. Heidegger, reading Aristotle through an ontological lens, observes that in Aristotle's natural interpretation of being-there, "being means being-present, being-completed" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 200--201). The Greek understanding of being is thus oriented by presence---by the way in which beings show themselves in the now of perceptual encounter---and the co-constitution of motion and time is the structural basis for this orientation.

## 4. Aisthesis in the Temporal Framework: How Perception Operates Within Aristotle's Temporal Structure

Aristotle's account of *aisthesis* (perception) is far more nuanced than a simple theory of sensory reception. Perception, for Aristotle, involves the soul's reception of the sensible form without the matter---the famous analogy of the wax receiving the impression of the signet ring. As Dorothea Frede observes, the sense-organ "is affected by the perceptible form of the object without the matter," and Aristotle "compares it to the wax that receives the imprint of a seal without its matter (gold or iron). In other words, the soul receives the colour without the paint (coloured surface)" (Frede, "The Cognitive Role of Phantasia in Aristotle," p. 3). Yet this reception is simultaneously an act of discernment (*krinein*): "perception is not the mere passive reception of such immaterial imprints. It is also treated as a critical faculty, for it is supposed to 'judge' (*krinei*) what it receives" (Frede, "The Cognitive Role of Phantasia in Aristotle," p. 3). The perceiving soul is thus not a blank surface upon which the world inscribes itself but an active, discriminating power that evaluates and differentiates the qualities it encounters.

The temporal dimension of perception emerges most clearly in Aristotle's analysis of the common sensibles and the unity of perceptual consciousness. Perception requires a medium---light for sight, air for hearing---and accordingly involves a temporal process of transmission. As Aristotle notes, "Seeing is due to an affection or change of what has the perceptive faculty, and it cannot be affected by the seen colour itself; it remains that it must be affected by what comes between" (Aristotle, *On the Soul (De Anima)*, pp. 29--30). Perception is mediated, and mediation entails temporal extension. More fundamentally, the perception of complex objects requires the unifying activity of what Aristotle calls the *koinon aistheterion* (common sense), which synthesizes the deliverances of the individual senses into a unified perceptual experience. This synthetic activity is intrinsically temporal: the discrimination of white from sweet, of a sound from a color, must occur in an undivided act even though the objects discriminated are qualitatively diverse. It is precisely this capacity for simultaneous discrimination that Aristotle explores when he asks whether the faculty that discriminates can be "at the same time divided in its being" while remaining "spatially and numerically undivided" (Aristotle, *On the Soul (De Anima)*, pp. 40--42). The answer reveals the paradoxical structure of perceptual synthesis: the soul is divisible in its being---it can entertain contrary qualities---but undivided in its act of discrimination, which must take place in an indivisible moment.

The perceptual encounter with the world is always, for Aristotle, an encounter with beings in motion. Since natural beings are defined by their intrinsic principle of motion and rest, to perceive a natural being is to perceive something that is changing or capable of change. Accordingly, the senses track these changes: sight follows the movement of color through a transparent medium, hearing registers the temporal unfolding of sound, touch discerns alterations in temperature and texture. A being that could not perceive change---that could not register the before and after of motion---would lack not merely temporal awareness but perceptual awareness altogether. Indeed, Aristotle explains why plants cannot perceive despite having a portion of soul: "they have no mean, and so no principle in them capable of taking on the forms of sensible objects but are affected together with their matter" (Aristotle, *On the Soul (De Anima)*, pp. 37--38). Without the capacity for discerning form apart from matter, there can be no perception, and without perception, no access to the temporal structure of the world.

## 5. Phantasia and Temporal Synthesis: How Imagination Enables the Binding of Temporal Experience

If *aisthesis* provides the initial contact with the changing world, it is *phantasia*---imagination or appearance---that enables the binding and retention of perceptual experience across time. Aristotle's treatment of phantasia in *De Anima* III.3 positions it as a faculty distinct from both perception and thought, yet dependent upon the former and prerequisite for the latter. Christina Papachristou emphasizes that for Aristotle, "phantasia is a kind of motion (*kinesis*) in the soul that cannot exist apart from sensation---it is caused by the activity of sensation---and supposition (*hypolepsis*)" (Papachristou, "Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima," pp. 12--13). Phantasia is thus generated by the activity of perception, yet it persists beyond the immediate perceptual encounter, carrying forward the sensory content in the form of images (*phantasmata*) that can be recalled, recombined, and deliberated upon. Indeed, it is this persistence that constitutes the temporal significance of the faculty: without phantasia, the perceiving soul would be confined to the immediate present---to the instantaneous now of actual sensory contact---and could neither remember what has passed nor anticipate what is to come.

The distinction between phantasia and both perception and belief is essential to understanding its temporal role. As Papachristou further notes, Aristotle asserts that "phantasia is different from sensation, and thought" and that his conception accordingly "differs from that of Plato's Sophist" (Papachristou, "Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima," pp. 12--13). For Plato, phantasia was a mixture of sensation and judgment; for Aristotle, it is a distinct capacity, one that is voluntary in a way that belief is not. Aristotle observes that "this affection is in our power, whenever we wish" (Papachristou, "Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima," pp. 13--14), whereas belief is constrained by truth and falsity. This voluntary character enables the recombination and transformation of images---the construction of scenarios never actually perceived---which is essential for practical deliberation, rhetorical persuasion, and theoretical speculation alike.

Phantasia also provides the images upon which thought itself operates. Papachristou records Aristotle's striking claim: "Thinking seems to be peculiar to the soul; But if thinking is a kind of phantasia, then it would not be possible even for thinking to be independent of the body" (Papachristou, "Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima," pp. 12--13). The connection between phantasia and thought reinforces the temporal character of cognition: thought works with and through images that carry within them the temporal structure of the perceptual encounters from which they derive. Similarly, Victor Caston has argued that phantasia belongs to every animal that possesses even the capacity for pleasure and pain, since "there cannot be desire of any sort on his view without phantasia" (Caston, "Why Aristotle Needs Imagination," pp. 2--3). Phantasia is thus not an optional supplement to perception but a necessary condition for any form of motivated action, which is itself inherently temporal---oriented toward a future good or away from a future harm.

Furthermore, phantasia synthesizes not only temporal but spatial dimensions of experience. As Ned O'Gorman observes, in the *De Anima* "the perception of magnitude---for instance, the size of the sun---is intimately tied to phantasia, which provides 'a kind of comparative seeing' embedded in a spatial logic" (O'Gorman, "Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric," pp. 6--7). This comparative seeing enables the soul to grasp relations of magnitude, distance, and proportion that exceed the deliverances of any single sense at any single moment. Debra Hawhee, accordingly, identifies phantasia as potentially one of the most significant Aristotelian faculties for rhetoric, noting that "perception, interpretation, desire, action, and judgment all cluster around the faculty of phantasia, thereby positioning phantasia as potentially one of the most important critical faculties for the art of rhetoric" (Hawhee, "Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes," p. 6). The clustering of these capacities around a single faculty is not incidental; it reflects the integrative, temporally synthetic function of phantasia as the faculty that binds the disparate moments of perceptual, affective, and cognitive life into a coherent experiential whole.

## 6. Heidegger's Reading of Aristotle: The Ontological Significance of Kinesis and the Temporal Structure of Being-There

Martin Heidegger's engagement with Aristotle, particularly in his 1924 lecture course published as *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, represents one of the most philosophically consequential reinterpretations of Aristotelian thought in the twentieth century. Heidegger reads Aristotle not as a taxonomist of substances and categories but as a thinker whose deepest insights concern the temporal and kinetic structure of human existence---of Dasein, or being-there. Central to this reading is Heidegger's reinterpretation of *kinesis*. Against the modern reduction of motion to uniform change of spatial position, Heidegger insists on recovering the full ontological breadth of the Aristotelian concept: "Here, it is really not a question of defining movement in some sense, but of making beings as moved visible in their being-there and holding fast to them" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 214). The task is not merely physical or mathematical but ontological---to bring the being-character of beings in motion into view.

Heidegger further emphasizes the priority of Aristotle's analysis over subsequent physical science. He observes that the modern definition of movement as uniform speed "itself already presupposes everything that Aristotle said about movement," and that what modern physics established was "the question of the system-of-relations of movement, not the question of movement itself, but rather movement itself with a view to the system-of-relations from which it is to be measureable" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 214). Aristotle's inquiry, by contrast, addresses motion as such---as a fundamental determination of the being of beings---and accordingly has a scope that no subsequent mathematical physics can supersede. The ontological question of what it means for a being to be in motion is logically and phenomenologically prior to any question about the measurable relations that obtain among motions.

Heidegger's ontological interpretation of *kinesis* extends to his reading of the *psyche* and its modes of engagement with the world. He interprets Aristotle's account of perception and disposition as disclosing the fundamental ways in which human being-there is moved. In a striking passage, Heidegger describes the basic orientations of the soul: "Disposition, *hedone*, has the two possibilities of *dioksis* and *phyge*, 'going-toward' the *sympheron* and 'shrinking-back before it.' *Dioksis* and *phyge* are basic ways of being moved for *psyche*, for being-there" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 205--206). Pursuit and avoidance are not merely psychological reactions but ontological structures of being-there, modes in which the soul's inherent motility expresses itself in its encounter with the world. The world is accordingly disclosed not as a neutral array of objects but as a field of significance structured by the soul's affective orientation toward what is beneficial and away from what is harmful. Heidegger identifies this structure as fundamental to understanding Aristotle's account of the causes operative in nature, observing that for natural beings there are two possibilities of the *dia ti*: "the *hou heneka*, (2) *hothen he arche tes kineseos*. 'That on account of which and that whence movement is'" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 157--158). Teleological orientation and kinetic origin are thus the two axes along which being-there encounters the natural world.

The ontological reinterpretation of motion connects directly to Heidegger's understanding of being as being-present. He observes that in Aristotle's natural interpretation, "being means being-present, being-completed. The being is not only there in its look; the being-character is itself explicitly there, in the sense of the explicitness of everyday seeing, considering, discussing" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 200--201). The prevailing intelligibility of the everyday world is governed by *doxa*: "Being-with-one-another is thoroughly governed by *doxa*. All speaking is oriented toward bringing the questionable, the unintelligible, into a definite familiarity" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 200--201). This insight connects the temporal structure of perception to the social structure of human existence: the world is disclosed temporally and communally, through shared practices of speaking, perceiving, and interpreting. Heidegger even suggests that the *logos* itself constitutes the shared world of human being-with-one-another, noting that "if *logos* constitutes the having-there-with-one-another of the world, the determination of being-with-one-another is constituted in it" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 55). The temporal and the communal are thus interwoven in the very structure of Dasein.

The scholars assembled in *Heidegger and Rhetoric* have further illuminated these connections. In Heidegger's account of Aristotelian psychology, "*kinesis* gives the authentic Da-Charakter to Dasein, the vital place-time of Being" (*Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). The passions, on this reading, are not static cognitive states but dynamic orientations: they "are not *Wissen*, static knowledge, but *Befindlichkeiten*, dispositions" (*Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Indeed, passions are dispositions oriented on others' dispositions that define our being-in-the-world. Heidegger's Aristotle is thus a thinker for whom the temporal, kinetic, and affective dimensions of human existence are inseparable---a vision that fundamentally shapes the project of *Being and Time*, where temporality (*Zeitlichkeit*) becomes the horizon for understanding the meaning of being as such.

## 7. Integration: The Temporal Fabric of Perception, Imagination, and Being

The threads of this analysis converge upon a unified insight: for Aristotle, and for Heidegger's Aristotle, the temporal structure of experience is not an abstract framework imposed upon a static world but the very medium through which beings come to presence and through which the soul engages with them. Motion (*kinesis*) is the fundamental ontological phenomenon---the actualization of potentiality that characterizes all natural beings. Time (*chronos*) is not a container but the measure of this motion, disclosed through the perceiving soul's act of distinguishing the before and after. Perception (*aisthesis*) is the soul's responsive engagement with beings in motion, an engagement that is itself temporally structured, requiring the synthesis of diverse sensory inputs into unified perceptual judgments at undivided moments. And phantasia---imagination, the power of image-making---is what enables the temporal continuity of experience, carrying forward the traces of perception, making memory, anticipation, and deliberation possible, and providing the images without which thought itself cannot operate.

Heidegger's reinterpretation of these Aristotelian structures reveals their deep ontological significance. By reading *kinesis* as a determination of the being of beings rather than a merely physical phenomenon, and by interpreting perception and disposition as modes of being-there rather than merely psychological processes, Heidegger shows that the temporal framework of Aristotelian thought is not simply a physics of time but an ontology of temporal existence. The two possibilities of *dioksis* and *phyge*---pursuit and avoidance---disclose the soul's being-in-the-world as an inherently motile, temporally oriented engagement with what matters. The concept of being-present, which Heidegger identifies as the guiding sense of being in Greek thought, is inseparable from the kinetic-temporal structure of *aisthesis* and *phantasia*: beings are present insofar as they show themselves to a perceiving, imagining, temporally situated soul. As Thomas Rickert has argued, rhetoric itself operates within precisely such a structure of ambient disclosure, revealing and constituting "the informational environment within which we flourish, even as it works in and through both the existent informational situation and the local material environs" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 53--54). The temporal fabric that Aristotle describes---woven from motion, perception, and imagination---is not merely the background of human experience but its constitutive medium.

This integrated picture has profound consequences for our understanding of Aristotelian philosophy and its legacy. It reveals that the domains typically separated in modern philosophy---physics, psychology, epistemology, ontology---are deeply intertwined in Aristotle's thought, united by the temporal structure of *kinesis*. The soul's capacity for perception and imagination is not an incidental feature of a being that happens also to exist in time; it is the very capacity through which the temporality of being becomes manifest. Indeed, Heidegger underscores this when he notes that the concept of something as something---the *as*-character of understanding---requires the interpretive structure of *logos*: "Concept: something as something---as-character. Beings as what? In their being" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262--263). To experience beings *as* beings, to have them there in their being, is already to be situated within the temporal-kinetic fabric that motion, perception, and phantasia together constitute. Heidegger's recovery of this insight, though filtered through his own distinctive philosophical vocabulary, illuminates dimensions of Aristotle's thought that had been obscured by centuries of scholastic and scientific reinterpretation---and it positions phantasia, that faculty in which perception, interpretation, desire, action, and judgment all cluster, as the linchpin of the temporal ontology that binds Aristotelian natural philosophy to the existential analytic of Dasein.
