Aristotle conceives kinesis not as a static property but as a distinctive mode of being-present characterized by the actualization of potentiality, thereby distinguishing incomplete activities (kinesis) from complete activities (energeia) within his ontological framework (Heidegger, 2009, p. 226)[4]. Indeed, as Heidegger observes, "Aristotle can also define movement in what follows as ἐνέργεια. Ἐνέργεια as a mode of being-there means nothing other than the being-at-work of something," indicating that motion is fundamentally a mode of actualization rather than mere change (Heidegger, 2009, p. 226)[5]. The genuine definition of movement reaches its aim in Physics Book III, Chapter 3, as "Movement as ἐντελέχεια τοῦ δυνάμει ποιητικοῦ καὶ παθητικοῦ," establishing motion as the entelechy of what exists potentially and thus situating kinesis within a determinate ontological register (Heidegger, 2009, p. 231)[6]. Specifically, Aristotle's determination asks "What is the determination of κίνησις as a definite type of being-present, κίνησις as ἐνέργεια, related to?" revealing that kinesis is understood as a specific ontological category of presence rather than as a derivative or accidental attribute (Heidegger, 2009, p. 174)[7]. Subsequently, Heidegger notes that "movement as kinesis possesses both a poietikon (capacity for creativity) and a pathitikon (capacity for feeling)," emphasizing the dual active-passive structure inherent in Aristotelian motion and thereby illuminating the complex ontological character that distinguishes kinesis from simple alteration (Heidegger, 2009, p. 236)[8].

These textual grounds establish that Aristotle's kinesis is not merely descriptive of physical change but constitutes an ontological category—a way of being-present that involves the actualization of what exists in potentiality (Heidegger, 2009, p. 226)[9]. The distinction between incomplete activities (kinesis) and complete activities (energeia) reveals that motion has its telos outside itself, making it fundamentally different from self-contained actualities, as Heidegger observes when noting that "kinesis is characterized by its incompleteness, its being-on-the-way toward an end that lies beyond the movement itself" (Heidegger, 2009, p. 231)[10]. This interpretation represents Heidegger's phenomenological appropriation of Aristotle, which may emphasize ontological dimensions that Aristotle himself articulated differently within his hylomorphic framework, particularly insofar as Heidegger's focus on being-present and temporal structure recasts Aristotelian categories through the lens of fundamental ontology rather than through the substance-accident metaphysics that dominates Aristotle's own systematic presentation (Heidegger, 2009, p. 174)[11].

Aristotle's investigation of natural beings (φύσει ὄντα) as living things provides the concrete context for understanding kinesis, as motion is not an abstract category but is always the motion of particular natural beings whose being is characterized by the capacity for self-movement and the actualization of inherent potentialities (Heidegger, 2009, p. 165)[12]. The methodological question "How does Aristotle see and determine the φύσει ὄντα as living things following the guide of the ἔργα τέχνης?" indicates that natural beings are understood through the analogy with products of craft, yet distinguished by their internal principle of motion, whereby living things possess an intrinsic source of change that artifacts lack (Heidegger, 2009, p. 165)[13]. Subsequently, the claim that "one can hardly determine beings in their being if one insists that there is only one ἀρχή," which "mistakes the sense of ἀρχή as such, insofar as there are already πολλά in the articulation of being," establishes that understanding natural beings requires multiple principles, including matter, form, and the principle of motion, rather than reducing ontological explanation to a single foundational element (Heidegger, 2009, p. 209)[14]. Hence, Heidegger's observation that kinesis possesses "both a poietikon (capacity for creativity) and a pathitikon (capacity for feeling)" emphasizes that motion in natural beings involves both active and passive dimensions, characteristic of living things that both act upon and are affected by their environment, thereby revealing motion as constitutive of the very being of natural entities rather than as a merely accidental attribute (Heidegger, 2009, p. 174)[15].

These grounds establish that Aristotle's analysis of kinesis is grounded in the concrete investigation of natural beings, particularly living things, rather than being a purely abstract or mathematical concept, for natural beings possess an internal principle (archē) of motion that distinguishes them from artifacts, whose movement originates from external causes (Heidegger, 2009, p. 165)[16]. The motion of natural beings involves the actualization of potentialities inherent in their nature, whereby the internal archē functions as both the source and direction of their characteristic activities, from growth and reproduction to locomotion and perception (Heidegger, 2009, p. 174)[17]. The dual structure of poietikon and pathitikon reflects the fact that living beings are both self-moving (active) and responsive to their environment (passive), making motion a fundamental characteristic of life itself rather than an accidental property that might be added to or subtracted from a static substance. Consequently, Aristotle's conception of kinesis emerges not from mathematical abstraction but from phenomenological attention to the self-directed yet environmentally responsive character of living organisms, whose very being consists in their capacity to actualize their inherent potentialities through motion (Heidegger, 2009, p. 209)[18].

Within the broader framework of Aristotelian Physics as 'ἀρχή-Research'—investigating the fundamental principles (archai) of natural beings—the analysis of chronos in Physics Book IV emerges as central to understanding the being of things in motion, particularly as this inquiry inherits the methodological structure established in Physics Book I, where Aristotle asks whether "the basic principles of nature are two or three or some greater number" (Aristotle, Physics I.6-7, p. 294)[1]. This methodological precedent proves decisive for the later analysis of time, which Aristotle defines as the number or measure of motion with respect to before and after, thereby establishing time as fundamentally dependent on motion while simultaneously raising the aporia of whether time requires the soul's counting activity to exist (Heidegger, 2009, p. 207)[19]. The complexity of this relationship becomes evident when Aristotle observes that the soul may perceive that "the 'nows' are two" without counting them, yet "the soul can also, no doubt, perceive that the 'nows' are three" (Aristotle, De Anima, 563a17-19, p. 6)[2], indicating that temporal discrimination involves both immediate perception and the capacity for numerical determination. This dual structure suggests that time's constitution requires multiple principles—motion, soul, and the now—operating in concert, such that time emerges neither as purely objective (independent of consciousness) nor as purely subjective (a mental construct), but rather as a phenomenon that discloses itself only through the soul's capacity to distinguish and measure the succession inherent in motion itself.

These grounds reveal that Aristotle's account of time is not merely a mathematical abstraction but emerges from the soul's engagement with motion through the discrimination of successive 'nows,' thereby establishing time as fundamentally dependent upon a measuring consciousness that distinguishes temporal phases within continuous change. The aporia concerning time's dependence on soul arises because time as measure requires a measuring activity (counting), yet motion itself seems to possess temporal extension independently of any observer, creating a tension between time's ontological status as an objective feature of natural processes and its epistemological dependence upon rational discrimination. Heidegger observes that this difficulty reflects Aristotle's recognition that "time is something that belongs to motion" while simultaneously requiring "the soul as that which does the counting" (Heidegger, 2009, p. 207)[20], such that neither motion alone nor soul alone suffices to constitute time as the numbered aspect of change. The precise nature of the soul's role in constituting time remains aporetic in Aristotle's text, as he acknowledges the difficulty of determining whether time could exist without a soul to count it, leaving unresolved whether temporal extension inheres in motion as a potential structure awaiting actualization through measurement or whether the soul's discriminatory activity genuinely brings time into being as a distinct ontological phenomenon rather than merely apprehending what already exists independently of consciousness.

The reciprocal interdependence between motion and time in Aristotle's physics generates an apparent circularity that he resolves through the concept of continuity (suneches), whereby time measures motion with respect to before and after while simultaneously requiring motion as the substrate that renders temporal measurement possible, such that neither can be adequately defined without reference to the other (Aristotle, Physics IV). This interdependence raises the fundamental question: "Why, however, must the movement corresponding to the time and the movement corresponding to the event be separate?" (Heidegger, 2009, p. 297)[21], suggesting that Aristotle distinguishes between time as a universal measure applicable to all motions and particular motions as the measured phenomena, yet acknowledges their essential inseparability insofar as time cannot exist independently of the kinetic substrate it measures. Heidegger further clarifies that Aristotle's analysis reveals that "That which never possesses a possibility, what did not arise, is already there in a distinctive sense, completed such that it does not need time" (Heidegger, 2009, p. 299)[22], indicating that eternal beings such as the unmoved mover exist outside the motion-time nexus altogether, thereby defining time's proper domain negatively through what lacks potentiality and requires no temporal actualization. Moreover, Aristotle's categorization demonstrates that motion manifests across distinct ontological dimensions—"in quality, white and black; in quantity, complete and incomplete. Similarly, in respect of locomotion, upwards and downwards" (Aristotle, Physics III, p. 30)[3]—each requiring temporal extension for its actualization, which reinforces that time functions as the universal measure spanning all categories of change while remaining ontologically dependent upon the continuous motion that provides its measurable substrate.

These grounds demonstrate that Aristotle resolves the apparent circularity by establishing motion as ontologically prior to time while acknowledging that time makes motion intelligible as measured change, whereby the continuity shared by motion, magnitude, and time allows time to function as a universal measure applicable to all motions, while particular motions serve as the substrate that time measures (Aristotle, Physics IV, p. 10). Heidegger elucidates this relationship by noting that "time is not simply dependent on motion in the sense that it would be a property of motion," but rather "time is the measure of motion" that makes motion accessible as a determinate phenomenon (Heidegger, 2009, p. 217)[23]. The existence of eternal, unchanging beings outside time confirms that time belongs specifically to the realm of potentiality and actualization, distinguishing the temporal domain of kinetic transformation from the atemporal domain of pure actuality (Aristotle, Physics IV, p. 10). Nevertheless, the precise mechanism by which time achieves independence as a universal measure while remaining dependent on particular motions remains philosophically complex in Aristotle's account, particularly regarding how the soul's counting activity relates to time's objective continuity and how time's universality emerges from its grounding in specific measurable movements.

The cognitive architecture underlying Aristotle's temporal framework depends fundamentally on two interconnected capacities: aisthēsis (sense-perception), which provides immediate apprehension of motion and discrimination of successive 'nows,' and phantasia (imagination), which enables the retention and ordering of sensory content across temporal intervals, thereby making memory and temporal measurement possible. Aristotle's analysis of phantasia itself depends heavily on his preceding account of sensation in De Anima III.3, establishing that phantasia emerges from and presupposes the sensory apprehension of changing phenomena (Aristotle, De Anima III.3, p. 11). The question "what is Aristotle's rationale in passing from the subject of sensation to the subject of thought?" reveals that phantasia occupies an intermediary position between immediate sensory perception and intellectual cognition, serving as the bridge that allows temporal experience to be retained and reflected upon (Aristotle, De Anima, p. 5). This hierarchical relationship becomes evident when examining temporal discrimination: the soul's perceptual capacity allows it to perceive that "the 'nows' are two" without counting them, demonstrating that aisthēsis provides immediate temporal discrimination, while the ability to perceive that "the 'nows' are three" suggests phantasia's role in retaining and comparing temporal moments (Aristotle, 563a17-19, p. 6). Heidegger observes that Aristotle seeks to apprehend the phenomenon of movement in the thing moved, asking "Which sense of being is meant with the experience of a being-that-is-moved," indicating that perception itself is directed toward beings-in-motion rather than static objects (Heidegger, 2009, p. 242)[24]. This orientation toward kinetic phenomena establishes the foundation upon which phantasia can operate, transforming fleeting sensory impressions into retained temporal sequences that enable the soul's counting activity and the measurement of time itself.

These grounds establish that Aristotle's account of temporal experience requires both aisthēsis and phantasia working in concert: aisthēsis apprehends the immediate present and discriminates successive moments, while phantasia preserves these moments and their order, enabling the soul to measure time by counting 'nows' (Aristotle, De Anima III.3). Without phantasia's retentive capacity, temporal measurement would be impossible because each 'now' would vanish immediately upon being perceived, leaving nothing to count or compare—a consequence that would undermine the soul's ability to perform the numerical activity that Aristotle identifies as essential to temporal awareness. The interdependence of these faculties emerges from their complementary limitations: aisthēsis provides discriminative access to the present moment but lacks the capacity to retain what has passed, whereas phantasia, though dependent upon prior sensory impressions, possesses precisely this preservative function that transforms discrete perceptual episodes into an ordered sequence available for measurement. While Aristotle clearly assigns these functions to aisthēsis and phantasia throughout De Anima, the precise mechanisms by which phantasia preserves temporal order and enables the perception of temporal distance remain somewhat underdetermined in his texts, leaving interpreters to reconstruct the operative processes through which retained impressions maintain their sequential arrangement and permit comparative judgments about duration.

Heidegger's interpretation in *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* effects a radical reorientation of Aristotle's concepts of kinesis, chronos, and psyche toward phenomenological ontology, reading motion not as a property of beings but as a fundamental mode of being itself and emphasizing that Aristotle's analysis of time and perception reveals the existential structures of Dasein's temporality (Heidegger, 2009)[25]. This hermeneutic approach reveals the ontological dimensions of Aristotelian kinesis as a mode of being rather than merely a property of beings, fundamentally reinterpreting the *Physics* as an investigation into being-in-motion rather than physical mechanics (Heidegger, 2009, p. 116)[26]. Heidegger's phenomenological reading extends even to Aristotle's treatment of rest, which he insists must be understood ontologically: "Rest is a distinctive ἀκινησία. The assertion of rest only has meaning for a being that can move itself. This phenomenon has been neglected in the customary interpretation" (Heidegger, 2009, p. 228)[27], thereby revealing that even immobility constitutes a mode of being for entities capable of motion rather than a mere absence of movement. Furthermore, Heidegger argues that temporal experience in Aristotle involves existential engagement rather than detached theoretical observation, noting that "The present of such a being is not thought up, but is seen in the movement of the heavens, indeed seen; yet not simply in mere observation, but experienced in fear" (Heidegger, 2009, p. 215)[28], which situates astronomical time-reckoning within the affective structures of human existence. By deploying the basic Aristotelian concepts of dynamis, entelechia, and energeia to represent the movements of potency and actualization in life phenomena, Heidegger connects Aristotle's ontological categories to the structures of living, temporal existence, thereby transforming what might appear as abstract metaphysical principles into descriptions of the fundamental ways in which beings comport themselves toward their own possibilities (Heidegger, 2009)[29].

These interpretive maneuvers collectively demonstrate that Heidegger's reading transforms Aristotle's apparently physical and psychological analyses into phenomenological investigations of being and temporality, thereby establishing a philosophical lineage that legitimates Heidegger's own existential analytic while simultaneously reorienting the reception of Aristotelian thought (Heidegger, 2009)[30]. By reading kinesis as a mode of being rather than a property of entities, Heidegger connects Aristotle's Physics to his own existential analytic in Being and Time, where Dasein's being is fundamentally characterized by temporality and the capacity for motion understood existentially as possibility and projection rather than as spatial displacement (Heidegger, 2009, p. 228)[31]. The emphasis on existential engagement—exemplified in the claim that astronomical time is "experienced in fear" rather than merely observed—aligns Aristotle's analysis with Heidegger's critique of the theoretical attitude and his insistence on the primacy of primordial, pre-theoretical experience as the proper domain of phenomenological inquiry (Heidegger, 2009, p. 215)[32]. It must be acknowledged, nevertheless, that Heidegger's interpretation is explicitly hermeneutical and appropriative rather than strictly historical-philological; it reads Aristotle through the lens of fundamental ontology and may attribute insights to Aristotle that reflect Heidegger's own philosophical concerns as much as Aristotle's explicit intentions, rendering the interpretation simultaneously illuminating and potentially anachronistic (Heidegger, 2009, p. 116)[33].
