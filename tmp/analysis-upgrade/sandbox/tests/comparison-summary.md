# The Phantasia-Orexis-Kinesis Circuit: Aristotle on Imagination, Desire, and Animal Movement 

## 1. Aristotle, Phantasia, and Imagination (φαντασία) The investigation of animal movement in Aristotle's corpus requires, at its foundation, a careful elaboration of the faculty of phantasia—a term that, as Christina Papachristou observes, resists straightforward translation into the English "imagination" and is perhaps better left untranslated so as to preserve its distinctive Aristotelian resonance (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 9–10). Indeed, the very decision to render φαντασία as "imagination" risks importing modern connotations of creative fancy that are largely alien to Aristotle's account, which is rooted not in productive invention but in the residual movement generated by actual sense-perception; accordingly, we shall proceed with due caution, attending closely to the primary text and its internal distinctions. At the most fundamental level, Aristotle situates phantasia as a capacity that arises from and depends upon αἴσθησις, perception, yet is not reducible to it: phantasia is that faculty through which an image or φάντασμα persists and can be entertained even after the sensible object has withdrawn from the perceptual field. It is thus a movement resulting from actual sensation, one that enables the living being to sustain cognitive and motivational contact with its environment beyond the immediate moment of perceptual encounter (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 36). The distinction between sensitive phantasia and deliberative phantasia constitutes one of the most consequential internal differentiations within Aristotle's psychology. The calculative dimension of deliberative phantasia involves measuring competing ends against a single standard, thereby introducing the possibility of practical reasoning into the motivational circuit.
