# Motion, Time, and the Soul's Inner Life: Aristotle's Kinēsis, Chronos, Aisthēsis, and Phantasia in Light of Heidegger's Interpretation

## 1. Kinēsis: The Ontological Grammar of Motion

To articulate Aristotle's understanding of motion—kinēsis—is to enter into the very heart of his natural philosophy, for motion is not merely one phenomenon among others but rather the fundamental ontological condition through which beings disclose themselves in their becoming. Aristotle's inquiry into nature is, at its deepest stratum, an inquiry into motion and what is "cogiven" with it; as Heidegger notes in his reading of the *Physics*, the "basic topic of μέθοδος περὶ φύσεως" is "κίνησις and what is cogiven in it (ἕπεται, τὰ ἐφεξῆς)" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263)[1]. This formulation is significant because it establishes kinēsis not as an isolable event but as a structural whole that draws along with it a constellation of related concepts—place, void, time, continuity—each of which can be understood only in relation to the movement that conditions them. Thus, to speak of motion is already to speak of the broader framework within which time, perception, and the soul's discriminative capacities operate. Heidegger further clarifies the ontological register of this inquiry by observing that kinēsis is not something "παρὰ τὰ πράγματα," that is, not something alongside or apart from things, but rather a "how of the being of beings" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263)[2]. Motion, accordingly, is not a separate entity but a mode in which beings exist; it characterizes the way substances unfold from potentiality into actuality, from δυνάμις into ἐντελέχεια. 262–263). The Aristotelian account of motion thus refuses any reduction of kinēsis to mere local displacement. While locomotion is one species of kinēsis, the concept encompasses qualitative alteration, quantitative growth and diminution, and substantial generation and corruption. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle reinforces this expansive understanding by arguing that "if there is movement, and something moved, and everything is moved out of something and into something, it follows that that which is moved must first be in that out of which it is to be moved, and then not be in it, and move into the other and come to be in it" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 130–131)[3]. This passage is crucial because it discloses the logical structure of motion as a passage between contradictories—a being that is first in one state, then not in that state, and subsequently in another. Motion, on this account, is the actualization of what exists in potentiality precisely as potential; it is the coming-to-presence of that which has not yet arrived at its end. Kenneth Burke, working from a different tradition but with a shared attentiveness to the grammar of motives, provides a useful parallel when (Hawhee 2011)[18] complex motivational structures can be subsumed beneath summarizing terms or "God terms," such that "when we confront a simplicity we must forthwith ask ourselves what complexities are subsumed beneath it" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 126–127)[4]. Similarly, the term kinēsis functions in Aristotle's philosophy as a kind of architectonic simplicity beneath which the full complexity of natural process is gathered. The reduction of motion to any single species—say, locomotion—would be precisely the kind of false simplicity that Burke warns against. Indeed, the relational character of motion is further underscored by the Aristotelian observation that self-movers, such as animals, are not always in continuous motion by their own agency but that "it is something else that moves them, itself being in motion and changing as it comes into relation with each several thing that moves itself" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 122)[5]. Motion, then, is constitutively relational; the moved always stands in relation to a mover, and even the self-mover is internally differentiated into that which moves and that which is moved. 

## 2. Chronos: Time as the Number of Motion

If kinēsis is the fundamental phenomenon of natural being, then chronos—time—is its most intimate companion, for Aristotle defines time as the number of motion with respect to the before and after. This definition, deceptively simple in its formulation, conceals a rich nexus of ontological, phenomenological, and psychological considerations. Time, for Aristotle, does not exist independently of motion; nor does motion occur outside of time. The two are structurally co-dependent: kinēsis presupposes chronos insofar as every change unfolds across a temporal interval, and chronos depends on kinēsis insofar as time is unintelligible apart from some change against which it is measured. This reciprocal dependency is one of the most distinctive features of Aristotle's temporal ontology and one that separates him from later conceptions—both Newtonian absolute time and the purely subjective temporality of certain modern philosophies. The Aristotelian account insists that we perceive time only when we perceive motion, and specifically only when we mark out the before and after within a given change. Where no change is perceived, no passage of time is registered; thus, time is not a mere container in which events occur but is rather disclosed through the soul's act of counting or numbering the phases of movement. This entails that time has an essential reference to the soul as the numbering agent, a point that raises profound questions about the ontological status of time: does time exist independently of the perceiving soul, or is it constituted by the soul's discriminative act? Aristotle's answer appears to be nuanced; time is not simply a mental construct, for it is grounded in the real structure of motion, yet it requires a soul capable of numbering in order to be actualized as time. Heidegger's interpretation sheds light on this ambiguity by situating it within the broader Greek understanding of being as being-present. (Hawhee 2011)[19], "In this interpretation of beingthere, being means being-present, being-completed" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 200–201)[6]. Time, on this reading, is the horizon within which beings come to presence and are completed; it is the measure by which the movement from potentiality to actuality is articulated and rendered intelligible. Specifically, the "before and after" in motion provide the ontological basis for temporal succession, while the soul's act of distinguishing them constitutes the formal determination of time as numbered. The now—the nyn—serves as the boundary between past and future, analogous to the point on a line; yet unlike the static point, the now is always in motion, always passing away and being replaced. This dynamic character of the now underscores the deep entanglement of time and motion: the now is the same in its substrate (as the moving body is the same throughout its trajectory) but different in its being (as the body occupies different positions). The complexity of this analysis resists any reduction of time to a simple sequence of discrete instants; rather, time is continuous, as motion is continuous, and both derive their continuity from the continuity of magnitude. In the *Metaphysics*, Aristotle contends that those who speak of "the indeterminate" while "fancying themselves to be speaking of being" are in reality "speaking about non-being; for that which exists potentially and not actually is the indeterminate" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 42–43)[7]. 

## 3. The Relationship Between Motion and Time

The co-dependency of motion and time in Aristotle's framework is neither a trivial correlation nor a mere definitional convenience; it constitutes a deeply structural claim about the nature of the physical world and the conditions of its intelligibility. Motion, as the actualization of the potential qua potential, unfolds necessarily in time, and time, as the number of motion with respect to the before and after, is disclosed necessarily through motion. This mutual implication means that any attempt to isolate one from the other results in an abstraction that distorts the phenomenon. If we attempt to conceive of time apart from all motion, we find ourselves with an empty formalism—a pure succession of instants that lacks content. If we attempt to conceive of motion apart from time, we confront an equally unintelligible notion—a change that has no duration, no before and after, no measurable passage. Accordingly, Aristotle's account insists on the inseparability of the two. The relational character of this inseparability is further illuminated by Heidegger's emphasis on the category of πρός τι—the "in relation to"—as one of the basic modes of being that Aristotle identifies in the opening of *Physics* III. Heidegger draws attention to the way in which Aristotle's analysis of kinēsis involves the "advanced giving of the basic modes of being: ὄν δυνάμει and ἐντελεχείᾳ, ὄν of the categories, with it πρός τι, the 'in relation to . . .'" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263)[8]. The "in relation to" is essential to motion because motion is always the motion of something with respect to something; it is the relation between the potentiality and the actuality of a given substrate. Similarly, time is relational: it is the number of motion, which means it is always time of some motion, measured by some soul. The relational ontology that underpins both concepts prevents us from treating either motion or time as a self-subsisting entity; each exists only in and through its relation to the other and, ultimately, to the perceiving and numbering soul. Thomas Rickert's discussion of kairos provides a suggestive analogy for understanding this relational structure. Rickert observes that "kairos, I have argued, originally had a spatial dimension, though this early usage was gradually lost in favor of meanings such as 'opportunity' and 'decorum,' among others" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 108–109)[9]. The spatialization of kairos reminds us that temporality in the Greek philosophical tradition is never purely abstract but is always emplaced, always situated within a world of material relations. Aristotle's chronos, similarly, is not a transcendental form imposed upon experience but emerges from within the moving world itself, from the relational interplay of bodies, places, and the soul's discriminative activity. Indeed, Rickert's broader argument that dwelling indicates "not a worldview but a principle of being: culture is materially enworlded" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 239–240)[10] resonates with the Aristotelian insistence that time is disclosed through engagement with the moving world rather than through detached theoretical contemplation. Furthermore, the structural relationship between motion and time carries implications for the understanding of causality and explanation in natural philosophy. Aristotle's rejection of the Platonic Forms as efficient causes of becoming—his observation that "when the Forms exist, still the things that share in them do not come into being, unless there is some efficient cause" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, pp. 18–19)[11]—underscores the necessity of motion as the medium through which causes operate and effects are realized. Time, as the measure of this operation, provides the framework within which efficient causality is intelligible. Without kinēsis, there can be no bringing-forth of the new; without chronos, there can be no ordering of the successive stages of that bringing-forth. 

## 4. Aisthēsis and Phantasia Within Aristotle's Temporal Framework

We turn now to the faculties of aisthēsis (perception) and phantasia (imagination), and to the question of how they function within the temporal framework established by the relationship between kinēsis and chronos. Aisthēsis is the soul's capacity to receive the forms of sensible objects without their matter—a capacity that is itself a kind of being-acted-upon, a pathos, and hence a species of kinēsis. As Aristotle observes in the *De Anima*, "If thinking is like perceiving, it must be either a process in which the soul is acted upon by what is capable of being thought, or a process different from but analogous to that" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)[12]*, pp. 44–45). This analogy between thinking and perceiving establishes a structural parallel: both are processes—movements of the soul—in which the soul is brought from potentiality to actuality by the action of an external or quasi-external object. Perception, specifically, is the actualization of the sense organ by the sensible form, and this actualization occurs in time; the discriminating power and the time of its exercise must be one and undivided, for the soul cannot perceive two distinct qualities at two distinct moments and yet judge them as simultaneous without a unifying temporal awareness. Phantasia, by contrast, is a more complex and contested faculty. Christina Papachristou clarifies that for Aristotle, "phantasia (φαντασία) is a kind of motion (κίνησις) in the soul that cannot exist apart from sensation (αἴσθησις)—it is caused by the activity of sensation—and supposition (ὑπόληψις)" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 12–13)[13]. Several features of this characterization deserve close attention. First, phantasia is itself a kinēsis—a motion within the soul—which means that it participates in the same ontological structure of potentiality and actuality that governs physical motion. Second, phantasia cannot exist apart from aisthēsis; it presupposes prior perceptual experience, even though it can operate in the absence of the perceived object. Third, phantasia is linked to ὑπόληψις, a term that, as Papachristou notes via Philoponus, includes "knowledge, right thinking/practical wisdom and opinion" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 12–13)[14]. This connection to supposition, and especially to opinion (doxa), is significant because it positions phantasia at the intersection of perception and judgment, making it the faculty through which raw sensory data are rendered available for cognitive and deliberative processing. The internal distinctions within phantasia are important for understanding its role in Aristotle's temporal framework. Scholars have identified grades or kinds of phantasia in the *De Anima*: specifically, sensitive (or sensory) phantasia, which is shared by all animals and involves the residual motion left behind by an act of perception; and deliberative phantasia, which belongs to rational beings and involves the capacity to construct composite images for the purposes of practical reasoning. Sensitive phantasia is involuntary and directly dependent on the perceptual encounter; deliberative phantasia, by contrast, admits of a degree of voluntariness, as the rational agent can summon and combine phantasmata in the service of planning and choice. The relation of phantasia to belief (doxa) is also noteworthy: while phantasia and doxa are distinct—for phantasia can present what is false without the agent thereby believing it, whereas doxa involves assent—they are functionally interrelated, as phantasia supplies the representational content upon which belief and judgment operate. A more detailed treatment of these internal distinctions, including the question of how habituated phantasia bridges the gap between perception and practical reasoning, will be developed in a subsequent chapter devoted specifically to the grades of phantasia and their rhetorical implications. Victor Caston's work on Aristotle's imagination underscores the difficulty of situating phantasia within the broader architecture of the soul. Caston draws attention to the longstanding scholarly challenge posed by Aristotle's treatment of this faculty, noting the assessment by George Henry Lewes that in the *De Anima* one encounters "the want of masterly and logical arrangement of subject, and the want of the elementary requisites of good composition" (Caston, *Why Aristotle Needs Imagination*, pp. 2–3)[15]. While this judgment may be excessively harsh, it registers a genuine philosophical difficulty: phantasia resists easy categorization because it is neither pure sensation nor pure intellection but occupies an intermediate position that is essential to both. It is precisely this intermediary status that makes phantasia indispensable to Aristotle's temporal framework, for phantasia is the faculty through which the soul retains the traces of past perceptions and projects them into the context of present deliberation and future action. Without phantasia, the temporal continuity of experience—the capacity to hold together the before and after of motion in a unified awareness—would be impossible. 

## 5. Heidegger's Interpretation and Its Broader Implications

Heidegger's reading of Aristotle in the *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* provides a hermeneutic lens through which the foregoing analysis can be deepened and radicalized. For Heidegger, Aristotle's philosophy of nature is not merely a regional inquiry into the physical world but an investigation into the being of beings as such—an ontology in which kinēsis occupies the central position. 262–263). On this interpretation, the study of motion is not simply the study of bodies in space but the study of the way in which beings come to presence, maintain themselves, and pass away. Similarly, Heidegger's reading of being as "being-present, being-completed" discloses the temporal horizon within which Aristotle's ontology operates: to be, for Aristotle, is to have arrived at or to be on the way toward completeness, and this arrival is constitutively temporal (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 200–201). Heidegger  the concept of ἕξις—a having or disposition—characterizes the way in which Dasein relates to its possibilities. A resolving-oneself, he notes, "is always determined by the fact it that resolves itself in opposition to something. If science is something to which beingthere can resolve itself, ἕξις, then this ἕξις is characterized by its being: πῶς ἔχομεν πρὸς ἄλλων—πῶς, that which it is πρός, 'against,' 'in relation to' something, from which it extricates itself" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 200–201)[16]. This passage is relevant to our analysis of phantasia because phantasia, too, can be understood as a kind of ἕξις—a habituated disposition of the soul through which it relates to the world. The phantasma is not a static image stored in the mind but a dynamic orientation, a residual kinēsis that disposes the soul toward certain kinds of engagement with its environment. Heidegger's emphasis on the relational and dispositional character of being-there thus enriches the Aristotelian account by drawing out the existential dimensions of phantasia. Similarly, (Heidegger 2009)[20] "human beings are the sort of living thing that can be φύσει by way of the πόλις: this distinctive being-with-one-another is not something that is brought to human beings, but is rather the being-possibility" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. The πόλις is not an external structure imposed upon human nature but the fulfillment of human nature's own movement toward completion. Phantasia and aisthēsis, accordingly, are not merely private faculties but are always already shaped by the communal world within which the human being exists. Burke's observation that motivation must be understood in terms of complex relational structures rather than reductive simplicities—that "a simplicity of motive being a perfection or purity of motive, the paradox of the absolute would admonish us that it cannot prevail in the 'imperfect world' of everyday experience" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 126–127)[17]—resonates with this Heideggerian point. The soul's perceptual and imaginative faculties are complex precisely because they operate within a world of practical, social, and material relations that resist reduction to any single principle. The emergent picture, thus, is one in which motion, time, perception, and imagination form an integrated whole. Kinēsis is the ontological ground; chronos is its measure and disclosure; aisthēsis is the soul's receptive engagement with the moving world; and phantasia is the residual motion within the soul that allows it to retain, combine, and project the deliverances of perception across time. Each element depends upon and presupposes the others, and none can be adequately understood in isolation. This integrated account, enriched by Heidegger's existential interpretation, enables the user to see Aristotle's natural philosophy not as a collection of discrete doctrines but as a unified investigation into the conditions under which beings come to presence and are known. 

## 6. Conclusion: Toward an Integrated Account of Motion, Time, and Soul

We have traced the arc of Aristotle's understanding of motion, time, and the soul's discriminative faculties, showing how kinēsis serves as the ontological ground upon which the concepts of chronos, aisthēsis, and phantasia are erected. Motion, understood as the actualization of the potential qua potential, is not a peripheral topic in Aristotle's philosophy but its central concern—the phenomenon through which the structure of being is most fully disclosed. Time, as the number of motion with respect to the before and after, is co-dependent with motion and requires the soul as numbering agent for its actualization; this co-dependency ensures that temporality is never a mere abstraction but always an emplaced, world-engaged phenomenon. Aisthēsis, the soul's capacity to be acted upon by sensible forms, is itself a species of kinēsis and thus participates in the temporal structure of natural change. Phantasia, as a residual motion caused by perception but capable of operating independently of the perceptual object, occupies the crucial intermediate position that enables the soul to integrate past, present, and future into a unified field of experience and deliberation. Heidegger's interpretation, as we have shown, deepens this analysis by disclosing the existential and ontological dimensions that are implicit in Aristotle's text. By reading kinēsis as a "how of the being of beings" rather than a mere physical event, Heidegger transforms the Aristotelian account into a proto-phenomenology of being-in-the-world. His emphasis on ἕξις, πρός τι, and being-present illuminates the relational and dispositional character of the soul's engagement with its environment—an engagement that is always already temporal, always already in motion. Burke's grammar of motives provides an additional lens through which the complexity of this account can be appreciated, reminding us that the apparent simplicity of foundational concepts like kinēsis and chronos conceals a richness that resists reduction. Rickert's attention to the spatial and ambient dimensions of kairos further enriches the picture by suggesting that Greek temporality is never purely abstract but always materially emplaced. Papachristou's clarification of the grades of phantasia, and Caston's identification of the hermeneutic challenges posed by Aristotle's treatment of imagination, together establish the scholarly context within which the foregoing analysis operates. The task that remains—and that subsequent chapters will undertake—is to trace the implications of this integrated account for questions of rhetoric, persuasion, and practical reasoning, where the interplay of motion, time, perception, and imagination finds its most consequential expression. Indeed, it is in the domain of rhetorical practice that the user is enabled to see most clearly how the theoretical structures articulated here bear upon the concrete activities of interpretation, judgment, and action within the moving, temporal world. ---
