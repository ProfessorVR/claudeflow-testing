# Phantasia, Disclosedness, and the Structuring of Situated Environments

## 1. Phantasia, Aisthesis, Perception, and Imagination (φαντασία)

Aristotle's account of phantasia in the *De Anima* is grounded in the prior operation of sense-perception (aisthesis), yet it cannot be reduced to mere sensation; indeed, the image-making faculty occupies a distinctive intermediary position between perceiving and thinking that renders it indispensable for how environments are disclosed to a situated subject. As Hawhee observes, Aristotle notes that "phantasia is different from either perceiving or thinking, though it is not found without perception, nor judgment without it" (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 4–6)[1]. Thus phantasia contrasts with aisthesis precisely because it can operate in the absence of the perceived object, generating appearances that persist beyond immediate sensory contact; yet it remains parasitic upon perception as its originary ground. Aristotle underscores this genealogical dependence through an etymology that ties phantasia to light itself: "Phantasia is derived from phaos (light)," because, he observes, "without light it is impossible to see" (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 4–6)[2]. The luminous metaphor is not merely decorative but philosophically significant, for it suggests that phantasia, like light, is a condition of visibility — a disclosive medium through which objects become present to the soul even when sensory stimulation has ceased. Papachristou's careful taxonomy distinguishes between grades of phantasia that range from the sensory, which remains close to aisthesis, to the deliberative, which involves rational calculation and is available only to beings capable of logos; between these poles lies a middle grade concerned with memory, emotion, and habituation (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[3]. Specifically, the soul is described as the first actuality of a natural organic body, and the powers or faculties (δυνάμεις) of the soul — including perception, phantasia, and intellection — are arranged in an ascending order of complexity (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[4]. The voluntariness of phantasia at higher grades, its entanglement with doxa (belief), and its capacity to present the absent as present all mark it as a faculty whose rhetorical significance is considerable. Burke similarly recognizes that imagination cannot be severed from persuasion, since to recommend the dictates of reason to the imagination is precisely to excite the appetite and will (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[5]. Accordingly, phantasia is not a passive afterimage of perception but an active, disclosive capacity through which the subject's environment is organized, interpreted, and rendered persuasive. A fuller treatment of the deliberative grade and its relation to doxa follows in subsequent sections. 

## 2. Phantasma, Phantasia, Imagination, and Image (φαντασία / φάντασμα)

The phantasma — the concrete image or appearance that phantasia produces — constitutes the indispensable medium through which the soul engages with beings. Heidegger, in his reading of Aristotle, insists that thinking is anchored not in neurological processes but in phantasia as the making-present-to-itself of the world: "Thinking: this is not an appeal to a brain process, but to φαντασία, the 'making-present-to-itself' of the world" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 149)[6]. This formulation is decisive, for it recasts the phantasma not as a subjective mental picture isolated within a Cartesian interior but as the mode in which worldly significance is disclosed to the living being. Similarly, the Aristotelian thesis that the soul never thinks without a phantasma implies that every act of intellection requires imagistic mediation — a claim whose scope Papachristou helpfully clarifies by distinguishing the sensory phantasma, which retains the qualitative character of the original perception, from the deliberative phantasma, which is composed and recomposed through rational synthesis and thus participates in the formation of doxa (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[7]. Indeed, the phantasma functions as a bridge between aisthesis and noesis precisely because it carries perceptual content into contexts where the original object is absent; hence its significance for memory, desire, and practical deliberation. Heidegger further elaborates that even thorough deliberation — when one does not have a matter perceptually present — is something like a phantasia or cannot proceed without it, so that thinking cannot exist without standing in the context of the entire life of a human being (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 149)[8]. The phantasma thus anchors the soul's relation to beings across temporal horizons, allowing the past to be re-presented and the future to be anticipated. Burke extends this insight into the rhetorical domain, noting that imagination mediates between reason and will; indeed, will itself is altered by religion, opinion, and apprehension, and imagination could be brought to the reinforcement of all three (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[9]. The phantasma is accordingly not merely an epistemological convenience but a constitutive element of rhetorical persuasion: it is through images that the orator discloses alternative futures and renders them present to the audience's deliberative faculty. The voluntariness of phantasia at its deliberative grade means that the production of phantasmata is subject to rational guidance, a point we shall develop further in the sections on doxa and desire. 

## 3. Phantasia, Mneme, Imagination, and Memory (φαντασία)

Memory (mneme) is intimately bound to phantasia because recollection depends upon the re-activation of phantasmata that were originally generated in the wake of perception. Heidegger's reading clarifies that phantasia as a making-present-to-itself of the world encompasses temporal modalities in which what has been perceived can be brought back before the soul; accordingly, even when one does not have a matter perceptually present, deliberative thought relies upon phantasia (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 149)[10]. The mnemonic phantasma is not a mere copy of the original sensory event but a transformed representation shaped by the subject's affective disposition and habitual orientation. Papachristou's taxonomy locates this mnemonic function within a middle grade of phantasia that mediates between the purely sensory and the fully deliberative, thus connecting bodily experience with rational reflection (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[11]. Burke's rhetorical framework illuminates the persuasive dimension of this mnemonic phantasia, since imagination serves to recommend the dictates of reason by making past experiences vivid and emotionally compelling; memory thus becomes a storehouse of persuasive material that the orator — or indeed the deliberating agent — can draw upon to orient present action (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[12]. Subsequently, Aristotle's own discussions of recollection as a kind of searching through places (topoi) suggest a spatial and environmental dimension to memory: one recalls by retracing a path through familiar associative landscapes, so that the environment itself functions as a mnemonic structure. Heidegger's emphasis on the life-context within which thinking occurs reinforces this point; the soul's phantasia does not operate in abstraction but within the full being-there of a situated human existence (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 149)[13]. Thus the relation between phantasia and mneme reveals that environments are not merely perceived but remembered, and that the remembered environment shapes the disclosure of present circumstances. The question of whether phantasia's relation to doxa — specifically, the capacity for false memory or distorted recollection — introduces a voluntary or involuntary dimension will be addressed more fully in the following section. 

## 4. Phantasia, Doxa, and Imagination (φαντασία)

The relationship between phantasia and doxa (opinion or belief) is among the most contested issues in Aristotle's psychology, for it bears directly upon the question of whether appearances can be true or false and whether they are subject to rational control. Aristotle insists in *De Anima* III.3 that phantasia is not identical with doxa, since animals possess phantasia but lack belief, and since one may have a phantasma that contradicts one's considered judgment — as when the sun appears small despite one's knowledge of its actual size (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[14]. Yet phantasia and doxa are not wholly independent, for at the deliberative grade, phantasia involves the composition and synthesis of images in light of beliefs about what is good or expedient; hence the deliberative phantasma participates in the formation of doxa even as it remains distinct from it. Heidegger captures the rhetorical significance of this relationship by noting that rhetoric aims at the construction of a view — a δόξα to be cultivated — and that the speaker must bring those with whom the decision lies into a corresponding frame of mind through discourse itself (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 124)[15]. Specifically, Heidegger writes: "since, ultimately, rhetoric has its sight on κρίσις, view-construction, a definite decision in the sense of a δόξα, not only must attention be necessarily directed to discourse as such, to the function of discourse as δεικνύναι, δηλοῦν, but the speaker must bring himself, and those with whom the decision will lie, into a corresponding frame of mind" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 124)[16]. This passage reveals that doxa is not a detached intellectual state but an affectively inflected orientation that phantasia discloses; the frame of mind in question is itself an environmental attunement that conditions what appears true or persuasive. Burke similarly observes that will is altered by opinion, religion, and apprehension, and that imagination reinforces all three (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[17]. Nussbaum further clarifies that all agents strive for the apparent good (phainomenon agathon) and that no one is in control of the appearing (phantasia), since the way the end appears to someone depends on what sort of person one is (Nussbaum, *The Role of Phantasia in Aristotle's Explanation of Action*, pp. 31–33)[18]. Accordingly, the interplay between phantasia and doxa is constitutive of the rhetorical situation, structuring the environment within which the subject encounters possibilities for action. 

## 5. Phantasia, Pathos, Imagination, and Emotion (φαντασία / πάθος / πάθη)

Phantasia's entanglement with the pathē (emotions or affections) is a crucial dimension of Aristotle's account, for it is through imagistic mediation that affective states are aroused, sustained, and transformed. Heidegger locates ēthos and pathos among the pisteis (means of persuasion) that constitute the rhetorical situation, observing that attention must be directed not only to discourse as such but to the affective dispositions of both speaker and audience (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 123)[19]. The pathē are not merely subjective reactions but modes of disclosure through which the environment is colored and structured; fear, for instance, discloses the threatening, while confidence discloses the navigable. Phantasia mediates these affective disclosures by generating images of the fearful or the desirable, images that are not neutral representations but emotionally charged appearances. Papachristou's middle grade of phantasia — situated between the sensory and the deliberative — is precisely the locus of this affective mediation, for it is at this grade that perception is suffused with pathetic significance (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[20]. Burke's rhetorical analysis extends this insight by noting that imagination recommends the dictates of reason in order to excite the appetite and will; the pathē are thus not obstacles to rational deliberation but channels through which phantasia orients the agent toward action (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[21]. Subsequently, Rickert's concept of attunement offers a complementary framework, suggesting that the ambient order from which knowledge emerges operates at a level that eludes conscious intention yet constitutes the conditions of discourse (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 321–322)[22]. This resonance between Aristotelian pathos and Heideggerian Stimmung — itself recast in Rickert's ambient rhetoric — reveals that emotional phantasia is not a private psychological event but a situated disclosure of the world's significance. The voluntariness of deliberative phantasia means that images can be deliberately constructed to arouse specific pathē; yet the sensory grade of phantasia operates involuntarily, so that emotional responses to sudden appearances precede and often override deliberate judgment. The tension between voluntary and involuntary dimensions of affective phantasia is thus constitutive of rhetorical practice. 

## 6. Phantasia, Taking-As, and Imagination (φαντασία)

The notion of "taking-as" — perceiving something as something — is central to both Aristotle's and Heidegger's accounts of how environments are disclosed to situated subjects. Heidegger's analysis of Aristotelian φαντασία reveals that the making-present-to-itself of the world is never a bare registration of data but always involves a hermeneutic dimension: the world is disclosed as significant, as organized into regions of relevance and meaning (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 149)[23]. Thus we do not first encounter brute sensory material and then overlay an interpretive structure upon it; rather, phantasia's disclosive work is already structured by the subject's concerns, habits, and affective dispositions. Heidegger's treatment of the ready-to-hand in *Being and Time* extends this point: we never perceive something purely present-at-hand and then take it as a door or a house; equipment is always already encountered within a referential totality (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 84–85)[24]. Specifically, the priority of Being-in-the-world as concern (Sorge)[25] means that the subject's environment is disclosed not as a collection of neutral objects but as a field of practical possibilities (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 84–85)[26]. Aristotle's account of phantasia complements this insight, for the phantasma presents the perceived object not as bare matter but as bearing significance for action — as food, as threat, as shelter. Papachristou's distinction between sensory and deliberative phantasia is relevant here, for the sensory grade already involves a minimal taking-as — the perception of an object's qualitative features — while the deliberative grade involves an explicit taking-as in which the agent assesses the object's relevance for a projected end (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[27]. Burke's recognition that imagination mediates between reason and will likewise implies a taking-as structure, since to imagine something as desirable or fearful is already to interpret it within a rhetorical framework oriented toward action (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[28]. The voluntary character of deliberative phantasia thus enables the rhetor to guide the audience's taking-as, constructing images that present a situation as demanding specific responses. 

## 7. Orexis, Phantasia, Imagination, and Desire (φαντασία / ὄρεξις)

Desire (orexis) and phantasia are co-constitutive in Aristotle's explanation of animal and human action, for the soul is moved to act only when an object is presented as desirable through an imagistic appearance. Hawhee's analysis of *Rhetoric* I.11 illuminates this nexus by detailing phantasia's relationship to suasion and desire, noting that people desire to see and possess many things after hearing about them; specifically, they long for things on the basis of persuasion, which indicates that phantasia operates as a weak perception that can nonetheless motivate pursuit (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 7–9)[29]. Nussbaum's account reinforces this point: "All men strive for the apparent good (phainomenon agathon): but no one is in control of the appearing (phantasia): the way the end appears (phainetai) to someone depends on what sort of a man he is" (Nussbaum, *The Role of Phantasia in Aristotle's Explanation of Action*, pp. 31–33)[30]. The deliberative grade of phantasia is accordingly essential for rational desire (boulēsis), since the agent must compose an image of a future good in order to deliberate about the means of attaining it; at the sensory grade, by contrast, desire responds to immediately perceived or imagined objects without rational mediation (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[31]. Burke's analysis of rhetoric as oriented toward the alteration of will through imagination, opinion, and apprehension further confirms that orexis is rhetorically structured: the orator's task is to generate phantasmata that present a particular course of action as desirable (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 93–96)[32]. Heidegger's account of the will as a striving that grasps the situation in the fullness of its time, in which the kairos calls for resoluteness and action, finds a parallel in Aristotle's integration of orexis with deliberative phantasia (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 156–157). Hence the environment is disclosed not as a static scene but as a field of desirable and avoidable possibilities, structured through the interplay of phantasia and orexis. 

## 8. Habituated Phantasia and Imagination (φαντασία)

Habituation shapes phantasia in ways that are decisive for how environments are disclosed, for the phantasmata one generates are conditioned by prior experience, training, and bodily practice. Hawhee's emphasis on the embodied character of Aristotelian perception suggests that the eyes themselves are trained through repeated exposure, so that the rhetorician who has cultivated a capacity for "looking into" situations perceives differently than the untrained observer (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 4–6)[33]. Nussbaum similarly stresses that the way the end appears to someone depends on what sort of person one is, a claim that directly ties the content of phantasia to the agent's habituated character (Nussbaum, *The Role of Phantasia in Aristotle's Explanation of Action*, pp. 31–33)[34]. Thus the habituated subject does not merely perceive the same environment differently but inhabits a different disclosed world — one in which certain features are salient, certain possibilities are apparent, and certain affective tones predominate. Papachristou's middle grade of phantasia — which includes affective and mnemonic dimensions — is the primary site of habituated phantasia, for it is at this grade that repeated experiences consolidate into stable patterns of imagistic response (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 1–3)[35]. The deliberative grade is also subject to habituation, since the capacity for rational image-construction improves with practice and education; the relation to doxa is likewise shaped by habituation, as beliefs about what is good or true are reinforced through repeated imagistic confirmation. Burke's treatment of the hierarchic motive — the principles of authority, reverence, fear, love, and desire that pervade social life — implies that habituated phantasia is deeply social, shaped by the rhetorical environment in which one is formed (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, p. 315)[36]. Accordingly, the concept of habituated phantasia bridges individual psychology and social rhetoric, revealing that the disclosure of environments is never a private achievement but a communally shaped attunement. 

## 9. Aristotle, Phantasia, and Imagination (φαντασία)

Aristotle's treatment of phantasia across the *De Anima*, the *Rhetoric*, and the natural philosophical works reveals a faculty that is irreducible to any single psychological function; it is at once perceptual, affective, mnemonic, deliberative, and kinetic. Papachristou's taxonomy of three grades — sensory, middle, and deliberative — captures this complexity by showing that phantasia operates at multiple levels of the soul's organization, from the involuntary persistence of sensory images to the voluntary construction of composite images for practical reasoning (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 9–10)[37]. The sensory grade remains closest to aisthesis; the middle grade introduces affective and mnemonic mediation; the deliberative grade enables rational projection and the assessment of future possibilities in light of doxa. Aristotle's insistence that phantasia is derived from phaos (light) underscores its disclosive function: phantasia illuminates the world for the soul, making beings available for engagement (Hawhee, *Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes*, pp. 4–6)[38]. In the *Physics*, Aristotle's analysis of motion as belonging to quality, quantity, and place — where each involves a pair of contraries — provides an ontological backdrop against which phantasia's disclosive work can be understood; for the soul's capacity to make present what is absent, to hold together contraries in a single imagistic synthesis, mirrors the structure of kinēsis itself (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 70)[39]. Similarly, the *Metaphysics* clarifies that the elements of substances — not the elements of all things indiscriminately — are the proper objects of inquiry, and that the reason in our soul stands to the most evident things of nature as the eyes of bats stand to the blaze of day (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 20)[40]. This analogy between intellectual and perceptual limitation reinforces the claim that phantasia mediates between the soul's finite capacities and the inexhaustible richness of being. Hence phantasia, far from being a marginal psychological curiosity, is the faculty through which environments are disclosed, structured, and rendered available for the situated subject's engagement. 

## 10. Aristotle and Disclosedness

The Aristotelian framework of phantasia finds its deepest resonance with Heidegger's concept of disclosedness (Erschlossenheit)[41] — the clearing in which beings show themselves and Dasein encounters itself. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle in *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* explicitly connects phantasia to the soul's being-in-the-world: thinking is rooted not in neurological processes but in the making-present-to-itself of the world through phantasia (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 149)[42]. The Aristotelian thesis that the soul is in a way all existing things (De Anima III.8, 432a1–3) is a locus Heidegger repeatedly engages, for it suggests that the soul's openness to beings constitutes a kind of primordial disclosure — an anticipation of what Heidegger will formalize as the existential structure of Being-in-the-world (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, pp. 25–27)[43]. Heidegger's *Being and Time* further specifies that the priority belongs to Being-in-the-world as concern, so that the ontological structure of life must be defined in terms of Dasein's engaged, caring relation to its environment (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 84–85)[44]. Uexküll's concept of the Umwelt — the closed perceptual-active world of a subject, composed of perception marks (Merkmal)[45] and effect marks (Wirkmal)[46] — offers a biological analogue to this philosophical structure of disclosedness; the organism encounters not a neutral objective world but a selectively organized environment structured by its species-specific capacities (von Uexkull, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans*, pp. 37–40). Similarly, Aristotle's *Physics* observes that it does not matter whether we take attunement, order, or composition for our illustration, since the principle is the same in all cases (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 8–10)[47]. Rickert's ambient rhetoric extends this insight by identifying an underlying order from which knowledge emerges — a level that eludes consciousness yet constitutes the conditions of discourse (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, pp. 321–322)[48]. Accordingly, the convergence of these traditions illuminates the fundamental question of how environments are disclosed and structured for the situated, embodied, rhetorically attuned subject. ---
