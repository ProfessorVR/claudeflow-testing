\subsection{$M_3 \rightarrow A_4$ and $A_4$: The Three Types of Action}

The actualization chain terminates at $A_4$, the completed action (\gk{πρᾶξις} \textit{praxis}), reached through the \textit{orektikon} transition $M_3 \rightarrow A_4$ in which desire (\textit{orexis} (\gk{ὄρεξις} ``desire'')) becomes overt motion of the bodily instrument. Across the three types of action Aristotle catalogs in \textit{Movement of Animals}, the kinetic-roles spine of \textit{De Anima} III.10, 433a31-b30 holds invariant: \textit{orexis} is the moved mover, characterized as ``that which at once moves and is moved'' (433b17-18); the realizable good is the unmoved originator that draws the desiring soul without being drawn in turn (433b10-18); and the animal via the bodily instrument---the ball-and-socket joint where ``pushing and pulling'' execute the action (433b21-25)---is that which is moved. The schema is preserved across all three types of action because it specifies the fundamental kinetic structure of any \textit{orectic} actualization, not the contingent character of any particular such actualization. What varies across action types is not the schema but the internal complexity of \textit{orexis}: in simple appetition, \textit{orexis} is generic appetite responding to the pleasant or painful object without further specification; in \textit{habitual-procedural action}, it is rationally articulated by settled \textit{doxa} whose content is craft-procedural and non-evaluative, without emotional concretion; in evaluatively complex action---which encompasses both spontaneous evaluative response and virtuously habituated comportment toward the matter at issue---it is the emotion-desire composite in which the cognitive evaluation, conative orientation, hedonic tonality, and physiological alteration are mutually constitutive aspects of one and the same \textit{pathos} (cf. \S 1.8).

Aristotle locates the somatic preparation of the organic parts internal to this transition rather than as a discrete subsequent stage: ``the organic parts are suitably prepared by the affections, these again by desire, and desire by imagination'' (\textit{MA} 702a17-19; cf. \S 1.5). The heating, cooling, trembling, and muscular tension that configure the body for the specific locomotor act are functionally distinguishable moments within $M_3 \rightarrow A_4$, not separate macro-nodes requiring architectural insertion. The chain's $A_0 \rightarrow A_4$ macro-skeleton is accordingly preserved: the \textit{MA}'s ``affections'' moment names a moment within the \textit{orektikon} transition, not a fifth node added to the four. The temporal duration of the transition itself varies with the complexity of the action and the resistance of the material conditions, but Aristotle's speed-claim establishes a structural ceiling: ``thinking that one ought to go and going are virtually simultaneous, provided that there is nothing to compel or to prevent'' (\textit{MA} 702a15-17). Under normal conditions the entire transition fires with a rapidity that compresses the preparation of the organic parts into a temporal interval that is functionally instantaneous, rendered possible by ``the natural correspondence of the active and passive'' (702a21)---each level of the chain being already disposed, in its first actuality, to receive the impulse transmitted from the level above.

The complete treatment of the three types requires a sharpened account of \textit{hexis} (\gk{ἕξις} ``settled disposition''). Heidegger's recovery of \textit{hexis} in GA 18, §17 is indispensable here: \textit{hexis} is not ``habit'' in the moralizing sense but a \textit{fundamental concept of being}---``the determination of the genuineness of being-there in a moment of being-composed as to something'' (GA 18, p. 119). It is, on Aristotle's own terms in \textit{Metaphysics} $\Delta$ 20 (1022b 4), the \gk{ἐνέργεια} (energeia, ``being-at-work'') of having---the genuine being-present of having as such, not a stored possession awaiting occasional use. Heidegger draws from this a structurally decisive distinction. In the case of \gk{τέχνη} (technē, ``craft''), where the \gk{ἔργον} (ergon, ``work,'' ``product'') is decisive, ``training has the precise sense of reducing deliberation insofar as it is through training that the completedness of attaining a result comes about'' (GA 18, p. 127); cultivated \textit{hexis} fires as routine. In the case of \gk{πρᾶξις} (praxis, ``action'') and \gk{ἀρετή} (aretē, ``virtue,'' ``excellence''), by contrast, ``the how is only appropriated in such a way that the human being enables himself \textit{to be composed at each moment}; not routine but holding-oneself-open'' (GA 18, p. 128); ``repetition does not mean the bringing-into-play of a settled completedness, but rather \textit{acting anew in every moment on the basis of the corresponding resolution}'' (GA 18, p. 128). The two modes of \textit{hexis} differ structurally: technē-\textit{hexis} reduces deliberation toward routine; praxis-\textit{hexis} cultivates the capacity for fresh resolution in the \gk{καιρός} (kairos, ``the right moment''). The three-types distinction this section develops tracks the technē/praxis distinction: simple appetition does not engage \textit{hexis} in either mode; habitual-procedural action operates through technē-\textit{hexis}; evaluatively complex action---when the agent has cultivated the relevant \textit{aretē}---operates through praxis-\textit{hexis}. Section \S 1.6 distinguished the variable presence of \textit{pathē} across action types and explicitly deferred the complete treatment to the chapter's final section; what follows delivers that treatment by working through the three types Aristotle catalogs.

In simple appetition, the chain runs $A_0 \rightarrow A_1 \rightarrow$ \textit{orexis} $\rightarrow$ action without engaging \textit{doxa} or the \textit{pathē}. Aristotle's compressed paradigm is the drinking case of \textit{Movement of Animals}:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
And so what we do without reflection, we do quickly. For when a man is actually using perception or imagination or thought in relation to that for the sake of which, what he desires he does at once. For the actualizing of desire is a substitute for inquiry or thinking. I want to drink, says appetite; this is drink, says sense or imagination or thought: straightaway I drink. In this way living creatures are impelled to move and to act, and desire is the last cause of movement, and desire arises through perception or through imagination and thought. (\textit{MA} 701a29-b1)
\end{adjustwidth}
\textit{Phantasia} (\gk{φαντασία} ``imagination'') is operative throughout the simple appetitive chain---the \textit{phantasma} (\gk{φάντασμα}) identifies the present object as drink---and basic \textit{resonant epithymia} is operative as the perception's pursue-able charge. What is absent is \textit{doxa}'s engagement: either no \textit{doxa} forms at all, or the \textit{doxa} that forms (``this is drink'') is too thin to engage the evaluative categories of the \textit{Rhetoric}. The action is, so to speak, emotionally transparent---it passes through the system without engaging the evaluative structures that produce fear, anger, or pity. Three observations sharpen the structural significance. First, Aristotle says explicitly that ``the actualizing of desire is a substitute for inquiry or thinking''---the \textit{orectic} actualization positively occupies the structural slot inquiry would otherwise fill, rather than leaving the slot empty. The position-taking moment is satisfied; what is bypassed is its propositional articulation. Second, the disjunction ``sense \textit{or} imagination \textit{or} thought'' explicitly includes perception alone as sufficient for the recognition that triggers desire-actualization, \textit{for the rational human as much as for the brute}; nothing in the passage restricts the appetitive route to non-rational animals. Third, ``what we do without reflection, we do quickly'' frames this unreflective swiftness as \textit{normal} for rational-animal appetitive action, not as an aberration from a \textit{doxa}-mediated default. The position-taking moment remains throughout (perception is \textit{kritikon}, basic valence is a \textit{phasis}-like stance; cf. \S 1.2), but the doxastic articulation does not concretize into determinate \textit{pathē}. The kinetic roles for Type 1 follow the spine: the unmoved originator is the realizable pleasant object as apprehended through the \textit{phantasma}; the moved mover is \textit{orexis} in its basic-appetitive form, responding directly to the perceived pursue-able; the animal-via-bodily-instrument is moved straightaway, the entire transition firing with the rapidity that the somatic correspondence between the active and the passive (\textit{MA} 702a21) makes possible.

In habitual-procedural action---the technē-mode of \textit{hexis}-grounded action---\textit{doxa} is fully operative, but the \textit{doxa}'s content is craft-procedural rather than evaluatively complex in the \textit{Rhetoric}'s sense, and so no \textit{pathē} concretize in the middle of the chain. Aristotle's paradigm is the practical syllogism:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
But how is it that thought is sometimes followed by action, sometimes not; sometimes by movement, sometimes not? What happens seems parallel to the case of thinking and inferring about the immovable objects. There the end is the truth seen (for, when one thinks the two propositions, one thinks and puts together the conclusion), but here the two propositions result in a conclusion which is an action---for example, whenever one thinks that every man ought to walk, and that one is a man oneself, straightaway one walks; or that, in this case, no man should walk, one is a man: straightaway one remains at rest. And one so acts in the two cases provided that there is nothing to compel or to prevent. (\textit{MA} 7, 701a7-16)
\end{adjustwidth}
The chain runs basic \textit{pathos} $\rightarrow$ \textit{doxa} $\rightarrow$ \textit{orexis} $\rightarrow$ action, without concretion. The ``good'' premise is a settled \textit{doxa} (``every man ought to walk'') rather than an appetitive want; the ``possible'' premise is a perceptual self-recognition (``I am a man''); the conclusion is the action itself. \textit{Doxa} is fully present at every step; what is absent is evaluatively-complex \textit{doxa}-content. No slight, no threat, no undeserved suffering, no public falling-short; the syllogism's terms do not engage the categories that produce anger, fear, pity, or shame. The agent who has internalized ``walking is good for the body'' and encounters the perceptual situation that this is a walking-opportunity straightaway walks: the chain runs basic \textit{pathos} (the body-as-feeling-confined, the morning-as-pleasant) $\rightarrow$ \textit{doxa} (``I should walk'') $\rightarrow$ \textit{orexis} (toward walking) $\rightarrow$ action, without anger or fear or pity arising. The expert pianist performing a well-rehearsed sonata makes the case sharper still. The pianist has internalized many settled \textit{doxai}: ``this passage takes the soft pedal,'' ``voice the inner line under the melody,'' ``release the chord before the next downbeat.'' Each \textit{doxa} fires almost automatically as the keyboard's unfolding demands present themselves perceptually; \textit{doxa} is fully engaged at every step; the pianist plays not from raw appetite but from rationally articulated craft-knowledge. And yet---barring a memory slip or a broken string---no \textit{pathē} arise. There is no anger at the arpeggio, no fear of the trill, no shame before the cadence.

What makes the practical syllogism fire ``straightaway'' (\textit{MA} 7, 701a13, 701a15) without fresh deliberation is that the universal premise functions as a technē-\textit{hexis}---a settled disposition that fires routinely. \textit{Hexis} here is not merely a held proposition; it is the settled disposition through which the \textit{doxa} is operative without conscious re-derivation. Without it, every walking-for-health decision would require fresh deliberation: the universal premise would have to be re-derived each time, the perceptual self-recognition rejoined to it, the conclusion recomputed. The structural condition for ``what we do without reflection'' is not the absence of \textit{doxa} but the presence of \textit{hexis}-grounded \textit{doxa}. The pianist case sharpens this: the craft-\textit{hexeis} are the cumulative product of thousands of past actualizations, sedimented into a standing rational capacity that fires through \textit{doxa} with sub-deliberative speed. The \textit{doxa} is fully present (the pianist knows the passage takes the soft pedal); the \textit{hexis} is what makes the \textit{doxa} operative without conscious re-deliberation. Heidegger's gloss on this mode is sharp: in technē-cultivation, ``the completedness of attaining a result comes about'' through training (GA 18, p. 127); the grammatikos who has internalized the prescription writes ``not by chance, but according to a prescription...without outside help...from out of himself'' (NE B 4, 1105a 22; GA 18, p. 127), but precisely because the prescription has been ``put further and further out of play'' through repetition. The kinetic roles for Type 2 follow the spine: the unmoved originator is the realizable good as it is articulated by the settled \textit{doxa} (health, musical excellence); the moved mover is \textit{orexis} shaped by the technē-\textit{hexis}-grounded \textit{doxa}; the animal-via-bodily-instrument is moved with the rapidity that the somatic correspondence (\textit{MA} 702a21) makes possible. The structural finding consolidates the variable-presence thesis: \textit{doxa} is necessary but not sufficient for \textit{pathē}; the gate is content-conditional, not \textit{doxa}-presence-conditional. \textit{Doxa} with non-evaluative content (Type 2) does not produce \textit{pathē}; \textit{doxa} with evaluative content (Type 3) does---and this holds whether the Type 3 chain is spontaneous or virtuously habituated, since virtuous habituation operates as praxis-\textit{hexis} rather than technē-\textit{hexis}, and praxis-\textit{hexis} does not reduce deliberation toward routine but cultivates the capacity for fresh resolution in the \textit{kairos}.

In evaluatively complex action---which encompasses both the spontaneous case in which an agent without cultivated virtue confronts an evaluatively determinate situation and the virtuously habituated case in which the agent has cultivated the relevant \textit{aretē}---\textit{doxa}'s content engages the evaluative categories of the \textit{Rhetoric}---slight, threat, undeserved suffering, public falling-short---and the \textit{pathē} concretize. The chain runs basic \textit{pathos} $\rightarrow$ \textit{doxa} $\rightarrow$ \textit{pathē} $\rightarrow$ action, with the somatic preparation internal to $M_3 \rightarrow A_4$ taking the specific form the emotion calls for: heated blood for anger that prepares the body for aggressive action, cooled blood for fear that prepares it for flight, sympathetic pain for pity that prepares it for relief. Emotion, as \S 1.8 established, is not a separate antecedent stage but \textit{is} the form desire takes when \textit{doxa} has disclosed evaluatively complex content. Anger \textit{is} the desire to retaliate insofar as it is structured by the perception of an unjustified slight and experienced in the somatic alteration of heated blood; fear \textit{is} the desire to flee insofar as it is structured by the cognitive evaluation of an anticipated destructive evil and experienced in the somatic alteration of chilled blood. Aristotle indicates that the affective force of \textit{doxa}-mediated desire is both immediate (\gk{εὐθύς}, \textit{DA} III.3, 427b23) and involuntary: ``it is not in our power to form opinions as we will'' (427b20), and the soul that believes something dangerous is already mobilized toward avoidance before deliberation can intervene, precisely because \textit{doxa}, unlike \textit{phantasia}, is not subject to the will. The involuntariness is not a defect of evaluative complex action but its structural signature: the agent who \textit{takes the object to be} fearful does not first decide to fear and then flee; the fear arises with the \textit{doxa}, and the desire to flee arises as the form the fear takes in the \textit{orektikon} transition. The chain runs through \textit{doxa} into \textit{pathē} into action with the same continuity that the simpler chains exhibit, but the \textit{orexis} that emerges into bodily motion is no longer generic appetite---it is the determinate desire structured by the cognitive-conative-hedonic-physiological composite the \textit{pathos} names. The kinetic roles for Type 3 follow the spine: the unmoved originator is the realizable good as articulated under the evaluative category (the just retaliation, the saved life, the relief of undeserved suffering); the moved mover is the emotion-desire composite (anger-as-desire-to-retaliate, fear-as-desire-to-flee, pity-as-desire-to-relieve); the animal-via-bodily-instrument is moved with the somatic preparation specific to the emotion's eidetic structure.

In Type 3, \textit{hexeis} (\gk{ἕξεις}) operate in a register distinct from their Type 2 role. Where Type 2 \textit{hexis} is technē-\textit{hexis}---settled \textit{doxa} furnishing the universal premise of the practical syllogism, fired routinely---Type 3 \textit{hexeis} are praxis-\textit{hexeis}: sedimented dispositions of emotional comportment, the diachronic residue of repeated \textit{pathos}-actualizations (cf. \S 1.7), cultivated as the standing capacity for rightly-disposed comportment toward the \textit{pathē} the situation demands. Heidegger states the structural relation directly: ``\textit{Hexis} is nothing other than a how of \gk{πάθος} (pathos), being-out-of-composure, in relation to being-composed-as-to'' (GA 18, p. 125). The Aristotelian texts confirm the bivalent character. \textit{Hexis} qua \gk{δύναμις} (dynamis, ``capacity'') is both active power and passive power (\textit{Met.} $\Delta$ 12, 1019a 15 ff.); the same settled capacity exercised as \gk{πρᾶξις} (praxis) is what is struck or displaced in \gk{πάθος} (pathos). \textit{Hexis} and \textit{pathos} are accordingly co-original structures of the same human being-in-the-world, not two separate phenomena later joined. Courage is the praxis-\textit{hexis} of fear; magnanimity the praxis-\textit{hexis} of anger; modesty the praxis-\textit{hexis} of shame; \textit{aretē} more generally is the cultivated \textit{hexis} that fits the \textit{kairos}, the right disposition before the right matter at the right moment, defined as Aristotle has it by ``the \gk{φρόνιμος} (phronimos) as the prudent one would define it'' (\textit{NE} B 6, 1106b 36). The praxis-\textit{hexis} structure of virtuous action explains why courage is not the absence of fear but the cultivated comportment-toward-fear: the brave person fears the genuinely fearsome (\textit{NE} III.7, 1115b 7-13), and what \textit{aretē} cultivates is the \textit{how} of the fearing---fearing rightly, in the right manner, at the right moment, before the right matter. These \textit{hexeis} modulate Type 3 chains in two distinct ways. First, they modulate the \textit{input} to \textit{doxa}: the courageous person's basic valence is differently shaped than the cowardly person's, because prior actualizations have biased the perceptual-affective field; the same threatening situation does not generate the same basic-valence input across the two agents. The cowardly person sees enemies in shadows where the courageous person sees only shadows; the basic-valence channel has already been re-saturated by prior fear-actualizations, and the perceptual encounter is conducted under the resulting \textit{Stimmung}-shading (cf. \S 1.7's diachronic analysis). Second, \textit{hexeis} modulate the \textit{gating} of \textit{doxa} into \textit{pathē}: the courageous person's \textit{doxa}-gate produces a regulated, action-prepared fear rather than a paralyzed fear; the irascible person's \textit{doxa}-gate fires anger more readily on thinner evaluative content than the magnanimous person's; the modest person's gate produces shame on circumstances the shameless person's gate ignores entirely. The \textit{hexis} shapes which \textit{pathos} arises, on what evaluative threshold, and with what intensity. The \textit{Rhetoric} II analyses presuppose this \textit{hexis}-modulation throughout: Aristotle's diagnostic categories specify ``those of such-and-such a character feel anger toward such-and-such persons,'' which is a description of \textit{hexis}-conditioning of the \textit{doxa}-gate. The structural distinction matters: Type 2 \textit{hexis} operates at the \textit{doxa} level (settled doxai enabling rapid practical inference); Type 3 \textit{hexeis} operate at both the basic-valence level (perceptual-affective biasing) and the \textit{doxa}-gate level (which \textit{pathē} arise and with what intensity). The same Aristotelian term names two structurally distinct dispositional functions across the two action types, and the recognition of the distinction is what enables the chain-apparatus to handle both rationally articulated craft-action and emotionally complex situational action without collapsing the two into a single dispositional model.

\textit{Phantasia}, \textit{phantasma}, \textit{doxa}, and \textit{pathē} together articulate every action the chain produces. Aristotle defines \textit{phantasia} as ``a movement resulting from actual perception'' (\textit{DA} III.3, 429a1-2), a residual motion that persists after the object has withdrawn and, by persisting, recombining, and re-presenting, supplies the condition for emotional response in the absence of immediate stimuli. \textit{Phantasia} operates in all three action types, but it operates differently in each: as the immediate \textit{phantasma} that identifies the present object in Type 1, where the perceptual stimulus is itself adequate to trigger desire-actualization; as the settled \textit{phantasma} informing the universal premise of the practical syllogism in Type 2, where the agent's craft-knowledge or ethical commitment supplies the inferential premise on which the situational \textit{phantasma} fires; and as the projected \textit{phantasma} over which \textit{doxa} operates in Type 3, where the evaluative articulation of the situation as slight, threat, or undeserved suffering depends on the soul's capacity to make-present what is not perceptually given. ``Both the man who remembers and the man who hopes,'' Aristotle observes, ``will be attended by an imagination of what he remembers or hopes'' (\textit{Rhetoric} I.11, 1370a28-30); the temporal depth of emotional life is supplied by \textit{phantasia}, and the feared evil that does not yet exist is given to the soul as \textit{phantasma}, the soul moving as though the feared thing were present. The \textit{phantasma} is what \textit{doxa} operates upon; what \textit{doxa} ratifies as true is the \textit{phantasma}'s \textit{taking-as}. The \textit{doxa}-gate is content-conditional across all three types: absent in Type 1, present-but-non-evaluative in Type 2, present-and-evaluative in Type 3. Emotion enters the chain wherever the appearance discloses evaluatively complex content that \textit{doxa} ratifies as true, and it enters as the form desire takes under that disclosure. What \textit{phantasia} presents, \textit{doxa} commits to; what \textit{doxa} commits to, emotion mobilizes; what emotion mobilizes, action completes.

The chain closes at $A_4$ but does not stop. The completed action transforms the perceptual environment, producing fresh sensible conditions for a new perceptual cycle starting at $M_0 \rightarrow A_1$. Each completed action reconstitutes the perceptual field and thereby occasions a new sequence of actualizations---a new passage from sensible object through \textit{phantasma}, cognition, desire, and movement. The chain is recursive, not linear: $A_4$ produces a refreshed perceptual situation that initiates a new $M_0 \rightarrow A_1$ cycle, and the cycle resumes with the world now altered by the action that closed the prior cycle. This recursivity is what makes the chain a chain rather than a sequence, and it is what makes the analysis of action a generative apparatus rather than a one-pass diagnostic. The completed action is not the terminus of a finished computation but the threshold of a new perceptual situation, in which the agent finds themselves disposed differently---basic valence biased by the action just completed, \textit{hexeis} subtly shifted by the actualization just lived through, the perceptual field reconfigured by the change the action has produced in the world. The recursive structure secures Heidegger's reading of \textit{hexis} as the determination of the genuineness of being-there in a moment of being-composed: each pass through the chain re-cultivates the dispositional ground, and the cumulative trajectory of the agent's chain-actualizations \textit{is} their \textit{hexis}-formation. The threefold structure of the \textit{pathē} that Aristotle isolates in \textit{Rhetoric} B 1, 1378a 24---``how we find ourselves, what we are attuned to, with respect to whom''---is, on Heidegger's reading, the proto-form of what \textit{Being and Time} §29 will name \textit{Befindlichkeit} (\gk{state-of-mind}, ``finding-oneself,'' attunement). Emotion is not, accordingly, an add-on to rationality, nor a disruption of cognition, but the way the soul's self-movement discloses significance at the precise point where significance must become motion. The chain analysis is complete; subsequent chapters can take up the rhetorical cultivation of \textit{hexeis}, the \textit{kairos}-relativity of appropriate \textit{pathē}, and the further questions the chain-analysis opens for finite, embodied, speaking beings whose way of being is \textit{zōē praktikē meta logou}.
