\subsection{Conclusion}

\inlinenote{Perhaps move this to the conclusion before we discuss the variable presence or where emotion fits in the chain.}
 Having worked through both Aristotle and Heidegger's reading of him, we can now see that each of the \textit{pathē} Aristotle treats in the \textit{Rhetoric}---anger, calm, friendship, enmity, fear, confidence, shame, kindness, pity, indignation, envy, emulation---is a determinate concretion of the same five-fold ontological structure. 

 \begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The disposition. Each \textit{pathos} is a determinate manner of \textit{finding-oneself-disposed}. It is not an inner state that he human being inspects; it is the way the human being \textit{is taken} in this moment, in this situation. Anger is finding-oneself-as-having-been-slighted-by-a-particular-person-who-could-have-acted-otherwise/ shame is finding-oneself-as-having-fallen-short-before-an-audience-that-matters; pity is finding-oneself-as-confronted-with-undeserved-suffering-of-one-with-whom-I-could-imagine-myself. 
        \item The toward-which and the toward-whom. Each \textit{pathos} has a determinate intentional structure: it is \textit{about} this and \textit{with respect to}these. The word-which articulates the world-side of the disposition (what is at issue); the toward-whom articulates its social-ontological side (the persons to whom and before whom it is articulated). Anger is \textit{with respect to} the slight, \textit{toward} the slighter; pity is \textit{with respect to} undeserved harm, \textit{toward} the one suffering. The two together articulate the \textit{aspectual} disclosure that the \textit{pathos} accomplishes: the way the situation is given \textit{as} this-and-that, \textit{for me}. 
        \item The bodily form. Each \textit{pathos} has a determinate bodily articulation that is not symptomatic but constitituve. The hot rush of anger, the cold of fear, the hot flush of shame, the welling of the eyes in pity---these are \textit{part of} the way the \textit{pathos} \textit{is} in the world. To strip a \textit{pathos} of its bodily form is to lose the phenomenon. The bodily form is \textit{logos-articulated}---it has the \textit{eidos} of the slight, the threat, the fall---but it is ``enmattered,'' meaning-bodily-bodied-forth. 
        \item The futural-temporal moment. Each \textit{pathos} is structured by a determinate temporality. Fear is the futural-coming-toward; anger is the present-recoil-from-having-been-slighted-with-a-prospective-vengeance; shame is the present-recoil-from-having-publicly-fallen-short with an indeterminate future of being-thus-marked; pity has a present-confrontation-with another's present-suffering with a backward look at the events produced it and a futural-anticipation that it might or could befall you. Each articulates being-there in its three temporal ecstases---having-bbeen, present, projected-futural---but with a \textit{characteristic profile} that distinguishes one \textit{pathos} from another. 
        \item The disclosive function: bringing to \textit{krisis}, bringing to \textit{logos}. Each \textit{pathos} shifts the hearer toward \textit{taking-a-position}. Each opens or closes specific possibilities of \textit{krisis}, of view-construction or decision. Each cultivates a disposition that, at its limit, \textit{brings to speech}: anger brings the slighted to confront, fear brings the threatened to confer, shame brings the fallen-short to defend or reform, pity brings the witness to alleviate. The cultivation of the \textit{pathos} is the cultivation of the \textit{bringing-to-discourse}, and rhetoric, on Heidegger's reading, is the systematic art of just this cultivation. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{adjustwidth}
These five elements together articulate the generic structure of the \textit{pathē}. Each Aristotelian emotion in the \textit{Rhetoric} is a specific filling-out fo the five---an articulational concretion in which the disposition, the world-side and social-side intentionality, the bodily form, the temporality, and the discursive function are \textit{just so}, and not otherwise. The catalog of the \textit{Rhetoric} is not a list of psychological types; rather, it is a phenomenology of the determinate ways in which a finite, embodied, speaking being who finds itself disposed before the matters that concern it.

\inlinenote{The \textit{pathē} are co-original with \textit{hexeis}. Both are fundamental concepts of being, articulating the being-structure of beings whose way of being is ``speaking-with-one-another'' \hl{Quote heidegger}. Each \textit{pathē} has its possible \textit{hexis}-correlate: courage as the \textit{hexis} of fear, magnanimity as the \textit{hexis} of anger, and \textit{aretē}  is the cultivation of \textit{hexis} that fits the \textit{kairos}.}

\inlinenote{The \textit{pathē} are temporally articulated, ecstatic, and disclosive. Each higher-order emotion has a characteristic temporal profile (fear is futural, anger is present-with-prospectivce-payback, pit is present-with-retrospect-and-possibile-prospection, shame is present-fall-with-indeterminate-future. The \textit{pathē} articulate being-there's three temporal ecstases--having-been, present, projected-futural---in determinate concretions, and the \textit{disclose} the world in \textit{tonal} aspects (as threatening, as slighting, as worthy of pity, as shame-inducing) that \textit{logos} presupposes.}

\inlinenote{The \textit{pathē} are the ground of \textit{logos}: ``\textit{the ground out of which speaking arises, and which what is expressed grows back into}, the \gk{πάθη} [\textit{pathē}], for their part, are \textit{the basic possibilities in which being-there itself is primarily oriented toward itself}, finds itself. The primary being-oriented, the illumination of its being-in-the-word is not a \textit{knowing}, but rather a \textit{finding-oneself} that can be determined differently, according to the mode of being-there of a being'' (176). Speaking is not a master operation that draws on the \textit{pathē} as resources; speaking \textit{arises out of} the \textit{pathē} and \textit{returns to} \hl{feedback loop} them. The rhetorical art is the cultivation of the dispositional ground that brings being-there to discourse, and ARistotle's \textit{Rhetoric} is therefore not a manual of persuasion-techniques but a phenomenology of the disclosive structures through which being-there finds itself in the \textit{polis}.}

\inlinenote{What Heidegger's recovery of Aristotle's analysis discloses is that the \textit{pathē} are not psychological phenomena to be added to a prior account of cognition; they are the \textit{prior}---they are the modes in which being-there has its world disclosed to it, and on th ebasis of which both cognition and speaking become possible. To analyze anger, fear, shame, pity, indignation, kindness---the family of higher-order emotions Aristotle catalogs---is, on Heidegger's reading, to undertake the \textit{phenomenology of disclosedness} itself: the inquiry inot how a finite, embodied, speaking being finds itself in the world before any explicit knowing or saying, and \textit{on the basis of which} knowing and saying become possible at all.}

If \textit{phantasia} supplies the appearance, \textit{doxa} is the moment of apophantic assertion: an unfolding from \textit{taking-as-something} to \textit{taking-something-as-true}. Aristotle insists that emotion follows from this involuntary assent rather than from the later activity of rational judgment. \textit{Doxa} is therefore the hinge between appearance and affective motion. This explains several aspects of how \textit{phantasia} leads to emotion:

\begin{itemize}
    \item False beliefs or opinions generate real emotions: The sound mistaken for a rattlesnake produces real fear. 

    \item Art produces emotion: One can respond fearfully or sorrowfully to \textit{phantasmatic} representations despite knowing they are unreal.

    \item Emotion can contradict judgment: Knowing the rope is not a snake does not neutralize the startle. Even knowing that the rope is not a snake after the fact does not necessarily neutralize the instinctual fear felt because emotion is keyed to \textit{appearance}, not to rational correction.

\end{itemize} 

This is where the deep Heideggerian resonance becomes visible. Heidegger calls rhetoric ``the hermeneutic of Dasein itself,''\footnote{Heidegger reaffirms this Aristotelian inheritance directly in \textit{Being and Time}: ``Aristotle investigates the \gk{πάθη} [affects] in the second book of his \textit{Rhetoric}. Contrary to the traditional orientation, according to which rhetoric is conceived as the kind of thing we `learn in school', this work of Aristotle must be taken as the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being with one another'' (178). This position thus retroactively possition's Heidegger's \textit{pathos}-analysis as thee preparatory work for the Befindlichkeit-analytic (``state of mind'').} the discipline in which ``the self-elaboration of Dasein is expressly executed'' (Basic Concepts (GA 18); Heidegger, Hediegger and Rhetoric ed. Gross p. 1). \textit{Doxa} and Emotion--as \textit{pathos}, as being-moved--was already the site where Aristotle located the fundamental dynamics through which human beings find themselves oriented in a world. Heidegger radicalizes this insight by reading \textit{pathos} as the primordial attunement--or mood(\textit{Stimmung})--that constitutes the clearing in which beings can appear at all. Thus, the Aristotelian apparatus of \textit{phantasia}, \textit{doxa}, and \textit{pathos} is not merely psychological: it sketches the pre-cognitive, affective, atmospheric structure of being-in-the-world itself. Because emotion is a self-motion of the soul toward or away from what appears good or bad, every emotion entails desire, orientation, and action-tendency.\footnote{Fear-the desire to flee; Anger-the desire for revenge; Love-the desire of the beloved's good.} Emotions are therefore \textit{teleological}: they set the direction of motion toward a perceived end. Emotion is not a barrier to action but the very medium in which purposive action takes shape. \textit{Phantasia} is the bridge here. \textit{Aisthēsis} (perception) presents neutral stimuli; \textit{phantasia} renders them as \textit{something}—purse/avoid-able, dangerous, insulting, pleasurable, enviable—and in doing so makes them significant. Once something is significant, the soul moves; and, when the soul moves, the body follows. Therefore, the actualization of evaluative action is not merely \textit{phantasia}'s \textit{taking-as-something}, though it begins there; it is not merely \textit{doxa}'s \textit{taking-as-something-true}, though \textit{doxa} articulates it as such; it is not merely emotion's \textit{taking-as-something-mattering-to-me}, though emotion concretizes it as teleological orientation; it is not merely bodily \textit{kinēsis}, though it is consummated in bodily \textit{kinēsis}. 

Because \textit{phantasia} generates appearances beyond the immediately present, Aristotle explicitly states that memory and anticipation rely on its operations. In \textit{De Memoria} he writes that we remember when the “movement caused by the object is preserved internally” (450a20–25). To remember grief is to feel grief again; to anticipate pleasure is to feel pleasure now in relation to a not-yet. When Aristotle writes in \textit{Rhetoric} I.xi that “both the man who remembers and the man who hopes will be attended by an imagination of what he remembers or hopes” (1370a), he articulates the temporal depth of emotional life. Emotion is not anchored to the present; it reaches into past and future by means of \textit{phantasmatic} appearance. Fear—“a pain or disturbance due to imagining some destructive or painful evil in the future” (1382a22–23)—is paradigmatic. The feared event does not exist; the \textit{phantasma} does. And the soul moves as though the feared thing were present. Thus, \textit{phantasia} is not a decorative adjunct to emotion; it is the condition of possibility for the soul being-moved by absences, possibilities, appearances, and counterfactuals.


Aristotle’s rhetorical theory makes explicit the practical, world-shaping power of emotion. To persuade is not primarily to offer proofs but to reshape the listener’s \textit{phantasmatic} field. The rhetor must supply images—concrete, narrative, affectively charged—that allow the audience to see what the rhetor wants them to see. It is this imagistic seeing that grounds the subsequent formation of \textit{doxa}, the ensuing movement of emotion, and the eventual act of judgment. If rhetorical persuasion succeeds, it is because it sets off the entire cascade of movement: new \textit{phantasma} → new \textit{doxa} → new emotion → new orientation → new action. Hence Aristotle’s insistence that fear\footnote{Fear = appearing that danger is approaching.}, pity\footnote{Pity = appearing that undeserved suffering befalls another.}, and anger\footnote{Anger = appearing to have been insulted.} each have characteristic appearances. These definitions reveal that \textit{pathos} is not merely a feeling but a \textit{way of world-disclosure}—a way things appear when moved in a certain direction. 

For Aristotle, emotion is the kinetic, affective, evaluative dimension of how the world appears to us. It is grounded in \textit{phantasia}, shaped through involuntary belief, felt as pleasure or pain, and expressed as motion toward or away from what the soul takes to be significant. Emotion is not an add-on to rationality, nor a disruption of cognition, but the way the soul’s self-movement discloses significance at all. In this sense, Aristotle’s theory anticipates later phenomenological accounts—Heidegger’s most explicitly—that interpret emotion as a mode of attunement, a way the world already “matters” before deliberation begins. Emotion, in its full Aristotelian sense, is nothing less than the \textit{affective architecture of being-in-the-world}.
