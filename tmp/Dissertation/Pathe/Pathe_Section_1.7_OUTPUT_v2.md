# Receptive Structures: *Hexis*, *Geworfenheit*, and the Convergence of Aristotelian and Heideggerian Ontologies of Affection 

## 1. Locus Identification: Bekker, SZ, and BCAP Coordinates The interpretive labor that follows depends upon a precise triangulation of loci across three textual architectures: the Bekker numbering system that organizes Aristotle's corpus, the Sein und Zeit pagination that structures Heidegger's fundamental ontology, and the GA 18 marginal apparatus that maps Heidegger's 1924 retrieval of Aristotelian philosophy. The Bekker locus 200b12–25 thus serves as the primary anchor for the Physics-side of the investigation, wherein Heidegger identifies the "basic topic of μέθοδος περὶ φύσεως" as κίνησις and what is cogiven in it (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 261). From *De Anima*, the soul's incompatibility with enforced movement confirms that motion in the psyche cannot be merely accidental but must engage the soul's receptive capacity at an essential level (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 11). Accordingly, the Bekker reverse-index, the BT-structured index, and the BCAP index converge on a single problematic: the receptive character of living being, articulated through pathos and disposition in Aristotle and through Befindlichkeit and Geworfenheit in Heidegger. ## 3. Supplementary Engagement: Heidegger's Retrieval of Aristotelian *Hexis* When the ChromaDB chunks prove insufficient for capturing the full weight of Heidegger's retrieval of Aristotelian hexis, we turn to the source PDFs in the rhetorical ontology corpus. Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's *Physics* in *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* establishes that being-in-movement is not a property appended to beings from outside but belongs to their very mode of presence; κίνησις, as the commentary on Physics Γ 1 makes clear, is "not παρὰ τὰ πράγματα" but rather the "how of the being of beings" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 261). This determination resonates with von Uexküll's biological epistemology, which similarly refuses to treat the organism as a passive substrate upon which external forces inscribe themselves; indeed, for von Uexküll, the subject encounters its environment through projections that constitute the perceptual field, such that "modern man confronts only himself" in the act of investigation (von Uexkull, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 240–242). Heidegger's early lectures develop a complementary position by treating εἶδος—"the look of a being that is there at each moment, and that has its look, insofar as it has a look"—as the formal determination that orients hexis toward actuality rather than empty potentiality (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 168–169). Subsequently, Heidegger's interpretation of "possibility" as "the being-character of beings in which they are possible; not in the sense of an empty possibility that could, so to speak, be held before being-there" underscores how hexis operates as a concrete dispositional structure rather than an abstract logical category (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 254). The integration of von Uexküll's Funktionskreis with Aristotelian hexis thus illuminates a shared insistence that receptivity is structured, oriented, and world-disclosing from the outset. 

## 4. Introduction: *De Insomniis* and the Problem of Receptive Imagination The Aristotelian treatise *De Insomniis* raises a problem that haunts every subsequent account of receptivity: how does the soul, in the absence of active sensation, continue to generate phantasmata that move the organism? This question—far from being confined to the psychology of dreaming—ramifies into the broader ontology of affection that connects perceptual encounter, rhetorical persuasion, and what von Uexküll calls the magical environment. Von Uexküll's account of children immersed in magical play provides a striking illustration; he describes how a child playing the story of Hansel and Gretel with matchsticks suddenly exclaims in genuine terror at the witch, demonstrating the autonomy of phantasia from present sensory input (von Uexkull, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 122–126). This magical environment contradicts the stimulus-response architecture of the Funktionskreis precisely because the image exceeds its sensory origin—an excess that Aristotle's *De Insomniis* theorizes as the residual movement left by actual perception. Thus, phantasia's functional autonomy in dreams, deliberation, and rhetorical reception marks a tension with its causal derivation from sensation. Accordingly, the introduction to this problematic must acknowledge that the receptive structures under investigation—whether Aristotelian hexis, Heideggerian Befindlichkeit, or Uexküllian Umwelt—all share this paradox: they are passive insofar as they receive, yet active insofar as they constitute the very field within which beings can show themselves. Von Uexküll the goal-pursuing quality in living beings cannot be reduced to mere mechanical response, insisting that "much-lauded human technology has lost all sense of Nature" (von Uexkull, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 199–201). 

## 5. Synchronic and Diachronic Dimensions: A Unified Formulation The distinction between synchronic and diachronic analysis is essential for comprehending how receptive structures operate at two levels simultaneously: the structural conditions that make affection possible at any given moment, and the temporal unfolding through which those conditions are transformed by successive encounters with world. On the synchronic axis, Aristotle's categorial determinations—τοιόνδε, τοσόνδε, and the other ways of being-present—establish the formal framework within which beings show themselves as "this here" or "so much" or "such," where the suffix -δε proceeds from δή, signifying "manifestly there," "present" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 220). This synchronic structure ensures that at any instant the organism or Dasein is already disposed toward its world in determinate ways, not floating in an indeterminate void. On the diachronic axis, however, hexis is not a static state but a dispositional trajectory shaped by previous affections; the ταραχή or "tumult" that Heidegger identifies at the heart of πάθος names precisely the transitional unrest through which one frame of mind gives way to another (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 138). Similarly, Heidegger's Zeitlichkeit in *Being and Time* provides the diachronic horizon for Befindlichkeit: Dasein's thrownness is always a having-been that conditions future projection. The Aristotelian *Physics* establishes that things "always come to pass in the same way" or "for the most part," indicating a regularity grounded in natural teleology rather than mechanical necessity (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 23). Hence, the unified formulation holds that the synchronic slice—the categorial structure of presence—and the diachronic movement—the dispositional transformation through affection—are codependent moments within a single ontology of receptivity. Neither can be isolated without distorting the phenomenon. This dual structure recurs, as we shall see, in the convergence of hexis and Geworfenheit at GA 18 p. 122. 

## 6. Two-Mechanism Analysis: Formal Structure and Transitional Movement Having distinguished the synchronic and diachronic axes, we can now identify two mechanisms through which receptive structures operate: a formal-structural mechanism that determines what can show itself, and a transitional mechanism that governs how affection transforms the receiving agent. The formal-structural mechanism corresponds to what Heidegger, reading Aristotle, calls the εἶδος—"the look of a being that is there at each moment"—which prescribes the categories under which beings present themselves (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 168–169). This mechanism is essentially synchronic; it answers the question of how, at a given instant, beings are organized within the receptive field. The transitional mechanism, by contrast, operates diachronically and is captured by Heidegger's analysis of πάθος as the "having-come-into-another-frame-of-mind," which he distinguishes from mere composure and characterizes through the restless movement of ταραχή (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 138). Gonzalez's analysis of φαντασία contributes a further dimension to this transitional mechanism, insofar as phantasia mediates between perception and deliberation and is thus vulnerable to both productive use and rhetorical abuse (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 4). Von Uexküll's biological framework reinforces this two-mechanism analysis; where only one functional cycle obtains, as in the case of Rhizostoma, "one can speak of a 'reflex animal,' since the same reflex runs from each bell to the muscle band on the edge of the umbrella" (von Uexkull, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 77–80). As organisms become more complex, the formal-structural mechanism diversifies and the transitional mechanism allows for genuine dispositional transformation. Thus, the two mechanisms—formal determination and affective transition—together constitute the full architecture of receptivity. ## 7. *Hexis*, *Geworfenheit*, and Their Convergence at GA 18 The three movements of this argument—Aristotelian hexis, Heideggerian Geworfenheit, and their convergence—can now be traced in light of the preceding analyses. First, Aristotelian hexis names the stable yet dynamic disposition through which a living being holds itself toward its world. As Heidegger's BCAP lectures demonstrate, this holding is not static possession but an active maintenance of the organism's receptive orientation; πάσχειν functions as "δέχεσθαι, 'to perceive' and that primarily as πάθος, 'becoming-affected,' 'disposition,' being related to the world as in it, being in dealings with it" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 277–280). This passage re-reads the loci identified in Section 1—the Bekker coordinates and BCAP index—by showing that Aristotelian hexis is already an ontological determination of being-in-the-world rather than a mere psychological trait. The chunked content queried in Section 2 confirms this reading through the correlation of πάθος with ταραχή, while the supplementary PDF engagement in Section 3 enriches it with Heidegger's gloss on εἶδος and possibility. With respect to the *De Insomniis* problematic introduced in Section 4, dreams can move the sleeper: the dispositional structure persists even when actual sensation ceases, sustaining the phantasmatic movement that constitutes dream experience. Second, Heideggerian Geworfenheit—thrownness—recasts this Aristotelian structure in existential-analytic terms. State-of-mind, or Befindlichkeit, is the existentiale through which Dasein finds itself always already delivered over to a world that it did not choose; Heidegger specifies that it is "ontically the most familiar and everyday sort of thing; our mood, our Being-attuned" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 172–173). Thus Heidegger re-reads the synchronic-diachronic distinction formulated in Section 5 as the temporal ecstases of Zeitlichkeit: the synchronic is Dasein's having-been as it conditions present disclosure, and the diachronic is the futural projection that transforms dispositional orientation. The two-mechanism analysis of Section 6 receives its Heideggerian correlate in the distinction between the formal disclosure of entities within mood and the affective transition through which moods shift, precisely the Aristotelian ταραχή transposed into the key of fundamental ontology. Furthermore, in the domain of political and rhetorical life, Heidegger recognizes that the polis functions as a "historical site virtually identical to the ontological site of Da-sein" in which a people "takes place" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 157); this recognition underscores that Geworfenheit is not a merely individual condition but extends to the communal rhetorical situatedness that constitutes shared world. Third, the convergence occurs at GA 18 p. 122, where Heidegger explicitly articulates the structural homology between hexis and Geworfenheit. The pivotal insight is that αἴσθησις and Befindlichkeit share a receptive character that is neither pure passivity nor autonomous activity but a constitutive openness to being-affected—what the BCAP lectures call being "affected by beings as world" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 277–280). Von Uexküll's parallel framework confirms this convergence from the biological side, insofar as every organism's perceptual world is structured by projections that simultaneously receive and constitute what is encountered (von Uexkull, *A Foray Into the Worlds of Animals and Humans with A Theory of Meaning*, pp. 240–242). Accordingly, the convergence at GA 18 does not collapse Aristotle into Heidegger; rather, the Aristotelian analysis of πάσχειν as dispositional affection supplies the ontological resources that Heidegger's existential analytic subsequently retrieves, transforms, and redeploys under the title of Befindlichkeit. 

## 8. Rhetoric as Hermeneutic: Cross-Reference to §1.2 Aristotle's *Rhetoric* constitutes the first systematic hermeneutic—a thesis developed at length in §1.2 of this dissertation—receives its warrant from the convergence traced above. If hexis and Befindlichkeit name the receptive structures through which world discloses itself, then rhetoric, as the art that addresses and transforms those very structures, occupies a foundational hermeneutic position. Heidegger himself signals as much in the BCAP lectures when he treats the λόγος of Parmenides—"beings are one," ἓν τὸ ὄν—as possessing a "dominance within the interpretation of being-there" that rhetoric must subsequently negotiate (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 203). The rhetorical dimension of this hermeneutic task emerges clearly in the *Heidegger and Rhetoric* volume, where the authors observe that Heidegger's 1924 lecture frames speaking as approaching the other in the manner of communicating, asserting that the success of a lecture depends not on the speaker alone but on the audience's receptive attunement (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 58–60). Gonzalez's analysis of φαντασία in *Rhetoric* III.1 deepens this point by showing that phantasia mediates between the speaker's rhetorical invention and the audience's perceptual field; yet this mediation is always vulnerable to distortion—phantasia might be censured as "mere show" or "ostentatiousness," even though Aristotle "never entirely sidelines" its rhetorical efficacy (Gonzalez, *The Meaning and Function of Phantasia in Aristotle's 'Rhetoric' III.1*, p. 4). The full development of this thesis—that rhetoric functions as hermeneutic precisely because it operates upon the receptive structures analyzed in the foregoing sections—belongs to §1.2. For the present argument, it suffices the convergence of hexis and Geworfenheit at GA 18 provides the ontological ground upon which rhetoric's hermeneutic vocation can be understood. Accordingly, the reader is directed to §1.2 for the sustained treatment that this claim demands, where the interplay of rhetorical affection, phantasia, and dispositional transformation receives its full articulation. --- ```
