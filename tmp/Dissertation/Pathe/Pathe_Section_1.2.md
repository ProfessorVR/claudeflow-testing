\subsection{\textit{Pathos} and \textit{Pathē}: Two Articulational Concretions}

With our terminological reckoning complete, I can now posit the overall \textit{pathetic} thesis for which I will argue:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    There is a single ontological structure\footnote{Heidegger's ``\gk{ἕξις} (\textit{hexis} ``settled disposition'') is nothing other than a how of \textit{pathos}, being-out-of-composure, in relation to being-composed-as-to'' (125)---opens the structural space for this two-hows reading. \textit{Pathos} admits of multiple how-modalities; the articulational distinction between basic \textit{resonant orexis} and the more articulationally concrete \textit{pathē} is one such how-distinction within the single \textit{pathos} structure.} of \textit{pathos}---articulated by the four senses of the \textit{Metaphysics} and the three constitutive aspects of the \textit{Rhetoric}---that is invariant across (a) basic \textit{resonant orexis} at the perceptual level, and (b) the higher-order \textit{pathē} at the \textit{doxa}-articulated level. The difference between the two is not categorical but articulational: (a) realizes the structure at minimal cognitive articulation (this is the pre-propositional, but not \textit{pre-positional} hedonic tonality that accompanies every act of perception \hl{or what Heidegger calls in \textit{Being and Time} the ``existential-\textit{hermeneutical} `as' (201)}); (b) realizes it a maximal cognitive articulation (a more articulationally concrete propositional evaluation under the categories of the \textit{Rhetoric} \hl{or what Heidegger calls the ``\textit{apophantical} `as''' (201)).}\footnote{Heidegger's hermeneutical/apophantical distinction is developed in \textit{Being and Time} §32-33: the apophantical ``as'' of predicative determination is a derivative mode of interpretation, leveled down from the prior hermeneutical ``as'' of involved understanding. The articulational structure of the present section---basic \textit{resonant orexis} as pre-propositional position-taking (hermeneutical ``as''), whereas the higher-order \textit{pathē} as propositional concretion via \textit{doxa} (apophantical ``as'')---instantiates this pattern at the \textit{pathos} level.}\footnote{The present analysis restricts itself to rational animals: those possessing \textit{logos} and therefore capable of \textit{doxa} (``opinion/belief''). For Aristotle insists that \textit{doxa} provides the articulational concretion turning the basic affective valence co-given with perception into the higher-order determinancy of shame, pity and their kin. As Aristotle states: ``there are some of the brutes in which we find imagination, without discourse of reason'' (\textit{On the Soul}, III.3, 428a23-24). Aristotle elsewhere attributes \textit{pathos}-like states to non-rational animals: \textit{Historia Animalium} IX, \textit{Nicomachean Ethics} III.10. On the present synthesis these attributions are \textit{analogical}: animal ``fear'' is a sustained dispositional orientation grounded in basic valence and persistent \textit{phantasma}, structurally similar to but not identical with the rational \textit{pathē}. The \textit{doxa}-gate is what differentiates the merely-similar from the genuine higher-order \textit{pathē}.}
\end{adjustwidth}

\inlinenote{\textit{NE} III.8, 1116b24-1117a5: ``Passion also is sometimes reckoned as courage; those who act from passion, like wild beasts rushing at those who have wounded them, are thought to be brave, because brave men also are passionate; for passion above all things is eager to rush on danger, ... Now brave men act for the sake of the noble, but passion aids them; while wild beasts act under the influence of pain; for they attack because they have been wounded or because they are afraid, since if they are in a forest they do not come near one. Thus they are not brave because, driven by pain and passion, they rush on danger without foreseeing any of the perils, since at that rate even asses would be brave when they are hungry; for blows will not drive them from their food; and lust also makes adulterers do many daring things. [Those creatures are not brave, then, which are driven on to danger by pain or passion.] The courage that is due to passion seems to be the most natural, and to be courage if choice and aim be added.'' Here Aristotle simultaneously (a) attributes \textit{pathos}-like states (courage, fear, pain) to non-rational animals and (b) denies them genuine virtue/courage on the ground that they lack the rational element (choice, foresight, motive): animal ``fear'' and animal ``courage'' are real \textit{pathē}-like  dispositions, but they fall short of the higher-order \textit{pathē} because the rational gating is absent. }

\inlinenote{\textit{History of Animals} IX 10-14: ``Of the animals that are comparatively obscure and short-lived the characters are not so obvious to our perception as are those of animals that are longer-lived. These latter animals appear to have a natural capacity corresponding to each of the passions of the soul: to good sense or simiplicity, courage or timidity, to good temper or to bad, and to other similar dispositions.''}

The articulational-concretion distinction I draw between \textit{resonant orexis} as the perceptual-level minimal articulation of \textit{pathos} and the higher-order \textit{pathē} of the \textit{Rhetoric} as the \textit{doxa}-mediated maximal articulation fills a gap that Heidegger's treatment of \textit{pathos} in his lectures leaves unaddressed. Heidegger's recovery of \textit{pathos} as a ``being-taken of human beings in their full [bodily] being-in-the-world'' (133), and the single-structure thesis he extracts from the fourfold sense of \textit{Metaphysics} and three constituent aspects of the \textit{Rhetoric}---which will be discussed below---are analytically and phenomenologically precise. What the undifferentiated treatment cannot accommodate, however, is the variable presence of \textit{pathos} across the action-types Aristotle himself catalogs. The practical syllogism of drinking Aristotle provides in \textit{Movement of Animals} (701a33-34)---perception, basic appetite, immediate action---does not engage the higher-order \textit{pathē} Heidegger analyzes, but neither is it \textit{pathos}-less: the perceiver is already disposed-toward the perceived object as pursue-able. Without an articulational distinction internal to the \textit{pathos}-structure, this case becomes either a counter-example to the single-structure thesis or a  phenomenon that simply falls outside Heidegger's analysis. The articulational distinction resolves both elements: basic \textit{resonant orexis} and the \textit{higher-order} \textit{pathē} are \textit{two articulational concretions of one ontological structure}, the same preservative (\textit{sōtēria)}\footnote{The original Greek used in this passage is: \gk{σωτηρία} (\textit{sōtēria} ``preservation''). \textit{On the Soul} II.5, 417b3. Aristotle. Aristotelis De Anima. Edited by W. D. Ross, Oxford University Press, 1956.}-\textit{paschein} realized at minimal (pre-propositional) and maximal (\textit{doxa}-mediated) articulation. 

The unifying ground of this single structure thesis is the distinction Aristotle draws between ``the expression `to be acted upon''': on the one hand it is used to express ``the extinction of one of two contraries by the other,'' on the other hand ``the maintenance of what is potential by the agency of what is actual and already like what is acted upon, as actual to potential'' (\textit{On the Soul} II.5, 417b2-7). In one sense being-affected is a destruction whereas on the other it is a preservation in which a potential is \textit{brought into its actuality}. Hearing a sound is a being-affected of the second sort: my hearing is brought to its full being, not destroyed. So too is being-moved by anger or fear. As I have already demonstrated, \textit{resonant orexis} or hedonic tonality is co-given with every moment of being-there: with every perceiving, every thinking, every considering, but equally with every \textit{pathē}. \hl{However, to reiterate, when the object of sense is actually present, we have the basic hedonic tonality, the basic affective valence of good or bad, pursue or avoid; \textit{resonant orexis}, on the other hand, is part of the \textit{resonant kineseis} that persists after the object has ceased to be. As such, perceiving elicits the basic affective valence whereas thinking and considering relies on the \textit{resonant orexis} as it is the \textit{phantasma} on which they operate.} There is no mode of being-there from which pleasure-or-pain is absent: every moment of our being there is colored by hedonic tonality. Accordingly, Both basic \textit{resonant orexis} and the higher-order \textit{pathē} are species of \textit{paschein}. What separates basic affective valence from the higher-order \textit{pathē} is, therefore, not a categorical division between two ontological strata but graduation along a continuous magnitude-axis\footnote{The magnitude-axis here has at least three dimensions: hedonic intensity (how pleasant/painful), existential weight (how mattering), and cognitive articulation (how propositionally specified). Higher-order \textit{pathē} are not simply \textit{more intense} than basic valence but more articulated and---often, not always---more existentially weighted; the articulational dimension carries most of the structural argument.}: it is an \textit{articulational} graduation rather than a difference in kind.

\inlinenote{The structural parallel between the articulational distinction between \textit{resonant orexis} and \textit{pathē} and Heidegger's later hermeneutical/apophantical ``as'' is not accidental. Heidegger's \textit{pathos}-analysis treats \textit{pathos} as a single ontological structure articulated by Aristotle's \textit{Metaphysics} and \textit{Rhetoric}, but does not draw a distinction at the \textit{pathos} level itself. \textit{Being and Time} generalizes the cut at the level of interpretation/assertion (hermeneutical ``as'' / apophantical ``as''). The present section occupies the structural position between the two: by drawing the articulational distinction at the \textit{pathos level} (basic \textit{resonant orexis} / higher-order \textit{pathē}) and identifying \textit{doxa} as the \textit{pathos}-level analogy of \textit{Aussage} (assertion), it specifies the \textit{pathos}-level instantiation of the \textit{Being and Time} pattern that his lectures undifferentiated treatment leaves implicit. My attempt, therefore, is to recover the structural pattern Heidegger's intellectual trajectory presupposes but does not fully articulate at the \textit{pathos level}.}

\inlinenote{The relations between basic affective valence/\textit{resonant orexis} is a \textit{structural relation}: \textit{pathē} are \textit{more concretely articulated actualizations of the same structure}that basic affective valence actualizes minimally. The Aristotelian frame is \textit{dynamis-energeia} (potentiality-actuality): basic affective valence is the actualization of the \textit{pathos}-structure at the perceptual level; \textit{pathē} are the actuality of the same \textit{pathos}-structure at the \textit{doxa}-mediated level. Both are actualities; neither is a potentiality only. The cut, so to speak, is between two \textit{energeia}(actualizations) with different articulational concretions, not between potentiality and actuality.}

\inlinenote{\textit{pathos} analysis in the lectures is the prep work for \textit{BT}'s derivation thesis (apophantical ``as'' derived from hermeneutical ``as''), and the actualization chain reinstantiates this pattern at the \textit{pathos} level. Three lines of evidence: chronological-textual, structural-philosophical, and an open \textit{BT} tension I resolve. }

``This prior disclosedness of the world belongs to Being-in and is partly constituted by one’s state-of-mind. ``The entity which is held in our fore-having—for instance, the hammer—is proximally ready-to-hand as equipment. Something \textit{ready-to-hand with which} we have to do or perform something, turns into something “about which” the assertion that points it out is made. Our fore-sight is aimed at something present-at-hand in what is ready-to-hand. Both \textit{by} and \textit{for} this way of looking at it [Hin-sicht], the ready-to-hand becomes veiled as ready-to-hand. Within this discovering of presence-at-hand, which is at the same time a covering-up of readiness-to-hand, something present-at-hand which we encounter is given a definite character in its Being-present-at-hand-in-such-and-such-a-manner. Only now are we given any access to \textit{properties} or the like. When an assertion has given a definite character to something present-at-hand, it says something about it \textit{as} a “what”; and this “what” is drawn \textit{from that} which is present-at-hand as such. \subsubsection{The \textit{Metaphysics} Fourfold: \textit{Pathos} from Alterability to Magnitude}

As Heidegger glosses in his reading of Aristotle's \textit{Metaphysics},\footnote{``We call an affection (1) a quality in respect of which a thing can be altered, e.g. white and black, sweet and bitter, heaviness and lightness, and all others of the kind.---(2) The already actualized alterations.---(3) Especially, injurious alterations and movements, and, above all, painful injuries.---(4) Experiences pleasant or painful when on a large scale are called affections'' (\textit{Metaphysics} 21, 1022b15-21). To verify that the four sense apply at the basic-valence level: (1) \textit{alterability}-perception's \textit{dynamis} is constitutionally alterable by its proper object (\textit{On the Soul} II.5, 416b33-417a20). (2) \textit{Actual alterations}-the perception is the actualization of the perceptual faculty, the basic valence of the actualization of the the faculty of desire at the perceptual actualization. (3) \textit{Harmful alterations}-destruction-by-excess at the perceptual limit (\textit{On the Soul} II.12, 424a28-32; III.2, 426a30-b8) shows that the harmful runs through the entire range of \textit{pashein}. (4) \textit{Magnitudes}-the perceptual \textit{logos} (ratio) admits of degree (\textit{On the Soul} II.12, 424a28; \textit{Sense} 7, 447b-448a, on stronger stimuli masking weaker stimuli), giving magnitude as a structural feature of basic valence. Heidegger reads the fourfold as a ``progressive narrowing''; the present synthesis treats this as specifying the magnitude-axis along which the same structure realizes at varying intensities, not as a stratum-distinction.} there are four general meanings of \textit{pathos}: 
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item In its broadest sense, \textit{pathos} ``characterizes a being as something that can in some way be affected by something. Something can \textit{happen} to such a being.'' It is a determination of beings with the character or possibility of ``becoming-otherwise'' (131). 
        \item \textit{Pathos} as the \gk{Ἐνέργεια} (\textit{energeiai} ``actualities'') of these alterations: ``the ``being-there'' of such a shifting occurring-to-one'' (131). 
        \item \textit{Pathos}, narrowing further, ``as the occurring-to-one that has the character of the unpleasant, of the \gk{βλαβερόν} [\textit{blaberon} ``harmful'']. That which happens to me is harmful to me in its happening. This is, indeed, the way that we use the expression ``to happen.'' But \gk{πάθος} [\textit{pathos}] is defined still more precisely: harmfulness is related mostly to \gk{λύπη} [\textit{lypē } ``pain, distress''], so that, as a result, \textit{my attunement to this occurring affects me}. It is a becoming-relevant of something, which aims at my attunement, a becoming-otherwise int he sense of becoming-depressed'' (131). The third meaning, here, refines the second: it is not just any occurrence, but the occurrence that \textit{tones} the living being \textit{down}. 
        \item Fourth, \textit{pathos} as the magnitude of the harmful. ``\gk{πάθος} [\textit{pathos}] designates the ``size,'' the ``measure,'' of that which happens to me, that which occurs to me in aharmful way. We have a corresponding expression for that: ``that is a blow to me'' (132). The fourth meaning narrows still further: it is not just the harmful occurrence, but the magnitude of harm: the gravity of the calamity that struck. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{adjustwidth}
The progression from one sense to the next is not arbitrary; rather, it moves from the most general ontological structure (alterability) through the actual occurrence of alteration (\textit{energeia} of alteration), to the qualitative determination of that occurrence as harmful, to the magnitude of the harm. Heidegger's phenomenological assessment gathers the four under a single encompassing thesis: ``From these four meanings, the \textit{genuine relatedness} of \gk{πάθος} [\textit{pathos}] becomes visible; it is related to the \textit{being of living things}, which is characterized by a thus-finding-oneself-again-and-again. The occurring to one befalls and strikes one in this disposition'' (132). Taken together, the four sense articulate \textit{pathos} as the structure of \textit{being-occurrable-to} that belongs to the way of being of a finite living being. To live, in the Aristotelian sense Heidegger recovers, is to be ontologically structured as \textit{that to which something can happen}---and to which, in its concrete factical existence, \textit{this and that, again and again, does} happen. This occurrence, as Heidegger specifies further, need not, without qualification, ``have the character of the harmful''; rather, ``Aristotle recognizes a \gk{μεταβολή} [\textit{metabolē} ``change, alteration''], \gk{κίνησις} [\textit{kinēsis} ``movement''], \gk{ἀλλοίωσις} [\textit{alloiosis} ``alteration, qualitative change''], in which \gk{πάσχειν} [\textit{paschein} ``being-affected''] has the character of \gk{σωτηρία} [\textit{sōtēria} ``preservation'']'' (132).\footnote{``Also the expression `to be acted upon' has more than one meaning; it may mean either the extinction of one of two contraries by the other, or the maintenance of what is potential by the agency of what is actual and already like what is acted upon, as actual to potential'' (\textit{On the Soul} II.5, 417b2-5).} There is not just one kind of \textit{paschein}: one is destruction by an opposite, the other is preservation by the actuality of what one was potentially. This is the philosophical point Heidegger emphasizes: \textit{not all being-affected is loss}. Hearing a sound is a being-affected by which my hearing is \textit{brought to its full being}, not destroyed. Learning is a being-affected by which I come into possession of what I am potentially. \textit{Pathos}, in its widest ontological reach, includes the structure by which a living being is \textit{brought into its proper actuality} through its encounter with the world. 

Each of the four senses of \textit{pathos} are structurally satisfied at the basic affective level of \textit{resonant orexis}: (1) Alterability. \textit{Aisthēsis} is \textit{constitutionally alterable}: the perceptual \textit{dynamis} (potentiality) is precisely the capacity to be altered by its proper object; perception begins as a \textit{dynamis}that requires an external agent to actualize it. (2) Actual alterations. The \textit{resonant aisthēma} itself is an \textit{energeia} (actuality) of the \textit{aisthētikon} (perceptive faculty) and \textit{resonant orexis} is the actuality of the \textit{orektikon} (appetitive faculty) at its perceptual-level actualization. Heidegger's gloss---``the ``being-there'' of such a shifting occurring-to-one'' (131)---is exactly what the affective valence is: the being-there of the soul's being-affected by the pleasant or painful object. (3) Harmful alterations. This moment is seemingly problematic given both Heidegger's and Aristotle's narrowing toward \textit{lupē} (pain); but, Aristotle's own treatment of perception explicitly includes both pain in the normal sensory range\footnote{``where there is sensation, there is also pleasure and pain'' (\textit{On the Soul} II.2, 413b24).} \textit{and} ``excess is painful or destructive'' (\textit{On the Soul} III.2, 426b8): excess sound deafens and excess light blinds. The harmful-alteration moment is, therefore, operative at the level of \textit{aisthēsis}. (4) Magnitudes. Aristotle's doctrine that perception has a \textit{logos} (ratio) (III.2, 426a28-b8), that excess destroys the ration, \textit{and} that stronger stimuli mask weaker stimuli,\footnote{``For we must suppose that the stimuli, when equal, tend alike to efface one another, since no one stimulus results from them; while, if they are unequal, the stronger alone is distinctly perceptible'' (\textit{Sense} 447b4-5).} establishes a \textit{magnitude-structure} at the basic perceptual level. Accordingly, all four senses of \textit{pathos} are present at \textit{resonant orexis}. Heidegger's reading of the fourfold as a ``progressive narrowing'' specifies the magnitude-axis along which the same structure is realized at varying intensities and articulations. 

\inlinenote{Magnitude. Heidegger's reading of magnitude is \textit{quantitative-existential}---magnitude as ``size of misfortune.'' But the magnitude-axis I invoke has at least three independent dimensions: \textit{hedonic intensity} (how pleasant/painful); \textit{existential weight} (how mattering); and, \textit{cognitive articulation} (how propositionally specified). \textit{pathē} are not just \textit{more intense} than basic valence---they are \textit{more articulated, more exisetntially weighted, and} (often, but not always) \textit{more intense}. The drinking case may even involve high hedonic intensity (real thirst is intense) without propositional articulation or existential weight in the rhetorical sense. So the magnitude-axis is multi-dimensional, and the articulational dimension is the one carrying most of the structural argument. }

\subsubsection{The \textit{Rhetoric}'s Constitutive Threefold: \textit{Pathos} as Change, \textit{Krisis}, and Hedonic Tonality}

The primary definition of emotion in Aristotle's corpus appears not in \textit{De Anima} or any of the traditionally categorized psychological treatises, but in the \textit{Rhetoric}: ``the emotions are all those feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements, and that are also attended by pain or pleasure---such are anger, pity, fear and the like, with their opposites''' (II.1, 1378a20-22). Though this definition appears within the context of rhetorical theory's practical applications, it carries substantial ontological weight; and, it is this definition along with the four senses of \textit{pathos} that must anchor any systematic analysis of \textit{pathos} in either of its articulational concretions. In his reading of this same passage from the \textit{Rhetoric}, Heidegger isolates the three constitutive aspects of every \textit{pathos}:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    (1) \gk{Μεταβάλλοντες} [\textit{Metaballontes} ``undergoing change'']: something along the way with respect to which ``a change sets in for us,'' through which ``we change'' from one disposition to another. (2) Combine with this change, \gk{διαφέρουσι πρὸς τὰς κρίσεις},\footnote{\textit{diapherousi pros tas kriseis} ``they make a difference to judgments.''} we ``differentiate ourselves'' from ourselves before the change in that which is the hearer's task: ``to take a position,'' ``to form a view.'' The formation of a view involves the manner and mode in which we change. (3) \gk{οἷς ἕπεται λύπη καὶ ἡδονή}:\footnote{\textit{hois hepetai lypē kai hēdonē} ``to which pain/distress and pleasure attend.''} not ``following,'' but rather ``co-given'' in combination with the \gk{πάθη} [\textit{pathē}] is a ``being-disposed-as-higher-or-lower'' of the being-there in question. (115)
\end{adjustwidth}
\textit{Kriseis} here are not external judgments altered by emotion-from-without; they are \textit{position-takings} of being-there \textit{from out of} the changed disposition. Heidegger's gloss emphasizes a differentiating-of-oneself-from-oneself, a \textit{being-different-from-what-one-was} in respect of how one stands toward the matter. Second, Heidegger, similarly, insists that pleasure and pain are not consequences that arrive after the emotion, but are \textit{co-given} with it as a ``being-disposed-as-higher-or-lower.'' The \textit{pathos} is the changed-disposition-with-its-position-taking; the pleasure/pain is the tonal-elevation/depression co-given with that change. Accordingly, every \textit{pathos}, as articulated in the \textit{Rhetoric} and Heidegger's gloss is a (1) change-in-disposition that (2) differentiates the subject's position-taking and is (3) co-given with hedonic tonality. 

This three-fold articulation is not, however, first introduced at the level of higher-order \textit{pathē}. The second moment in particular---``we-differentiate-ourselves with respect to position-takings''---is already operative at the perceptual level on Heidegger's own analysis. He reads Aristotle's explanation of \textit{aisthēsis} as establishing that perception is itself ``a \gk{μέσον} [\textit{mesotēs} ``mean''] with the character of \gk{κριτικόν} [\textit{kritikon} ``discerning''], of the ``ability-to-separate'' one thing from another.... It is a being-positioned toward possible objects, which is a \gk{δύναμις} [\textit{dynamic} ``potentiality''] in the sense of \gk{κριτική} [\textit{kritikē} ``faculty of judgment'']'' (126). Aristotle confirms this when he argues the perceiving soul is said to make an ``affirmation or negation'' and to pursue or avoid accordingly (\textit{On the Sou} III.7, 431a8-12). This basic \textit{resonant orexis}---the organism's pre-propositional orientation toward the pleasant and away from the painful \hl{Heeideggerian mood)}---is co-given with perception from the earliest moment of the actualization chain. It is dispositional; it colors the perceptual field with the most general affective tonality of good or bad, pursue or avoid. The position-taking moment is therefore \textit{constitutive} of perception with affective valence; it is not introduced anew by \textit{doxa}. \textit{Resonant orexis} satisfies the differentiation aspect \textit{pre-propositionally} but not \textit{pre-positionally}---as the perceiver's already-being-disposed-toward the perceived as pursue-able or avoid-able---while \textit{doxa} later re-articulates this position-taking (existential-\textit{hermeneutical ``as''}) \textit{propositionally} (\textit{apophantic ``as''}). The remaining two aspects similarly hold at the basic level. The first---\textit{metaballontes}(``those-undergoing change'')---is satisfied trivially: ``being pained or pleased, or thinking'' are themselves \textit{kineseis} of the soul (\textit{On the Soul} I.4, 408b5-7), and basic affective valence is just this \textit{kinetic} mode at perceptual actualization. The third---co-givenness of pleasure and pain---is just what \textit{resonant orexis} is. Emotion (\textit{pathē}), by contrast, is more articulationally concrete. Anger is not undifferentiated displeasure but ``desire accompanied by pain for conspicuous revenge for a conspicuous slight'' (\textit{Rhetoric} II.2, 1378a30). Each emotion, in fact, in Aristotle's taxonomy specifies a propositional evaluation,\footnote{For anger, the slight; for fear, the anticipated evil, for pity, the undeserved suffering (\textit{Rhetoric} II.2, 1378a30-32 for anger; II.5, 1382a21-22 for fear; II.8, 1385b13-15 for pity).} a conative orientation,\footnote{the \textit{desire} to retaliate, the \textit{desire} to flee, and the \textit{desire} that the suffering cease \hl{(CITE)}.} and a hedonic tonality (the pain or pleasure that attends the whole complex). It is this tripartite determinacy that separates emotion from the basic affective valence of pursuit-and-avoidance. The \textit{Rhetoric}'s three-aspect articulation is therefore not a distinguishing mark of the higher-order \textit{pathē} but the general structure of any \textit{pathos}, applicable at every articulational concretion.
