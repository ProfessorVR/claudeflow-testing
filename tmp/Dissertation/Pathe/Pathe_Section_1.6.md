\subsection{Variable Presence: Basic Affective Valence/\textit{Resonant Orexis} and Articulationally Developed \textit{Pathē}}

\inlinenote{different subsection title.}

In light of analyzing emotion as Aristotle presents it across \textit{De Anima}, \textit{Rhetoric}, and \textit{Movement of Animals}, we can surmise the perception-to-movement chain (the actualization of desire) of a rational animal confronting an emotionally significant object. Sense-perception (or thinking) delivers the \textit{resonant aisthema} (formal content) together with the \textit{resonant orexis} (basic affective valence): the object is registered as pleasant or painful, and the soul's assertoric or \textit{apophantic} pursuit/avoidance orientation is engaged. This constitutes the first moment of the chain. The \textit{phantasma} that results from perception carries this affective charge as a dispositional property---the image of the perceived object retains its valence---but the charge is latent rather than \hl{mobilizing}. At this stage, the soul ``remains unaffected'' in the higher-order sense: there is no fear, no anger, no pity, only the basic orientation of pursuit or avoidance. The transition to genuine emotion occurs when \textit{doxa} forms on the basis of the \textit{phantasma}. The rational agent \textit{takes the object to be} genuinely fearful, offensive, pitiable, or shameful and ``emotion is immediately produced.'' The \textit{doxa} transforms the \textit{phantasma's} latent affective valence---or in the case of direct perception, the basic hedonic tonality---into a determinate emotional state: the undifferentiated ``this is bad'' becomes the specific ``this is a destructive future evil'' (fear), or ``this is an undeserved slight'' (anger). Each emotion specifies a particular evaluative structure, a particular conative orientation, and a particular pattern of physiological preparation. The emotion then does genuine causal work. Its physiological component---the heating, cooling, trembling, blushing, etc. associated with the emotions---prepares the organic parts for the specific kind of movement that the emotion's conative component requires. The boiling of blood in anger prepares the body for aggressive action; the cooling of blood in fear prepares the body for flight or freezing. In this way, emotion is the hinge between the cognitive evaluation (\textit{doxa}) and the bodily movement (\textit{kinesis}): it translates judgment into somatic readiness. The resulting desire (\textit{orexis})---the ``moved mover''---is thus not a separate faculty that operates independently of emotion but is, in cases of emotional action, the conative component of the emotion itself. The desire for retaliation that constitutes part of anger is the \textit{orexis} that moves the angry agent toward aggressive action. The desire to flee that constitutes part of fear is the \textit{orexis} that moves the fearful agent toward flight. Emotion, on my reading, is the actualization of the \textit{orektikon} capacity into a specific form of desire directed at a specific object under a specific evaluative description.

A critical question we must now ask is whether emotion is a necessary intermediate for every action or whether it is variably present depending on the character of the object and the nature of the desire involved. My reading of the texts suggests the later. However, I must note that a complete treatment of action belongs in the final section of this chapter. Here a brief demonstration of the variable presence of emotion in relation to movement must suffice, as one of my justifications for the differentiation between basic affective valence/\textit{resonant orexis} and the \textit{pathē} depends on it. Consider the drinking example Aristotle gives us in \textit{Movement of Animals}: ``I want to drink, says appetite; this is drink, says sense or imagination or thought: straightaway I drink'' (701a32-33). Here, the chain runs from appetite directly to action, mediate either by \textit{aisthēsis} or the \textit{phantasma}. The action is, so to speak, emotionally transparent: it passes through the system without engaging the more articulationally concrete \textit{pathē}.

The presence of emotion, therefore, seems to be variable and tracks the distinction between simple appetitive actions and evaluatively complex actions. Where the object is straightforwardly pleasant or painful and the desire is basic appetite or aversion, emotion in the higher-order sense is not required; basic affective valence, \textit{resonant orexis}, suffices to direct the organism toward or away from the object. Where the object engages the evaluative categories of the \textit{Rhetoric}, emotion is a necessary intermediate that specifies the particular form of desire, the particular pattern of somatic preparation, and the particular character of the resulting action. This distinction corresponds, at least approximately, to the distinction between actions mediated by \textit{phantasia} alone and actions mediate by \textit{doxa}: since higher-order emotion requires \textit{doxa}, and since \textit{doxa} requires \textit{logos}, emotion enters the chain specifically at the point where the rational faculties engage with the \textit{phantasmatic} content. In the drinking case, the appetitive faculty responds to \textit{phantasia} without \textit{logos}; in the fear case, the rational faculty forms a \textit{doxa} on the basis of the \textit{phantasma}, and emotion follows. 

 Emotion follows not judgment (\textit{krisis}) but appearance-laden belief: e.g. that \textit{seems} dangerous. In this sense, emotion is not the outcome of rational deliberation but a pre-rational movement of the soul in response to a \textit{phantasmatic} presentation. Emotion responds to \textit{how things appear}, not to how reason later adjudicates them. It operates, as Heidegger would later say, at the level of \textit{Stimmung} or \textit{mood}: the atmospheric attunement that precedes and conditions intelligibility itself (\hl{BCAP 13-18}). Aristotle confirms the internal motional character of basic affective valence and emotion when he writes that ``being pained or pleased, or thinking, are movements (each of them a being-moved), and the movement is originated by the soul'' (\textit{DA} 408b5-7). Pain and pleasure are not passive accompaniments but dynamic modifications of the soul's orientation towards perceived goods and evils. They indicate how the soul is already oriented \textit{toward} or \textit{away} from something perceived as good or evil. The soul, in short, \textit{moves itself}---and what we call emotion is the form this self-movement takes when shaped by particular appearances. This is precisely Aristotle's point in \textit{Movement of Animals}: animals and humans move ``for the sake of something,'' and all such movement arises from intellect, imagination, appetite, and desire (701a16-24). \hl{Except for intellect}, all of these are reducible to desire (\textit{orexis}); and desire, in turn, depends on \textit{phantasia's} presentational content. As Nussbaum notes in her commentary, \textit{phantasia} is indispensable because it supplies the \textit{material} for practical reasoning and action (\hl{CITE}). Emotion, therefore, is not merely a cause of later motion: emotion is a motion, the soul's kinetic alignment toward a world disclosed as mattering in a particular way.
