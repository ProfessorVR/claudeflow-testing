## Dow 2011 — "Aristotle's Theory of the Emotions: Emotions as Pleasures"

### Q-DOW-01
- Locus: p. 246 (DA 427b21-4 commentary)
- Verbatim: "Furthermore, whenever we believe something terrible or fearsome, we immediately have an emotion, and likewise if it is something encouraging. Whereas in the case of appearance, we are in the kind of state we would be if seeing terrible or encouraging things in a painting."
- Supports: Doxa-gate thesis; emotion is "immediately produced" once doxastic uptake occurs.
- Insertion: Section 1.4 near "emotion is immediately produced" (DA III.3, 427b21-24 quotation in opening paragraph).
- Use as: in-text citation (Dow's translation as alternative rendering)

### Q-DOW-02
- Locus: p. 246
- Verbatim: "The contrast here is between belief and phantasia, as the context makes clear, in relation to a similar object. Aristotle's point is that belief is sufficient for emotion, whereas phantasia is not. Taken together, this strongly suggests (though it is not explicit) that Aristotle's position is that belief is also necessary for emotion."
- Supports: Doxa as the necessary cognitive gate that converts phantasma into pathē.
- Insertion: Section 1.4 near "doxa, for rational animals, serves as not only a condition but also as a cognitive gate through which emotion must pass."
- Use as: in-text citation

### Q-DOW-03
- Locus: p. 246
- Verbatim: "Aristotle's assertion about fear in this passage is very close to what he says in Rhetoric II.5, 1382b33, 'those who believe they might suffer something necessarily are afraid.'"
- Supports: Fear requires doxastic uptake; the believing-it-to-be-the-case-for-me Heidegger emphasizes.
- Insertion: Section 1.4 near Heidegger's gloss "the one who is brought into fear must once 'believe' that the definite thing that threatens, threatens him."
- Use as: footnote

### Q-DOW-04
- Locus: p. 247 (concluding the DA III.3 reading)
- Verbatim: "this passage seems to be a rather general observation about emotional experience, and as such suggests that Aristotle took it to be simply obvious that emotions involve beliefs."
- Supports: Belief/doxa is constitutive for higher-order pathē, not an optional accessory.
- Insertion: Section 1.4 supporting the "involuntary belief that the object is genuinely fearful" claim.
- Use as: footnote

### Q-DOW-05
- Locus: p. 192-193 (Rhet 1378a19-21 commentary)
- Verbatim: "what seems to be needed is for Aristotle to be insisting that emotions have as an essential feature pain and pleasure that is connected in the right kind of way to the rest of the emotion. In fear, it is not merely any pain that is involved, but pain that is connected to the apparent advent of something bad."
- Supports: Hedonic tonality is constitutive (not consequent) of pathos's three-aspect structure.
- Insertion: Section 1.2 (Rhetoric Threefold) near "hedonic tonality (the pain or pleasure that attends the whole complex)."
- Use as: in-text citation

### Q-DOW-06
- Locus: p. 196
- Verbatim: "if these lines are an adequate definition of emotion, this suggests that Aristotle's view is that emotions involve distinctive judgements. … Aristotle's choice of vocabulary here seems to make his definition over-restrictive. 'Krisis' generally means a verdict or decision reached after considering both sides of an issue."
- Supports: Krisis-aspect of pathos = position-taking (Heidegger's translation), not external verdict.
- Insertion: Section 1.2 near Heidegger's gloss "Kriseis here are not external judgments altered by emotion-from-without; they are position-takings."
- Use as: footnote (provides philological support for Heidegger's reading)

### Q-DOW-07
- Locus: p. 258 (Appendix)
- Verbatim: "our proposal is that Aristotle thinks that emotions are pleasures and pains. … 1. To have an emotion is to experience pain, pleasure or both. 2. The pain and pleasure involved in having an emotion is … intentional and representational: it is pain/pleasure at the emotion's object or 'target' and involves that target being represented in ways that give 'grounds' for the particular emotion experienced."
- Supports: The hedonic-evaluative composite of pathē; emotion as intentional pleasure/pain.
- Insertion: Section 1.4 near "each emotion specifies a cognitive content, a hedonic valence, and (in most cases) a conative component."
- Use as: in-text citation

### Q-DOW-08
- Locus: p. 263
- Verbatim: "An account of emotions as pleasures and pains also seems well-placed to offer an appropriately unified account of various features of emotions. The features in question are the cognitions and consequent rational and inferential relations, the phenomenology, associated motivations, and possibly also the physiological changes involved."
- Supports: Composite (cognitive + conative + hedonic + physiological) account converging at the emotion node.
- Insertion: Section 1.3 near "no analysis or explanation of emotion that reduces it to any one of these components will suffice."
- Use as: in-text citation

### Q-DOW-09
- Locus: p. 263 (pity example)
- Verbatim: "Pitying Colin, on this account, might be the painful judgement that he is suffering undeservedly (where what is painful about the judgement is its object: that he is suffering undeservedly). The fact that this is a judgement means (e.g.) that it is rational … But additionally, for the judgement to be genuinely painful probably requires the deployment of some specific bodily apparatus in the way the judgement is instantiated."
- Supports: Pity as enmattered logos — judgment realized in body, with motivational/conative consequence.
- Insertion: Section 1.4 in the pity discussion ("the cognitive content is complex … the conative element could be the desire to avoid or avert harm").
- Use as: footnote (supports pity's tripartite analysis)

### Q-DOW-10
- Locus: p. 264 (Conclusion)
- Verbatim: "Aristotle insists on two key features in his account of the passions. One is that they play a role (or are capable of playing a role) in verdict formation. The other is that they involve pleasure and pain. We have argued for the view that pleasure or pain is essential to Aristotelian emotions … This requires a representational theory of pleasure and pain, not merely one specified in terms of physiological process."
- Supports: Pathē are doxa-modifying AND hedonic; both features are essential, irreducibly tied.
- Insertion: Section 1.9 conclusion; supports the closing list of constitutive elements of pathē.
- Use as: in-text citation


## Corcilius 2013 — "Aristotle's Model of Animal Motion"

### Q-COR-01
- Locus: p. 54 (DA I.4, 408b1-18 commentary)
- Verbatim: "Aristotle does not wish to deny that affections in question are motions. On the contrary, he accepts that they are. What he denies is that the soul is the proper subject of these motions. … the soul is only the terminus and the origin of motions that take place inside the body, not something that itself undergoes these motions."
- Supports: Pathē-as-kinēsis thesis (Section 1.1); emotion is genuinely motion, with the soul as origin/terminus.
- Insertion: Section 1.1 near "being pained or pleased, or thinking, are movements" (DA I.4, 408b5-7).
- Use as: footnote

### Q-COR-02
- Locus: p. 54
- Verbatim: "anger or fear consists in the heart being moved in such and such a way, and thinking consists perhaps in this being moved or something else; of these modifications some arise when certain parts are moved locally, others when certain parts are altered."
- Supports: The enmattered-logos thesis: anger/fear ARE bodily motions, not symptoms of hidden judgments.
- Insertion: Section 1.3 ("Enmattered Accounts") near "the shaking and the blood-rush are not symptoms of a hidden judgment."
- Use as: in-text citation (Aristotle's Greek through Corcilius's translation)

### Q-COR-03
- Locus: pp. 65-66
- Verbatim: "object → perception (alteration) → pleasure and pain / desire (thermic alteration). The object causes perception, which is itself an alteration, and this in turn causes thermic alteration, heating or chilling, which corresponds to the feeling of pleasure or pain as well as to the appetite or revulsion."
- Supports: Causal chain at perceptual-orexis level (basic affective valence), the schema underlying the Section 1.5 actualization chain.
- Insertion: Section 1.5 near "the causal chain presented by Aristotle in Movement of Animals."
- Use as: footnote (provides structural diagram support)

### Q-COR-04
- Locus: p. 65 (MA 701b-702a commentary)
- Verbatim: "are accompanied by heating and chilling — although we do not notice this when it happens in a small part — for the painful is avoided and pleasant pursued, and painful and pleasant things are almost all accompanied by some chilling and heating."
- Supports: Somatic-preparatory function of pathē in MA causal chain.
- Insertion: Section 1.5 near "the object we pursue or avoid in the field of action is … the origin of movement … there necessarily follows a heating or chilling" (701b33-702a2).
- Use as: in-text citation

### Q-COR-05
- Locus: p. 66 footnote 15
- Verbatim: "In the immediate sequel of the text Aristotle will give examples for this: emotions, feelings and the experience of all bodily pleasurable and painful things, he says, are accompanied by heatings and chillings of the body."
- Supports: Direct attribution of heating/cooling mechanism to "emotions, feelings" in MA — confirms pathē as somatic preparation.
- Insertion: Section 1.5 supporting "the physiological changes associated with emotion are not incidental accompaniments but the mechanism by which desire is realized in bodily movement."
- Use as: footnote

### Q-COR-06
- Locus: p. 73 (MA 702a17-19)
- Verbatim: "For the affections [i.e. the hardening and softenings of the flesh around the joints] suitably prepare the instrumental parts [i.e. the limbs], desire the affections, and phantasia the desire; and phantasia comes about either through thought or through perception."
- Supports: Confirms the Aristotelian sequence: perception/thought → phantasia → desire → affections → organic parts.
- Insertion: Section 1.5 opening near "sense-perception or thinking → imagination → desire → affections → organic parts → movement."
- Use as: in-text citation (or epigraph for Section 1.5)

### Q-COR-07
- Locus: p. 67
- Verbatim: "desire, in its simplest form, is a motion — that is a thermic alteration — in the animal body, and it comes about whenever the animal perceives an object which is conducive or detrimental to the bodily state in accordance with the animal's nature. Such a desire has no intentional content distinct from what is available to the animal through the senses."
- Supports: Basic appetition (Section 1.6 drinking case) — perceptually grounded desire without doxastic articulation.
- Insertion: Section 1.6 near "the chain runs from appetite directly to action, mediated either by aisthēsis or the phantasma."
- Use as: footnote

### Q-COR-08
- Locus: p. 68 (MA 7, 701b16-23, citing Nussbaum)
- Verbatim: "Alteration is caused by phantasiai and sense-perceptions and thoughts. For sense-perceptions are at once a kind of alteration and phantasia and thinking have the power of the actual things. For it turns out that the form conceived of the warm or cold or pleasant or fearful is like the actual thing itself. That is why we shudder and are frightened just thinking of something."
- Supports: Phantasia carries causal/affective force of its object — basis for appearance-laden emotion (Section 1.7 feedback loop, Section 1.9 false belief generates real fear).
- Insertion: Section 1.9 near "False beliefs or opinions generate real emotions: The sound mistaken for a rattlesnake produces real fear."
- Use as: in-text citation

### Q-COR-09
- Locus: p. 77
- Verbatim: "the alteration in the heart which is produced by an external object and which on its part brings about thermic alteration and its subsequent mechanical effects, is not a mere alteration, but an alteration with intentional dimension."
- Supports: Intentionality enters the chain at perception; affection chain is "lit by intentionality" — supports pathos as a kinetic-meaningful event, not blind mechanism.
- Insertion: Section 1.5 near "the causal chain … gives us the clearest illustration of the relation between psychological states and action."
- Use as: footnote

### Q-COR-10
- Locus: p. 80 (DA 432a9f. paraphrase + MA 7)
- Verbatim: "phantasia brings with it a significant emancipation from causal determination by the animal's immediate environment available to perception. Animals in possession of phantasia are spontaneous in the sense that they can move even if the objects actually available to their senses are not really good or bad for them but only appear so, or even in the absence of any available object."
- Supports: Phantasia as bridge between absent objects and present affect — foundational for Section 1.9 ("emotion can contradict judgment … emotion is keyed to appearance").
- Insertion: Section 1.9 near "phantasia is not a decorative adjunct to emotion; it is the condition of possibility for the soul being-moved by absences, possibilities, appearances, and counterfactuals."
- Use as: in-text citation
