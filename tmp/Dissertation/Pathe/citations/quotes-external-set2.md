## Christensen 2016 — "Aristotle on Anger, Justice and Punishment" (UCL MPhil thesis)

Relevance: directly bears on Section 1.3 (anger as the paradigmatic enmattered *pathos*), Section 1.4 (*doxa* and the cognitive content of emotion), and Section 1.6 (anger's somatic preparation in the causal chain).

### Q-CHR-01
- Locus: pp. 7–8
- Verbatim: "we may take the participles to emphasise rather that the participles emphasises that the definition explains how things appear to the angry person, how he takes things to be. Thus, it is intrinsic to anger that one thinks one has been slighted, but not that one has actually been slighted."
- Supports: Anger turns on *appearance* and *taking-as*, not on the actual fact — substantiates the *doxa*-as-cognitive-gate thesis (the *appearance-laden* belief grounding emotion).
- Insertion: Section 1.6 near "Emotion follows not judgment (\textit{krisis}) but appearance-laden belief: e.g. that \textit{seems} dangerous."
- Use as: in-text citation

### Q-CHR-02
- Locus: p. 8
- Verbatim: "The participles draw out two features of anger (and emotions in general) in Aristotle's analysis. First, they show that anger involves cognitive content, a set of beliefs or impressions about the world. The angry person takes himself to have been undeservedly slighted, and therefore come to desire what he thinks will constitute revenge."
- Supports: Anger has both cognitive content (the perceived slight) and a conative orientation (the desire for revenge) — directly substantiates the tripartite structure asserted in 1.3 and 1.4.
- Insertion: Section 1.4 near "the cognitive content is the perceived slight; the conative component is the desire for revenge; and, the hedonic component is pain."
- Use as: footnote

### Q-CHR-03
- Locus: p. 9
- Verbatim: "Angry people, he writes, 'dwell in their minds (dianoiai) on taking revenge; and thus the imagination (phantasia), which occurs, creates pleasure, as in dreams' (Rhet. 1378b8-10). So while angry, we are not yet taking pleasure in actual revenge, but have a quasi-perceptual experience (phantasia) of the revenge to come, which either is itself pleasant or causes us to feel pleasure."
- Supports: The pleasure of anger is *phantasia*-mediated — anger reaches into the future via *phantasma*, illustrating temporal depth of *pathos*.
- Insertion: Section 1.9 near "Emotion is not anchored to the present; it reaches into past and future by means of \textit{phantasmatic} appearance."

### Q-CHR-04
- Locus: pp. 9–10
- Verbatim: "Aristotle certainly thinks that spirited desire (thumos), of which anger is an expression, is painful (EE. 1225b30–31). Certainly, Aristotle conceives of anger as an embodied phenomenon (e.g. in De Anima, Aristotle describes anger as involving 'boiling blood' around the heart (DA 403a29-b1).)"
- Supports: Anger as enmattered: the somatic disruption is not contingent symptom but constitutive of the painful character of anger.
- Insertion: Section 1.3 near "anger is the \textit{desire} for retaliation \textit{realized in} the boiling of blood around the heart."

### Q-CHR-05

- Supports: Direct statement of the enmattered-accounts thesis: emotions involve both material change and rich cognitive structure — load-bearing for 1.3.
- Insertion: Section 1.3 directly opening: "Aristotle establishes the ontological framework for understanding emotion when he argues that all affections of the soul are 'enmattered accounts'."

### Q-CHR-06

## Caston 2021 — "Aristotle and the Cartesian Theatre" (chapter 7 of edited volume)

Relevance: directly bears on Section 1.2 (the articulational distinction between *phantasia* and the higher-order *pathē*), Section 1.4 (*phantasma* + *doxa* → emotion), and Section 1.5 (*De Insomniis* feedback loop / the disabled "controlling sense"). Caston's representational-but-not-imagistic reading of *phantasma* directly supports the chapter's *resonant kinēsis* thesis.

### Q-CST-01
- Locus: pp. 170–171
- Verbatim: "phantasmata are representations and in some sense likenesses of what they represent, they are not themselves viewed and do not look like the objects they are about. They are states of our body, bearing representational content, in virtue of which we can imagine or visualize things, as well as remember, desire, dream, and even think. But they are not themselves the objects of these states and activities: the objects, rather, are what they represent."
- Supports: *Phantasmata* are bodily states with representational content — a residual *kinēsis*, not an inner image. Directly supports the *resonant kinēsis*/*resonant aisthēma* identification of Section 1.1.
- Insertion: Section 1.1 inline note: "the residual affective valence that forms part of the residual motion (\textit{resonant kinesis} is the \textit{resonant orexis})."
- Use as: in-text citation (load-bearing)

### Q-CST-02
- Locus: p. 171
- Verbatim: "Being in such a state, he continues, is something 'like viewing fgures in a painting' (ὥσπερ ἂν εἰ θεώμενοι ἐν γραφῇ, 427b23–24) insofar as it needn't produce an emotional reaction in us, even when what is depicted is arousing or terrifying."
- Supports: Direct gloss on DA III.3 427b21–24 — the very passage the chapter uses to establish that bare *phantasia* without *doxa* leaves the soul "unaffected" in the higher-order sense.
- Insertion: Section 1.4 directly at "Aristotle's statement that when merely imagining we remain 'unaffected' superficially seems to be a counter-example."
- Use as: in-text citation (load-bearing)

### Q-CST-03
- Locus: pp. 175–176
- Verbatim: "Phantasmata, I would argue, are like this. They are not miniature paintings or theatre productions, but something more like image fles or phonograph records. But in general they do not themselves resemble or look like what they represent."
- Supports: *Phantasmata* are not images-on-display but kinetic states that generate experience under appropriate activation — coheres with the chapter's account of *phantasma* as causal-representational, not picture-like.
- Insertion: Section 1.2 near "the \textit{phantasma} that results from perception carries this affective charge as a dispositional property---the image of the perceived object retains its valence---but the charge is latent rather than mobilizing."

### Q-CST-04
- Locus: p. 176
- Verbatim: "The change (κίνησις) that constitutes phantasia is distinct from both the experience of visualization and the objects that seem to appear in such experiences. - Supports: *Phantasma* is a *kinēsis* that physically traverses the body to the central organ — anchors the chapter's claim that emotion-as-*kinēsis* is not a "psychic event" but a bodily-kinetic process.
- Insertion: Section 1.6 near "The 'boiling of blood around the heart' that Aristotle identifies as the material component of anger in \textit{De Anima} is the same phenomenon that he identifies as the 'affection' that prepares the organic parts for movement in \textit{Movement of Animals}."

## Sheehan 2015 — *Making Sense of Heidegger: A Paradigm Shift* (Rowman & Littlefield)

Relevance: useful for Section 1.1 framing and Section 1.5 *Befindlichkeit*/thrownness. Sheehan's book is a wide-angle reconstruction of Heidegger's whole project; the *Pathe* chapter draws principally on the Aristotle-Rhetoric-rooted lectures (GA 18) and on *Being and Time* §29–33. Only those Sheehan passages that genuinely advance or clarify the chapter's argument are extracted; the book's relevance is real but selective.

### Q-SHE-01
- Locus: pp. 49–50
- Verbatim: "movement is incomplete reality; reality is completed movement.… Aristotle's thought managed to hold together both incompletion and completion under the aegis of οὐσία, while reserving absolute stability and permanence—in fact, absolute self-presence—to the self-coincident Unmoved Mover."
- Supports: The *kinēsis*/*entelecheia* structure that grounds *paschein* as a mode of being — orients the chapter's claim that emotion is *kinēsis* of the soul, not a static "psychic experience."
- Insertion: Section 1.1 near "They are a \textit{movement} (kinēsis) of the soul, a dynamic reorientation within the field of appearances that alters how the world shows up."
- Use as: footnote

### Q-SHE-02
- Locus: p. 50 (note 94)
- Verbatim: "The deity, as the ideal of unchanging reality, moves everything else in the world via 'final causality'—that is, by being desired by all nature (κινεἶ ὡς ἐρώμενον), just as the desired object (ὀρεκτόν) initiates and moves the desire for it (ὄρεξις)."
- Supports: Direct gloss on the *kinoun-orekton-orexis* chain of *DA* III.10 — Aristotle's three-factor schema that the chapter at Section 1.6 explicitly invokes ("the moved mover").
- Insertion: Section 1.6 near "The three-factor schema of \textit{DA} III.10 is preserved---\textit{orexis} remains the moved mover, the realizable good remains the unmoved originator."
- Use as: footnote

### Q-SHE-03
- Locus: p. 161
- Verbatim: "We are most immediately aware of our factical thrown-openness through feelings or moods. Heidegger anchors these in the structural moment of ex-sistence that he calls Befindlichkeit, the condition of affective familiarity with a given context of meaning and its contents. Such affective attunement is the primordial way that a world of meaning is opened up to us."
- Supports: *Befindlichkeit* (*pathos*-grade *finding-oneself*) is the *primordial* opening of world-as-meaningful — load-bearing for Section 1.1's claim that *pathos* is "the primary being-oriented" (Heidegger 176).
- Insertion: Section 1.1 near "the \gk{πάθη} are not merely an annex of psychical processes, but are rather \textit{the ground out of which speaking arises}."
- Use as: in-text citation (load-bearing)

### Q-SHE-04
- Locus: p. 161
- Verbatim: "Ordinary moods disclose to our embodied and affective understanding the meaning of individual things and the encompassing context (the 'world') that bestows such meaning.… Moods are a form of absorption not just in things but also and more basically in the meaning-giving world in which they are situated—absorption to the second power, as it were."
- Supports: Mood discloses both the individual matter and the encompassing world-context — supports Section 1.7's "disclosive function" and Section 1.5's claim that *pathos* discloses *being-in-the-world as a whole*.
- Insertion: Section 1.5 directly at "\textit{The mood has already disclosed, in every case, Being-in-the-world as a whole, and makes it possible first of all to direct oneself towards something} (BT 176)."
