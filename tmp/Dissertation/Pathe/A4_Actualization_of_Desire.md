\subsubsection*{Actualization of Desire: \textit{Phantasia} in Every Action}

The foregoing account places \textit{phantasia} at the center of every moment of the chain, and its centrality deserves explicit statement in closing. Aristotle defines \textit{phantasia} as ``a movement resulting from actual perception'' (\textit{DA} III.3, 429a1--2), a residual motion that persists after the object has withdrawn and, by persisting, recombining, and re-presenting, supplies the condition for emotional response in the absence of immediate stimuli. Remembering, hoping, fearing, pitying: each is a case of the soul being-moved by a \textit{phantasma} of what is not-present but which nevertheless appears \textit{as if} present. ``Both the man who remembers and the man who hopes,'' Aristotle observes, ``will be attended by an imagination of what he remembers or hopes'' (\textit{Rhetoric} I.11, 1370a28--30). The temporal depth of emotional life is accordingly supplied by \textit{phantasia}: the feared evil does not yet exist, yet the \textit{phantasma} of it does, and the soul moves as though the feared thing were present. Fear---``a pain or disturbance due to imagining some destructive or painful evil in the future'' (\textit{Rhetoric} II.5, 1382a21--22)---is paradigmatic, for it names the emotion whose object is definitionally an anticipated \textit{phantasma} rather than a present sensible.

The centrality of \textit{phantasia} extends beyond the temporal horizon of emotion to the very possibility of practical reasoning and rhetorical persuasion. Nussbaum observes that \textit{phantasia} is indispensable because it supplies the material for practical reasoning---the images upon which deliberative \textit{phantasia} operates when it synthesizes alternatives into unrealized possibilities and measures them against a single standard.\footnote{Nussbaum, \textit{The Fragility of Goodness}, on \textit{phantasia}'s role as the material supplier of practical reasoning; see also Nussbaum's commentary on the \textit{De Motu Animalium}.} Hawhee demonstrates, correlatively, that rhetorical persuasion depends on the rhetor's ability to activate the audience's phantasmatic capacity---to supply images concrete, narrative, and affectively charged enough to allow the audience to see what the rhetor wants them to see (\cite{hawhee_aristotles_eyes}, 145). The imagistic seeing that rhetoric induces grounds the subsequent formation of \textit{doxa}, the ensuing movement of emotion, and the eventual act of judgment. The rhetorical cascade follows the actualization chain precisely: new \textit{phantasma} $\rightarrow$ new \textit{doxa} $\rightarrow$ new emotion $\rightarrow$ new orientation $\rightarrow$ new action.

O'Gorman captures the synthesis: \textit{phantasia} ``forms the objects of belief and desire, even gives birth to belief and desire and provides the images that are the objects of deliberative desire, ethical choice, and political judgment'' (\cite{ogorman_aristotles_phantasia}, 34). Every action, on Aristotle's account, is mediated by \textit{phantasia}, because every action is mediated by an appearance---whether the immediate \textit{phantasma} that identifies the present object as drink, or the settled \textit{phantasma} that informs the universal premise of a practical syllogism, or the projected \textit{phantasma} that deliberative reasoning synthesizes into an anticipated possibility, or the vivid \textit{phantasma} that rhetorical \gk{ἐνέργεια} induces in the audience. Emotion enters the chain wherever such an appearance discloses evaluatively complex content that \textit{doxa} ratifies as true, and it enters as the form desire takes under that disclosure. What \textit{phantasia} presents, \textit{doxa} commits to; what \textit{doxa} commits to, emotion mobilizes; what emotion mobilizes, action completes. Emotion is not, accordingly, an add-on to rationality, nor a disruption of cognition, but the way the soul's self-movement discloses significance at the precise point where significance must become motion. The continuity between the Aristotelian analysis of \textit{pathos} and the Heideggerian analysis of \textit{Stimmung} is evident: Heidegger reads \textit{pathos} as the primordial attunement that constitutes the clearing in which beings can appear at all, radicalizing the Aristotelian insight that emotion is a mode of world-disclosure rather than a psychological addendum. For Aristotle, emotion is the kinetic, affective, evaluative dimension of how the world appears to us. It is grounded in \textit{phantasia}, shaped through involuntary belief, felt as pleasure or pain, and expressed as motion toward or away from what the soul takes to be significant. Emotion is not an add-on to rationality, nor a disruption of cognition, but the way the soul’s self-movement discloses significance at all. In this sense, Aristotle’s theory anticipates later phenomenological accounts—Heidegger’s most explicitly—that interpret emotion as a mode of attunement, a way the world already “matters” before deliberation begins. Emotion, in its full Aristotelian sense, is nothing less than the \textit{affective architecture of being-in-the-world}.


\textit{Phantasia} and the \textit{phantasma}, therefore, serve a specific function in the articulational concretization of the \textit{pathē}. The \textit{phantasma}, via \textit{phantasia}, carries the \textit{resonant orexis} or affective valence forward into the \textit{doxa}-gate for the \textit{phantasma} is what \textit{doxa} operates upon; what \textit{doxa} ratifies as true is the \textit{phantasma}'s \textit{taking-something-as} \hl{(hermeneutical-as)}. The basic valence is the disposition charge the \textit{phantasma} carries forward from perception. 

Consider the drinking example Aristotle gives us in \textit{Movement of Animals}: 
 \begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    And so what we do without reflection, we do quickly. For when a man is actually using perception or imagination or thought in relation to that for the sake of which, what he desires he does at once. For the actualizing of desire is a substitute for inquiry or thinking. I want to drink, says appetite; this is drink, says sense or imagination or thought: straightaway I drink. In this way living creatures are impelled to move and to act, and desire is the last cause of movement, and desire arises through perception or through imagination and thought. And things that desire to act make and act sometimes from appetite or impulse and sometimes from wish. (701a29-b1)
 \end{adjustwidth}
 Here, the chain runs from appetite directly to action, mediated by a \textit{phantasma} that identifies the present object as drink. No \textit{doxa} intervenes; no emotion is mentioned; the desire is simple appetite responding to a sensory presentation. The action is, so to speak, emotionally transparent---it passes through the system without engaging the higher-order evaluative structures that produce fear, anger, or pity. By contrast, fear involves emotion as a necessary intermediate. The person who \textit{takes the object to be} fearful does not merely desire to flee; the person \textit{fears}, and it is the fear---the composite of cognitive evaluation, conative orientation, and physiological preparation---that specifies the form of the ensuing desire and action. Three observations can sharpen the case. First, Aristotle says explicitly that ``the actualizing of desire is a substitute for inquiry or thinking''---the \textit{orectic} actualization is not nothing; it positively occupies the structural slot that inquiry would otherwise fill. The position-taking moment is satisfied; what is bypassed is its propositional articulation. Second, the disjunction ``sense \textit{or} imagination \textit{or} thought'' explicitly includes perception \textit{alone} as sufficient for the recognition that triggers desire-actualization, \textit{for the rational human as much as for the brute}. Third, ``what we do without reflection, we do quickly'' frames this unreflective swiftness as \textit{normal} for rational-animal appetitive action, not as an aberration from a \textit{doxa}-mediated default. A third case occupies the middle ground: habitual-rational action. Aristotle gives the practical syllogism: a universal premise (the agent's standing \textit{doxa} of what is good or to-be-pursued, a particular premise (perceptual recognition of the present circumstance), and an action as conclusion. \textit{Doxa} is operative here, but its content is not evaluatively complex in the \textit{Rhetoric}'s sense---it does not engage slight, threat, undeserved suffering or disgrace. The chain, therefore, runs basic \textit{pathos}  \ra \textit{doxa} \ra \textit{orexis} \ra action without \textit{pathē} concretizing in the middle. The pattern across the three cases---simple appetition (no \textit{doxa}), habitual-rational action (\textit{doxa} without evaluative complexity), evaluatively complex action (\textit{doxa} with the \textit{Rhetoric}'s categories)---shows that \textit{doxa} is \textit{necessary but not sufficient} for the higher-order \textit{pathē}: the gate is \textit{content-conditional}. 

\subsubsection{Types of Action}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Simple appetition (drinking). $A_0 \to A_1$: basic \textit{pathos} with valenced perception of drink-as-pursue-able is operative. \textit{Doxa} may form (``this is drink'') but its content is not evaluatively complex, and, therefore, no articulationally concrete \textit{pathē} arise. The chain runs from $A_0 \to A_1$ \textit{orexis} \ra action without engaging higher-order articulation. The \textit{position-taking} is operative throughout (perception is \textit{kritikon}, basic valence is a \textit{phasis}(apophantic/assertion)-like stance) but the \textit{doxastic} articulation does not concretize into \textit{pathē}.
    
    \item Habitual-rational action. The practical syllogism that fires from settled \textit{doxa} or \textit{hexis}. Basic \textit{pathos} is operative---\textit{doxa} engages (the universal premise as settled \textit{hexis})---BUT the \textit{doxa}'s content is not evaluatively complex. Therefore, no \textit{pathē}. The chain: basic \textit{pathos} \ra \textit{doxa} \ra \textit{orexis} \ra action, without higher-order concretion. 
    
    \item Evaluative complex action. Basic \textit{pathos} \ra \textit{doxa} \ra \textit{pathē} \ra action. \textit{Doxa}'s content engages the rhetorical evaluative categories: the gate conretizes basic valence into determinate higher-order \textit{pathē}; somatic preparation specific to the emotion follows.
\end{enumerate}
\textit{Doxa} is necessary but not sufficient for the more articulationally concretized \textit{pathē}. What is required is (a) \textit{doxa} forms (involuntary \textit{taking-as-something-true}) AND (b) the \textit{doxa}'s content engages evaluatively complex categories. When (a) holds without (b), one has habitual-rational action without \textit{pathē}. When (a) and (b) both hold, one has evaluatively complex action with full higher-order \textit{pathē}.

``But how is it that thought is sometimes followed by action, sometimes not; sometimes by movement, sometimes not? What happens seems parallel to the case of thinking and inferring about the immovable objects. There the end is the truth seen (for, when one thinks the two propositions, one thinks and puts together the conclusion), but here the two propositions result in a conclusion which is an action---for example, whenever one thinks that every man ought to walk, and that one is a man oneself, straightaway one walks; or that, in this case, no man should walk, one is a man: straightaway one remains at rest. And one so acts in the two cases provided that there is nothing to compel or to prevent'' (\textit{MA} 7, 701a7-16). 

In Aristotle's example here, we see a habitual-rational action that engages \textit{doxa} without engaging the \textit{pathē} of \textit{Rhetoric}. The ``good'' premise is a settled doxa (``ever man ought to walk'' because walking is good for health) rather than an appetitive want (``I \textit{want} to drink'' or ``I \textit{want} to walk''); the ``possible'' premise is the perceptual self-recognition (``I am a man''); the conclusion is the action itself. \textit{Doxa} is fully operative; what is absent is \textit{evaluatively-complex} \textit{doxa}-content (no slight, threat, undeserved suffering, disgrace, etc.)

For example: having internalized the \textit{doxa} ``walking is good for thee body'' and encountering the perceptual situation that this is a walking-opportunity (``the morning weather is cool'' or ``I have time now''), the agent straightaway walks. The chain runs: basic \textit{pathos} (the body-as-feeling-confined, the morning-as-pleasant) \ra \textit{doxa} (``I should walk'') \ra \textit{orexis} (toward walking) \ra action (walking). No anger, no fear, no pity, no shame; this is not appetitive in the simple sense of the drinking case. The agent's action is \textit{rationally structured} by a settled doxa, not driven by raw appetite. 

Pianist: Contemporary example for the chapter: an expert pianist performing a well-rehearsed sonata. The pianist has internalized  many settled \textit{doxai}: "this passage takes the soft pedal," "voice the inner line under the melody," "release the chord before the next downbeat." Encountering the perceptual situation (the keyboard under their hands, the score's unfolding demands), each settled \textit{doxa} fires almost automatically, generating the next musical action. \textit{Doxa} is fully engaged at every step; the pianist is not playing from raw appetite but from rationally articulated craft-knowledge. And yet---barring a memory slip or a broken string---no higher-order \textit{pathē} arise. There is no anger at the arpeggio, no fear of the trill, no shame before the cadence. The chain runs through \textit{doxa} without concretizing as anger or fear, because the \textit{doxa}'s content (procedural-musical craft-knowledge) does not engage the evaluative categories of the \textit{Rhetoric}.

This is the cleanest case for the content-conditional reading of the doxa-gate. Case 1 (drinking) shows that \textit{doxa} is not necessary for some actions; Case 2 (walking-for-health, expert pianist) shows that \textit{doxa} is not \textit{sufficient} for more articulationally concrete \textit{pathē}---its content must engage evaluatively-complex categories (slight, threat, undeserved suffering, disgrace) before the higher-order \textit{pathē} arise. The pattern across Cases 1, 2, 3 establishes that the \textit{doxa}-gate is content-conditional, not doxa-presence-conditional: \textit{doxa with non-evaluative-complex content} (Case 2) does not produce \textit{pathē}; \textit{doxa with evaluatively-complex content} (Case 3) does.
