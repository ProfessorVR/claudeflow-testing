# Section 1.7: The Feedback Loop — Synchronic Chain, Diachronic Saturation

## 1. The De Insomniis Mechanism and the Residual Motion of Pathos

Aristotle's *De Insomniis* supplies a model of affective persistence that, when read alongside the dispositional architectures of the *Rhetoric* and the *Nicomachean Ethics*, discloses what we may call a feedback loop between singular pathē and the durable hexeis they progressively consolidate. The treatise's central insight — that residual sensory motions (φαντάσματα) persist in the blood and the perceptual apparatus long after their originating stimuli have ceased — provides a physiological register for a phenomenon whose ontological significance extends well beyond the explanation of dreams. Specifically, the residual motion that survives the departure of the sensible object is not merely a diminished copy of sensation; it is a movement that continues to act upon the perceiving organism, altering the conditions under which subsequent perception will occur. Thus the *De Insomniis* account establishes that affective experience leaves material traces in the body, and these traces in turn modulate the organism's receptive capacities — its readiness to be moved again in determinate ways. The model is, accordingly, not linear but recursive: each instance of πάθος reshapes the very medium through which the next instance will be received, producing a circuit that we designate the *pathos* feedback loop. This interpretive reconstruction, it must be acknowledged, goes beyond the explicit letter of Aristotle's text; it synthesizes the dream-physiology of *De Insomniis* with the dispositional ethics of the *Nicomachean Ethics* and the rhetorical psychology of *Rhetoric* II. Yet the synthesis is warranted by the shared vocabulary — πάθος, φαντασία, ἕξις — that runs across all three treatises, and by Aristotle's own insistence that nature provides a principle or cause of being moved and of being at rest "in that to which it belongs primarily, in virtue of itself and not accidentally" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 15–17). What belongs to the ensouled organism primarily, in the domain of affective life, is precisely this recursive susceptibility — the capacity to be moved that is itself shaped by prior movings. How, then, does this feedback loop operate, and what are the temporal registers in which it does its work? To answer this question, we must distinguish between the synchronic and the diachronic dimensions of the mechanism, a distinction that structures the remainder of this section. As Daniel Gross has observed, the social world conditions perception, action, emotion, and cognition down to their most minute details, such that the "terrible discomfort" of a given situation is never merely private but is always already shaped by structures of class, habit, and expectation that constitute the field in which affect circulates (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, p. 58). Indeed, Gross's analysis of how narrative perspective produces "second-order discomfort in a reader who alone can register the irony" of socially saturated situations provides a contemporary analogue for the Aristotelian insight that the medium of reception is never neutral (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, p. 58). The organism that receives an affective impression is always an organism already disposed — already thrown, in the Heideggerian idiom — into a determinate configuration of sensibility. 

## 2. The Synchronic Register: Stimmung-Saturation and the Momentary Chain

The synchronic register of the feedback loop concerns the way in which a present attunement (*Stimmung*) saturates the entire field of perceptual and affective receptivity at a given moment, thereby conditioning what can show up as salient, threatening, pleasurable, or indifferent. On this register, the mechanism operates as a chain: a given πάθος — fear, anger, pity — does not simply occur and subside but rather suffuses the perceptual apparatus so that subsequent stimuli are encountered through a field already colored by the initial affection. Heidegger captures this synchronic saturation with precision when he characterizes state-of-mind (*Befindlichkeit*) as "itself the existential kind of Being in which Dasein constantly surrenders itself to the 'world' and lets the 'world' 'matter' to it in such a way that somehow Dasein evades its very self" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 178–179). The key phrase is "constantly surrenders": the disclosure of the world through mood is not episodic but pervasive, operating as a continuous modulation of what the world can mean and how beings within it can affect us. Thus the synchronic chain describes how a single affective event — the onset of fear, the surge of anger — propagates through the whole structure of disclosure, altering the conditions under which the next affective event will be registered. Specifically, the first mechanism within this synchronic register is what we may call *Stimmung*-saturation: the way an operative mood permeates the totality of Dasein's being-in-the-world so thoroughly that it is not experienced as a discrete emotion but as the very medium of encounter. Heidegger insists that a state-of-mind "not only discloses Dasein in its thrownness and its submission to that world which is already disclosed with its own Being" but constitutes the existential modality through which the world matters at all (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 178–179). Similarly, Aristotle's analysis in *De Insomniis* of how residual phantasmata persist and interact with incoming sensory data provides the physiological ground for this phenomenological insight: the dreamer mistakes residual images for present realities precisely because the affective charge of those images saturates the perceptual field in the absence of corrective waking sensation. The corrective faculty — the capacity of the waking subject to discriminate between phantasma and aisthēma — is itself subject to impairment; indeed, it constitutes the second mechanism within the synchronic register. When the corrective faculty is overwhelmed, of intense passion, illness, or sleep, the feedback loop runs unchecked, and the organism's receptivity becomes entirely determined by its prior affective residues. Kenneth Burke's analysis of identification as operating in an "intermediate area of expression that is not wholly deliberate, yet not wholly unconscious" illuminates this dynamic from the rhetorical side; the processes by which group perspectives are organized through circulating rhetoric depend on precisely this kind of sub-deliberative affective saturation (Burke, *The War of Words*, p. 25). Accordingly, the synchronic chain and the corrective-faculty mechanism together describe how a single moment of affective life is always already recursive — how, at any given instant, the organism's receptive state is a product of prior affective events that continue to exert force upon the present. 

## 3. The Diachronic Register: Dispositional Sedimentation and Hexis-Formation

The diachronic register concerns a different temporal scale: not the momentary saturation of the perceptual field but the long-term sedimentation of repeated affective experiences into stable dispositions — ἕξεις. Where the synchronic chain describes how a present mood shapes the reception of the next impression, the diachronic register describes how the accumulation of such chains over time transforms the organism's very capacity for being affected. Hence the diachronic dimension of the feedback loop is the mechanism by which πάθος becomes ἕξις: by which singular, transient affections harden into durable orientations of character. Aristotle's account of habituation in the *Nicomachean Ethics* provides the classical locus: we form hexeis by repeatedly undergoing and responding to the pathē in determinate ways, so that the disposition is not innate but produced through the very circuit of affection and response that the *De Insomniis* physiology describes. The organism that has been repeatedly frightened does not merely carry a record of past fears; it has become fearful, its perceptual and affective apparatus reconfigured so that it is now more readily moved to fear by lesser stimuli. This diachronic sedimentation is what gives the feedback loop its ontological weight, for it means that the affective life of the organism is not a sequence of discrete episodes but a cumulative process of self-constitution. As Aristotle determines in the *Physics*, being-moved is not an accidental feature imposed upon a substance from without but belongs to the nature of the thing itself — nature being "a principle or cause of being moved and of being at rest in that to which it belongs primarily, in virtue of itself and not accidentally" (Aristotle, *Physics*, pp. 15–17). Applied to the ensouled organism, this principle entails that the organism's affective susceptibility is not a passive surface upon which external forces inscribe themselves but an active, self-modifying capacity that participates in its own formation. Thus the diachronic register reveals that the feedback loop is not merely a physiological mechanism but an ontological structure: the structure through which the being of the affected organism is progressively constituted by its own history of being affected. Burke captures a cognate structure in his analysis of hierarchy and motive when he identifies the "principle of hierarchy" as infusing "any person, thing, or situation" with the hierarchic motive, producing configurations of "authority or resistance to authority; reason; object or source of reverence" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, p. 315). The hierarchic principle, like the Aristotelian hexis, is not a singular event but a sedimented structure that shapes all subsequent encounters within the field it governs. Similarly, Burke's observation that men "induce themselves and others to act by devices that deduce 'let us' from 'we'" captures the recursive logic of diachronic saturation: the collective disposition — the "we" — is both the product and the condition of the rhetorical acts that sustain it (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 356–358). 

## 4. Hexis, Geworfenheit, and the Temporality of Pathos-Grade Thrownness

The convergence of Aristotelian ἕξις and Heideggerian *Geworfenheit* constitutes the deeper the feedback loop is designed to ground. Both concepts name a structure in which the being of the affected entity is shaped by something that has already happened to it — by a past that it did not choose but that nonetheless determines the horizon within which all future experience will unfold. The Aristotelian dimension of this convergence proceeds from the analysis of ἕξις as a stable disposition produced through habituation: through the repeated undergoing of πάθη in determinate circumstances, the organism acquires a settled orientation toward the world that is neither purely active nor purely passive but constitutes the medium through which activity and passivity alike are channeled. Heidegger, in his early lectures on Aristotle, recognized in this structure something essential about the being-there of human beings, specifically the way in which πάσχειν operates as "becoming-affected," as "being related to the world as in it, being in dealings with it" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 277–280). The Heideggerian reading of πάθος thus elevates what might seem a merely psychological category to the level of an existential determination: to be affected is not one thing that Dasein does among others but a fundamental mode of its being-in-the-world. The second movement in this argument concerns Heidegger's own concept of *Geworfenheit* — thrownness — as it appears in *Being and Time*. Thrownness names the facticity of Dasein's being delivered over to a situation that it has not made and cannot fully comprehend; it is disclosed pre-thematically through *Befindlichkeit*, the existentiale of state-of-mind or mood. Heidegger insists that anxiety, as a fundamental mode of *Befindlichkeit*, "liberates him from possibilities which 'count for nothing' ["nichtigen"], and lets him become free for those which are authentic" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 394–396). This liberation is not an escape from thrownness but a deeper appropriation of it; authentic anxiety discloses the very structure of thrownness as such, revealing that Dasein is always already disposed — always already attuned — in ways that precede and condition all deliberate engagement with the world. The temporal structure of this disclosure is crucial: thrownness is essentially a having-been (*Gewesenheit*), a past that has never been present as a moment of free choice but that nonetheless constitutes the ground of all present and future possibility. The convergence of these two structures — Aristotelian ἕξις and Heideggerian *Geworfenheit* — occurs most explicitly in Heidegger's early Aristotle lectures collected as *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, where the analysis of πάθος as an encounter-character of being-in-the-world directly anticipates the *Being and Time* analysis of *Befindlichkeit*. As Heidegger reads Aristotle, the being-there of human beings is characterized by being-in-the-world, and the world is initially defined by encounter-aspects — by the ways in which beings show up as significant, threatening, useful, or indifferent (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 193–196). The πάθος that accompanies such encounters is not a subjective coloring added to an otherwise neutral perception; it is the very mode in which the world discloses itself. Subsequently, the ἕξις that consolidates through repeated πάθη is not a psychological trait superadded to a pre-given subject but the sedimented form of the subject's worldly involvement — its thrown character, its *Geworfenheit*. Accordingly, Heidegger's reading of Aristotle reveals that the feedback loop we have reconstructed from *De Insomniis*, the *Rhetoric*, and the *Nicomachean Ethics* is not merely a psychological or physiological mechanism but an ontological structure of Dasein's temporality: the structure through which the having-been of prior affection constitutes the ground of present disclosure. The *pathos* feedback loop, in its synchronic and diachronic registers alike, is thus a concrete instantiation of *pathos*-grade thrownness — the condition in which our being-in-the-world is always already shaped by a history of being affected that we did not choose and cannot fully recover, yet that determines the horizon within which all future affection, perception, and action will unfold. As we argued in §1.2, rhetoric provides the first systematic hermeneutic for this thrown affective life; the feedback loop analyzed here specifies the temporal mechanism through which that hermeneutic finds its ontological ground. Indeed, the link between Aristotelian αἴσθησις and Heideggerian *Befindlichkeit* is grounded in the receptive character common to both: for Aristotle, perception is fundamentally a being-acted-upon (πάσχειν) by the sensible form without its matter; for Heidegger, *Befindlichkeit* discloses Dasein as always already delivered over to a world that affects it prior to any act of cognition or volition. Heidegger identifies in the tumult (ταραχή) associated with πάθος a "peculiar unrest" that characterizes the movement from one state-of-mind to another — "the having-come-into-another-frame-of-mind" and "being on the way from one state to another" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 138). This unrest is not a deficiency but the very dynamism of affective life, the engine of the feedback loop through which singular affections sediment into durable dispositions and durable dispositions modulate the reception of new affections. Thus the feedback loop — operating synchronically through *Stimmung*-saturation and corrective-faculty impairment, and diachronically through the sedimentation of πάθη into ἕξεις — constitutes the temporal mechanism that binds singular affective events to the durable structure of thrownness. It is this mechanism that prevents the analysis of *pathos* from collapsing into either a mere catalogue of emotions or a static dispositional typology, revealing instead the recursive, self-constituting temporality of affective existence as such.
