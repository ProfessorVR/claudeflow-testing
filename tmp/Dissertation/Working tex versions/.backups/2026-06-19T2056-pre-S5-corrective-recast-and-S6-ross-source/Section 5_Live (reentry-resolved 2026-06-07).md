the forms: nutritive, sensitive, appetitive, locomotive, thinking

``Now matter is potentiality, form actuality' and actuality is of two kinds, one as e.g. knowledge, the other as e.g. reflecting'' (DA II.1, 412a9-11)

``Hence the soul must be a substance in the sense of the form of a natural body having life potentially within it. But substance is actuality, and thus soul is the actuality of a body as above characterized. now there are two kinds of actuality corresponding to knowledge and to reflecting. It is obvious that the soul is an actuality like knowledge; for both sleepign and waking presuppose the existence of soul, and of these waking corresponds to reflecting, sleeping to knowledge possessed but not employed, and knowledge of something is temporally prior.'' (\textit{DA} II.1, 412a20--26). 

``But if we we are to express what each is, viz. what the thinking power is, or the perceptive, or the nutritive, we must go farther back and first give an account of thinking or perceiving; for activities and actions are prior in definition to potentialities. If so, and if, still prior to them, we should have reflected on their correlative objects, then for the same reason we must first determine about them, i.e. about food and the objects of perception and thought.” (\textit{DA} II.4, 415a16--22). 

each form of the soul is to be understood through their activities and the objects of those activities: food as object of nutritive, objects of perception, desire, and thought. 

each form of the soul is a type of actuality insofar as it is a form of the soul, while each is also a power or potentiality insofar as it is a faculty ordered to an activity : matter is potentiality, form actuality, and the soul is the actuality of a natural organized body having life potentially within it. So if nutritive, perceptive, appetitve, locomotive, and thinking forms are genuine forms or parts of suol, then they belong on the side of actuality rather than matter. but aristotle regularly describes them as powers or faculties. soul is a first actuality: a standing realized capacity, not yet the ongoing operation itself. sight as the soul of the eye is an actuality of the eye, while actually seeing is a further exercise of that actuality. each form of soul is a kind of actuality, but specifically a first actuality that grounds a corresponding second actuality or activity such as nourishing, perceiving, desiring, moving, or thinking. that is why aristotle can speak of them both as forms and as powers without contradiction. 

faculty, form, and power mostly render three core greek words: Faculty/\gk{δύναμις}/\textit{dýnamis}: a capacity or power a soul has to carry out certain activities (e.g., the dynamis of perceiving, thinking, moving). Form/\gk{εἶδος}/\textit{eîdos}: form, look, or kind. soul as the form or essence of a living body, in perception as receiving form without matter. ALso, \gk{μορφή}/\textit{morphḗ}: form or shape; more typical word for the structured configuration a thing has. soul is the morphe of a antural body. Power/\gk{δύναμις}/\textit{dýnamis}: the key term for power, capacity, or potentiality: powers of nutrition, perception etc that together make up the soul's faculties. 

For Aristotle, each form of the soul is a \textit{dýnamis} that only comes into its own through motions and affections—growth, sensation, desire, learning—so that the soul is intrinsically bound up with \textit{páthos}, with being-affected. Yet the soul itself is not a motion; it is the first actuality that grounds and guides these kinéseis, and its highest exercises (such as settled knowing) are energeíai—completed modes of being-in-the-world that arise from prior being-affected but are no longer themselves “actualities of the potential \textit{qua} potential.”

\inlinenote{For Aristotle, the faculties of soul are not isolated powers but differentiated participations in one ontological structure of potency, affection, and actuality: each faculty names a determinate way a living being can receive form, undergo alteration, and, in that very undergoing, become a source of further movement or realization. This is possible because, in Metaphysics V, potency is defined both as a source of change in another thing or in the same thing qua other and as the principle by which something is capable of being moved or suffering, so the active and receptive poles belong to one shared field rather than to two unrelated kinds of being. Thus the threptikon is affected by nourishment and turns what it receives into growth and maintenance; the aisthētikon is affected by sensible objects; the orektikon is affected by what appears pleasant or painful and becomes pursuit or avoidance; the phantastikon preserves and reactivates movements arising from sensation; and the kinētikon kata topon carries these perceptual and appetitive actualizations into bodily movement in place. The noētikon shows this structure most sharply, because Aristotle distinguishes a receptive intellect that receives intelligible form from an active intellect that “makes all things” and is separable and impassible, so even here the noetic domain contains both the receptive and the productive moments of one process of actualization. The gennētikon belongs to the same whole in another register, since Aristotle defines cause as that from which change first begins and names the father and the semen as causes of the child, which means that generative power is the productive pole of the same structure whose correlates are receptive matter and coming-to-be. Accordingly, all the faculties partake in the overall structure of affecting and affectability, not because each is passive in the same sense, but because each marks a distinct way life receives, transmits, and actualizes form.}

emotion as motion: As both a motion (in the energeia ateles sense) and a pathos it must consists with the fivefold ontology of any pathos \textit{and} follow the three-factor schema of motion.
\begin{enumerate}
    
    \item unmoved mover: Always object of desire / apparent good. The world being disclosed as mattering in some way as an object of desire: as an object to pursue or to avoid, for it is only the object of desire ``that itself remaining unmoved originates the movement by being apprehended in thought or imagination'' (\textit{DA} III.10, 433b11--13). Thus, the unmoved mover of emotion has a dual structure: the occasion/object (the \textit{toward-which}): the \textit{doxa}-ratified evaluative situation---the conspicuous slight, the impending evil, the undeserved suffering; and, secondly, the good it discloses (the final cause proper): the apparent good the emotion reaches toward---revenge, safety, relief. The slight \textit{discloses} revenge-as-pursuable; it is the \textit{doxa}-ratification that makes the situation present \textit{this} good. So the unmoved mover = the apparent good as disclosed through the \textit{doxa}-ratified evaluative object. It moves the desire as an \textit{intentional/final} cause: apprehended, not pushed---which is exactly why it stays ``unmoved.''

    \item Moved Mover: the \textit{orektikon}. ``that which at once moves and is moved is the faculty of appetite [desire] (for that which is moved is moved insofar as it desires, and appetite in the sense of actual appetite \textit{is} a kind of movement)'' (433b17--19). desire-in-actuality is a kind of motion or an actuality. \gk{ ἡ ὄρεξις κίνησίς τις ἢ ἐνέργεια} (433b16-18). The cognition (\textit{doxa}) is not a fourht factor---it belongs to the constitution of the \textit{unmoved mover} (it is what makes the object an evaluatively-determinate apparent good). The moved mover stays \textit{orexis}; the cognition only \textit{determines which form the orexis takes}. Emotion is the actualization of the \textit{orektikon} into a specific form of desire. So emotion \textit{is} the moved mover, in its \textit{doxa}-determined actuality: \textit{orexis} specified by its evaluative object. 

    \item That which is moved: the body and the somatic preparation is the \textit{joint}. The moved mover grips the moved through the \textit{enmattered} affection. In the MA mechanism, the \textit{orexis}-actualization moves the limbs by heating or chilling the region around the heart. So the ``boiling of blood'' is doing double duty: under the \textit{formal} account it is the enmattered form of the anger (moved-mover side); under the \textit{material} account it is the somatic preparation of the organic parts (moved side). That is the \textit{enmattered-account} one object two accounts (\textit{logoi}): the affection is the boundary where the moved mover terminates in the moved. So when we say the moved mover has to prepare the somatic parts, we have located not a separate step, but \textit{how the moved mover moves the moved at all}. 
\end{enumerate}

Emotion as \textit{energeia ateles}

\begin{list}

    \item somatic preparation is the motion's bodily \textit{unfolding}, not its terminus. The boiling blood is the anger \textit{happening} not what the anger is \textit{for}.

    \item the \textit{telos} lies beyond, in the action itself: for anger, the \textit{conspicuous revenge}---and, beyond the act, the good it achieves (the slight avenged, worth restored). The anger is ``for the sake of'' the revenge; it is the \textit{ateles} until then and discharges when the revenge is taken. That's the MA chain reaching A4 (completed action). We know the motion by the actualization it terminates in: the desire-to-flee is not equal to the desire-to-retaliate \textit{because their termini differ}. The end individuates the emotion. Fear is known by \textit{flight-to-safety}, anger by \textit{revenge}, pity by \textit{relief}. So, the end of anger = the hostile action/revenge, with somatic preparation as the \textit{en-route} bodily phase. 

    \item The original Greek: \gk{κινεῖται γὰρ τὸ κινούμενον ᾗ ὀρέγεται, καὶ ἡ ὄρεξις κίνησίς τίς ἐστιν, ἢ ἐνεργείᾳ} (\textit{kineîtai gàr tò kinoúmenon hȇi orégetai, kaì hē órexis kínēsís tís estin, ḕ energeíāi}). This is Aristotle's own hedge, sitting inside the definition of the moved mover. He defines \textit{orexis} as \gk{κίνησίς τίς} (\textit{kínēsís tís}) (``a kind of motion'') and then immediately qualifies it: \gk{ἢ ἐνεργείᾳ} (``in actuality''). \hl{As an incomplete actuality, emotion is an interesting case as it seems both complete and simultaneously incomplete: (1) qua motivation orientation (toward action) it is a \textit{kinesis} / \textit{energeia ateles}: incomplete, end-beyond-itself, the anger-toward-revenge. (2) qua felt disclosure (the present painful disturbance, the \textit{now} of being-angry) it looks like \textit{energeia teleia}, complete at every moment: you do not ``incompletely feel'' anger, you \textit{fully} feel it now, ``at once feeling and having felt''. Thus, it is lived as both, complete as feeling and having felt and incomplete in the end-toward-which the emotion reaches: the revenge of anger, flight of fear, etc.}.   
    
\end{list}

\begin{enumerate}
    
    \item Disposition (finding-oneself-disposed). \textit{Energeia}: to be angry is to be in a determinate attunement, whole at this instant — there is no half-disposed; you are disposed-as-slighted, "at once disposing and having-disposed." A complete present state. \textit{Kinēsis}: yet the disposition is a change of disposition (Heidegger's \textit{metaballontes} — "a change sets in; we change from one disposition to another") — a re-attuning, a being-moved-out-of-composure. The "e-" of e-motion. \textit{Coincidence}: the mood is complete-now precisely as a having-been-moved-into-it.

    \item The toward-which / toward-whom (intentionality). \textit{Energeia}: the grasp is complete — anger fully has the slight (toward-which) and is fully turned on the slighter (toward-whom), like the actuality of perceiving, "at once seeing and having seen." \textit{Kinēsis}: but that grasp is a reaching-toward — \textit{orexis} drawn to the not-yet-accomplished revenge, the toward-whom as the target of an unfinished redress. \textit{Coincidence}: to grasp the slight is to be drawn toward its answer; the intentional actuality is the motivational motion.

    \item The bodily form (enmattered alteration). \textit{Kinēsis}: the boiling of blood is the manifestly kinetic moment — an \textit{alloiōsis}, a heating that prepares the organic parts for hostile action; a process en route to readiness. \textit{Energeia}: yet that boiling is the anger fully bodied-forth now (the enmattered \textit{eidos}, complete) — and, by DA II.5, a preservative \textit{paschein} (\gk{σωτηρία}), an actualization of the orectic potential, not a \textit{kinēsis}-toward-destruction. \textit{Coincidence}: one bodily event under two modes — your "one object, two accounts" doubled: the preparatory alteration is the complete embodied actuality. (This is where your \gk{σωτηρία} claim lives: the \textit{energeia} pole.)

    \item The temporal range (the threefold span). This is the load-bearing one. \textit{Kinēsis}: the emotion projects toward a futural not-yet — fear toward the impending evil, anger toward the revenge-to-come. The end lies ahead; the motion strains toward its terminus. \textit{Energeia}: but it co-temporalizes all three ecstases at once, whole in the present — thrown having-been, felt present, projected futural, held together now. The ecstatic unity is a complete present actuality; you don't "incompletely span" the three. \textit{Coincidence} — and the payoff: whether an emotion tips kinetic or energeic is fixed here, by whether its futural ecstasis has an attainable terminus. Anger's future (revenge) is realizable → the kinetic vector is live → anger is \textit{kinēsis}-dominant. Grief's "future" has no attainable terminus (the loss is irrevocable, the good non-realizable) → the kinetic vector is dead → grief is \textit{energeia}-dominant: it dwells, complete, in the having-been, with no end-beyond. That is your "complete at A₃, issues in no deed" — now derived, not stipulated. Grief is the \textit{energeia teleia} case; anger the \textit{kinēsis} case; most \textit{pathē} are mixed.

    \item The disclosive function (\textit{to krisis}, \textit{to logos}). \textit{Kinēsis}: a bringing-to — the emotion moves the agent toward position-taking, toward speech, toward the deed (the slighted to confront, the threatened to confer); productive, terminating in \textit{praxis}. \textit{Energeia}: but the disclosure itself is complete now — the world is fully lit-up as mattering-for-me, "at once disclosing and having disclosed," like \textit{nous}, before any deed is done. \textit{Coincidence}: the complete present disclosure is the impetus to act — the world's mattering-now is the motion toward \textit{logos}.

    \item The whole, then: emotion is \textit{energeia} in its disclosive-felt vector (it fully discloses significance now — complete) and \textit{kinēsis} in its productive-projective vector (it moves toward the action that answers that significance — end beyond). The five-fold is the joint: the same disclosure that is complete-now is the motion-toward-deed. And that is just \gk{κίνησις ἐνεργείᾳ} spelled out across five moments — an actual motion, a \textit{kinēsis} whose every phase is a complete present \textit{energeia}. So you can keep 433b18 after all — not as a hedge between two categories, but as the one-word compression of your entire five-fold: emotion, the actual motion of the soul disclosing significance toward action.
    
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{E\textit{motion} is \textit{Motion}: The Affective Architecture of Aristotelian Being-Moved (\textit{Paschein})}

When it comes to Aristotle’s account of emotion in the \textit{Rhetorica}, I am in agreement with Heidegger: ``\gk{πάθη} [\textit{pathē}] are not ``psychic experiences,'' are not ``in consciousness,'' (\textit{BCAP} 133) nor are they an epiphenomenon sitting atop otherwise rational operations of the human psyche. Rather, as Heidegger continues, they are:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    a being-taken of human beings in their full being-in-the-world. That is expressed by the fact that the whole, the full occurrence-context, which is found in this happening, in being-taken, belongs to the \gk{πάθη}. The so-called ``bodily states'' of anxiety, joy, and so forth, are not symptoms, but also belong to the characteristic being of beings, of human beings. (133)
\end{adjustwidth}    
They are a \textit{movement} (\textit{kinēsis}) originated by and terminates in the soul,\footnote{For, as Aristotle states: ``[t]he soul is the cause or source of the living body. The terms cause and source have many senses, but the soul is the cause of its body alike in all three senses which we explicitly recognize. It is the source of movement, it is the end, it is the essence of the whole living body'' (\textit{DA} II.4, 415b9--12).} a dynamic reorientation within the field of appearances that alters how the world shows up and, in so doing, alters how one is disposed to act within it. Emotion is not simply something that happens inside a subject; it is the soul’s \textit{being-moved} by what appears significant to it.\footnote{Daniel Gross captures this significance masterfully: ``Heidegger characterizes pathos (variously `passion,' `affect,' `mood,' or `emotion') as the very condition for the possibility of rational discourse, or \textit{logos}. No cynical and crowd-pleasing addition to logos, pathos is the very substance in which propositional thought finds its objects and its motivation. Without affect our disembodied minds would have no heart, and no legs to stand on. We would have no grounds for concern, no time and place for judging, no motivation to discourse at all'' (Gross, ``Introduction'' 4). Struever's parallel reading anchors the thesis in \textit{kinēsis}: ``Heidegger grasps the emphasis on change, and therefore time. In his account of Aristotelian psychology, kinēsis gives the authentic \textit{Da-Charakter} to Dasein, the vital place-time of Being. Thus, the ontological significance of the emotions lies in our capacity for change, the \textit{Veränderlichkeit} of Being. Emotion itself is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering'' (Struever 109).} Emotion is therefore inseparable from \textit{phantasia}—the representational, imagistic, quasi-perceptual capacity through which things “show up” in the first place (DA III.3-8). Indeed, the very word, \textit{emotion}, already reflects the kinetic ontology that Aristotle and Heidegger attribute to \textit{pathos}. According to the Oxford English Dictionary, \textit{emotion} derives from Middle French \textit{émotion} (“a moving, stirring, agitation,” especially in the sense of social or political unrest) and ultimately from Latin \textit{emovēre}, meaning “to move out, remove, agitate.”\footnote{“\textit{Emotion}, \textit{n}.” Oxford English Dictionary, 3rd ed., OUP, 2001. The OED gives the earliest French usage c. 14th century with meanings related to “political agitation or disturbance,” and Latin \textit{emovēre}: “to move out or away; to stir up.”} Morphologically composed of e- (“out, forth”) and movēre (“to move, set in motion”), the term literally designates a moving-out, a displacement or perturbation that carries the subject beyond a prior equilibrium. The modern English form follows this lineage closely, its internal structure—e- (out) + mot- (move) + -ion (denoting the act or process)—naming not a static inner feeling but the \textit{event} of being-moved, the process through which the animal is set into motion in response to what appears significant.\footnote{“\textit{Emotion}, \textit{n}.” OED. See the morphological note under “Etymology”: the noun-forming suffix -ion indicating “action or condition,” attached to \textit{emovēre} via the French \textit{émotion}.} The word’s deeper Indo-European root, *meue- (“to push, move, stir”), likewise links \textit{emotion} to \textit{motion}, \textit{momentum}, and \textit{motivate}, reinforcing that its semantic horizon is fundamentally dynamic.\footnote{“\textit{Move}, \textit{v}.” OED, 3rd ed., OUP, 2002. The entry traces \textit{movēre} to the Proto-Indo-European root *meue-, meaning “to push, move, stir,” which also gives rise to English \textit{motion}, \textit{mobile}, \textit{moment}, etc.}  Thus, even at the level of etymology, \textit{emotion} mirrors the Aristotelian claim that the actualization of desire is a form of \textit{kinēsis}\footnote{``[T]hat which at once moves and is moved is the faculty of appetite [\textit{orexis}, ``desire''] (for that which is moved is moved insofar as it desires, and appetite [desire] in the sense of actual appetite [desire] \textit{is} a kind of movement)'' (\textit{DA} III.10, 433b16--19). In the Greek for this passage, only various inflections of \gk{ὄρεξις} (\textit{orexis}, ``desire'' as the genus) are present; the Greek for appetite, \gk{ἐπιθυμία} (\textit{epithymia}) is not present and is only translated as such. For the original Greek, see the Ross ed.}: a being-moved originating in the soul toward or away from perceived goods and evils.\footnote{Hawhee anchors the \textit{energeia}/\textit{kinēsis} identification in the \textit{Rhetorica} (III.11.1412a.9--10), where Aristotle's reading of Homer culminates in the explicit equation: ``[h]e makes everything move and live, and \textit{energeia} is motion (1412a.9-10). Movement and life, \textit{kinēsis} and \textit{zōos}, are what confer rhetorical presence'' (Hawhee 154). I reference the etymology not as evidence, but rather as an interesting coincidence in that the word emotion seems to echo or reflect the same kinetic ontology.} The \textit{e}- of \textit{emotion} is precisely the ``from without'' of being-taken of Heidegger's formulation, and the ``\textit{motion}'' is the \textit{kinēsis} of being-in-the-world \textit{itself}. To undergo emotion is therefore to be shifted or displaced by the force of appearance already in motion, already reoriented. In this way, the etymology of \textit{emotion} echoes the ancient insight: emotion is the embodied signature of significance, the soul’s kinetic exposure to what matters. 

However, before I can more fully analyze Aristotelian \textit{pathos}, \textit{pathē}, and Heidegger's reading of such, a brief terminological reckoning is required, for Aristotle himself does not deploy \textit{pathos} in a single, univocal sense. As previously discussed, the term ranges across at least four distinguishable registers in his corpus. In its broadest, ontological sense, \textit{pathos} is the determination of any being whose constitution admits of alteration---``a quality in respect of which a thing can be altered'' (\textit{Metaphysica} V21, 1022b15)---a sense that ranges over the entirety of natural beings and not merely the affective life of animals. In the \textit{aisthētic} sense, \textit{pathos} names not just the actualization of the sense organ but the basic hedonic tonality (pleasant/painful) co-given---with the \textit{eidetic} aspect---in the \textit{kritikon} of the perceptual event, where ``the soul makes a sort of affirmation or negation, and pursues or avoids the object'' (\textit{DA} III.7, 431a8--12). This hedonic tonality (good/bad, pleasure/pain, pursue/avoid) is co-given with every moment of being-there: with every perceiving, every thinking, every considering, and, equally, with every \textit{pathos} and emotion. Here I must pause to reiterate the architecture I have previously established: \textit{aisthēsis} and the \textit{orektikon} are one substrate but different in being (\textit{DA} III.7, 431a13--14), and the \textit{orectic} aspect co-given in the perceptual event is, what I call, \textit{epithymia}---the only species of \textit{orexis} (with \textit{thymos} and \textit{boulēsis}; \textit{DA} II.3, 414b1--6) that requires no cognitive enrichment beyond what \textit{aisthēsis}'s hedonic disclosure supplies. Its persisting tone, carried forward by the \textit{resonant kinēsis} after the object has withdrawn, I have coined \textit{resonant epithymia}; these are not two different species but rather the same \textit{orectic} valence in its two temporal phases. Perceiving, therefore, elicits \textit{epithymia}, whereas thinking and considering---both of which operate on the \textit{phantasma} of the original object of sense---are accompanied with \textit{resonant epithymia}.

In the sense articulated in the \textit{Rhetorica}'s catalog (anger, fear, pity, shame, and their kin), \textit{pathos} names the determinate higher-order emotions: defined as ``feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements'' (II.1, 1378a21-22). I henceforth call these the \textit{emotions}: the cataloged species of being-affected (\textit{paschein}) through which the speech-bearing animal is constituted in its shared world as being-with-others. The emotions mark the contrast with the basic hedonic tonality co-given in \textit{aisthēsis}: they are the more articulationally concrete species of \textit{paschein}, arising not from that basic tonality worked up by \textit{doxa} but as their own determinate concretions---elicited when \textit{doxa} affirms evaluative propositional content, and each bearing its own \textit{orectic} charge and hedonic tonality as structural to it, just as the hedonic tonality is structural to \textit{aisthēsis}. Lastly, in its bodily-physiological sense, \textit{pathos} names the enmattered alteration through which the formal-hedonic structure of an emotion is realized: ``a boiling of the blood or warm substance surrounding the heart'' (\textit{De Anima} I.1, 403a30--b1). These senses overlap, shade into one another, and are deployed by Aristotle without consistent demarcation; the Greek is used across the ontological, perceptual, emotional, and physiological registers, and what one says of \textit{pathos} at one register need not, without further argument, hold at another.\footnote{Corcilius and Gregoric's analysis of \textit{De Anima} I.4, 408b5--18 clarifies what is at stake when Aristotle treats emotions as motions: ``Aristotle does not wish to deny that affections in question are \textit{motions}. On the contrary, he accepts that they are. What he denies is that the \textit{soul} is the proper subject of these motions ... the soul is only the terminus and the origin of motions that take place inside the body, not something that itself undergoes these motions'' (Corcilius and Gregoric 55). The four-register distinction drawn here thus tracks an internal articulation of \textit{paschein} as a movement; for, as Aristotle states, ``[i]t is doubtless better to avoid saying that the soul pities or learns or thinks, and rather to say that it is the man who does this with his soul'' (\textit{DA}) I.4, 408b13--14).} However, both hedonic tonality and the emotions are treated as two articulational concretions of a single ontological structure: the preservation aspect of \textit{paschein} (``being-affected'') that I discuss below. 

\subsection{\textit{Pathos} and \textit{Emotion}: Two Articulational Concretions}

With our terminological reckoning complete, I can now posit the overall \textit{pathetic} thesis for which I will argue:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    There is a single ontological structure\footnote{Heidegger's ``\gk{ἕξις} (\textit{hexis} ``settled disposition'') is nothing other than a how of \gk{πάθος}, being-out-of-composure, in relation to being-composed-as-to'' (BCAP 125) opens the structural space for this two-hows reading. \textit{Pathos} admits of multiple how-modalities; the articulational distinction between basic hedonic tonality and the more articulationally concrete emotion is one such how-distinction within the single \textit{pathos} structure.} of \textit{pathos} for the soul or what Heidegger refers to as Dasein---articulated by the four senses of the \textit{Metaphysica}, the three constitutive aspects of the \textit{Rhetorica}, the enmattered account of the \textit{De Anima},\footnote{The enmattered account is no addendum but a condition of the whole: because the being so affected is bodily-in-the-world, any affection it undergoes is at once an affection of the body, on pain of contradicting the hylomorphism of the \textit{DA} (I.1, 403a25--b19).} and the threefold ecstatic temporality that \textit{phantasia} holds together---that is invariant across the most basic sensory perception and the most complex, \textit{logos} dependent, and \textit{doxa}-articulated emotions. The single structure is, therefore, at \textit{every} level of its articulation, at once a formal-and-material unity---an \textit{eidos} inseparable from the \textit{hylē} in which it is realized---and an ecstatic-temporal one, co-temporalizing the \textit{having-been}, the \textit{present}, and the \textit{projected-futural} in a single span. This temporal articulation is no external overlay: because \textit{pathos} is a \textit{kinēsis}---a being-moved---it is temporal through and through, the three ecstases being the very span across which the motion of being-affected unfolds.\footnote{The present analysis restricts itself to rational animals: those possessing \textit{logos} and therefore capable of \textit{doxa}. The higher-order emotions---shame, pity, anger, and their kin---require \textit{doxa}'s ratification of a propositional content, and so fall only to animals possessing \textit{logos}. The bare hedonic tonality co-given with perception is structural to \textit{aisthēsis} and is shared with the brutes; it is not \textit{converted} by \textit{doxa} into these emotions. Rather, the emotion is its own \textit{doxa}-elicited concretion---bearing its own \textit{orectic} charge and hedonic tonality---arising when \textit{doxa} ratifies an evaluative content that only \textit{logos} can supply. As Aristotle states: ``there are some of the brutes in which we find imagination, without discourse of reason'' (\textit{DA} III.3, 428a23--24). Aristotle elsewhere attributes \textit{pathos}-like states to non-rational animals; the relevant evidence is dense and worth considering at length, since it bears on whether the articulational distinction tracks a real ontological distinction. In the \textit{Ethica Nicomachea}, Aristotle writes: ``Passion also is sometimes reckoned as courage; those who act from passion, like wild beasts rushing at those who have wounded them, are thought to be brave, because brave men also are passionate; for passion above all things is eager to rush on danger.... Now brave men act for the sake of the noble, but passion aids them; while wild beasts act under the influence of pain; for they attack because they have been wounded or because they are afraid ... The courage that is due to passion seems to be the most natural, and to be courage if choice and motive be added'' (III.8, 1116b23--1117a9). Here Aristotle simultaneously (a) attributes \textit{passion}-like states (courage, fear, pain) to non-rational animals and (b) denies them genuine virtue or courage on the ground that they lack the rational element of choice, foresight, and motive: animal ``fear'' and animal ``courage'' are real \textit{passion}-like dispositions, but they fall short of the higher-order emotions because the rational articulation is absent. The \textit{Historia Animalium} confirms the pattern from a different direction: ``Of the animals that are comparatively obscure and short-lived the characters or dispositions are not so obvious to recognition as are those of animals that are longer-lived. These latter animals appear to have a natural capacity corresponding to each of the passions: to cunning or simplicity, courage or timidity, to good temper or to bad, and to other similar dispositions of mind'' (IX.1, 608a--14). On the present synthesis these attributions are \textit{analogical}: animal ``fear'' is a sustained dispositional orientation grounded in hedonic tonality and persistent \textit{phantasma}, structurally similar to but not identical with the rational emotion. The \textit{doxa}-mediation is what differentiates the merely similar from the genuine higher-order emotions.}
\end{adjustwidth}
The articulational concretion distinction I draw between hedonic tonality as the perceptual-level minimal articulation of \textit{pathos} and the higher-order emotions of the \textit{Rhetorica} as the \textit{doxa}-mediated, maximal articulation fills a gap that Heidegger's treatment of \textit{pathos} in his lectures leaves unaddressed. Heidegger's recovery of \textit{pathos} as a ``being-taken of human beings in their full [bodily] being-in-the-world'' (\textit{BCAP} 133), and the single ontological structure he extracts from the fourfold sense of the \textit{Metaphysica} and the three constitutive aspects of the \textit{Rhetorica} are analytically and phenomenologically precise. What the undifferentiated treatment cannot accommodate, however, is the variable presence of \textit{pathos} across the action-types Aristotle himself catalogs. The practical syllogism of drinking that Aristotle gives in \textit{De Motu Animalium}---perception, basic \textit{epithymia} (``appetite''), immediate action---does not engage the more articulationally concrete emotions Heidegger analyzes, but neither is it \textit{pathos}-less: the perceiver is already disposed toward the perceived object as pursue-able. Without an articulational distinction internal to the \textit{pathos}-structure, this case becomes either a counter-example to the single ontological structure or a phenomenon that simply falls outside Heidegger's analysis. The articulational distinction resolves both elements: hedonic tonality and the higher-order emotions are \textit{two articulational concretions of one ontological structure}, the same preservative \textit{sōtēria}: \textit{paschein} realized at minimal (pre-propositional) and maximal (\textit{doxa}-mediated) articulation.\footnote{Gonzalez's reading in the rhetorical tradition---that \textit{phantasia} acquires \textit{pistis} only when \textit{doxa} ratifies it---specifies the articulational threshold at which the higher-order emotion of the \textit{Rhetorica} come into determinate being: ``the goal of rhetorical practice is \textit{phantasia} with \textit{pistis} [``conviction''], but \textit{pistis} cannot exist without \textit{doxa}'' (101n6).}

The single ontological structure without an internal articulational distinction commits one to either of two positions, both of which Aristotle's text resists. On the first position, only the emotion of the \textit{Rhetorica} count as genuine emotions, and the affective coloring co-given in the perceptual act is something else: a mere reflex or a physiological tone. The textual evidence appears to resist this: Aristotle insists that ``where there is sensation, there is also pleasure and pain'' (\textit{De Anima} II.2, 413b24), and the structure he names \textit{paschein} extends across the entire range of perceptual and rational activity without internal partition: the same \textit{paschein} that operates in hearing-a-sound operates in being-moved-by-anger, with the relevant difference falling along an internal axis rather than between two adjacent kinds. On the second position, the emotions are continuous with basic hedonic tonality in such a way that no real distinction obtains between them, and the determinate emotions of the \textit{Rhetorica} are simply intensifications of the same perceptual coloring. Aristotle's analyses of anger, fear, and pity in the \textit{Rhetorica} do not seem to support this either as they specify propositional contents---the conspicuous slight, the anticipated evil, the undeserved suffering---\textit{and} hedonic tonality (the pain of the slight in anger with the pleasure of anticipated revenge) that could not exist without \textit{doxa}-mediation. The articulational distinction I am attempting to draw lies in the middle: it preserves the structural unity that the single ontological structure demands while allowing the differentiated determinacy that Aristotle's per-emotion analyses require. Heidegger's lectures, working at the level of the structural unity, never need to draw the articulational differentiation; the present analysis, working through the chain of actualizing desire (perception-to-movement), must.

The unifying ground of this single ontological structure is the two senses of \textit{paschein} Aristotle establishes in \textit{De Anima} as the structural register for perceptual reception: one sense of being-affected is destruction by an opposite, the other is preservation in which a potential is brought into its actuality (II.5, 417b2--7). The present section deploys the second, preservative sense at the level of the higher-order emotions. There is no mode of being-there from which pleasure or pain is absent: every moment of our being-there is colored by hedonic tonality. Accordingly, both perception with its co-given hedonic tonality and the emotions are species of \textit{paschein}. The magnitude-axis along which they differ is, however, multi-dimensional. It runs along three independent dimensions: hedonic intensity (how pleasant or painful the affective episode is); existential weight (how deeply the episode bears on the agent's mattering-structure); and cognitive articulation (how propositionally specified the episode's content is).\footnote{I must point out that the third and fourth sense of \textit{pathos} (the unpleasant character of the occurring to me and the magnitude of that unpleasantness), as analyzed by Heidegger, ``does not need, without qualification, to have the character of the harmful, that of \gk{φθορά} [\textit{phthora}, ``destruction'']. Rather, Aristotle recognizes a \gk{μεταβολή} [\textit{metabolē}, ``change''], \gk{κίνησις} [\textit{kinēsis}, ``motion''], \gk{ἀλλοίωσις} [\textit{alloiōsis}, ``alteration''], in which \gk{πάσχειν} [\textit{paschein}, ``to be affected''] has the character of \gk{σωτηρία} [\textit{sōtēria}, ``preservation'']'' (\textit{BCAP} 132).} The emotions are not simply more intense than basic hedonic tonality; they are more articulated and---often, though not always---more existentially weighted. Heidegger's reading of the \textit{Metaphysica} fourfold's fourth sense treats magnitude as both quantitative and existential, the size of the misfortune that strikes (\textit{BCAP} 132); the conception for which I am arguing preserves this dimension while specifying that the articulational dimension is the one carrying most of the structural weight. The drinking case Aristotle gives in \textit{De Motu Animalium} may involve high hedonic intensity---real thirst is intense---without propositional articulation or substantial existential weight. Conversely, certain reflective episodes may carry high existential weight and propositional articulation without acute hedonic intensity: the considered grief one carries quietly,\footnote{Grief presses on the requirement, set out above, that the chain proceed to action only where a \textit{realizable} good is disclosed (and otherwise terminate at $A_3$): the grief borne for an irrevocable loss, like the longing \textit{boulēsis} (``rational wish'') may form even ``for impossibles,'' has no $A_4$. Konstan goes so far as to doubt ``that grief might not qualify as a \textit{pathos} at all'' for Aristotle. For, on the classical conception of the emotions, ``which looked more to agency and effect on social standing, than to one's inner state, grief and anger look radically different'' (258). But the difficulty dissolves once the apparent good is seen to be \textit{phantasia}-and-\textit{doxa}-borne: an evaluative ratification can present a \textit{non-present} object---a good now lost, an evil that cannot be undone---and the \textit{pathos} is constituted by that ratified appearance, complete at $A_3$, whether or not any realizable course of action follows. What terminates the chain in the speculative case is the disclosure of \textit{no} good or evil at all; grief, by contrast, discloses a genuine and present pain over an absent good, and so remains an emotion though it issue in no deed.} the determinate shame one acknowledges without active distress. The articulational dimension is, therefore, independent of the intensity dimension: differential articulation is the distinction at issue, not differential strength. What separates hedonic tonality in \textit{aisthēsis} from the emotions are, accordingly, not a categorical division between two ontological strata but graduation along this multi-dimensional magnitude-axis: it is an \textit{articulational} graduation rather than a difference in kind. Thus, one might walk down a dark alley at night and experience a diffuse uneasiness, a bare sense that ``something-should-be-avoided''; and, then, on seeing a hooded figure holding what you perceive to be a weapon (\textit{taking-as}), pass into determinate fear of ``I-am-fearful-now-of-this-person-hurting-me-in-the-near-future-and-must-flee.'' The hedonic tonality is unpleasant in both cases; but, what changes is the episode's articulational concretion: its propositional degree and existential weight: this specific person is a threat \textit{now} because they \textit{will} cause pain or evil imminently (anticipated/projected future pain or destructive evil). 

What is articulated across this graduation---filled minimally in perception's basic hedonic tonality, maximally and determinately in the higher-order emotions---is the single ontological structure: the fourfold ontology of alteration of the \textit{Metaphysica}, the three constitutive aspects of the \textit{Rhetorica}, the enmattered form-material unity of the \textit{De Anima}, and the ecstatic temporality that \textit{phantasia} holds together. The sections that follow develop each articulation in turn---the temporal, least explicit in the three Aristotelian schemata, in a section of its own---and show how the structure so articulated grounds \textit{logos}. Each articulation is filled but barely at the perceptual pole, co-given with bare \textit{aisthēsis}, and determinately at the rhetorical pole, once \textit{doxa} ratifies the appearance as true and the higher-order emotion---anger, fear, pity, shame, and their kin---thereby comes into its determinate being.

\subsubsection{From the Lectures to \textit{Being and Time}}

The articulational distinction drawn here between hedonic tonality in \textit{aisthēsis} and the emotions are, what I believe to be, the recovery of a structural pattern that Heidegger's own intellectual trajectory presupposes but does not fully articulate at the \textit{pathos} level. Firstly, Heidegger's \textit{Being and Time} retrospectively names Aristotle's \textit{Rhetorica} as the first systematic hermeneutic of attunement (\textit{Stimmung}).\footnote{``The different modes of state-of-mind and the ways in which they are interconnected in their foundations cannot be Interpreted within the problematic of the present investigation. The phenomena have long been well-known ontically under the terms ``affects'' and ``feelings'' and have always been under consideration in philosophy. It is not an accident that the earliest systematic Interpretation of affects that has come down to us is not treated in the framework of `psychology.' Aristotle investigates the \textit{\gk{πάθη}} [affects] in the second book of his \textit{Rhetoric}. Contrary to the traditional orientation, according to which rhetoric is conceived as the kind of thing we `learn in school,' this work of Aristotle must be taken as the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being with one another. Publicness, as the kind of Being which belongs to the ``they,'' not only has in general its own way of having a mood, but needs moods and `makes' them for itself. It is into such a mood and out of such a mood that the orator speaks. He must understand the possibilities of moods in order to rouse them and guide them aright'' (\textit{BT} 178).} Heidegger here performs an explicit retrospective citation: the \textit{Rhetorica} is the first Aristotelian prefiguration of what \textit{Being and Time} will analyze as attunement (\textit{Stimmung}) and the disclosive structure of disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}). The chronological arc from the lectures \textit{pathos}-analysis to the \textit{Being and Time}'s analysis of disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}) is drawn by Heidegger himself. \footnote{The translation of Heideggerian \textit{Befindlichkeit} as disposedness  has been authoritatively traced by Heidegger scholars. Pöggeler states the identification cleanly: ``The speaking person is characterized by \textit{ethos} or attitude (\textit{Haltung}) and by \textit{pathos} or one's feeling of attunement (\textit{Befindlichkeit})'' (Pöggeler 169). Michalski supplies the most explicit formulation: ``we should think of Aristotle's notion of \textit{pathos}---which Heidegger, let it be noted, translates in his lecture course as ``attunement'' (\textit{Befindlichkeit}), and which is thereby designated as the historical impulse for Heidegger's own existential-ontological thematization of the attunement of Dasein'' (Michalski 75). Gadamer's first-person recollection of the 1924 lecture confirms the framing: ``\textit{In the 1924 lecture Heidegger especially emphasized `how logos had its ground in pathe.' The pathe are `the fundamental possibilities, by which Dasein primarily orients itself, is attuned.... The possibility to speak about things is first presented within the so-characterized attunement [Sich-Befindens] and being-in-the-world, to the extent that the appearance, which the thing has in its immediate environment, has been removed.}''' (Gadamer 57).}

Second, the structural-relation follows the grounding-direction Heidegger establishes in the lectures:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    Insofar as the \gk{πάθη} [\textit{pathē}] are not merely an annex of psychical processes, but are rather \textit{the ground out of which speaking arises, and which what is expressed grows back into}; the \gk{πάθη}, for their part, are \textit{the basic possibilities in which being-there itself is primarily oriented toward itself}, finds itself. The primary being-oriented, the illumination of its being-in-the-world is not a \textit{knowing}, but rather a \textit{finding-oneself} that can be determined differently, according to the mode of being-there of a being. (\textit{BCAP} 176).
\end{adjustwidth}
 The companion passage from \textit{Being and Time} makes the structural identity explicit. Disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}), Heidegger writes, discloses Dasein ``\textit{prior to} all cognition and volition, and \textit{beyond} their range of disclosure'' (\textit{BT} 175); indeed, ``\textit{the mood has already disclosed, in every case, Being-in-the-world as a whole, and makes it possible first of all to direct oneself towards something}'' (\textit{BT} 176). The ``finding-oneself'' of the lectures becomes the disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}) of \textit{Being and Time}. The grounding direction is identical in both texts: the emotions, in the lectures, ground \textit{logos} and serve as the ``primary being-oriented,'' and disposedness grounds ``understanding'' and ``discourse'' in \textit{Being and Time}.\footnote{The grounding is established in \textit{Being and Time}: disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}) is existentially equiprimordial with understanding (\textit{Verstehen}) and discourse (\textit{Rede}), but in the order disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}) discloses first: ``a mood assails us. It comes neither from `outside' nor from `inside', but arises out of Being-in-the-world, as a way of such Being'' (176). As Heidegger argues earlier, ``only because the ``there'' has already been disclosed in a state-of-mind [\textit{Befindlichkeit}] can immanent reflection come across `Experiences' at all'' (\textit{BT} 175). Heidegger's retrospective gloss names Aristotle's \textit{Rhetorica} as ``the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being-with-one-another'' (178). In naming it so, \textit{Being and Time} ratifies in its own idiom the grounding-direction the lectures established: disposedness, like the emotions it succeeds, is that out of which discourse first arises.} Both groundings operate at the most basic level: pre-cognitive, pre-volitional, constitutive of the disclosive medium in which entities can show up at all as mattering. The actualization chain established in the prior section reinstates the same direction at the \textit{pathos}-grade: basic \textit{epithymia} at $A_1$ is pre-\textit{doxa}, pre-\textit{logos}, and therefore prior to all cognition and volition; the chain therefore exhibits attunement (\textit{Stimmung}) as the disclosive ground from which \textit{doxa} and the emotions proceed.

The fourfold articulation of \textit{pathos} establishes the perceptual case as the structural ground from which the higher-order emotions of the \textit{Rhetorica} proceed. Each of the four senses Heidegger gathers from \textit{Metaphysica}---alterability, the \textit{energeia} of alteration, harmful alteration, and the magnitude of the harmful---was there shown to be satisfied at the perceptual level, with the structural register of preservative \textit{paschein} specifying the manner of its operation. The present section accordingly takes the fourfold as established and turns to its articulationally concrete instantiation at the higher-order emotional level: the determinate emotions of anger, fear, and pity, with their propositional contents, conative orientations, and hedonic tonalities. The same single ontological structure specified at minimal articulation in the perceptual case is here realized at maximal articulation (the \textit{doxa}-mediated case); the emotions of the \textit{Rhetorica} are not a second \textit{pathos}-structure but a second articulational concretion of the one ontological structure the perceptual case already exhibits. The four senses, taken as established at the perceptual level, must here be understood as satisfied on both the perceptual and \textit{orectic} sides: the \textit{aisthēma}/\textit{resonant aisthēma} is the \textit{energeia} of the \textit{aisthētikon} and \textit{epithymia}/\textit{resonant epithymia} the \textit{energeia} of the \textit{orektikon}, co-given as one in substrate but different in being. Heidegger's reading of the fourfold as a progressive narrowing (\textit{BCAP} 132) specifies the magnitude-axis along which the same structure is realized at varying intensities and articulations: hedonic intensity (how pleasant or painful), existential weight (how mattering), and cognitive articulation (how propositionally specified).

\subsubsection{The \textit{Rhetorica}'s Constitutive Threefold: \textit{Pathos} as Change, \textit{Krisis}, and Hedonic Tonality}

The primary definition of emotion in Aristotle's corpus appears not in \textit{De Anima} or any of the traditionally categorized psychological treatises but in the \textit{Rhetorica}: ``the emotions are all those feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements, and that are also attended by pain or pleasure. Such are anger, pity, fear and the like, with their opposites'' (\textit{Rhetorica} II.1, 1378a21--23). Though the definition appears within the context of rhetorical theory's practical applications, it carries substantial ontological weight; and it is this definition along with the four senses of \textit{pathos} that must anchor any systematic analysis of \textit{pathos} in either of its articulational concretions. In his reading of this same passage from the \textit{Rhetorica}, Heidegger isolates the three constitutive aspects of every \textit{pathos}:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
(1) \gk{Μεταβάλλοντες} [\textit{Metaballontes} ``undergoing change'']: something along the way with respect to which ``a change sets in for us,'' through which ``we change'' from one disposition to another. (2) Combined with this change, \gk{διαφέρουσι πρὸς τὰς κρίσεις}, we ``differentiate ourselves'' from ourselves before the change in that which is the hearer's task: ``to take a position,'' ``to form a view.'' The formation of a view involves the manner and mode in which we change. (3) \gk{οἷς ἕπεται λύπη καὶ ἡδονή}: not ``following,'' but rather ``co-given'' in combination with the \gk{πάθη} is a ``being-disposed-as-higher-or-lower'' of the being-there in question. (\textit{BCAP} 115)
\end{adjustwidth}
\textit{Kriseis}, here, are not external judgments altered by emotions from without, but rather they are position-takings of being-there from out of the changed disposition. Heidegger's gloss emphasizes a differentiating-of-oneself-from-oneself, a being-different-from-what-one-was in respect of how one stands toward the matter. Heidegger insists, similarly, that pleasure and pain are not consequences that arrive after the emotion but are co-given with it as a being-disposed-as-higher-or-lower. The \textit{pathos} is the changed-disposition-with-its-position-taking; the pleasure or pain is the tonal elevation or depression co-given with that change. Accordingly, every \textit{pathos}, as articulated in the \textit{Rhetorica} and Heidegger's gloss, is a (1) change-in-disposition that (2) differentiates the subject's position-taking and is (3) co-given with hedonic tonality.

The three-fold articulation is not, however, first introduced at the level of the emotions. The second moment in particular---``we differentiate ourselves with respect to position-takings''---is already operative at the perceptual level: Heidegger reads Aristotle's account of \textit{aisthēsis} as a \textit{mesotēs} [``mean''] with the character of \textit{kritikon}, a being-positioned toward possible objects that is itself a capacity for separation and discrimination (\textit{BCAP} 126), with Aristotle himself confirming that the perceiving soul is said to make an ``affirmation or negation'' and to pursue or avoid accordingly (\textit{DA} III.7, 431a8--12). This hedonic tonality---the organism's pre-propositional orientation toward the pleasant and away from the painful, similar to what Heidegger names the disclosive attunement (\textit{Stimmung}) of disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit})\footnote{``What we indicate ontologically by the term `state-of-mind' [\textit{Befindlichkeit}] is ontically the most familiar and everyday sort of thing; our mood, our Being-attuned [\textit{Stimmung}]'' (\textit{BT} 172).}---is co-given with perception from the earliest moment of the actualization chain. It is dispositional; it colors the perceptual field with the most general affective tonality of good or bad, pursue or avoid. The position-taking moment is therefore constitutive of perception with hedonic tonality; it is not introduced anew by \textit{doxa}. Basic hedonic tonality satisfies the differentiation aspect pre-propositionally but not pre-positionally---as the perceiver's already-being-disposed toward the perceived as pursueable or avoidable---whereas \textit{doxa} articulates this position-taking propositionally. The remaining two aspects similarly hold at the basic level. The first---\textit{metaballontes} (``those-undergoing change'')---is satisfied trivially: ``being pained or pleased, or thinking'' are themselves \textit{kinēseis} of the soul (\textit{DA} I.4, 408b5--7), and hedonic tonality is just this \textit{kinetic} actualization of desire in the perceptual event. The third---co-givenness of pleasure and pain---is just what hedonic tonality is. Emotion, by contrast, is more articulationally concrete. Anger is not undifferentiated displeasure but ``a desire accompanied by pain, for a conspicuous revenge for a conspicuous slight'' (\textit{Rhetorica} II.2, 1378a31--32). Each emotion, in Aristotle's taxonomy, specifies a propositional evaluation,\footnote{For anger, the slight; for fear, the anticipated evil; for pity, the undeserved suffering (\textit{Rhetorica} II.2, 1378a30--32 for anger; II.5, 1382a21--22 for fear; II.8, 1385b13--15 for pity).} a conative orientation,\footnote{Anger names the conative orientation explicitly: ``a desire accompanied by pain, for a conspicuous revenge'' (\textit{Rhetorica} II.2, 1378a30). For fear the conative orientation is interpretive as Aristotle does not explicitly state it. From the following passage we can surmise---from the formal structure of avoidance---that the conative component is the desire to avoid or flee the anticipated/impending evil: ``Fear may be defined as a pain or disturbance due to imagining some destructive or painful evil in the future. For there are some evils, e.g. wickedness or stupidity, the prospect of which does not frighten us: only such as amount to great pains or losses do. And even these only if they appear not remote but so near as to be imminent: we do not fear things that are a very long way off; for instance, we all know we shall die, but we are not troubled thereby, because death is not close at hand. From this definition it will follow that fear is caused by whatever we feel has great power of destroying us, or of harming us in ways that tend to cause us great pain. Hence the very indications of such things are terrible, making us feel that the terrible thing itself is close at hand; and this---the approach of what is terrible---is danger'' (1382a22-32).} and a hedonic tonality (the pain or pleasure that attends the whole complex). It is this tripartite determinacy that separates emotional \textit{paschein} from the hedonic tonality of perceptual \textit{paschein}. The \textit{Rhetorica}'s three-aspect articulation is therefore not a distinguishing mark of the \textit{pathē} but the general structure of any \textit{pathos}, applicable at every articulational concretion.

\subsection{Enmattered-Accounts}

Aristotle establishes the ontological framework for understanding emotion when he argues that all affections of the soul are ``enmattered accounts'' (\textit{De Anima}, I.1, 403a25-b19) that cannot be abstracted from their material instantiation. The dialectician, Aristotle observes, would define anger as ``the appetite for returning pain for pain, or something like that.'' The physicist, by contrast, would define it as ``a boiling of the blood or warm substance surrounding the heart'' (403a31--b1). Both are determinate ways of \textit{addressing} anger and bringing it to language. The first yields the \textit{eidos} (the form or ``look'') of anger; the second yields the \textit{hylē} ( the corporeal medium). However, neither account is sufficient without the other; the full definition requires both: anger is the \textit{desire} for retaliation \textit{realized in} the boiling of blood around the heart. In this sense, the emotions are meaning-articulations whose meaning is inseparable from their bodily occurrence. The formal and material accounts are complementary rather than competing; indeed, Aristotle argues that anything less would fail to capture the entirety of the phenomenon. Heidegger's remark here is rather telling: ``Only in \textit{phenomenology} has this begun. No division between ``psychic'' and ``bodily acts''!'' (134). Thus, in his description of fear, he states: ``\gk{εἶδος} [\textit{eidos}, ``form''] of fearing also has the primary relatedness to a finding-oneself of the body'' (139).\footnote{Gross supplies the most concentrated English rendering of Heidegerr's position regarding the emotions or \textit{pathē} as enmattered: ``[a]nd pathē are not merely psychological emotions that unhappily rule those animal-like individuals who suffer from insufficiently traine dminds. They are not abstract (there is no such thing as pure fear, detached from a person), nor are they simply physical-material alterations (``boiling blood,'' or a ``cold sweat''). Rather the pathē indicate possible ways of being-moved that tie humans in a unique way to their embodiment. They do so not by providing a definite material body upon which to work, but rather by determining the possibilities for moving about a shared world'' (``Introduction'' 26).}  Anger is not the desire for retaliation plus, contingently, a hot flush. It is the bodily being-toward-others as having-been-slighted, in which the \textit{eidetic} structure---the dialectician's formal account, comprising the conative aim at vengeance, the hedonic tonality of pain at the slight and pleasure at future revenge, and the cognitive evaluation of the slight as undeserved---is \textit{already} the form of a bodily occurrence: the \textit{eidos} and the bodily realization are not two separable contents that the body subsequently expresses, but the single articulated determination of anger's mode of \textit{bodily}-being-in-the-world-with-others. The shaking and the rush of blood are not symptoms of a hidden judgment; they are part of the way the judgment is \textit{bodily there}. Conversely, the bodily occurrence is not a brute physiological event happening to a soul that judges separately; it is the bodily form of \textit{just this} judgment---the slight and its pain, the perpetrator, the desire for retaliation---\textit{articulated} in flesh. It is this double-\textit{articulation} that distinguishes the genuine philosopher of nature who sees the phenomenon as it is: bodily-formed, \textit{eidos}-articulated, world-engaged, being-occurred-to. The emotions are not material processes, nor are they immaterial \textit{ideai} or forms; they are structural moments of the way a finite living being finds itself in the world. The implications presented here are considerable. If anger is constitutively enmattered---simultaneously a propositional evaluation (the perceived slight), a conative orientation (the desire to retaliate), a hedonic tonality (the pain from the slight and the pleasure from anticipated vengeance), and a physiological alteration (the heating of blood)---then no analysis or explanation of emotion that reduces it to any one of these components will suffice or contain true explanatory value. Emotion is not merely judgment, though it includes judgment; it is not merely desire, though it includes desire; it is not merely pleasure or pain, though it includes pleasure and pain; it is not merely a bodily change, though it is inseparable from bodily change. The enmattered account thus imposes a structural constraint---which I will explore more thoroughly in a later subsection---on any placement of emotion in the actualization chain: emotion must be located at a point where assertive or propositional, conative, hedonic, and physiological processes converge, and it must be understood as something \textit{irreducible} to any one of them.

\subsection{The Articulational Concretion: From \textit{Doxa} to Emotion}

As we have discussed the structural components of which any and all of the emotions must consist, we must now turn to the conditions required for emotion to be articulationally concretized: ``[f]urther, when we think something to be fearful or threatening, emotion is immediately produced, and so too with what is encouraging; but when we merely imagine we remain as unaffected as persons who are looking at a painting of some dreadful or encouraging scene'' (\textit{De Anima} III.3, 427b21-24). Aristotle's statement that when merely imagining we remain ``unaffected,'' on the surface, seems to be a counter-example: how can the soul be ``unaffected'' if hedonic tonality is supposedly always operative? When we look at the painting of a dreadful scene, we are \textit{not entirely} unaffected. We register the painting as more or less compelling, more or less aesthetically pleasing or displeasing, and, thus, hedonic tonality is operative. Similar too, with mere imagining; as previously established, every \textit{phantasma} is dual in being: it consists of both \textit{resonant aisthēsis} (\textit{eidetic} content, the ``what it is'') and \textit{resonant epithymia} (hedonic tonality, the ``how it matters''). What we lack is the emotion: there is no real fear, no real grief.\footnote{Caston's reading of the same passage clarifies what is structurally at stake: ``phantasia ... allows us to conjure up images freely, independent of our views about what is true (427b17--20).... Because it can function apart from acceptance, moreover, it is possible to remain emotionally unaffected by its contents (3.3, 427a23--24). Belief, in contrast, affects us immediately (427a21--22)'' (Caston 45--46). The unaffected/affected contrast Aristotle draws in \textit{De Anima} (III.3, 427b21--24) is therefore not between absence and presence of \textit{phantasia} but between unratified and ratified \textit{phantasma}. The bare image is \textit{phantasia} without \textit{doxa}; the affecting image is \textit{phantasia} with \textit{doxa}'s assertoric uptake. This is what I call \textit{doxa}-ratification.} Thus, ``unaffected,'' here, specifies the absence of the more articulationally concrete emotions, not absence of all \textit{pathos}.\footnote{In \textit{Poetica}, Aristotle explicitly deploys tragedy as a case to argue for \textit{catharsis}: ``A tragedy, then, is the imitation of an action that is serious and also, as having magnitude, complete in itself; in language with pleasurable accessories, each kind brought in separately in the parts of the work; in a dramatic, not in a narrative form; with incidents arousing pity and fear, wherewith to accomplish its catharsis of such emotions'' (6, 1449b24-28). If looking at the dramatic representation of dreadful scenes were \textit{entirely apathic}, tragedy could have no cathartic function.} 

While Aristotle deploys this passage to distinguish \textit{phantasia} from \textit{doxa}, he does so on an affective basis: the bare \textit{phantasma}---the fearful scenario merely entertained---leaves the soul unaffected, and it is only when we \textit{take-something-as-truly-fearful-now} that ``emotion is immediately produced.'' What concretizes the higher-order emotions, accordingly, is not \textit{phantasia} alone but the soul's involuntary \textit{taking-as-true}: \textit{doxa}'s ratification of the \textit{phantasma}'s evaluative content as disclosing how things \textit{are}. This ratification is immediate---it admits no perceptible interval between the yes-saying and the onset of emotion---and it is no \textit{acceptance}. We no more \textit{accept} an appearance than we \textit{accept} an opinion: both befall us involuntarily, both stand open to truth and falsity, and only subsequent deliberation can adjudicate the veracity of either. Jessica Moss introduces the Stoic term \gk{συγκατάθεσις} (\textit{synkatathesis}) commonly translated as ``acceptance'' to resolve this tension.\footnote{``On the Stoic view,'' as Moss introduces it, ``all sentient creatures (animals and people) are subject to \textit{phantasiai}, appearances. Rational creatures have an ability which non-rational ones lack: we can criticize and question these appearances. If we decide an appearance is false we reject it; if we decide it is true we assent to it. Only once we have assented do we have belief (\gk{δόξα} [\textit{doxa}]).... Let's introduce the term `acceptance' to name a genus which includes both active assent to appearances and passive failure to question them. We can then say that while for people experiencing an appearance does not yet amount to accepting it, for animals the distinction between experiencing and accepting simply does not arise'' (\textit{Apparent Good} 92).} To call the emotional uptake an ``acceptance'' is to interpose a voluntary assent---the Stoic \gk{συγκατάθεσις}---that Aristotle's psychology nowhere contains; between the \textit{phantasia} that makes-present and the \textit{doxa} that says \textit{yes} there is no third faculty of endorsement whose verdict the soul confers at will. This is where the present account parts company with its sharpest rival. Moss has put the contrary case in its strongest form. The passions rest on \textit{phantasia} \textit{alone}: a quasi-perceptual appearance of something as good or bad, and ``exercises of [and seated in] a non-rational part of the soul incapable of belief'' (\textit{Apparent Good} 98).\footnote{Perhaps use this as an opportunity to argue against, in a Heideggerian fashion, the faculties of the soul as if it consists of determinate, compartmentalized aspects: aisthetikon, orektikon, threptikon, etc. Rather, each of these aspects, which aristotle refers to as "faculties, forms, powers" in the Greek are most commonly referred to as "dyanmis" (potentiality), are, rather, names for different ways in which being-affect to is made present in the soul: aisthetikon = being-affected by objects of sense; orektikon = being-affected by objects of desire; etc. Each a potentiality best understood by its activity and objects of that activity, but all of which participate in motion, energeia, and being-affected.} Accordingly, as the \textit{Ethica Eudemia} has it, ``appearance [\textit{phantasia}] and opinion [\textit{doxa}] do not reside in the same part of the soul'' (VII.2 1235b25--29; also qtd. in Moss, \textit{Akrasia} 129). The qualifiedly pleasant ``appears pleasant and good without qualification just as the sun appears a foot wide,'' and ``is best understood as a default yielding rather than as active rational assent'' (\textit{Akrasia} 127, 138); the belief corresponding with the particular passion, on her account, is at most a ``normal, default accompaniment,'' ``essentially independent'' of the passion---one feels anger and yet ``the belief that they have done you wrong'' is ``essentially independent of'' the anger being felt (\textit{Apparent Good} 98--99).

With Moss's \textit{mechanism} I have no quarrel, and I adopt it outright with one qualification. The mechanism for which she argues is that an appearance is taken-as-\textit{true} by default; it is \textit{accepted} unless the rational faculty actively contradicts it.\footnote{Moss's proof is the illusion of the \textit{De Insomniis}: a finger pressed beneath the eye makes one object appear as two, and ``if it escaped someone's notice that a finger was pressed under his eye, one thing would not only appear to be two but he would believe that it was (\gk{δόξει}), while if it did not escape his notice it would appear but he would not believe'' (\textit{De Insomniis} 461b29--462a8; qtd. in Moss, \textit{Akrasia} 133--34). The appearance is thus \textit{believed}---taken-as-true---by default, and disbelieved only when something (here, awareness of the finger) contradicts it. Moss, quoting Gallop, calls this ``acceptance by default'' (135): ``belief `simply fails to oppose''' the appearance (135), so the uptake is ``best understood as default yielding rather than as active rational assent'' (138). Perhaps this comes down to differences in translation, but the primary lines of this passage seem to be the point of contention. In my translation, they are rendered: ``one object will not only present two visual images, but will create an opinion of its being two objects'' (462a1--2). My translation further underscores the involuntariness of opinion formation, and O'Gorman's point that the world about us forms our opinions in that it is the ``one object'' that ``create[s] an opinion of its being two objects.''} Reality itself is made-present as stable barring impairment experienced in things like fevers or drug-induced states. As such, I would argue that an appearance, rather than taken-as-\textit{true}, is taken-as-\textit{is}; for to take something as true or false presupposes cognitive evaluation, or discourse of reason (\textit{logos}). What I deny is the \textit{separability} of \textit{doxa} from the emotions. For the higher-order emotions, whose objects---a slight, an undeserved suffering---are not perceptible features to be read off the world but are \textit{doxa}-constituted, the ratification is no accompaniment that may or may not attend the appearance: it is the act through which the appearance becomes the emotion at all. The default \textit{acceptance} of \textit{phantasia}'s making-present (taking-as-true) which, Moss argues, is the sole source of emotion, cannot be the work of a part \textit{incapable} of belief: to take what is made-present \textit{as true} is already to say \textit{yes} to it, and that yes-saying---the involuntary \textit{phasis}---is the minimal act of \textit{doxa} itself. The default, undeliberated character of the assertion renders the \textit{doxa} involuntary, not absent; there is no \textit{taking-as-true} that falls short of belief, for the taking \textit{is} belief's act. What Moss casts as a sub-\textit{doxastic} ``acceptance,'' the reading her Stoicizing lens supplies (\textit{Apparent Good} §4.7), is, therefore, \textit{doxa} in its default, uncorrected mode---as Aristotle's own word attests: of the uncorrected illusion one does not merely entertain but \textit{believes} it (\textit{doxei}) (\textit{DI} 3, 462a1). 

My quarrel with Moss is thus exact and narrow---we share the mechanism and dispute only its \textit{modality}: whether the \textit{doxastic} ratification is, as she holds, a separable accompaniment the appearance may or may not carry, or, as I hold, the constitutive act through which an appearance generates determinate emotion.\footnote{The judgmental reading Moss opposes has a long pedigree, which the present account inherits and completes. W.~W. Fortenbaugh makes ``the thought of outrage as the efficient cause mentioned in the essential definition'' of anger (Fortenbaugh 12)---though a belief without \textit{phantasia} cannot answer recalcitrance. David Konstan reads the emotions as judgment-laden socially inflected interpretations: ``Aristotle and other Greek philosophers held that animals and small children did not have emotions [\textit{pathē}] ... in the proper sense'' (xii). His position, that emotions essentially involve some kind of cognition, is friendly to the present account, differing only in \textit{where} it locates the cognition: in culturally-shaped interpersonal judgment, rather than in the \textit{doxa}-ratification of an appearance. Jamie Dow, defending against the emotions as deriving from mere appearance, presents Aristotle's view ``that all the passions involve taking their representational contents to be the way things are, if not wholeheartedly, then at least to some extent'' (Dow 106, 198). What Dow refers to as ``affirmation'' is what I take to be \textit{doxa} proper rather than an attitude weaker than belief. The \textit{doxa}-ratification defended here is what carries the line past both Fortenbaugh's belief, which cannot run against standing judgment, and Dow's affirmation, which stops short of it.} When one startles at the rope, \textit{taken-as} a snake, in the shadowy corner of the shed no higher-order emotion is formed, only a basal startle: the sensitive \textit{phantastic} response Aristotle assigns to the creatures that have \textit{phantasia} but no \textit{doxa}. Such responses I concede to Moss's \textit{phantastic} reading: the basic hedonic tonality (\textit{epithymia}) co-given with \textit{aisthēsis}---the \textit{resonant epithymia} carried by the \textit{phantasma}---is itself \textit{phantastic} and pre-\textit{doxastic}, and requires no ratification to move the animal. Aristotle's own contrast in \textit{De Anima} is, after all, drawn not between a voluntary and an involuntary \textit{phantasia} but between \textit{phantasia} and \textit{doxazein}: it is when we \textit{believe} (\textit{doxasōmen}) something fearful, and not when we merely picture it, that we are at once affected (III.3, 427b20--24).\footnote{The Greek bears out the contrast. `\textit{Phantasia} is up to us, but ``\gk{δοξάζειν} [\textit{doxazein}, ``to believe''] is not: \gk{δοξάζειν δ' οὐκ ἐφ' ἡμῖν} (\textit{De Anima} III.3, 427b20). For the Greek text, see Ross ed.} It is, therefore, \textit{doxa}'s \textit{taking-as-true} that is \textit{constitutive} for the higher, articulationally concrete emotions: the involuntary ratification of propositional content is the condition through which determinate emotions come into being and enter the actualization chain. 

Just as the hedonic tonality (pain or pleasure) and the \textit{orectic} charge (avoid or pursue) is structural to \textit{aisthēsis}, so too with emotion. The \textit{orectic} charge present in emotion is not \textit{resonant epithymia} worked up or converted into emotion by \textit{doxai}. The \textit{resonant} register touches a present emotion only through recollection. When the content a past episode lays down in memory is itself an articulationally concrete emotion---the anger felt at a slight, the joy felt at a pleasure---what the \textit{phantasma}---or, in this case, the memory-\textit{phantasma}---preserves is no longer the bare perceptual \textit{resonant aisthēma} and the \textit{orectic resonant epithymia}, but that determinate emotional content as such. This I call \textit{resonant emotion}: the \textit{doxa}-articulated third member of the \textit{resonant} family, the anger or grief laid down \textit{with} the memory of its occasion and recovered, felt anew, when that memory is summoned. It is distinct both from the emotion presently felt and from the fresh emotion that re-engaging the memory can kindle---a category Aristotle comes close to but does not himself isolate. The involuntariness of \textit{doxa} is what gives emotion its distinctive phenomenological character as something that \textit{befalls} the agent rather than something the agent elects to undergo. One does not choose to be afraid; one finds oneself afraid because one has involuntarily \textit{taken-something-to-be-threatening-now}. One does not \textit{choose} to find an object pleasant or painful; we simply \textit{find} them so. Heidegger pinpoints this specifically in his analysis of fear: 
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    The one who is brought into fear must once ``believe'' that the definite thing that threatens, threatens \textit{him}, and further, that what is threatening proceeds \textit{from this definite human being}, and that he threatens him \textit{now}. What is threatening must be there not only in the sense that I know that one day it could befall me---not being oriented toward the possibility of threat, but rather \textit{being-for, believing,} that I have to expect this or that, that something is now happening to me by this person. (173-174)
\end{adjustwidth}
Without this dispositional uptake---this \textit{believing/opining}-it-to-be-the-case-for-me---fear cannot arise. Heidegger then draws out the negative cases---``those for whom it goes swimmingly'' and ``those who believe that nothing more can happen to them''---``stand outside the possibility of fear'' (174). The lack of fear does not derive from a failure of perception (they may see the threat clearly) but a failure of \textit{appropriating} the threat into \textit{belief about themselves}: ``appropriated as mattering to one'' (174).

The individual definitions for various emotions presented in the \textit{Rhetorica} flesh out the position-taking or evaluative structure Aristotle identifies in \textit{De Anima} using anger as his representative example. Each definition, as previously articulated, specifies a cognitive content, a hedonic tonality, and---at least implicitly---a conative component. His definition of shame offers a useful explanatory case. ``Shame may be defined as pain or disturbance in regard to bad things, whether present, past, or future, which seem likely to involve us in discredit; and shamelessness as contempt or indifference in regard to these same bad things'' (\textit{Rhetorica} II.6, 1383b15-17). The cognitive evaluation is the judgment of one's own disgrace; that some act, trait, circumstance, etc. (whether already done, happening in the now, or anticipated) will cause others to look upon or judge them unfavorably. The conative component, though not explicitly stated, is easier to parse given the contrastive definition of \textit{shamelessness}. In light of this, the conative element of shame is likely one caring about their standing (``saving face,'' to use a colloquial idiom), and, therefore, the desire to avoid, conceal, or escape whatever threatens them. The shameless person has the same potential cognitive evaluation available (they could recognize the same facts as discrediting) but they lack the motivational care or concern that would make those facts harmful; even though the shameful and shameless person are exposed to the exact same orienting structure, the shameful person experiences no dispositional uptake---the belief or opinion that the state of affairs is damaging or discrediting---without which shame cannot arise.

That an emotion's \textit{doxa} is, for the most part, \textit{sedimented}---laid down as a \textit{resonant, having-been} rather than reasoned out in the moment---is what reconciles \textit{doxastic}-ratification with the immediacy of feeling. It might seem that anger, at least, refutes any \textit{doxa}-requirement: I am slighted and at once incensed, with no interval in which to form a judgment. But three \textit{doxai} must be told apart. There is the \textit{resonant} view, the standing and encultured opinion that to leave a greeting unreturned, for example, is a slight; there is the \textit{occurrent} ratification, the involuntary and immediate \textit{taking-as-true-of-this-now} as a slight to me; and, there is the \textit{deliberative} \textit{doxa} of later reflection, which may confirm or overturn the first two. The anger that flares when a colleague passes my greeting in silence draws on the first and consists in the second; it does not wait upon the third. This is why the affect can appear to spring from sheer appearance. The making-present---the curated dispositional grounds---is indeed immediate---\textit{colleague-ignored-me}---but it presents the event \textit{as a slight} only because a \textit{resonant} \textit{doxa} has made ``slight'' an available aspect at all. Strip away the encultured opinion that to be passed over so is to be belittled, and the same perceptual event offers nothing to resent. The appearance is, in this precise sense, \textit{doxa}-constituted: its evaluative aspect is the deposit of a prior history of \textit{taking-as-true}, and the anger that rides upon it is not the bare appearance that a colleague was silent but the \textit{having-been} that such silence, toward me, counts as a slight. Should I later catch sight of the earbuds her hair had hidden and reason my way to the contrary \textit{doxa}---that it was no slight---the anger lapses; and that it lapses upon a change of \textit{doxa} is the surest sign that a \textit{doxa} was constitutive of the emotion throughout.\footnote{The point is legible in the very passage the \textit{phantastic} reading enlists against the \textit{doxastic} one. Moss cites \textit{Ethica Nicomachea} VII.6 1149a32--34 to show that the slight may be shown by \textit{phantasia} rather than by belief; yet her own translation prints the clause she passes over: ``\textit{Logos} or \textit{phantasia} has shown that something was \textit{hubris} or a slight, and spirit (\textit{thumos}), as if having reasoned that one must fight against such a thing, is distressed right away'' (Moss, \textit{Apparent Good} 88). The ``as if having reasoned that one must fight'' is precisely the sedimented, evaluative major premise (the standing \textit{doxa} that slights are to be resented) which the fast, \textit{phantasia}-borne minor premise (``this is a slight'') presupposes. The disjunction ``\textit{logos} or \textit{phantasia}'' marks not the dispensability of \textit{doxa} but the two routes to the minor premise, the deliberative and the immediate; the evaluative major premise is reasoned, or sedimented from prior reasoning, in either case.} Aristotle himself lets the minor premise of such an inference be supplied by more than one faculty: ``this is drink,'' he writes, is pronounced by ``perception or \textit{phantasia} or thought'' (\textit{De Motu Animalium} 7, 701a). But ``this is drink'' is a recognition that bare \textit{phantasia} can deliver, whereas ``this is a slight'' is an evaluation, and a slight is no sensible property to be read off the surface of things. The \textit{phantasia} that presents an act \textit{as} a slight is therefore not the sensory \textit{phantasia} shared with the animals but the \textit{logos}-dependent, deliberative \textit{phantasia} (\textit{DA} III.11, 434a5--11), in which a \textit{resonant} \textit{doxa} has already done its silent work---which is why the diachronic depth of \textit{pathos}, the \textit{resonant having-been} it carries, is not incidental to the emotions but the very ground on which their appearances come to be evaluative at all.

\subsection{Somatic Preparation: Emotion in the Causal Chain}

The causal chain presented by Aristotle in \textit{De Motu Animalium} gives us the clearest illustration of the actualization of desire. To reiterate: sense-perception or thinking \ra imagination \ra desire \ra affections \ra organic parts \ra movement (8, 702a17--19). The chain suggests that the emotions have a somatic-preparatory role: they are what translate desire into bodily readiness and this is further indicated by the enmattered account and bodily accompaniment.\footnote{Nussbaum reads \textit{De Motu Animalium} 8 (702a18 ff.) as establishing the causal chain: ``For the affections suitably prepare the organic parts, desire the affections, and \textit{phantasia} the desire; and \textit{phantasia} comes about either through thought or through sense-perception (702a18 ff.). \textit{Phantasia}, then, is involved in every action; it must `prepare' the desire whether or not actual perceiving is going on'' (Nussbaum ``\textit{Phantasia}'' 233; cf. 221 for the joint necessity of \textit{phantasia} and desire in \textit{De Anima} (432b15 ff.).} The nature of this readiness has a precise form: the object of pursuit or avoidance is the origin of movement, the thought or imagination of which is immediately followed by ``a heating or chilling. For what is painful we avoid, what is pleasing we pursue, and anything painful or pleasing is generally speaking accompanied by a chilling or heating'' (701b34--702a2). The physiological changes accompanying emotion are not incidental but the mechanism by which desire is realized in bodily movement:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    One may see this by considering the affections. Blind courage and panic fears, erotic motions, and the rest of the corporeal affections, pleasant and painful, are all accompanied by heating or chilling, some in a particular member, others in the body generally. So, memories and anticipations, using things of this kind as likenesses, are now more and now less causes of the same changes of temperature. (\textit{MA} 8, 702a2--7)
\end{adjustwidth}
This convergence between the enmattered account of \textit{De Anima} and the causal chain of the \textit{De Motu Animalium} demonstrates emotions' genuine causal role in the actualization of desire (perception-to-movement) sequence. The ``boiling of blood around the heart'' that Aristotle identifies as the material component of anger in \textit{De Anima} is one such affection that prepares the organic parts for movement in \textit{De Motu Animalium}---the somatic substrate through which the determinate emotion is realized. Furthermore, the fact Aristotle gives the affections a step of their own, distinct from desire, within the causal chain suggests that emotion includes desire without being reducible to it. In each of the emotions discussed, the desire-component is part of the formal structure of the emotion: to define what anger \textit{is} requires mentioning what anger \textit{wants}. Desire (\textit{orexis}) is the moved mover of animal locomotion (\textit{DA} III.10, 433b10-18)---the faculty that, moved by the apparent good, moves the animal. If emotion were simply identical with desire, there would be no reason to distinguish them in the causal chain, and the placement of ``affections'' as a separate stage would be redundant. The ontological status of emotion as separate from desire consists precisely in its composite character: emotion is desire \textit{together with} physiological change \textit{structured by} cognitive content. The desire for retaliation is a component of anger, but anger is the desire for retaliation \textit{as experienced in the boiling of blood, in response to a perceived slight}. It is this composite---not the desire alone---that prepares the organic parts for movement.

As we have already discussed, the role \textit{doxa} plays in emotion formation reveals that emotion is not a raw feeling or mere physiological disturbance; rather, it is a state of the soul that necessarily involves, as a constitutive condition, a truth-committal cognitive act. The person who fears does not merely \textit{feel} afraid; the person who fears \textit{takes-something-as-truly-fearful-now}, and it is this \textit{taking-as-true} that produces the fear. Indeed, Aristotle's formulation suggests the \textit{doxastic} element is the cognitive evaluative component of the emotional state, with the \textit{orectic} and physiological components co-given with the truth-commitment or assertion rather than preceding it. However, to reiterate, Aristotle's \textit{doxa} is not a deliberate formation of a considered judgment arrived at through careful reasoning; it is the involuntary \textit{taking-of-something-as-being-truly-the-case}, a cognitive event that befalls the rational agent rather than one the agent deliberately performs. The speed with which emotion follows \textit{doxa}---``immediately''---suggests that the \textit{doxastic} component of emotion is closer to perceptual recognition than to discursive inference. When the rational agent ``perceiving by sense that the beacon is fire, it recognizes in virtue of the general faculty of sense that it signifies an enemy, because it sees it moving'' (\textit{DA} III.7, 431b5-6), the \textit{doxa} ``this signals danger'' may form almost instantaneously, and fear may follow without any perceptible interval. 

\subsection{The \textit{Pathetic} Loop: Synchronic Chain, Diachronic Saturation}

One highly important aspect of Aristotle's definition of emotion in the \textit{Rhetorica} has yet to be discussed: namely, emotion as ``judgement modifying.'' If a judgment via \textit{doxa} (\textit{taking-as-true}) is necessary for emotion to articulationally concretize, then how can emotion modify judgment? The linear chain $A_0 \rightarrow A_4$ established in prior sections captures the synchronic transitions through which \textit{aisthēsis} grounds the \textit{phantasmata}, which ground the cognitive orientations and \textit{doxa}, \textit{doxa} concretizes the emotions, and the emotions mobilize action; yet emotion, once concretized, alters the very conditions under which subsequent \textit{doxai} will be formed, under which subsequent judgments are made. Specifically, the chain is not a closed circuit describable through forward transitions alone: each actualization persists as dispositional ground for what follows. This section names that persistence the \textit{pathetic} loop and analyzes its structure on two registers, the synchronic and the diachronic, before grounding the diachronic loop in the temporality of, what Heidegger calls, thrownness (\textit{Geworfenheit}).

The seminal text for the feedback's articulation in Aristotle's psychological corpus is \textit{De Insomniis}. Aristotle there observes that: 
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    we are easily deceived respecting the operations of sense-perception when we are excited by emotions, and different persons according to their different emotions; for example, the coward when excited by fear, the amorous person by amorous desire; so that, with but little resemblance to go upon, the former thinks he sees his foes approaching, the latter, that he sees the object of his desire; and the more deeply one is under the influence of the emotion, the less similarity is required to give rise to these illusory impressions. Thus, too, both in fits of anger, and also in all states of appetite, all men become easily deceived, and more so the more their emotions are excited. (2, 460b2--10)
\end{adjustwidth}
The passage isolates a specific cognitive mechanism: under the sway of an active emotion, the controlling sense---the corrective faculty that in ordinary waking cognition intercepts misleading \textit{phantasmata} before they can issue in \textit{doxa}---is impaired, and \textit{phantasmata} that would otherwise be filtered are instead taken as true. Aristotle traces the impairment, indeed, to a structural fact of the cognitive apparatus: the faculty by which the controlling sense judges is not identical with the faculty by which \textit{phantasmata} come before the mind, and the latter can override the former when the perceiving organism is sufficiently agitated. The result is a self-reinforcing diachronic loop. An initial \textit{doxa} concretizes an initial emotion; the emotion impairs the corrective faculty; subsequent \textit{phantasmata} affirmed by \textit{doxa} unopposed; the new \textit{doxai} concretize further emotions; and the cycle continues until external intervention, somatic exhaustion, or recovery of the corrective faculty breaks the loop. The mechanism Aristotle describes is, accordingly, sufficiently general to render intelligible a range of phenomena that are otherwise puzzling. The escalation of anger through sustained rumination on a perceived slight, the intensification of fear through repeated imagining of an anticipated evil, the susceptibility of the dreamer, the ill, and the emotionally agitated to \textit{phantasmatic} deception (\textit{De Insomniis} 460b3-16; 461b3-11): each reflects the same loop running unchecked in conditions where the corrective faculty is structurally weakened.\footnote{Caston's reading further clarifies why the loop functions this way: ``[p]hantasia is, in effect, an \textit{echoing} of the initial stimulation in the sense organs: a side-effect, like the original stimulation in character, but unable to compete with fresh, incoming stimulations'' (Caston 47, citing \textit{De Insomniis} 2, 459a23--b23). The competition with incoming stimulation he observes is what the \textit{De Insomniis} mechanism specifies: when waking perceptual stimulation is reduced or when emotion lowers the corrective threshold (in agitation, illness, sustained emotion), the \textit{phantasma} can override the controlling sense.}

It is essential to read this impairment correctly. The discriminating faculty is not \textit{shut down}---if \textit{logos} was wholly absent, no \textit{doxa} could form, and, thus, no emotion---but \textit{impaired}: \textit{discourse of reason} still issues its involuntary yes-saying, only with its power to tell the veridical from the illusory degraded. Conviction thereby comes apart from accuracy. The drunk man, his discernment dulled, forms the sincere and convinced \textit{doxa} that the lady across the room is beautiful; sober the next morning, the same faculty restored, he opines that she is not. What the strong emotion produces, then, is not appearance \textit{in place of} belief but a \textit{false} belief---an uncorrected ratification. Moss's \textit{phantastic} reading construes this as a following \textit{without} belief: the impaired faculty, she holds, is ``simply silent,'' the appearance being accepted by default rather than active assent (\textit{Akrasia} 135). But an impaired faculty is not a \textit{silent} one---it still issues its involuntary yes-saying, only with its discernment degraded. The coward who sees his enemies in the shadows does not merely entertain the image of his enemies, he \textit{believes} in them. This is the plainest instance of \textit{doxa}-ratification without the corrective faculty: the appearance moves the agent \textit{because}, his discernment impaired, nothing in him contradicts it, and so it is involuntarily \textit{taken-as-true}. 

Two structural features of the mechanism deserve emphasis. First, the loop is not a malfunction of the cognitive system but an intrinsic feature of the relationship between emotion and \textit{doxa} in rational animals. Aristotle's claim is not that the corrective faculty fails because of a defect to be remedied but that the architecture of judgment is such that emotional intensification induces, of itself, a corresponding lowering of the threshold at which \textit{phantasmata} are \textit{taken-as-true} by \textit{doxa}. Second, the diachronic loop is not symmetric in time: an initial emotion lowers the threshold for the \textit{next} \textit{doxa}, which in turn elicits an emotion whose intensity reflects the lowered threshold. Thus, the temporal direction of the modulation runs from past to present: prior emotions modulate present \textit{doxa}-formation, and present \textit{doxa}-formation feeds forward into present, and future, emotion. This asymmetry will become decisive when, in the analysis to follow, the feedback is shown to belong to the diachronic rather than the synchronic register of the chain.\footnote{The \textit{pathetic} loop structure identified here is established by Aristotle, as I understand him, across three treatises, each describing the same phenomenon---\textit{pathos} as the structure of being-affected (\textit{paschein})---under a different analytic angle. \textit{De Anima} III.3, 427b21--24 establishes the cognitive condition: \textit{doxa} produces emotion immediately, with no perceptible interval between the yes-saying and the affective response. \textit{De Insomniis} 460b3--16 establishes the recursive mechanism: emotional states permit \textit{phantasmata} to be \textit{taken-as-true} when the controlling faculty is impaired, lowering the threshold for further \textit{doxa}-formation. \textit{Rhetorica} II.1, 1378a20--22 establishes the structural consequence: the emotions modify \textit{kriseis}, altering the ground on which subsequent judgments take shape. The cross-treatise reading is therefore not a synthesis imposed from outside but the methodological corollary of the claim I stated at the start, that Aristotle's rhetorical ontology must be reconstructed across his treatises rather than read off any one of them---a strategy Heidegger himself follows in Aristotelian lectures.}

Two registers are at work in the \textit{pathetic} loop, and they must be distinguished if the loop's structural function is not to be confused with the chain's directional architecture. The synchronic register concerns the structural transitions in any single actualization of being-affected: perception is actualized $A_1$, carried forward as \textit{resonant kinēsis} by the \textit{phantasma} at $A_2$; cognition and \textit{doxa} engages at $A_3$, articulating the evaluation, whereupon the determinate emotions arise as a form of desire; the emotions mobilize the organic parts through $M_3 \rightarrow A_4$ into completed action at $A_4$. These transitions are directional and irreversible within a given chain-actualization. The diachronic register, by contrast, concerns how the resonances of prior actualizations modulate subsequent actualizations: the resonance of a \textit{pathos} actualization persists, sedimented in the affected organism's dispositional ground and shaping the next actualization. There is no pure actualization, if you will; rather, every actualization is influenced by prior actualizations, moods, opinions, and emotions. Accordingly, the \textit{De Insomniis} \textit{feedback}---the escalation by which one emotion lowers the threshold for the \textit{doxa} that follows it---is a clear example of the diachronic register at work, where it operates at two timescales. The faster is the loop compressed within a single protracted episode. As anger mounts while one broods upon a slight, each \textit{doxastic}-ratification is already a fresh actualization, and the feedback by which the prior emotion lowers the threshold for the next runs \textit{across} these rapidly successive actualizations rather than within any one of them.
The slower is the sedimentation of disposition grounds---what Aristotle and Heidegger refer to as \textit{hexis} and which will be discussed below---that conditions actualizations at later times and across occasions. The properly courageous man does not one day just decide to be courageous. The man must curate courage as the proper response to fear by repeatedly doing just so. It is a disposition formed over time through numerous instances of being afraid and choosing to face that fear. Thus, when faced with danger, the coward runs while the courageous man stands and confronts it. The reason the coward, when overcome with fear, sees his enemies in the shadows is that his fear re-enters the chain at the \textit{phantastic} motion, $M_1 \rightarrow A_2$, and not at the perceptual motion $M_0 \rightarrow A_1$. Bare \textit{aisthēsis} of the proper sensibles ``is never in error or admits the least possible amount of falsehood'' (\textit{De Anima} III.3, 428b18--19), and the \textit{De Insomniis} concedes as much in allowing the frightened man but ``little resemblance to go upon'' (460b6): the shadow itself is veridically perceived. The deception and the affective filling-in belong instead to the \textit{phantasma}, where ``imaginations are for the most part false'' (428a11--12) and where the perceived shape is \textit{taken-as} an enemy; the \textit{resonant epithymia} that the fear carries forward works the faint resemblance up into the determinate figure. And because the \textit{phantasma} at $A_2$ is the already-actualized image presented to the mind, the fear must enter the motion that \textit{forms} it---before the image is settled---rather than the completed actuality, for one cannot re-bias what has already come to be. What the perceptual motion $M_0 \rightarrow A_1$ inherits is only the diffuse hedonic tonality of the standing fear-\textit{hexis}: the dark alley is pre-disclosed as ominous, its affective tone colored at $A_1$, while the determinate figure is first constituted at $A_2$. The chain remains structurally directional from $A_0$ to $A_4$ within any single actualization; the ``loop'' is the persistence of these resonances, whether across an episode's successive ratifications or across the longer life of a disposition, as ground for what follows.

This temporal-dispositional persistence is what Aristotle elsewhere analyzes under the doctrine of \textit{hexis} (\gk{ἕξις}, ``settled disposition'') and---though not quite the same---what Heidegger names throwness (\textit{Geworfenheit}); both name a structure in which prior dispositional actualizations persist as the ground from which present disclosure takes its character. The Aristotelian articulation begins with the analysis of habituation in \textit{Ethica Nicomachea}, where Aristotle states that ``moral excellence comes about as a result of habit'' (II.1, 1103a16--17). The claim's significance for the present analysis lies in its specification of \textit{how} the disposition is acquired: through repeated actualization of the chain itself. As Aristotle articulates it, ``for the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them, e.g. men become builders by building and lyre-players by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts'' (II.1, 1103a32--b2). Each actualization leaves a residual modification of the dispositional ground; over time, repeated actualizations consolidate into \textit{hexeis} that condition subsequent chain-actualizations. \textit{Hexis} is therefore not a stage of the chain alongside the perceptual, phantastic, cognitive, and orectic transitions; it is the \textit{temporal sedimentation} of repeated chain-actualizations into the dispositional ground that conditions subsequent passes through the chain.

The Heideggerian articulation runs structurally parallel. Heidegger specifies in \textit{Being and Time} that disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}) discloses Dasein in three essential respects: it discloses Dasein in its thrownness; it has already disclosed Being-in-the-world as a whole, thereby making it possible to direct oneself toward something at all; and it discloses the worldhood of the world in the mode of circumspective concern (176). Thrownness names, accordingly, the diachronic persistence of prior dispositional states: ``this characteristic of Dasein's Being---this `that it is'---is veiled in its ``whence'' and ``whither'', yet disclosed in itself all the more unveiledly; we call it the ``thrownness'' [\textit{Geworfenheit}] of this entity into its ``there''; indeed, it is thrown in such a way that, as Being-in-the-world, it is the ``there'''' (174). Dasein, in other words, is \textit{always already} disposed; the disposition is not a contingent psychological state superadded to a pre-existing self but the existential-temporal ground from which any present self-relation takes its bearings. Heidegger's decisive temporal claim, however, is that the mood or attunement (\textit{Stimmung}) itself temporalizes primarily in the past, the \textit{resonant}  \textit{having been} (\textit{Gewesenheit}): ``[u]nderstanding is grounded primarily in the future; one's \textit{state-of-mind}, however, temporalizes itself \textit{primarily} in \textit{having been}. Moods temporalize themselves---that is, their specific ecstasis belongs to a future and a Present in such a way, indeed, that these equiprimordial ecstases are modified by having been'' (390). Thus, the having-already-found-itself-disposed of disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}) is not a past \textit{now stored in memory} but a \textit{having been} (\textit{Gewesenheit}) that \textit{currently} conditions how the entities of the world are unconcealed. The thrown disposition is operative \textit{as} the present disclosure's condition, not as a remembered antecedent of it.

The convergence of the Aristotelian and Heideggerian articulations is, accordingly, no mere analogy: it is the recognition of one phenomenon---prior dispositional actualization persisting as the ground from which present disclosure takes its character---described at different ontological registers. Aristotle's articulation operates at the level of perception and affection within the soul's structure; Heidegger's operates at the level of Dasein's fundamental disclosedness. The Aristotelian lecture course of 1924 makes the convergence explicit. There Heidegger derives four basic aspects of being-there from the Aristotelian analysis of \textit{hexis}, and the fourth specifies that ``Being-there must, \textit{for itself}, take the opportunity to cultivate this being-composed as a possibility'' (\textit{BCAP} 122). \textit{Hexis}, Heidegger insists, can have its genesis only ``\textit{in being-there itself}'' (\textit{BCAP} 122). The lectures' formulation, as I understand it, is the prefiguration of what \textit{Being and Time} will name \textit{thrownness} (\textit{Geworfenheit}): the dispositional ground that is neither imposed upon Dasein from without nor chosen by it from within but cultivated, in the Aristotelian register, through the very activity that the disposition itself conditions. The \textit{pathetic} loop, accordingly, is not a structurally distinct direction added to the chain's synchronic direction; it is the \textit{thrownness-character} of the chain: the way each present actualization is always already shaped by prior actualizations that have sedimented as dispositional ground. The chain runs synchronically from $A_0$ to $A_4$ within any single actualization event, but each moment's $A_1$ is itself the\textit{ having-been}-shaped of prior runs. The actualization chain is, in this precise sense, synchronically directional but diachronically saturated. With this temporal structure now specified---the chain's synchronic directionality and its diachronic temporality both made explicit---the analysis can turn to the question of emotions' specific causal function within the chain.

The emotions, moreover, are co-original with \textit{hexeis}. Both are fundamental concepts of being, articulating the being-structure of beings whose way of being is one in which the emotions, ``in their way, more proximally determine being-in-the-world, being-in-the-moment'' (BCAP 129)---they ``characterize the entire human being in its \textit{disposition in the world}'' (\textit{BCAP} 129).\footnote{Heidegger articulate the co-originality of \textit{hexis} and \textit{pathos}: ``\textit{hexis} is nothing other than a how of \textit{pathos}, being-out-of-composure, in relation to being-composed-as-to'' (\textit{BCAP} 125). The two together are fundamental concepts of being in the sense that they articulate the being-structure of human existence as practical-rational life lived in speaking-with-one-another.} Each emotion has its possible \textit{hexis}-correlate: courage as the \textit{hexis} of fear (\textit{NE} III.7, 1115b7--1116a15), good temper as the \textit{hexis} of anger (IV.5, 1125b27--1126b10), and excellence, in its most general sense, as the cultivated \textit{hexis} that fits \textit{kairos}.\footnote{``For instance, both fear and confidence and appetite and anger and pity and in general pleasure and pain may be felt both too much and too little, and in both cases not well; but to feel them at the right times, with reference to the right objects, towards the right people, with the right aim, and in the right way, is what is both intermediate and best, and this is characteristic of excellence'' (\textit{NE} II.6, 1106b19--23).} The diachronic-register analysis of the \textit{pathetic} loop above specifies the temporal mechanism: \textit{hexeis} are what consolidate over time through the repeated actualization of \textit{pathos}-chains, so that the present \textit{pathos} is always shaped by the dispositional ground that prior actualizations have sedimented. The single structure of any given \textit{pathos} is therefore not a snapshot but a moment within a temporally extended cultivation; the emotions are the hinge between the singular event of being-affected and the durable ethical character that consolidates from many such events. That dispositional ground is not, however, uniform. The \textit{hexis} through which an emotion is taken up may run as the transparent routine of a \textit{technē} or as the open, \textit{kairos}-responsive composure of ethical character; and how this bivalence conditions the formation of \textit{doxa}---and, is in turn resedimented by each completed action---belongs to the analysis of \textit{praxis} to the final section where the chain's completion at $A_4$ is shown to feed back into the very dispositional ground from which the next actualization proceeds. 

\subsection{Emotion's Ecstatic Temporal Unity}

Each \textit{pathos}, including each emotion, is structured by a determinate temporality that articulates being-there across all three temporal ecstases at once. The ecstases are not modes one \textit{pathos} possesses to the exclusion of others; each is constituted by the \textit{ecstatic unity of having-been, present, and projected-futural}---a unity grounded in \textit{phantasia} as the capacity that retains what has elapsed, makes-present-something-as-good-or-bad, and projects what is yet to come. The Aristotelian textual ground is threefold. First, Aristotle defines memory, perception, and expectation as the three temporal modes of soul's relation to its objects: ``to remember what is future is not possible---that is an object of opinion or expectation ... nor is there memory of what is present, but only sense-perception ... But memory relates to what is past'' (\textit{De Memoria} 449b10--15); and, ``[a]ll memory, therefore, implies a time elapsed; consequently only those animals which perceive time remember, and the organ whereby they perceive time is also that whereby they remember'' (449b28--30). Memory and expectation are precisely what \textit{phantasia} makes possible: ``imagination is a feeble sort of sensation, and there will always be in the mind of a man who remembers or expects something the imagination of what he remembers or expects'' (\textit{Rhetorica} I.11, 1370a28--30). Because the affective response is itself grounded in \textit{phantasia}, and because one and the same \textit{phantasia} retains what has elapsed (memory), presents what is at hand as already \textit{taken-as-good-or-bad} (\textit{aisthēsis}), and projects what is yet to come (anticipation), every \textit{pathos} is temporalized not by three faculties operating side by side but by the single capacity that holds the three together. Accordingly, \textit{phantasia} grounds not merely each ecstasis but their \textit{ecstatic unity}, the co-temporalizing of past, present, and future through which it is the soul's temporal medium.

Aristotle states in \textit{De Memoria} that we remember when the movement caused by the object is preserved internally (1, 450a20--25). This preserved internal movement, when the original object of preservation was itself an articulationally concrete emotion, is the \textit{resonant emotion}.\footnote{The temporal range Aristotle attributes to emotion is not a distinction between live and non-live emotion but a distinction in what \textit{triggers} a present-felt experience---present perception, future expectation, or memory re-engagement.}. To remember grief is to feel grief again; to anticipate pleasure is to feel pleasure \textit{now} in relation to a not-yet. As Aristotle states it in the \textit{Rhetorica}: 
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
    [s]ome pleasant feeling is associated with most of our appetites; we are enjoying either the memory of a past pleasure or the expectation of a future one, just as persons down with fever, during their attack of thirst, enjoy remembering the drinks they have had and looking forward to having more. So also a lover enjoys talking or writing about his loved one, or doing any little thing connected with him; all these things recall him to memory and make him as it were present to the eye of imagination. (I.11, 1370b14--21)
\end{adjustwidth}
Because \textit{phantasia} generates appearances beyond the immediately present, the temporal depth of emotional life follows directly. Why? Because \textit{phantasia} retains, makes-present, and provides the \textit{phantasmata} from which future-projection is synthesized; \textit{phantasia} provides the \textit{phantasmata} which ground thought and which \textit{doxa}-ratifies, and that ratification is an inextricable, constitutive component that makes emotion what it is. Emotion is not anchored, as Aristotle makes abundantly clear in the passage above, but reaches into past and future by means of the \textit{phantasmatic} appearance. Fear, paradigmatically, is a imagined anticipation of a future evil felt in the present as pain or a disturbance. The feared event in and of itself has yet to come to pass, it does not exist, but the \textit{phantasma} does and the soul moves. \textit{Phantasia} is not, therefore, a decorative accompaniment to emotion; rather, it is the condition of possibility for the soul's being-moved by absences, possibilities, appearances, and counterfactuals---which is to say, the condition of possibility for the entire range of higher-order emotions placed at the center of evaluatively complex action.

Aristotle's per-emotion definitions distribute their primary temporal anchor while preserving traces of all three ecstases---and in fear each ecstasis Aristotle names is answered by a determination of Heidegger's own reading. As Heidegger observes, there are three temporal components to fear as defined by Aristotle: fear is ``a pain or disturbance due to imagining some destructive or painful evil in the future'' (\textit{Rhetorica} II.5, 1382a21). Heidegger categorizes Aristotle's imagined ``destructive or painful evil in the future'' as \gk{μέλλοντος} (\textit{méllontos}, ``what is to come''): as ``a \textit{disposition that is set before an approaching possibility that pertains to me, comes toward me, and as such announces itself, specifically through the announcement}'' (\textit{BCAP} 169). This futural component is not free-standing but is simultaneously grounded in the past (having been) and the present. ``\textit{Being afraid} must be characterized as an \gk{οἴεσθαι} [\textit{oíesthai}]. The one who is brought into fear must once ``believe'' that the definite thing that threatens, threatens \textit{him}, and further, that what is threatening proceeds \textit{from this definite human being,} and that he threatens him \textit{now}'' (\textit{BCAP} 173-4). The bodily ``pain or disturbance'' is felt \textit{now} as---what Heidegger refers to as---a \gk{ταραχή} [\textit{ταραχή}], ``disquiet'' that is itself temporally indexed: ``[t]he \textit{disquiet} is nothing other than the \textit{opposedness of \gk{οἴεσθαι} and \gk{ἐλπίς}}: believing oneself to be lost, and hoping nonetheless'' (\textit{BCAP} 174). The present \textit{disquiet} braids together a \textit{having-been} belief (\gk{οἴεσθαι}) that orients the `now' of fear with a \textit{futural} hope (\gk{μέλλοντος}) that serves as the \textit{orectic} element in the particular instance of fear: the desire to escape, flee, or avoid the imagined, impending ``dustructive or painful evil in the future.'' That dispositional belief (\gk{οἴεσθαι}) is the \textit{having-been}---``what is threatening is appropriated as mattering to one'' (\textit{BCAP} 174), the believing-oneself-vulnerable that being-there has disclosed---without which fear cannot exist at all: ``[i]t follows therefore that fear is felt by those who believe something to be likely to happen to them, at the hands of particular persons, in a particular form, and at a particular time'' (\textit{Rhetorica} II.5, 1382b33-1383a1).\footnote{Heidegger further underscores Aristotle's point in his differentating between feeling threatened and feeling fear: ``Where this distinctive \gk{οἴεσθαι} is wanting, one can perhaps be aware of a threatening thing, but it cannot be feared. This being-for remains absent from those for whom it goes swimmingly, who regard themselves as safe from every threat, those who have riches, physical strength, followers and influence at their disposal. These are never brought into fear; with them there arises a distinctive disdain, arrogance, and impudence'' (\textit{BCAP} 174; Heidegger seems to be referencing \textit{Rhetorica} II.5, 1383a1--7).} The \textit{having-been} belief of vulnerability, \textit{present} disquiet, and \textit{futural} desire to flee are not separate stages or layers, but are experienced simultaneously as the co-temporalization of the three ecstases. 

Anger is ``a desire accompanied by pain, for a conspicuous revenge for a conspicuous slight'' (II.2, 1378a31--32): the slight has already occurred (\textit{having-been}) orienting the pain and pleasure felt now (\textit{present}) for an anticipated revenge (\textit{futural}). Indeed, Aristotle remarks, citing Homer, that the anticipation of revenge is sweeter ``by far than honeycomb dripping with sweetness'' (II.5, 1378b7; citing \textit{Iliad} XVIII 109). Pity is ``pain at an apparent evil, destructive or painful, which befalls one who does not deserve it, and which we might expect to befall ourselves or some friend of ours, and moreover to befall us soon'' (II.8, 1385b13--16): the witnessing is present, the production of suffering reaches back to its past and orients the present, and the \textit{phantasmatic} projection---``might befall ourselves'' or our friends and soon---opens the futural projection through which the witness is implicated in what is witnessed. Lastly, Aristotle's definition of shame makes the co-temporalizing structure of the three ecstases explicit: ``Shame may be defined as pain or disturbance in regard to bad things, whether \textit{present}, \textit{past}, or \textit{future}, which seem likely to involve us in discredit'' (II.6, 1383b15--16). Aristotle's own formulation names all three ecstases explicitly; regardless of the temporally indexing for the cause of the shame (past, present, or future), the pain is felt in the present, conditioned by the past, and projected onto the future. What Heidegger's reading adds to Aristotle's distribution is the \textit{hexis}-correlate of the \textit{having-been}: the dispositional ground into which being-there has already settled is what makes \textit{courage} possible as the cultivated capacity to fear well. ``Fear has, as a determinate \gk{πάθος} [\textit{pathos}], the possibility of a \gk{ἕξις} [\textit{hexis}]. Such a possibility is \textit{courage}.... \textit{Fear is the condition of the possibility of courage}'' (\textit{BCAP} 175). Thus, Heidegger's threefold \gk{οἴεσθαι} (\textit{having-been-dispositioned}), bodily felt \gk{ταραχή} (\textit{present-disquiet}), and \gk{μέλλοντος} (\textit{futural-projection}) are derived from Aristotle's own analytic, recovered as the ecstatic temporal articulation of affect itself.\footnote{Indeed, Heidegger anchors his temporal analysis of fear in Aristotle's own definition and cites him directly: ``Aristotle rightly defines ``fear'' as \gk{λύπη τις ἢ ταραχή}---as ``a kind of depression or bewilderment''.'' (\textit{BT} 392).} 

\textit{Being and Time} makes the framework general: ``[j]ust as understanding is made possible primarily by the future, and moods are made possible by having been, the third constitutive item in the structure of care---namely, \textit{falling}---has its existential meaning in the \textit{Present}'' (\textit{BT} 396-397). Each existential structure has its primary ecstasis: understanding is primarily futural, disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}) primarily grounded in having-been, falling primarily in the present. Yet each ecstasis co-temporalizes with the other two, fear paradigmatically so. ``The specific ecstatical unity which makes it existentially possible to be afraid, temporalizes itself primarily ... as a mode of having been,'' which ``modifies its Present and its future in their own temporalizing'' (392). The phenomenon of fear, accordingly, is constituted not by a single tense but by the ecstatic unity of all three. The point generalizes to every \textit{pathos}: each is a determinate configuration of how the three ecstases co-temporalize in unity, with a primary ecstasis that gives the affect its distinctive phenomenal character, and the other two ecstases co-active as the ground (having-been) and the projection (futural) that sustain the present feeling-itself of the affect. 

The Aristotelian lectures formulate the principle in its most concentrated form---and they state it of \textit{concern} and \textit{concernful speaking} together: [b]eing in itself as concern and concernful speaking is \textit{temporal}, concerns the \textit{not-yet-present}, speaks about what \textit{has-happened-already}, treats the \textit{existing-there-right-now}'' (\textit{BCAP} 131). The emotions stand on both sides of this formulation at once: as modes of disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}) they attune the concern it names, and as the ground out of which speaking arises they are what lends concernful speech its tense. The threefold span the passage predicates of concern and \textit{logos} is  not one the emotions borrow from elsewhere; it is the span they already articulate, and which concern and speech inherit from them. The temporal manifold is, accordingly, no external imposition on the emotions but their own internal articulation raised to explicitness in the speaking it grounds. The primary ecstasis is what gives the affect its name and phenomenal character, while the co-active ecstases supply the conditions of possibility that hold the affect in being.

\subsection{Emotion's Function in the Chain} 

The coordinated notation for the actualization chain has now been settled: nodes $A_0$ through $A_4$ for the major actualization stages, with $A_3$ as the completed cognitive actuality at which \textit{doxa} ratifies the evaluative content; and transitions $M_n \rightarrow A_{n+1}$ for the motions between nodes, with $M_3 \rightarrow A_4$ designating the motion that emotion \textit{is}. Aristotle is quite clear on this: ``being pained or pleased, being bold or fearful, being angry, perceiving, thinking. All these are regarded as modes of movement'' not of the soul but movements originating or terminating in the soul (\textit{DA} I.4, 408b1--18). Therefore, if emotions are a movement it must participate in the three-factor schema Aristotle lays out: unmoved mover, moved mover, and that which is moved. The unmoved mover of animal action is always the object of desire and real or apparent good. The world being disclosed as mattering in some way as an object of desire: an object to pursue or to avoid, for it is only the object of desire ``that itself remaining unmoved originates the movement by being apprehended in thought or imagination'' (III.10, 433b11-13). For emotion, the apparent good is the \textit{toward-which} that is \textit{doxastically}-ratified. If we consider anger, the apparent good is the anticipated  revenge articulated resulting from \textit{doxa}'s ratification that an undeserved slight has occurred. The slight \textit{discloses} revenge-as-good-and-pursuable and it is \textit{doxa}'s ratification that articulates the situation such that this particular good is revealed. The moved mover remains the \textit{orektikon}: ``that which at once moves and is moved is the faculty of appetite [\textit{orexis}] (for that which is moved is moved insofar as it desires [\textit{orexis}], and appetite [\textit{orexis}] in the sense of actual appetite [\textit{orexis}] is a kind of movement)'' (III.10, 433b17-19). \textit{Orexis} remains the moved mover, and the cognitive \textit{doxastic} evaluation only \textit{determines which form the desire takes}. Emotion is the actualization of the \textit{orektikon} into a specific form of desire. Emotion, then, \textit{is} the moved mover in its \textit{doxa}-articulated actualization: a form of \textit{orexis} specific to the cognitive evaluation of the object. Lastly, that which is moved is the body and the somatic preparation of the ``organic parts'' is the \textit{joint}. Through enmattered affection, emotion prepares the organic parts by heating or chilling. This affection is the boundary where the moved mover terminates in the moved; it is not a separate step but \textit{how the moved mover moves the moved at all}. 


Emotion, accordingly, is not a node in the chain but the \textit{form} that desire takes within it. Depending upon \textit{doxa}-ratification at $A_3$ for its cognitive-evaluative component, emotion is the determinate form \textit{orexis} takes in the $M_3 \rightarrow A_4$ motion---the form of desire that at once prepares the somatic parts and orients the animal toward a specific action. Fear is the exemplary case: the \textit{doxa} that affirms an anticipated evil \textit{as} destructive-for-me is the condition under which \textit{orexis} takes its determinate form as the desire-to-flee---a conative element individuated by just that evaluative content and structural to the fear, with the chilling of the blood that readies the body and the flight toward which that readied body is already turned. What the chain requires at $M_3 \rightarrow A_4$, under evaluatively complex conditions, is the concretization of \textit{orexis} into a determinate form of desire directed at a specific object under a specific evaluative description. Emotion is precisely this concretization. Without it, the chain would have a generic moved mover---\textit{orexis} set in motion by the apparent good---but no mechanism to specify \textit{which} form of desire corresponds to \textit{which} disclosure of the apparent good. The desire to flee is not the desire to retaliate; the desire to relieve is not the desire to conceal. Each determinate conative orientation is individuated by the cognitive evaluative structure of the emotion that specifies it, and each draws upon a determinate pattern of somatic preparation---the cooling of fear, the heating of anger, the sympathetic pain of pity---that configures the body for the action the emotion calls for.

Emotion does not precede desire as a separate antecedent stage; emotion \textit{is} the form desire takes when \textit{doxa} has disclosed evaluatively complex content. Fear is not something that happens before the desire to flee; fear \textit{is} the desire to flee insofar as it is structured by the cognitive evaluation of an anticipated destructive evil, experienced in the somatic alteration of chilled blood, and accompanied by the presently felt disturbance at the future evil and the pleasure of prospective escape. Anger is not something that happens before the desire to retaliate; anger \textit{is} the desire to retaliate insofar as it is structured by the perception of an unjustified slight, experienced in the somatic alteration of heated blood, and accompanied by the presently felt pain of the slight with the pleasure at the thought of anticipated revenge. The \textit{Rhetorica}'s definitions confirm this composite structure: each emotion is a desire of a certain kind, attended by pain or pleasure, directed at a certain kind of object, under a certain evaluative description. The composite is not several parallel components stacked together but a single articulated determination in which all are mutually constitutive aspects of one and the same \textit{emotion-as-being-affected}. 

The three-factor schema of \textit{De Anima} III.10 (433a9-b30) is preserved without revision. What the schema's original formulation leaves unspecified is the \textit{internal} complexity the moved mover acquires when the apparent good is articulated under the evaluative categories of the \textit{Rhetorica}---when the slighter, the threat, the undeserved sufferer, the public falling-short have entered the soul through \textit{doxa} as evaluatively determinate disclosures of the apparent good. Emotion names this internal complexity. It is the actualization of the \textit{orektikon} into a specific form of desire directed at a specific object under a specific evaluative description; it is the form in which the movement that originates in the soul becomes the body's being-moved; it is the mechanism through which the world's disclosure as mattering in a certain way becomes the animal's motion in the corresponding direction. 

The causal function emotion supplies is therefore not universal across the chain but specific to the evaluatively complex case. Where the apparent good is straightforwardly pleasant or painful and the desire is basic \textit{epithymia} or aversion---the drinking case of \textit{De Motu Animalium} (701a32-33)---basic hedonic tonality alone suffices to direct the organism toward or away from the object. At $M_3 \rightarrow A_4$ in the \textit{epithymetic} case, the chain runs without internal complexity: \textit{orexis} is generic, and the body's being-moved is the immediate consequence of the perception's hedonic tonality. The concretization of emotion is reserved for the cases in which \textit{doxa} has done its articulational work. 

\subsection{Conclusion}

Having worked through both Aristotle's analyses and Heidegger's reading of them, the section's central claim can now be stated in its most concentrated form. Each \textit{pathos}---taking \textit{pathos} as the all-encompassing term for being-affected in the broadest sense described in the \textit{Metaphysica}---is a determinate concretion of one ontological structure: the structure articulated by the four senses of the \textit{Metaphysica}, the three constitutive aspects of the \textit{Rhetorica}, the enmattered account of the \textit{De Anima}, and the ecstatic temporality that \textit{phantasia} holds together. That structure covers the full register that runs from \textit{epithymia} (hedonic tonality in which the object of \textit{orexis} is actively present to the perceiving animal, as water is to the thirsty), through \textit{resonant epithymia} (hedonic tonality in which the object is not present and \textit{phantasia} is operating, the \textit{phantasma} substituting for the perceived object),\footnote{It bears reiterating that the \textit{orectic} side of hedonic tonality---\textit{epithymia} when the object is present, \textit{resonant epithymia} when the object is absent and \textit{phantasia} operates on the \textit{resonant} \textit{kinēsis} to present the \textit{phantasma}---is co-given throughout with the perceptual side: the \textit{aisthēma} (the perceptual disclosure of the object's form) and \textit{resonant aisthēma} (its persisting \textit{eidetic} tone carried by the \textit{phantasma}) are one substrate but different in being with the \textit{orectic} side (\textit{De Anima} III.7, 431a13--14). The two sides cannot be separated; wherever the perceptual disclosure occurs, the \textit{orectic} aspect is co-given, and vice versa. Each \textit{pathos} actualization is, therefore, always already a co-given disclosure of perceptual and \textit{orectic} content, whether at the minimal articulation of pure \textit{epithymetic} \textit{aisthēsis} or the maximal articulation of the higher-order emotions.} to the most articulationally concrete emotions of the \textit{Rhetorica}. The differences across this register are not differences of ontological structure but of \textit{articulational concretion}: the structure is filled maximally in the emotions and minimally---but never absently---in \textit{aisthēsis}. The section's central thesis is, therefore, not a phenomenology proprietary to the \textit{Rhetorica}'s catalog but an ontology of being-affected as such, of which the catalog is the case disclosed at the most maximal level of articulation.\footnote{Gross's \textit{Uncomfortable Situations} develops a parallel social-ontological reading of emotion: ``the tradition after Aristotle's \textit{Rhetorica} offers alternatives for understanding emotions as social phenomena, which is something that leading humanists like Martha Nussbaum and Richard Sorabji should have remembered as they rushed toward the latest brain science'' (3). And: ``[r]hetoric, as a humanistic form of affordance theory, posits a situation that is not neutral but is rather `persuasive,' threatening and promising with respect to our human being-in-the-world'' (20). The structure developed here---integrating the ontology of alteration, the constitutive aspects of \textit{pathos}, the form-material unity, and the ecstatic temporality---gives the structural-ontological architecture that Gross's transactional-rhetorical reading articulates at the contemporary register; both reject the inner-state model of emotion in favor of a being-in-the-world account.}

This one structure integrates its four articulations without reducing to any. The \textit{Metaphysica}'s fourfold supplies the underlying ontology of alteration---the bedrock in virtue of which \textit{pathos} is the kind of being-affected it is, and from which the \textit{Rhetorica}'s generic ``change'' inherits its content; the \textit{Rhetorica}'s threefold gives the constitutive aspects of any \textit{pathos}---the change-in-disposition, the \textit{krisis} or position-taking that \textit{doxa} ratifies, and the hedonic tonality co-given with both; and, lastly, the ecstatic temporality that \textit{phantasia} holds together gives the threefold span of \textit{having-been}, \textit{present}, and \textit{projected-futural} across which every \textit{pathos} articulates being-there. The decisive addition is the enmattered account of the \textit{De Anima} that secures the structure's reach across the whole register: because every \textit{pathos} is bodily-in-the-world, the dryness of thirst is as constitutive of its \textit{pathos} as the boiling of blood is of anger, and no affection---from the barest hedonic tonality to the most articulate emotion---occurs except as an affection of the body. The inseparability of \textit{eidos} from \textit{hylē} is the condition on which the whole register is one structure rather than two adjacent kinds. 

The structure of emotion has its philosophical center of gravity, accordingly, in the claim that the emotions are the ground of \textit{logos}:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
The \gk{πάθη} (\textit{pathē}) are not merely an annex of psychical processes, but are rather \textit{the ground out of which speaking arises, and which what is expressed grows back into}; the \gk{πάθη}, for their part, are \textit{the basic possibilities in which being-there itself is primarily oriented toward itself}, finds itself. The primary being-oriented, the illumination of its being-in-the-world is not a \textit{knowing}, but rather a \textit{finding-oneself} that can be determined differently, according to the mode of being-there of a being. (\textit{BCAP} 176)
\end{adjustwidth}
Speaking, on this reading, is not a master operation that draws on the emotions as resources; speaking arises out of the emotions and returns to them, the feedback structure of which the \textit{pathetic} loop analysis above has specified at the \textit{pathos} level itself. The rhetorical art, accordingly, is the cultivation of the dispositional ground that brings being-there to discourse, and Aristotle's \textit{Rhetorica} is, therefore, not a manual of persuasion-techniques but a phenomenology of the disclosive structures through which being-there finds itself as being-with-others.\footnote{Kisiel's reading of supplies the matched political and philosophical articulation: ``\textit{care} is deeply ensconced in the life of pathos, mood. The cultivation of the appropriate mood of the auditor by the skilled speaker, so that the attentive listener will then speak with the speaker by speaking after him or her (\textit{Nachreden}) in contagious attunement, suggests that speech finds its deepest roots in mood ([GA] 177). Discourse is not only rational, but impassioned, or better, passional in its rationality \ldots\ As Heidegger puts it, `\textit{ethos} and \textit{pathos} are constitutive of \textit{legein} itself' ([GA] 165)'' (Kisiel 145).} What Heidegger's recovery discloses is that the emotions are not psychological phenomena to be added to a prior account of cognition; they are the prior. They are the modes in which being-there has its world disclosed to it, and on the basis of which both cognition and speaking become possible. This is why position-taking runs the whole register while speech does not: every \textit{pathos} carries a \textit{krisis}---even the thirsty animal discriminates \textit{this-as-drink}---but only the higher-order emotions carry that position-taking into \textit{logos}, into the speech-with-another before whom one is angered, ashamed, or moved to pity. To analyze anger, fear, shame, pity, indignation, kindness---the family of higher-order emotions Aristotle catalogs---is to undertake the phenomenology of disclosedness itself: the inquiry into how a finite, embodied, speaking being finds itself in the world before any explicit knowing or saying, and on the basis of which knowing and saying become possible at all. Heidegger's retrospective citation of the \textit{Rhetorica} as ``the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being-with-one-another'' (\textit{BT} 178) is, in this light, not a passing historical note but a precise diagnostic: the \textit{Rhetorica}'s catalog of emotions is the first sustained phenomenology of attunement (\textit{Stimmung}), and the Aristotelian lectures are the preparatory work in which that phenomenology is recovered for the disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}) of \textit{Being and Time}.

The disclosive role that the emotions discharge in the actualization of desire (perception-to-action) chain depends on a feature named at several points above but that warrants explicit final statement: emotion responds to appearance, not to corrected judgment. If \textit{phantasia} supplies the appearance, \textit{doxa} is the moment of the soul's yes-saying to propositional content, the unfolding from \textit{taking-as} to \textit{taking-as-true}; the emotion that follows is a being-moved keyed to the appearance, not to whatever rational correction the agent may apply later on. This is precisely what makes the chain run with the speed and inevitability that Aristotle ascribes to it (\textit{De Anima} III.3, 427b21--24). For example, the sound mistaken for a rattlesnake produces real fear, even though the corrected judgment will dissolve the mistake and diffuse if not eliminate the fear elicited; false beliefs generate real emotions because emotion is keyed to the \textit{phantasmatic} appearance, not to its eventual correction. Art produces emotion, because the \textit{phantasmatic} representation suffices to occasion the affective movement, even when the audience knows the represented event is unreal; and, emotion can contradict one's standing judgment, as when the knowledge that the coiled rope in the corner is not a snake does not stop the muted fear from rising because it still \textit{appears} snake-like.

The practical, world-shaping power of emotion that Aristotle's rhetorical theory makes explicit follows from this analysis. To persuade is not primarily to offer proofs but to reshape the listener's \textit{phantasmatic} field. The rhetor must supply images---concrete, narrative, affectively charged---that allow the audience to see what the rhetor wants them to see, since this imagistic seeing grounds the subsequent formation of \textit{doxa}, the ensuing movement of emotion, and the eventual act of judgment. If rhetorical persuasion succeeds, accordingly, it is because it sets off the entire cascade of movement: new \textit{phantasma}, new \textit{doxa}, new emotion, new orientation, new action. For Aristotle, in sum, emotion is the kinetic, affective, evaluative dimension of how the world appears to a finite, embodied, speaking being. It is grounded in \textit{phantasia}, shaped through involuntary \textit{doxa}, felt as pleasure or pain, prepared somatically, and expressed as motion toward or away from what the soul takes to be significant. Emotion is not an add-on to rationality, nor a disruption of cognition, nor a mere physiological disturbance, but the way the soul's self-movement discloses significance at all. The single ontological structure---formal-material and ecstatic-temporal, articulated through the fourfold of the \textit{Metaphysica}, the threefold of the \textit{Rhetorica}, and the enmattered account of the \textit{De Anima}---specifies the architecture of this disclosure for each of the determinate emotions the \textit{Rhetorica} catalogs. Emotion, in its full Aristotelian sense, is nothing less than the affective architecture of \textit{bodily-being-in-the-world-with-others}.
