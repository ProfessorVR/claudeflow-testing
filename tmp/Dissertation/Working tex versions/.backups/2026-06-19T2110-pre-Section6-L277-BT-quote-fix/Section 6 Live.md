the forms: nutritive, sensitive, appetitive, locomotive, thinking

``Now matter is potentiality, form actuality' and actuality is of two kinds, one as e.g. knowledge, the other as e.g. reflecting'' (DA II.1, 412a9-11)

``Hence the soul must be a substance in the sense of the form of a natural body having life potentially within it. But substance is actuality, and thus soul is the actuality of a body as above characterized. now there are two kinds of actuality corresponding to knowledge and to reflecting. It is obvious that the soul is an actuality like knowledge; for both sleepign and waking presuppose the existence of soul, and of these waking corresponds to reflecting, sleeping to knowledge possessed but not employed, and knowledge of something is temporally prior.'' (\textit{DA} II.1, 412a20--26). 

``But if we we are to express what each is, viz. what the thinking power is, or the perceptive, or the nutritive, we must go farther back and first give an account of thinking or perceiving; for activities and actions are prior in definition to potentialities. If so, and if, still prior to them, we should have reflected on their correlative objects, then for the same reason we must first determine about them, i.e. about food and the objects of perception and thought.” (\textit{DA} II.4, 415a16--22). 

each form of the soul is to be understood through their activities and the objects of those activities: food as object of nutritive, objects of perception, desire, and thought. 

each form of the soul is a type of actuality insofar as it is a form of the soul, while each is also a power or potentiality insofar as it is a faculty ordered to an activity : matter is potentiality, form actuality, and the soul is the actuality of a natural organized body having life potentially within it. So if nutritive, perceptive, appetitve, locomotive, and thinking forms are genuine forms or parts of suol, then they belong on the side of actuality rather than matter. but aristotle regularly describes them as powers or faculties. soul is a first actuality: a standing realized capacity, not yet the ongoing operation itself. sight as the soul of the eye is an actuality of the eye, while actually seeing is a further exercise of that actuality. each form of soul is a kind of actuality, but specifically a first actuality that grounds a corresponding second actuality or activity such as nourishing, perceiving, desiring, moving, or thinking. that is why aristotle can speak of them both as forms and as powers without contradiction. 

faculty, form, and power mostly render three core greek words: Faculty/\gk{δύναμις}/\textit{dýnamis}: a capacity or power a soul has to carry out certain activities (e.g., the dynamis of perceiving, thinking, moving). Form/\gk{εἶδος}/\textit{eîdos}: form, look, or kind. soul as the form or essence of a living body, in perception as receiving form without matter. ALso, \gk{μορφή}/\textit{morphḗ}: form or shape; more typical word for the structured configuration a thing has. soul is the morphe of a antural body. Power/\gk{δύναμις}/\textit{dýnamis}: the key term for power, capacity, or potentiality: powers of nutrition, perception etc that together make up the soul's faculties. 

For Aristotle, each form of the soul is a \textit{dýnamis} that only comes into its own through motions and affections—growth, sensation, desire, learning—so that the soul is intrinsically bound up with \textit{páthos}, with being-affected. Yet the soul itself is not a motion; it is the first actuality that grounds and guides these kinéseis, and its highest exercises (such as settled knowing) are energeíai—completed modes of being-in-the-world that arise from prior being-affected but are no longer themselves “actualities of the potential \textit{qua} potential.”

\inlinenote{For Aristotle, the faculties of soul are not isolated powers but differentiated participations in one ontological structure of potency, affection, and actuality: each faculty names a determinate way a living being can receive form, undergo alteration, and, in that very undergoing, become a source of further movement or realization. This is possible because, in Metaphysics V, potency is defined both as a source of change in another thing or in the same thing qua other and as the principle by which something is capable of being moved or suffering, so the active and receptive poles belong to one shared field rather than to two unrelated kinds of being. Thus the threptikon is affected by nourishment and turns what it receives into growth and maintenance; the aisthētikon is affected by sensible objects; the orektikon is affected by what appears pleasant or painful and becomes pursuit or avoidance; the phantastikon preserves and reactivates movements arising from sensation; and the kinētikon kata topon carries these perceptual and appetitive actualizations into bodily movement in place. The noētikon shows this structure most sharply, because Aristotle distinguishes a receptive intellect that receives intelligible form from an active intellect that “makes all things” and is separable and impassible, so even here the noetic domain contains both the receptive and the productive moments of one process of actualization. The gennētikon belongs to the same whole in another register, since Aristotle defines cause as that from which change first begins and names the father and the semen as causes of the child, which means that generative power is the productive pole of the same structure whose correlates are receptive matter and coming-to-be. Accordingly, all the faculties partake in the overall structure of affecting and affectability, not because each is passive in the same sense, but because each marks a distinct way life receives, transmits, and actualizes form.}

emotion as motion: As both a motion (in the energeia ateles sense) and a pathos it must consists with the fivefold ontology of any pathos \textit{and} follow the three-factor schema of motion.
\begin{enumerate}
    
    \item unmoved mover: Always object of desire / apparent good. The world being disclosed as mattering in some way as an object of desire: as an object to pursue or to avoid, for it is only the object of desire ``that itself remaining unmoved originates the movement by being apprehended in thought or imagination'' (\textit{DA} III.10, 433b11--13). Thus, the unmoved mover of emotion has a dual structure: the occasion/object (the \textit{toward-which}): the \textit{doxa}-ratified evaluative situation---the conspicuous slight, the impending evil, the undeserved suffering; and, secondly, the good it discloses (the final cause proper): the apparent good the emotion reaches toward---revenge, safety, relief. The slight \textit{discloses} revenge-as-pursuable; it is the \textit{doxa}-ratification that makes the situation present \textit{this} good. So the unmoved mover = the apparent good as disclosed through the \textit{doxa}-ratified evaluative object. It moves the desire as an \textit{intentional/final} cause: apprehended, not pushed---which is exactly why it stays ``unmoved.''

    \item Moved Mover: the \textit{orektikon}. ``that which at once moves and is moved is the faculty of appetite [desire] (for that which is moved is moved insofar as it desires, and appetite in the sense of actual appetite \textit{is} a kind of movement)'' (433b17--19). desire-in-actuality is a kind of motion or an actuality. \gk{ ἡ ὄρεξις κίνησίς τις ἢ ἐνέργεια} (433b16-18). The cognition (\textit{doxa}) is not a fourht factor---it belongs to the constitution of the \textit{unmoved mover} (it is what makes the object an evaluatively-determinate apparent good). The moved mover stays \textit{orexis}; the cognition only \textit{determines which form the orexis takes}. Emotion is the actualization of the \textit{orektikon} into a specific form of desire. So emotion \textit{is} the moved mover, in its \textit{doxa}-determined actuality: \textit{orexis} specified by its evaluative object. 

    \item That which is moved: the body and the somatic preparation is the \textit{joint}. The moved mover grips the moved through the \textit{enmattered} affection. In the MA mechanism, the \textit{orexis}-actualization moves the limbs by heating or chilling the region around the heart. So the ``boiling of blood'' is doing double duty: under the \textit{formal} account it is the enmattered form of the anger (moved-mover side); under the \textit{material} account it is the somatic preparation of the organic parts (moved side). That is the \textit{enmattered-account} one object two accounts (\textit{logoi}): the affection is the boundary where the moved mover terminates in the moved. So when we say the moved mover has to prepare the somatic parts, we have located not a separate step, but \textit{how the moved mover moves the moved at all}. 
\end{enumerate}

Emotion as \textit{energeia ateles}

\begin{itemize}

    \item somatic preparation is the motion's bodily \textit{unfolding}, not its terminus. The boiling blood is the anger \textit{happening} not what the anger is \textit{for}.

    \item the \textit{telos} lies beyond, in the action itself: for anger, the \textit{conspicuous revenge}---and, beyond the act, the good it achieves (the slight avenged, worth restored). The anger is ``for the sake of'' the revenge; it is the \textit{ateles} until then and discharges when the revenge is taken. That's the MA chain reaching A4 (completed action). We know the motion by the actualization it terminates in: the desire-to-flee is not equal to the desire-to-retaliate \textit{because their termini differ}. The end individuates the emotion. Fear is known by \textit{flight-to-safety}, anger by \textit{revenge}, pity by \textit{relief}. So, the end of anger = the hostile action/revenge, with somatic preparation as the \textit{en-route} bodily phase. 

    \item The original Greek: \gk{κινεῖται γὰρ τὸ κινούμενον ᾗ ὀρέγεται, καὶ ἡ ὄρεξις κίνησίς τίς ἐστιν, ἢ ἐνεργείᾳ} (\textit{kineîtai gàr tò kinoúmenon hȇi orégetai, kaì hē órexis kínēsís tís estin, ḕ energeíāi}). This is Aristotle's own hedge, sitting inside the definition of the moved mover. He defines \textit{orexis} as \gk{κίνησίς τίς} (\textit{kínēsís tís}) (``a kind of motion'') and then immediately qualifies it: \gk{ἢ ἐνεργείᾳ} (``in actuality''). \hl{As an incomplete actuality, emotion is an interesting case as it seems both complete and simultaneously incomplete: (1) qua motivation orientation (toward action) it is a \textit{kinesis} / \textit{energeia ateles}: incomplete, end-beyond-itself, the anger-toward-revenge. (2) qua felt disclosure (the present painful disturbance, the \textit{now} of being-angry) it looks like \textit{energeia teleia}, complete at every moment: you do not ``incompletely feel'' anger, you \textit{fully} feel it now, ``at once feeling and having felt''. Thus, it is lived as both, complete as feeling and having felt and incomplete in the end-toward-which the emotion reaches: the revenge of anger, flight of fear, etc.}.   
    
\end{itemize}

\begin{enumerate}
    
    \item Disposition (finding-oneself-disposed). \textit{Energeia}: to be angry is to be in a determinate attunement, whole at this instant — there is no half-disposed; you are disposed-as-slighted, "at once disposing and having-disposed." A complete present state. \textit{Kinēsis}: yet the disposition is a change of disposition (Heidegger's \textit{metaballontes} — "a change sets in; we change from one disposition to another") — a re-attuning, a being-moved-out-of-composure. The "e-" of e-motion. \textit{Coincidence}: the mood is complete-now precisely as a having-been-moved-into-it.

    \item The toward-which / toward-whom (intentionality). \textit{Energeia}: the grasp is complete — anger fully has the slight (toward-which) and is fully turned on the slighter (toward-whom), like the actuality of perceiving, "at once seeing and having seen." \textit{Kinēsis}: but that grasp is a reaching-toward — \textit{orexis} drawn to the not-yet-accomplished revenge, the toward-whom as the target of an unfinished redress. \textit{Coincidence}: to grasp the slight is to be drawn toward its answer; the intentional actuality is the motivational motion.

    \item The bodily form (enmattered alteration). \textit{Kinēsis}: the boiling of blood is the manifestly kinetic moment — an \textit{alloiōsis}, a heating that prepares the organic parts for hostile action; a process en route to readiness. \textit{Energeia}: yet that boiling is the anger fully bodied-forth now (the enmattered \textit{eidos}, complete) — and, by DA II.5, a preservative \textit{paschein} (\gk{σωτηρία}), an actualization of the orectic potential, not a \textit{kinēsis}-toward-destruction. \textit{Coincidence}: one bodily event under two modes — your "one object, two accounts" doubled: the preparatory alteration is the complete embodied actuality. (This is where your \gk{σωτηρία} claim lives: the \textit{energeia} pole.)

    \item The temporal range (the threefold span). This is the load-bearing one. \textit{Kinēsis}: the emotion projects toward a futural not-yet — fear toward the impending evil, anger toward the revenge-to-come. The end lies ahead; the motion strains toward its terminus. \textit{Energeia}: but it co-temporalizes all three ecstases at once, whole in the present — thrown having-been, felt present, projected futural, held together now. The ecstatic unity is a complete present actuality; you don't "incompletely span" the three. \textit{Coincidence} — and the payoff: whether an emotion tips kinetic or energeic is fixed here, by whether its futural ecstasis has an attainable terminus. Anger's future (revenge) is realizable → the kinetic vector is live → anger is \textit{kinēsis}-dominant. Grief's "future" has no attainable terminus (the loss is irrevocable, the good non-realizable) → the kinetic vector is dead → grief is \textit{energeia}-dominant: it dwells, complete, in the having-been, with no end-beyond. That is your "complete at A₃, issues in no deed" — now derived, not stipulated. Grief is the \textit{energeia teleia} case; anger the \textit{kinēsis} case; most \textit{pathē} are mixed.

    \item The disclosive function (\textit{to krisis}, \textit{to logos}). \textit{Kinēsis}: a bringing-to — the emotion moves the agent toward position-taking, toward speech, toward the deed (the slighted to confront, the threatened to confer); productive, terminating in \textit{praxis}. \textit{Energeia}: but the disclosure itself is complete now — the world is fully lit-up as mattering-for-me, "at once disclosing and having disclosed," like \textit{nous}, before any deed is done. \textit{Coincidence}: the complete present disclosure is the impetus to act — the world's mattering-now is the motion toward \textit{logos}.

    \item The whole, then: emotion is \textit{energeia} in its disclosive-felt vector (it fully discloses significance now — complete) and \textit{kinēsis} in its productive-projective vector (it moves toward the action that answers that significance — end beyond). The five-fold is the joint: the same disclosure that is complete-now is the motion-toward-deed. And that is just \gk{κίνησις ἐνεργείᾳ} spelled out across five moments — an actual motion, a \textit{kinēsis} whose every phase is a complete present \textit{energeia}. So you can keep 433b18 after all — not as a hedge between two categories, but as the one-word compression of your entire five-fold: emotion, the actual motion of the soul disclosing significance toward action.
    
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{$A_4$ in the Chain: Completed Action and the Three Structures of Its Analysis}

\inlinenote{Aristotle's \textit{hexis}: ``The frequently is, precisely, that which characterizes the temporality of being-there. Aristotle cannot say \gk{ἀεί} (\textit{aei}) insofar as human being-there does not so comport itself constantly and always. It can constantly be otherwise. The \textit{always} of a being like being-there is the \textit{frequently of repetition}. It is the being-there of human beings, as determined by \textit{historicality}, to see entirely different time-contexts in relation to which the remaining time-determinations break down'' (129). provides a temporal framework: memory is a mode of \textit{Gewesenheit}, a living dimension of \textit{Dasein}'s being that continues to determine present understanding and future projection. The having-been is not a dead past but a living orientation that conditions the soul's current cognitive engagement.}

Completed action stands as the terminal actuality of the synchronic chain---the moment at which a perceiving, desiring, and deliberating soul has converted its grasp of the realizable good into enacted bodily motion. Completed action is not, however, of a single kind. Aristotle distinguishes two ways in which an actualization comes to its end, and the chain may terminate in either, or in both at once. The first is \textit{poiēsis}---making, production---the action whose \textit{telos} lies beyond the doing, in a product (\textit{ergon}) that outlasts it; it is the actualization that \textit{technē} governs, the craft-knowledge perfected in bringing such results reliably about (\textit{NE}, VI.4, 1140a1--10). The second is \textit{praxis}---action, doing in the strict sense---the action whose \textit{telos} is immanent in the doing itself, so that in acting well one has, in the same stroke, acted well; it is governed not by a craft but by an ethical \textit{hexis}, the cultivated \textit{how} of one's comportment toward the world---a manner of being, not a technique for producing (\textit{NE}, VI.5, 1140b6--7). The contrast is, at bottom, one between two kinds of actualization: \textit{poiēsis} is a \textit{kinēsis}, complete only when its product stands forth, whereas \textit{praxis} is an \textit{energeia}, whole at every moment of its exercise (\textit{Metaphysics} IX.6, 1048b18--34). A single completed action may answer to both descriptions at once---a making, in respect of the product it leaves behind, and a doing, in respect of the comportment it enacts---and the bivalence of \textit{hexis} this implies, dividing the disposition that contracts deliberation toward routine from the one that holds the agent open to the \textit{kairos},\footnote{\textit{Kairos} (\gk{καιρός}) is the opportune or decisive moment---the qualitatively right time for action, as against \textit{chronos}, time as measured duration. A \textit{kairos}-responsive disposition is cultivated not to apply a fixed rule but to meet the singular demand of the situation as it arises---the readiness Heidegger glosses as ``holding-oneself-open'' rather than routine (\textit{BCAP}, p.~128).} is what the analysis below draws out in full.

Beneath both kinds of action lies a more rudimentary register the chain must also accommodate: the bare animal action shared by everything that perceives. Here a creature perceives something and responds at once---startling from a shadow, recoiling from what springs toward it---with no deliberation, no taking-as-true, no cultivated disposition brought to bear. In the very act of perceiving, \textit{aisthēsis} discriminates what it meets as to-be-pursued or to-be-shunned (\textit{DA} III.7, 431a8--12); sensitive \textit{phantasia} attaches the answering tone, attractive or aversive; and the simplest \textit{orexis}, \textit{epithymia}, reaches or recoils accordingly. Such movement is formally a doing and not a making, and so stands on the side of \textit{praxis}; but it is not yet the \textit{praxis} of an ethical \textit{hexis}, for it engages neither \textit{doxa} nor any cultivated \textit{how} of comportment. It is the floor of action---the limit at which perception and bare desire alone suffice to move the animal---the case every richer action presupposes.

Whichever way it ends---in a work made or a deed done---$A_4$ is the gathering-point at which the whole chain converges, and three structures, nested one within another, organize that convergence and supply the means of analyzing it. The first is the three-factor schema of motion: every animal movement involves an unmoved originator, a moved mover, and a moved (\textit{DA} III.10, 433b13--18). The second is the doctrine of the four causes, the explanatory whys by which to know a thing is to know its cause (\textit{Phys}. II.3). The third is the \textit{Rhetorica}'s sorting of all human action into seven causes, divided by whether the action springs from the agent or not (\textit{Rhet}. I.10, 1368b33--1369a6). These are not three rival accounts but three lenses of widening grain: the three factors give the bare anatomy of self-movement; the four causes set that anatomy within the general form of explanation; the seven causes individuate, for human action, which source moves the agent and whether the motion is the agent's own. Together they furnish at once the ontology of completed action---what it is for the soul to move itself to an end---and the method by which any discrete action may be diagnosed. The rest of this section sets out the three in turn and then sets them to work on a single case.

\subsubsection{The Three Structures of Action: Ontology and Method}

Aristotle's analysis of animal movement turns on three factors: that which originates the movement without being moved, the realizable good; that which both moves and is moved, the faculty of desire; and that which is moved, the body (\textit{DA} III.10, 433b13--18). Desire is thus the source of all animal action whatever, the moved mover through which every motion of the living body passes; at its most basic it is \textit{epithymia}, the bare pursuit of the good and avoidance of the bad that the discriminating dimension of \textit{aisthēsis} institutes in the very act of perceiving (\textit{DA} III.7, 431a8--12), and which every perceiving animal exercises.\footnote{Throughout, \textit{epithymia} is used in this broad register---the elementary desiderative pursuit-and-avoidance shared by all animals with sense---which includes but exceeds the narrower bodily appetite the \textit{Rhetorica} names among the seven causes. In the broad sense it is the floor of animal desire as such; in the narrow sense, one source of human action among several.} What varies is not the schema but the point of entry---the mode in which the realizable good is made present to the animal so as to set desire in motion. Aristotle names three: ``I want to drink, says appetite; this is drink, says sense or imagination or thought: straightaway I drink'' (\textit{MA} 7, 701a32--33). Sense, imagination, and thought are not three chains but three doors onto one chain. And because each is a making-present of the object to the soul, \textit{phantasia} is essential to all three: it is the kinetic medium in which the object is held before the soul whether perceived, remembered, or anticipated, so that even thought ``never thinks without an image'' (\textit{DA} III.7, 431a16--17). The non-rational animal enters only through sense and \textit{phantasia}; the human may enter also through \textit{logos}-dependent thought---and it is this difference of entry, not any difference in the schema, that divides bare animal action from action proper to a rational being.

That all four causes meet at $A_4$---and that the three factors are themselves a partial reading of them---is no peculiarity of action but an instance of Aristotle's general doctrine of explanation, worth making explicit, for it yields the diagnostic instrument by which any completed action may be analyzed. To know a thing, Aristotle holds, is to know its cause, and \textit{cause} is said in four ways (\textit{Metaphysica} I.3, 983a24--32); a full account therefore answers four distinct questions---the four explanatory ``whys'' of the \textit{Physica}: out of what is the thing made (the material), in virtue of what is it this rather than that (the formal), what first set it going or altered it (the efficient), and that for the sake of which it comes to be (the final) (II.3, 194b24--195a3). These four converge in the single completed action---to diagnose it is to read them off that convergence. Yet none is exhausted there: each opens a series the act does not contain---the efficient running back through the movers that moved the mover, into the agent's sedimented history and the dispositional ground that disposes one to be moved as one is; the final running up through subordinate ends toward the good that orders the agent's conduct as a whole. To ask not merely what the four causes are but why \textit{this} agent was moved \textit{so} is to follow the efficient series back into disposition---the depth the diachronic loop, traced below, takes up.

The first two structures locate the source of an action without yet identifying it. The three-factor schema tells us \textit{that} the moved mover is desire---but desire (\textit{orexis}) is a genus, and it does not say which of its species moves in a given case; the four causes tell us \textit{that} the efficient cause is the \textit{archē}, the origin from which the motion springs---but ``\textit{archē}'' names a position to be filled, not the particular source that fills it. \textit{Which} desire, then, and \textit{what} origin? For human action the \textit{Rhetorica} answers, cataloguing the springs of conduct for the forensic orator, who must know what moves those to be prosecuted or defended. Aristotle sorts every action first by whether its origin lies in the agent at all:

\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
Now every action of every person either is or is not due to that person himself. Of those not due to himself some are due to chance, the others to necessity; of these latter, again, some are due to compulsion, the others to nature. Consequently all actions that are not due to a man himself are due either to chance or to nature or to compulsion. All actions that are due to a man himself and caused by himself are due either to habit or to desire; and of the latter, some are due to rational desire, the others to irrational. Rational desire is wishing, and wishing is a desire for good---nobody wishes for anything unless he thinks it good. Irrational desire is twofold, viz.\ anger and appetite. Thus every action must be due to one or other of seven causes: chance, nature, compulsion, habit, reasoning, anger, or appetite (Aristotle, \textit{Rhetorica}, I.10, 1368b33--1369a6).
\end{adjustwidth}

These seven are no second list beside the four causes but a specification of one of them---the efficient \textit{archē}, ``that which originates the movement'' (\textit{DA} III.10, 433b13--16)---sorted along two cuts. The first fixes voluntariness: chance, nature, and compulsion lodge the origin outside the agent and so are involuntary; habit, reasoning, anger, and appetite lodge it in the agent and so are the agent's own.\footnote{The involuntary three mark by default what voluntary action requires: chance has no determinate \textit{archē}; nature has an internal origin but one bypassing \textit{orexis} and deliberation (growth, the body's regular processes); compulsion is external force moving the agent against their own \textit{orexis}. This last must be kept distinct from the appetitive excess it resembles---the runaway appetite of the intemperate has its \textit{archē} \textit{within} the agent and remains their own, voluntary and answerable however much they disown it (\textit{Rhet}. I.11, 1370a6--9; \textit{NE} III.5, 1114a31--b3); the distinction bears weight when the diachronic loop is traced.} The next distinction divides the voluntary origins by the species of desire that sources them. Since the completed action at $A_4$ is the actualization of a desire, the kind of action follows the kind of desire that drives it. Appetite (\textit{epithymia}) sources \textit{epithymetic} action, and spiritedness (\textit{thymos}) sources \textit{thymotic} action---and because \textit{thymos} answers a slight, it is irreducibly social, always borne toward another, a \textit{toward-whom}. The third voluntary desire is the rational wish the \textit{Rhetorica} simply calls reasoning; but this single cause divides, in the finer-grained practical works, into two moments, and so yields not one kind of action but two. The wish for an \textit{end} (\textit{boulēsis})---which may reach even toward the impossible---sources \textit{boulētic} action, while the deliberate choice of the \textit{means} to that end (\textit{prohairesis}) sources \textit{prohairetic} action. Habit, the last of the voluntary causes, sources action through no species of \textit{orexis} but through the familiarity that repetition lays down: ``acts are done from habit which men do because they have often done them before'' (\textit{Rhet}. I.10, 1369b6--7), and what is often done becomes, in effect, second nature---``as soon as a thing has become habitual, it is virtually natural'' (\textit{Rhet}. I.11, 1370a6--8). This habituated way of acting must not be conflated with the \textit{hexis} taken up below: habituation is the means by which a \textit{hexis} is cultivated, but the standing tendency to repeat what one has already done is not the settled dispositional ground it helps to form.

\textit{Prohairesis} is the fullest of the four, for in it deliberation completes itself \textit{as} an action. Aristotle's practical syllogism exhibits the form:

\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
But how is it that thought is sometimes followed by action, sometimes not; sometimes by movement, sometimes not? What happens seems parallel to the case of thinking and inferring about the immovable objects. There the end is the truth seen (for, when one thinks the two propositions, one thinks and puts together the conclusion), but here the two propositions result in a conclusion which is an action---for example, whenever one thinks that every man ought to walk, and that one is a man oneself, straightaway one walks; or that, in this case, no man should walk, one is a man: straightaway one remains at rest. And one so acts in the two cases provided that there is nothing to compel or to prevent (Aristotle, \textit{De Motu Animalium}, 701a7--16).
\end{adjustwidth}
The conclusion is no proposition but the deed itself---``straightaway one walks.'' These four sources are not exclusive compartments but name the desire that \textit{predominantly} sources an action; one episode may pass through several, as pity may prompt a bouletic wish to help and then a prohairetic choice of the means.

Three further distinctions, however, cut across these four types, each independent of the type and of one another. The first is the voluntary/involuntary cut already drawn---whether the action's \textit{archē} lies within the agent or outside. The second is whether the end lies beyond the doing, in a product (\textit{poiēsis}), or is immanent in the doing itself (\textit{praxis})---the bivalence taken up below. The third is whether a \textit{doxa} ratifies the appearance at all, and, if it does, with what content. Knowing an action's type settles none of these: an action sprung from any one of the four desires may be voluntary or not, a making or a doing, ratified by \textit{doxa} or not. This independence is what keeps an intemperance from being mistaken for a compulsion. The addict's runaway appetite can look compelled, yet it is the agent's own through and through: voluntary on the first cut, its \textit{archē} the agent's own appetite and no force from without; a \textit{praxis} on the second, a doing---if a degraded one; and the issue of an entrenched \textit{hexis}, a disposition the agent has laid down, not an external constraint. Compulsion would be the reverse---an origin outside the agent, overriding their own desire---which is just what the craving is not.

Any method of analysis, just like that which it proposes to analyze, will be best known through its end. As such, let us work through an example: We are hiking a hear a sound similar to a baby's rattle. We stop, trying to make out what it is and where it came from; we hear it again, and we take the sound \textit{as} a rattlesnake---though we see no snake, no creature at all---and, hoping not to provoke what we have not seen, we back slowly away and return as we came. To diagnose the retreat is to answer the four whys at once. Its \textit{matter} is the body moved, with the bodily alteration the fear itself is---the quickened pulse, the caught breath, the muscles gathering to withdraw (\textit{MA}, 7, 701b16--32). Its \textit{form} is the retreat \textit{as} retreat, the \textit{eidos} of the motion, an avoidance of the apparent destructive evil; what makes the motion \textit{this} withdrawal and not an advance or a standing-firm is the species of desire that sources it (fear) together with its object, the rattlesnake-taken-as-impending-harm. Here the formal determination is supplied wholly by \textit{phantasia}'s \textit{taking-as}, for nothing is given to sight: the sound is present, but the snake and its threat are present only as \textit{phantasia} renders them. Its \textit{efficient} cause is the \textit{orektikon} in act---the desiring part, moved by its object and moving the body through the organic parts---and behind the desire, as the standing disposition from which this very taking-of-the-sound-as-fearful proceeds, the agent's \textit{hexis}, the cultivated \textit{how} from which the coward's flight and the courageous person's holding-firm alike take their orientation. Its \textit{end} is the escape from the impending evil, the safety for the sake of which the whole motion is set going. Set beside the three-factor schema, these are not four findings laid side by side but the schema seen from the side of explanation: the object of desire is at once unmoved originator and final cause---safety as the good to attain, the rattlesnake as the evil to shun---the fear-specified \textit{orexis} is the moved mover and efficient cause, the body is what is moved and the matter in which the motion is realized, and the formal cause is read off their convergence, the determinate evil the appearance presents.

In the order of experience the motion runs forward---the object strikes sense, sense and \textit{phantasia} actualize, desire is moved, the body moves, the end is attained; but in the order of analysis we must begin where execution ends, with the completed act, and recover its principles in reverse. Aristotle warrants the inversion twice: the object defines the act and the act the faculty, so that activities are prior in account to the potentialities they actualize, and prior to both stand their correlative objects (\textit{DA} II.4, 415a16--22); and, since the actuality is the end, the potency takes its very name from the act it issues in (\textit{Met}. IX.8, 1050a21--25). The completed act is what is first \textit{for us}---given, evident, the thing to be explained---while the object and end it realizes are first \textit{in nature}; analysis is the passage from the one to the other (\textit{Phys}. I.1, 184a16--23). So we begin by fixing the \textit{energeia}, the retreat accomplished: this names the \textit{explanandum} and, in the same stroke, the realized \textit{telos}, since the completed act \textit{is} its own end.

From that terminus the three structures unfold together, each supplying what the others cannot. The three-factor schema is already a partial four-cause analysis of self-movement: the object of desire, as unmoved originator, is the final cause; the \textit{orektikon} in act, as moved mover, is the efficient cause---the fear that, moved by its object, moves the body; and the animal moved is the matter in which the motion is realized. Three of the four causes thus lie within the three factors; the formal cause alone stands outside them, read off their convergence as the \textit{eidos} of the motion. What the three factors leave open is \textit{which} source occupies the moved mover's place, and this only the seven rhetorical causes decide: they individuate the \textit{archē} and fix its voluntariness. Where the agent is the \textit{archē}, the source is one of the three desires---\textit{boulēsis}, \textit{thymos}, \textit{epithymia}---that may fill the moved mover's place, with habit as the dispositional ground beneath them Where the \textit{archē} lies otherwise, the source is nature working as an internal formal-final principle, or chance, or compulsion; and, only once the source is fixed does the form complete itself: the generic \textit{eidos}, avoidance, was legible from the object-as-evil at the first step, but whether the flight is a reflexive bolt, a \textit{thymotic} recoil, or a calculated retreat does not crystallize until the source is named. The form is doubly determined, its second determination downstream of the seven-cause individuation.

This is why the same end or \textit{telos}, escape or flight in this case, may spring from different sources. The flight of bare animal action and the flight of deliberated fear both make for safety, but they are not one motion. When the evil is \textit{in the percept}---the rattle sounding, present and already aversive---the \textit{phantasma} is aversive from the first, and the flight can discharge at once, no \textit{doxa} required: this is the floor of action the perceiving animal shares. Where the evil is \textit{not} in the percept but must be read from a sign---Aristotle's beacon taken as the mark of an advancing enemy, the destructive evil still wholly to come---nothing aversive is yet present to be fled, and the motion cannot fire until \textit{doxa} ratifies the inference. The rattlesnake heard but unseen falls between: the sound is present and aversive, enough to move even an animal, yet to take it \textit{as} a rattlesnake, and the rattlesnake as deadly and impending, is already to have crossed into the \textit{taking-as-true} that only \textit{doxa} affords. The threshold is just here---where \textit{phantasia}'s bare aversive tone passes into the evaluatively complex \textit{doxa} that fear proper requires---and it is the threshold an ethical \textit{hexis} commands: the hiker, fear answering the sound, retreats; the naturalist, the same fear answering the same sound but met by a cultivated courage, holds, listens again, and moves to identify what sounded. One \textit{phantasma}, one \textit{doxa} of danger, two shapes of action, divided by the \textit{hexis} that orients the fear.

\subsubsection{\textit{Hexis} Bivalence: \textit{Technē} and Ethical Hexis}

\textit{Hexis} is the settled, durable disposition from which an agent's \textit{doxai} and actions proceed---``the having \ldots\ a kind of activity of the haver and the had, something like an action or movement'' (\textit{Met}. V.20, 1022b5)\footnote{The original Greek translated as ``having'' and ``activity'' is, respectively, \gk{Ἕξις} (\textit{hexis}) and \gk{ἐνέργειά} (\textit{energeia}); Greek text in W. D. Ross, ed., \textit{Aristotle's Metaphysics}, vol.~1 (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1924).}---marked by its resistance to easy displacement, so that knowledge and moral excellence count as paradigmatic \textit{hexeis} while passing moods and fleeting opinions do not (\textit{Cat}. 8, 8b25--9a13). It is not itself a \textit{doxa}: a \textit{doxa} is a \textit{taking-as-true}, and the universal premises an agent draws on in acting---``every man ought to walk,'' and the like---are \textit{held doxai} a \textit{hexis} may incline one to hold, not \textit{hexeis} themselves (\textit{MA} 7, 701a7--16); nor is it the habit that cultivates it, for habituation forms a \textit{hexis} without being the disposition it forms. Heidegger's recovery of the term fixes what a \textit{hexis} is with precision: it is ``nothing other than a how of \textit{pathos}, being-out-of-composure, in relation to being-composed-as-to ...'' (\textit{BCAP} 125)---not a cognitive deposit but the dispositional form, the cultivated \textit{how} of standing in an emotion, as courage is to fear. The dispositional ground does not operate in a single register: the same genus that, as ethical \textit{hexis}, governs the \textit{praxis} whose end is immanent also appears as the \textit{hexis} of \textit{technē} that governs the \textit{poiēsis} whose end is a product beyond it. It is bivalent, and Heidegger's 1924 reading of the \textit{Rhetorica} draws the two poles apart.

The first pole is the \textit{hexis} of \textit{technē}---and the designation is Aristotle's own: \textit{technē}, he says, is ``identical with a state of capacity to make, involving a true course of reasoning'' (\textit{NE} VI.4, 1140a9--10), where the ``state'' is the \textit{hexis} and the ``capacity to make'' (\textit{poiētikē}) marks it as the \textit{hexis} of \textit{poiēsis}.\footnote{Greek: \gk{ταὐτὸν ἂν εἴη τέχνη καὶ ἕξις μετὰ λόγου ἀληθοῦς ποιητική} (\textit{tautòn àn eíē téchnē kaì héxis metà lógou alēthoûs poiētikḗ}); text from the Oxford Classical Text, \textit{Aristotelis Ethica Nicomachea}, ed. I. Bywater (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1962). The genus is \gk{ἕξις} (\textit{hexis}); its differentia \gk{ποιητική} (\textit{poiētikḗ}, ``of making'') sets it against the \gk{πρακτική} (\textit{praktikḗ}, ``of acting'') by which \textit{phronēsis} is defined at \textit{NE} VI.5, 1140b4--5.} The being of a \textit{technē} depends on the \textit{telos} as the disposition cultivated through training in a productive art, where the \textit{ergon}---the work, the product---is decisive: ``[s]hoemaking depends upon the shoe ... being a good, suitable shoe'' (\textit{BCAP} 123). Its structural logic is the reduction of deliberation. Heidegger states:

\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
Through practice, by frequently-undergoing, it comes about that being-oriented puts the prescription further and further out of play. Training has the precise sense of reducing deliberation insofar as it is through training that the completedness of attaining a result comes about. (\textit{BCAP} 127)
\end{adjustwidth}

The trained craft fires as routine---the grammarian writes ``not by chance, but according to a prescription ... from out of himself'' (\textit{BCAP} 127)---and its structural analogue at the existential level is the ready-to-hand equipment that withdraws from explicit attention into the work it serves: the hammer in the master carpenter's grip disappears into the hammering itself, becoming thematic only when it breaks, goes missing, or stands in the way (\textit{Being and Time} 98).\footnote{As Heidegger argues regarding the readiness-to-hand of equipment in \textit{Being and Time} : ``The hammering itself uncovers the specific `manipulability' [``Handlichkeit''] of the hammer. The kind of Being which equipment possesses---in which it manifests itself in its own right---we call ``\textit{readiness-to-hand}'' [\textit{Zuhandenheit}]. Only because equipment has \textit{this} `Being-in-itself' and does not merely occur, is it manipulable in the broadest sense and at our disposal. No matter how sharply we just \textit{look} [Nur-noch-\textit{hinsehen}] at the `outward appearance' [``Aussehen]'' of Things in whatever form this takes, we cannot discover anything ready-to-hand. If we look at Things just `theoretically', we can get along without understanding readiness-to-hand. But when we deal with them by using them and manipulating them, this activity is not a blind one; it has its own kind of sight by which our manipulation is guided and from which it acquires its specific Thingly character. Dealings with equipment subordinate themselves to the manifold assignments of the `in-order-to'. And the sight with which they thus accommodate themselves is \textit{circumspection}'' (98). The structural homology with \textit{technē} is precise: trained craft-knowledge operates transparently while the work proceeds, becoming thematic only when the equipment fails or something interferes with the process.} The \textit{technē}, like the hammer, withdraws while it operates. Its chain-architectural signature is compression. A cultivated \textit{technē} adds no stage to the actualization chain but compresses its passage through one: the deliberative arc at $A_3$ collapses into a transparent procedural recognition, so that the chain runs from $A_2$ through $A_3$ to $A_4$ as if at a single stroke. The \textit{doxa} is not thereby bypassed---no stage drops out---but passed through transparently: what operates at $A_3$ keeps the \textit{doxatic} form of a pre-thematic ``this is how it is done,'' a craft-directive relied upon rather than weighed. It remains a \textit{doxa}---so \textit{technē} stays a rational disposition, its \textit{logos} retained but rendered transparent---yet a \textit{doxa} of a peculiar authority. Where an ordinary \textit{doxa} is held on the standing of those who hold it, the artisan's recognition answers to the \textit{eidos} of the product: the shoe meets the form of a good shoe or it does not, an \textit{archē} that is not merely social.\footnote{That the mature \textit{technē} answers to the \textit{eidos} does not free it of \textit{doxa} at the root. The artisan's procedural recognition is drawn from a standing fore-having (\textit{Vorhabe})---the already-interpreted craft-world into which the apprentice grows, what counts as good leather or a well-made shoe---received first on the authority of masters and the craft tradition, that is, as \textit{doxa} in the ordinary social sense. The \textit{eidos} that later anchors the \textit{technē} independently is thus itself first given \textit{doxatically}; repeated production refines this inherited view into a reliable knowing-one's-way-around, anchored in the work's success or failure, without ever fully evacuating the \textit{doxatic} structure from the craft's operational floor.} And just because the appearance is taken under this aspect---as the means to a determinate \textit{ergon}, not under the evaluatively charged aspect that would summon an emotion---the higher-order evaluative axis remains unengaged so long as the craft runs transparently. The deliberative passage to action is itself a motion, ``a kind of activity---an imperfect kind'' (\textit{DA} II.5, 417a16), which \textit{technē} does not carry through but abbreviates. This compression is defeasible, however: let the work resist---the tool breaks, the material refuses to comply, the case falls outside the routine---and $A_3$ re-thickens, deliberation and the evaluative axis alike returning with the situation now to be weighed, just as the hammer that had withdrawn into the hammering phenomenologically reappears the moment it breaks in the hand.

The second pole is the ethical \textit{hexis}, and its structure is the contrary of routine. The being of the human being, unlike the being of the shoe, ``is determined in its \gk{ἔργον} [\textit{ergon}] as \gk{πρᾶξις} [\textit{praxis}] ... [which] has the \gk{τέλος} [\textit{telos}] in itself \ldots\ It depends on \gk{ἕξις} [\textit{hexis}]'' (\textit{BCAP} 123). What such a \textit{hexis} cultivates is not a routine but a readiness:

\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
Cultivating \gk{ἕξις} [\textit{hexis}] never depends on an operation, a routine. In an operation, the moment is destroyed. Every completedness, as settled routine, breaks down in the face of the moment. Appropriation and cultivation of \gk{ἕξις} through habituation means nothing other than correct repetition.... The distinction lies in the fact that \gk{πρᾶξις} [\textit{praxis}] depends on the \textit{how}. The how is only appropriated in such a way that the human being enables himself \textit{to be composed at each moment}; not routine but holding-oneself-open, \gk{δύναμις} [\textit{dynamis}] in the \gk{μεσότης} [\textit{mesōtes}]. (\textit{BCAP} 128)
\end{adjustwidth}
Where the \textit{technē} contracts deliberation toward transparent routine, the ethical \textit{hexis} sustains the standing capacity for fresh resolution---the \textit{prohairesis}, the deliberate choice of what to do---in the face of the \textit{kairos}. It does not withdraw into transparency but holds the agent open for renewed \textit{prohairetic} engagement on each occasion; and Heidegger is explicit that no \textit{technē} can stand in for it: ``this determination should not be conceived as though there were a \gk{τέχνη} [\textit{technē}] for this taking-opportunities and venturing-out into the \gk{δεινά} [\textit{deina}] of life'' (\textit{BCAP} 122--123). The \textit{kairos} is precisely what routine forecloses. The canonical practical-rational \textit{hexis} of this kind is \textit{phronēsis}, ``a true and reasoned state of capacity to act with regard to the things that are good or bad for man,'' which Aristotle marks off from \textit{technē} on just this ground---that \textit{praxis} has no external \textit{ergon} but is its own \textit{telos} (\textit{NE} VI.5, 1140b4--5).\footnote{For Aristotle's full discussion of \textit{phronēsis} or practical wisdom, see \textit{NE} VI.5, a24--b30.}

These are therefore not two species of one function but structurally distinct modes of being-composed, and their difference tells directly in the chain. A \textit{hexis} is not a \textit{doxa}, yet it conditions \textit{doxa}-formation: it enters the motion from \textit{phantasma} to \textit{doxa} ($M_2 \rightarrow A_3$) as a relative unmoved originator alongside the \textit{phantasma} itself, helping determine what the agent takes the appearance to be---for ``the end appears to each man in a form answering to his character,'' so that one is in part answerable for how things appear (\textit{NE} III.5, 1114a31--b3).\footnote{``Now some one may say that all men aim at the apparent good, but have no control over how things appear to him; but the end appears to each man in a form answering to his character. We reply that if each man is somehow responsible for the state he is in, he will also be himself somehow responsible for how things appear'' (\textit{NE} III.5, 1114a31--b3).} The two poles condition it differently. Where the \textit{technē} contracts the chain to a transparent routine, the ethical \textit{hexis} exercises a two-level reach, working beneath the \textit{doxa}, on the hedonic tone of perception, and at the \textit{doxa} itself. The lower reach is the harder to see. Beneath any \textit{taking-as-true}, the \textit{hexis} inflects the basic hedonic tonality with which perception completes itself at $A_1$, and the mechanism is internal to what a \textit{hexis} is. The hedonic tonality at $A_1$ is nothing other than the discriminating dimension of \textit{aisthēsis} disclosing a present object as attractive or aversive in the very act of perceiving it; and a \textit{hexis} is the cultivated \textit{how} of being-affected (\textit{paschein}), the settled tuning of that very susceptibility. So the disposition inflects the tone in which the object is disclosed---tilting one and the same occasion to register as more aversive to a timorous soul, less so to a courageous one---and it does so before any \textit{doxa} is formed: the perceptual field arrives already tonally pitched, not a neutral given awaiting judgement. At the upper level the \textit{hexis} then conditions which \textit{taking-as-true} the \textit{phantasma} settles into at $A_3$---and here the agent is not the author of the \textit{doxa} but its subject, for a \textit{doxa} is an involuntary \textit{taking-as-true}, a being-convinced one undergoes rather than an endorsement one issues. The \textit{hexis} does not let the agent affirm a \textit{doxa} at will but inclines which one takes hold, the appearance settling into the construal that answers to the agent's character. The two levels are continuous, the lower already half the work of the upper: an appearance that arrives aversively pitched is the readier to settle into a \textit{doxa} of danger---which is how the \textit{hexis} shapes the \textit{doxa} without the agent ever electing it, and so conditions which determinate emotion issues. Cultivating the \textit{hexis} does not eliminate the emotion---cultivating courage does not abolish fear, nor temperance the craving for the pleasant---but orients it: two people meeting the same sound, already pitched differently for each, are brought by their contrary \textit{hexeis} to contrary \textit{doxai}, the one's fear readying for flight, the other's readying to stand and meet the danger. One occasion, two dispositions, two shapes of action.

The bivalence is nowhere clearer than in a single practitioner exercising both dispositions. In suturing a wound the surgeon exercises \textit{technē}: the end is a detachable product---the closed wound---and the work is judged instrumentally, by the efficiency and quality with which that result is attained. In sitting with a dying patient the same surgeon exercises an ethical \textit{hexis}: here there is no detachable artifact, only the conduct itself---truthful, courageous, compassionate---in which the excellence wholly consists. The end of the first lies beyond the doing, in the work; the end of the second is immanent in the doing. And because an ethical \textit{hexis} is the cultivated \textit{how} of standing in an emotion, it is named for the comportment it achieves and not the affect it presupposes: we call the surgeon courageous, not fearful, though fear is the very condition courage masters (\textit{NE} III.6--7). This \textit{teleological} seam is the one the opening named---\textit{poiēsis} a \textit{kinēsis}, whole only when its product stands forth; \textit{praxis} an \textit{energeia}, whole at every moment of its exercise (\textit{Met}. IX.6, 1048b18--34)---and Aristotle marks it by a tense-test: ``it is not true that at the same time we are \ldots\ building and have built \ldots; but it is the same thing that at the same time has seen and is seeing \ldots. The latter sort of process, then, I call an actuality, and the former a movement'' (\textit{Met}. IX.6, 1048b30--36).

This bivalence is not a fifth kind of action set beside the four but a distinction that cross-cuts them, as already marked: any of the four source-desires may terminate in a making, whose end is a product beyond the doing, or in a doing whose end is internal to it. What varies is not the source-desire but the dispositional mode through which the chain runs. Appetite that fires pre-\textit{doxastically} engages no settled disposition at all, running from $A_1$ through $A_2$ to $A_4$ without additional dispositional mediation. Where the chain runs through \textit{technē}, the cultivated craft-recognition fires it on a trained judgment, its passage through $A_3$ transparent; and, where the agent meets the situation in fresh resolution, it routes through the ethical \textit{hexis}, \textit{doxa} ratifying the \textit{phantasma} in a genuine encounter with the \textit{kairos}. No completed action at $A_4$ is disposition-neutral: each sediments back into the dispositional ground, the kind deposited keyed to the route it ran. Production deepens the \textit{technē}, consolidating routine and reducing deliberation; a doing whose end is its own performance deepens the ethical \textit{hexis}, strengthening the capacity for fresh evaluative openness; and even the pre-\textit{doxastic} appetite, which ran through no disposition, leaves its own residue over repetition---the temperance or intemperance that disposes the agent toward the body's pleasures. It is this diachronic sedimentation---the dispositional ground reshaped by the very actions it enables---that constitutes the recursive structure of the \textit{pathetic} loop.

\subsubsection{The \textit{Pathetic} Loop: Synchronic Pass and Diachronic Sedimentation}

\inlinenote{$M_3 \rightarrow A_4$: affect/desire??? Is it the motivating / mobilizing movement / force? If this is the mobilizing orientation, what determines its form? Emotion is one form this motion can take, but basic epithymetic desire is another. need to work through this.}

The \textit{pathetic} loop operates in two temporal registers---synchronic and diachronic---and the relationship between them is what gives the actualization chain its recursive, spiraling character. In its synchronic register, the loop describes a single, directional chain-pass from $A_0$ through $A_4$ within one discrete event: the dyadic encounter of sensible object and \textit{aisthētikon} yields completed perception at $A_1$, whose dual-resonance residues are stabilized as \textit{phantasma} at $A_2$, oriented cognitively at $A_3$, and consummated in \textit{praxis} at $A_4$. Within this pass, whether \textit{doxa} ratifies an evaluatively complex content at $A_3$ determines whether the \textit{phantasma} produces higher-order emotions; and when it does, the resultant affective-orectic mobilization catalyzes species-specific desire---anger, fear, pity, shame, etc.---that drives $M_3 \rightarrow A_4$. This synchronic register is what the \textit{Rhetorica}'s canonical definition of the emotions articulates: feelings ``that so change men as to affect their judgements'' (Aristotle, \textit{Rhetorica}, 1378a20--22). The judgement-modifying function is not an accidental feature of emotion but the very structure of the synchronic pass at the $A_3$ node, where \textit{doxa} and emotion meet in a single operation.

The diachronic register, by contrast, describes what happens \textit{across} chain-passes. Residues of prior actualizations do not simply vanish upon the completion of $A_4$; they sediment as dispositional ground---as \textit{hexis}, \textit{technē}, or, most simply, the small change in the world that grounds the next pass from $A_0 \rightarrow A_4$---and modulate each subsequent pass through a single mechanism, \textit{Stimmung}-saturation, which enters the chain before any \textit{doxa} forms. What the perceptual motion $M_0 \rightarrow A_1$ inherits from the standing disposition is only a diffuse hedonic tone---the dark alley pre-disclosed as ominous, the shape itself still veridically seen; the determinate distortion enters one motion on, at the phantastic motion $M_1 \rightarrow A_2$, where the \textit{resonant epithymia} the emotion carries forward works a faint resemblance up into a determinate figure---the shadow \textit{taken-as} an enemy---which the \textit{doxa} then ratifies at $A_3$. Aristotle provides the canonical description in \textit{De Insomniis}: ``even when the external object of perception has departed, the impressions it has made persist, and are themselves objects of perception,'' and under the influence of emotion ``the coward when excited by fear'' thinks he sees his foes approaching, while ``the amorous person'' sees the object of desire, ``and the more deeply one is under the influence of the emotion, the less similarity is required to give rise to these illusory impressions'' (2, 460b1--10). What Aristotle specifies is not a marginal pathological case but the general structure of affective priming, and the passage names the two sources the one saturation draws on: the standing disposition---the \textit{type} of person one has become---and the occurrent emotion---the intensity of the present \textit{pathos}, which deceives even the steady person, so that the more deeply one is gripped, the less the occasion need supply. The dispositional ground laid down by prior chain-passes saturates the receptive capacity of \textit{aisthēsis}, so that the perceptual field is always already tonally inflected. Heidegger names this inflection disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}), the existentiale through which Dasein finds itself always already disposed in a world that is disclosed not neutrally but atmospherically, as \textit{Stimmung} (\textit{Being and Time} 176). The receptive character shared by \textit{aisthēsis} and \textit{Befindlichkeit} is thus not merely analogical; both name the structure whereby an entity is affected by beings prior to any thematic judgement,\footnote{``Only we must not be misled by this into denying that ontologically mood is a primordial kind of Being for Dasein, in which Dasein is disclosed to itself \textit{prior to} all cognition and volition, and \textit{beyond} their range of disclosure'' (Heidegger, \textit{Being and Time} 175).} a structure that is itself constitutively shaped by what has come before.

This ratification at $A_3$ is no second mechanism but the same saturation carried through: the \textit{phantasma} the residue has already inflected is the \textit{phantasma} the \textit{doxa} takes as true. What emotion weakens is the discernment that might otherwise hold the inflected image off, for ``the faculty in virtue of which the controlling sense judges is not identical with that in virtue of which images come before the mind'' (\textit{De Insomniis} 2, 460b16--18);\footnote{The faculty Beare renders ``controlling sense'' is \gk{τὸ κύριον} (\textit{tò kýrion}), the authoritative or governing part of perception; what it does is \gk{κρίνειν} (\textit{krínein}, ``to judge''), the verb at the root of \textit{kritikon}. The distinction in full: \gk{τὸ μὴ κατὰ τὴν αὐτὴν δύναμιν κρίνειν τό τε κύριον καὶ ᾧ τὰ φαντάσματα γίνεται}---the controlling part does not judge by the same \gk{δύναμις} (\textit{dýnamis}, ``power'') as that by which the \textit{phantasmata} arise (460b16--18). Greek text from W. D. Ross, ed., \textit{Aristotle: Parva Naturalia} (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1955).} and under excitement the latter overpowers the former (461b3--7), so the inflected \textit{phantasma} is \textit{taken-as-true} unopposed. The loop thus introduces no new transition into the chain; it biases the existing nodes---perception, \textit{phantasma}, \textit{doxa}---through which every pass already runs. The dispositional ground is not a container for emotions but the very manner in which the agent is susceptible to being affected---the \textit{how} of \textit{paschein} rather than its \textit{what}.

Von Uexküll's account of the child at play furnishes a vivid instance. A child playing ``the story of Hansel and Gretel, the witch, and the gingerbread house'' becomes so gripped by the imagined witch that she cries, ``[g]et the witch out of here; I can't stand to see her repulsive face any more!'' (119). The \textit{phantasma} has become vivid enough to grip the child affectively despite her standing \textit{doxa} that the scene is merely play; the affective response is keyed to the appearance, not to the judgment she could still avow, and for the moment the \textit{doxa} that had governed the activity no longer holds her. The episode shows at once that a \textit{doxa} is held only so long as it continues to convince---\textit{held-as-true} until revised---and that a sufficiently charged \textit{phantasma} can override a standing \textit{doxa}.

Indeed, the \textit{Rhetorica}'s judgement-modifying definition and the \textit{De Insomniis} mechanism are not two separate doctrines but two articulations of one loop-structure operating across two temporal registers. Synchronically, emotion modifies the very judgement that would otherwise check it; diachronically, the sedimented residue of that modification reshapes the perceptual and \textit{doxastic} conditions for every subsequent pass. Accordingly, the chain is not merely iterative but spiraling: $A_4$ completion sediments, and the next chain-pass enters at a new $A_0$ with the dispositional ground subtly---or perhaps drastically---shifted. The agent who has just acted in anger is not the same agent who will next encounter a perceived slight, because the act itself has reshaped the affective architecture through which the world is disclosed. Thus, emotions ``alter judgements'' precisely where they ``intrude on \textit{logos}'' (Struever 109), and this intrusion is not an episodic disruption but the ongoing self-modification of the dispositional ground. For the ``passions are dispositions oriented on others' dispositions that define our in-the-worldness,'' and, therefore, ``logos functions only in a Dasein defined as a lively practice of doxa, and of cares, and of passions'' (109). Gross captures the anti-internalist force of this thesis: ``Heidegger characterizes \textit{pathos} (variously `passion,' `affect,' `mood,' or `emotion') as the very condition for the possibility of rational discourse, or \textit{logos}. No cynical and crowd-pleasing addition to \textit{logos}, \textit{pathos} is the very substance in which propositional thought finds its objects and its motivation''; for we, without affect, ``would have no grounds for concern, no time and place for judging, no motivation to discourse at all'' (Gross ``Introduction'' 4). \textit{Pathos} is not added to a neutral deliberative process; it constitutes the medium in which deliberation occurs at all.

This recursion has a precise mechanism. As Nussbaum observes, translating the \textit{Nicomachean Ethics}, ``All men strive for the apparent good (\textit{phainomenon agathon}): but no one is in control of the appearing (\textit{phantasia}): the way the end appears (\textit{phainetai}) to someone depends on what sort of a man he is'' (``{Phantasia}'' 245). The sort of man one is---one's sedimented \textit{hexis}---conditions the very \textit{phantasia} through which the good presents itself, and so conditions the content available for doxastic ratification. The loop is therefore no simple feedback mechanism but a genuinely recursive structure: disposition shapes appearance, appearance shapes action, and action, through its \textit{pathetic} residue, reshapes disposition. This is what makes ethical transformation so difficult on Aristotle's account: a vicious \textit{hexis} reproduces itself by conditioning the very appearances available to the agent, narrowing the content that might otherwise present a more adequate good,\footnote{``For it is not any and every belief that pleasant and painful objects destroy and pervert, e.g. the belief that the triangle has or has not its angles equal to two right angles, but only beliefs about what is to be done. For the principles of the things that are done consist in that for the sake of which they are to be done; but the man who has been ruined by pleasure or pain forthwith fails to see any such principle---to see that for the sake of this or because of this he ought to choose and do whatever he chooses and does; for vice is destructive of the principle'' (Aristotle, \textit{Nicomachean Ethics}, VI.5, 1140b13--19).} so that the loop, once established, runs with a momentum not easily interrupted from within.

A personal example makes the appetitive route concrete. Having given up smoking after fifteen years, I find that long after the physiological withdrawal has passed the craving persists: the cigarette still presents itself as desirable even as I judge, without wavering, that it is harmful. Years of indulgence sedimented an appetitive \textit{hexis}---an intemperance, in Aristotle's terms (\textit{Nicomachean Ethics} III.10--12)---that continues to condition the \textit{phantasia} through which the object appears, exactly as Nussbaum's gloss predicts: the appearing is not in my control. Abstention slowly cultivates the contrary disposition, temperance, but the entrenched \textit{hexis} does not dissolve at once, and its persistence is just the self-reinforcing momentum of the loop. This is the appetitive analogue of the graded feedback that distinguishes the other routes: as \textit{poiēsis} sediments \textit{technē} and \textit{praxis} sediments virtue, so repeated appetitive action sediments experience (\textit{Metaphysics} I.1, 980b28--981a12) together with the temperance or intemperance that disposes one toward the body's pleasures. No completed action is disposition-neutral; each leaves its residue in the kind of agent one is becoming.

What Heidegger names \textit{historicality} (\textit{Geschichtlichkeit})---Dasein's historicity---is, at its deepest structural level, this diachronic register of the \textit{pathetic} loop's recursive self-modification. ``As historical, Dasein is possible only by reason of its temporality, and temporality temporalizes itself in the ecstatico-horizonal unity of its raptures'' (Heidegger, \textit{Being and Time}, \S 74, H.385--386; Eng.~p.~437). The Heideggerian articulation does not supplement the Aristotelian structure; it names what the Aristotelian text already specifies---that the totality of involvements through which \textit{orexis} finds its objects is never fixed but perpetually reconstituted by the sedimented history of the agent's own affective life. The agent's dispositional ground is, accordingly, not a static substrate but a living, self-revising archive---the diachronic dimension of the \textit{pathetic} loop spiraling forward into each new encounter with the world.

\subsubsection{Synthesis: The Actualization Chain as the Architecture of Being-in-the-World}

With completed action the analysis of this part reaches its term. From the joint actuality of sensible object and \textit{aisthētikon} at $A_0$, through the perceptual deposit at $A_1$, the \textit{phantasma} stabilized at $A_2$, and the \textit{doxa} that ratifies it at $A_3$, to the \textit{praxis} consummated at $A_4$, the soul has been shown to move itself to its end along a single chain---one analyzable by the three structures, terminating in action of two kinds, and governed at its disposition by the bivalence of \textit{hexis}. What this articulates is not a psychology of inner states but the structure of a being for whom the world is never neutral. That is the upshot Gross names \textit{affordance}: ``Rhetoric, as a humanistic form of affordance theory, posits a situation that is not neutral but is rather `persuasive,' threatening and promising with respect to our human being-in-the-world'' (Gross, \textit{Uncomfortable Situations}, p.~20). The soul's being-affected is, on Heidegger's recovery, no private episode but a mode of disclosure---\textit{paschein} as \textit{dechesthai}, ``being related to the world as in it, being in dealings with it'' (Heidegger, \textit{Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy}, pp.~277--280), ``a being-taken of human beings \textit{in their full being-in-the-world}'' (\textit{BCAP}, pp.~133--134) rather than anything occurring behind the eyes.

This returns the analysis to the typology with which it began. The seven causes by which the \textit{Rhetorica} sorts all human action are not a region apart from this structure but its full extension: anger and appetite are the irrational desires whose evaluative disclosure has been the burden of the analysis; reasoning is the rational desire this same structure pervades, since no end appears as choiceworthy except to a soul already affectively disposed; habit is the sedimented residue through which the loop reconstitutes the ground of each subsequent pass; and the involuntary causes mark, by contrast, what a \textit{praxis} that is the agent's own requires. The disclosure is, moreover, itself a source of motion: a constituted emotion is doubly kinetic---itself a being-moved and, once established, a source of further motion---not the originator of the synchronic chain, which remains the realizable good working through \textit{orexis}, but the impetus that carries the diachronic loop from $A_4$ back through \textit{hexis}-sedimentation into the dispositional ground of the next encounter. The actualization chain is, in this, the architecture through which the soul, met by a world that already matters, moves itself to action and lays down the disposition from which its next disclosure arises.
