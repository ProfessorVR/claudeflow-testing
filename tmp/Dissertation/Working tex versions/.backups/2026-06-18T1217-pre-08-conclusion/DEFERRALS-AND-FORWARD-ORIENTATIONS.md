## §1.1 — Being-in-Motion: The Ontological Horizon of Motion and Time

*(Originally at L137–L139 in `1.1 - A0 - Motion and Time as Ontological Horizon-v2.tex`)*

### Deferrals and Forward Orientations

Three matters bear future development and are flagged here for the reader's orientation. First, the interpretive reading of *Physics* IV.14, 223a21–26 adopted in this section — that time without soul is ontologically incomplete rather than merely unnumbered — is defensible on priority-of-actuality grounds, but the textual warrant is cautious-conditional. Alternative readings that treat time as fully actual independently of soul merit extended engagement, with Coope's strong reading (*Time for Aristotle*, Oxford 2005, pp. 41–52) as the principal sympathetic interlocutor and Broadie's weak reading (*Nature, Change, and Agency*, Oxford 1982, pp. 146–148; "Physics IV 14," in Frede and Charles eds., Oxford 2012, pp. 203–204) as the principal critical interlocutor; Coope's verdict that "time is essentially something that can be counted" aligns with the present chapter's priority-of-actuality move, while Broadie's distinction between time-as-*arithmos* and the before-and-after-of-change supplies the textual constraint on how far the strong reading can be pressed without overreach.

Second, the relation between time as a common sensible (*De Anima* III.1, 425a16), the discriminating faculty's unity across the now (*De Anima* III.2, 426b17–427a1), and the role of *phantasia* in producing continuity across discrete perceptual instants bears on how the time of the encompassing horizon becomes the temporal manifold articulated by the chain's downstream actualities. The *De Memoria* passages on memory requiring the recognition of past-qua-past (449b22–25, 450a11–13), together with *De Anima* III.10, 433b5–10 on the sense of time co-implicated with deliberative *phantasia*, specify the psychological apparatus through which the cognitive articulation of time occurs; the systematic treatment of *phantasia* as the temporal-narrative-synthesizing capacity is the chapter's structural pivot and belongs to the sections on *aisthēsis*, *phantasia*, and the orientational modes at $M_2 \rightarrow A_3$.

Third, the entelechial structure displayed by the chain at each completed actuality — the way each $A_n$ functions as both *telos* and *archē* — suggests an affinity with the rhetorical theory of motivated action subsequent chapters will develop; the full articulation of this affinity lies beyond the present section. With $A_0$ specified, the analysis can now turn to $A_1$, where the natural beings, sensible objects, and rational souls whose first actualities the motion-time horizon licenses begin to enter the chain in their own structural roles.

## §1.2 — M_0 to A_1: The Actualization of *Aisthēsis*

*(Originally at L143–L145 in `1.2 - M0 to A1 - The Actualization of Aisthesis-v2.tex`)*

### Deferrals and Forward Orientations

Three matters bear future development and are flagged here for the reader's orientation. First, the dual-trace thesis advanced in §F treats the inheritance of affective valence as analytically entailed by sensation in all animals. This reading draws on *De Anima* II.3, 414b1–6, which makes the entailment explicit for sensation as such. Whether every sensory affection in every animal inherits both traces, however, bears on the wider Aristotelian claim that appetite is the single source of all animal movement (*De Anima* III.10, 433a27–31); the question whether primitive organisms with only the sense of touch carry both traces or only the formal one merits engagement in a more extended treatment of animal locomotion, with Nussbaum's commentary on the *De Motu Animalium* (Nussbaum 1985, *The Role of Phantasia in Aristotle's Explanation of Action*, esp. pp. 240–255 on *phantasia* as preparation-of-desire) and Caston's analysis of *phantasia*'s role in animal cognition (Caston 1996, *Why Aristotle Needs Imagination*, esp. pp. 26–52 on *phantasia* as Aristotle's solution to the problem of error) as the principal interlocutors.

Second, the mechanism by which the medium's sequential affection translates into the unified perceptual act at $A_1$ is not fully articulated in *De Anima* and requires supplementary analysis from *Sense and Sensibilia* and the *De Motu Animalium*. The sequential character of the medium's affection (*Sense and Sensibilia* 6, 447a3–7) implies that the sensible form reaches the organ over a temporal extension that varies by modality, yet the perceptual act itself at the moment of completion presents the object as a unified formal whole; the mechanism through which the temporally-extended transmission issues in the temporally-unified perceptual experience is a genuine aporia that the corpus does not fully resolve, requiring treatment of the *koinē aisthēsis* analysis at *De Anima* III.1–2 and the further articulation of *phantasia*'s synthesizing role in subsequent sections.

Third, the dual-trace thesis developed here distinguishes basic valence (*resonant epithymia*, pleasant-or-painful, pursue-or-avoid) from the determinate higher-order *pathē* of the *Rhetoric* (anger, fear, pity, shame, kindness, indignation, envy, emulation). The basic valence is co-constitutive with perception from $A_1$ onward and is inherited as a dispositional property by the *phantasma* at $A_2$; the higher-order *pathē* arise only downstream of *doxa*'s orthogonal ratification of $A_3$ content, requiring the rational cognitive apparatus and the committal dimension of opinion that this section's animals do not necessarily possess. The structural distinction between *epithymia* (basic valence, *resonant epithymia*) and *pathē* in the higher-order sense is load-bearing for the chapter and is developed fully in the emotion section. With $A_1$ specified and its dual trace deposited, the analysis can now turn to $A_2$, where *phantasia* takes up the residue and renders it as the *phantasma* proper.

## [2026-06-13] Held for §06 Hexis-Bivalence / Diachronic revision — Uexküll child + Struever
Cut from the condensed §A_4 (old 05-DoxRatification). Re-offer when revising the hexis-bivalence / diachronic-sedimentation sections; it makes the "a sufficiently charged phantasma can override a standing doxa" point.

Von Uexküll's account of the child at play furnishes a vivid instance. A child playing ``the story of Hansel and Gretel, the witch, and the gingerbread house'' becomes so gripped by the imagined witch that she cries, ``Get the witch out of here; I can't stand to see her repulsive face any more!'' (von Uexküll, \textit{A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans}, pp.~122--126). The \textit{phantasma} has become vivid enough to grip the child affectively despite her standing \textit{doxa} that the scene is merely play; the affective response is keyed to the appearance, not to the judgment she could still avow, and for the moment the \textit{doxa} that had governed the activity no longer holds her. The episode shows at once that a \textit{doxa} is held only so long as it continues to convince---held-as-true until revised---and that a sufficiently charged \textit{phantasma} can override a standing one. Struever, reading Heidegger's account of the \textit{pathē}, places the moment of their force at the point of articulated judgement: emotions ``intrude on logos---here articulated judgements'' (Struever, \textit{Heidegger and Rhetoric}, p.~109).
