\section{What is A Virtual Thing?}

The conception of the \textit{virtual} and \textit{virtual reality}, as Michael Heim argues, can be traced as far back as Plato’s cave (\cite{heim1998_virtual} 26). Philosophically speaking, as Heim observes, “virtual” is a term meaning “not actually, but as if” (\cite{heim1998_virtual} 220). Etymologically, “virtual” evolved from post-classical Latin’s virtualis: “of or relating to power or potency,” that which has the power to produce an effect (“virtual”). Contemporarily speaking, it is used to refer to the virtual environments, objects, persons, and events produced by digital technologies. Considering the wide range of things to which \textit{virtual} can be—and has been—applied, my use of \textit{virtual reality} refers to computer-generated—and mediated—scenes, objects, persons, and events that manifest an interactive virtual environment into which the user is immersed through devices such as Head Mounted Displays (HMD) and handheld controllers.

It is, likely, due to this history and understanding of the virtual and virtual reality technologies that people commonly compare virtual objects to the non-virtual objects they supposedly represent, suggesting that they are not “real” or not as “real” as the things of objective reality.\footnote{I want to note that I use the term “objective reality” to refer to the everyday reality outside of or external to that produced by VR technologies. I use “non-virtual” to refer to the objects experienced in everyday, objective reality.} However, I believe this to be a mistaken assumption. Our, often instinctual, responses to virtual objects is an indication of their reality: insofar as a virtual object or thing produces or provokes a response from the user experiencing it is grounds for thinking that a real object is present. 

Thus, I occupy a position similar to that taken by Heim and David Chalmers: evinced by their causal powers that are able to affect users, I argue that virtual objects and virtual reality are very much real things, they are just virtual things. However, while I agree with Chalmers and accept that virtual objects are real and not merely fictional or illusory objects, I disagree with the identity claim he makes and which serves as the foundation for his argument: that virtual objects are digital objects. Just as we need to make a distinction between a non-virtual object and a virtual object, so too do we need to make a distinction between a virtual object and the digital object on which it depends.\footnote{I use “depend” here and throughout to refer to ontological dependence.}  Accordingly, I will argue against Chalmers and suggest that digital objects and virtual objects are two different \textit{kinds} of objects: a digital object is a \textit{functional} object in that it functions to produce a specific virtual object, whereas a virtual object is a \textit{perceptual} object that is the rendered output of the specific digital object on which it depends. Our work, then, is to pin down the nature of a digital object, digital property, and digital event versus a virtual object, virtual property, and virtual event.\footnote{Here I am taking Jaegwon Kim’s notion of an event as an object and a property that is temporally situated. }

I believe we can start to get at the distinction between the two kinds of objects by thinking through the following questions: (1) On what does a virtual object and a digital object ontologically depend? (2) What is the difference between a digital property of a digital object versus a virtual property of a virtual object? (3) What is the difference between a digital event and a virtual event? 

\subsection{On What Does A Virtual Thing Ontologically Depend?}

A virtual object, like the lightsaber one is able to wield in the VR program \textit{Trials on Tatooine}, consists of various lines of code which determine its visual properties—such as size, shape, color—as well as what kinds of actions it can and cannot perform or be a part of. This segment of code, and the program overall, is then located in the hard drive of a computer that reads the code and renders the virtual object that is mediated through the HMD. The fact that a specific segment of code or data structure constitutes a certain virtual object leads Chalmers to argue that virtual objects are “digital objects constituted by computational processes on a computer” (\cite{chalmers2017} 317).

Thus, he understands virtual objects as digital objects that “can be regarded as data structures, which are grounded in computational processes which are themselves grounded in physical processes on one or more computers" (\cite{chalmers2017} 317). The perceptible properties experienced when we see a virtual lightsaber “directly reflects the 
properties of this data structure” (\cite{chalmers2017} 317). Moreover, the interaction of two virtual objects, say the lightsaber and the storm trooper’s blaster fire (Fig.~\ref{fig:Trials On Tatooine}), is generated “by causal interaction among these data structures” which are themselves “causally active on real computers in the real world” (\cite{chalmers2017} 317). Therefore, Chalmers’ identity claim derives from and is supported by his argument from causal power: “(1) Virtual objects have certain causal powers (to affect other virtual objects, to affect users, and so on). (2) Digital objects really have those causal powers (and nothing else does). –— (3) Virtual objects are digital objects” (\cite{chalmers2017} 318).

Arguing that virtual objects are digital objects that are \textit{grounded} in computational processes which are \textit{grounded} in physical computers, Chalmers attempts to explain the existence and ontological status of virtual objects in terms of the code, data structures, or digital objects that constitute them which in turn are explained by the computational process of the physical computer. However, while I agree with Chalmers’ characterization of a digital object as a data structure which determines a virtual object’s perceptible properties, there seems to be complications with Chalmers’ identity claim. Firstly, his definition of a digital object could easily be applied to more traditional objects such as the icon for \textit{Microsoft Word} and the program itself would also be qualified as such. Thus, his definition fails to capture the distinction between a virtual object experienced in VR versus other types of digital objects. Secondly, and more important, it seems that a digital object can exist without the virtual object, but a virtual object cannot exist without the digital object on which it depends. This state of affairs is our first clue that virtual objects and digital objects are not identical.

The digital object only depends on the computer which stores and reads it, whereas the virtual object ontologically depends on a particular digital object, the computer that renders it, and the HMD which mediates our experience of it. Even if the computer is turned off the digital object still exists in the computer’s memory, but the rendered virtual object does not. While we might be able to open the program and observe the actual code or digital object as it is stored in the computer, the virtual object which arises from it cannot be perceived without the VR technology that mediates the final rendered output. Virtual objects are real objects insofar as they are images composed of light—a massless object—projected and mediated by the HMD. A specific virtual object, therefore, can only exist when the computer reads the digital object and actively renders it as a virtual object which is projected and mediated through the HMD; the existence of a digital object, on the other hand, needs only the computer in which it is stored. Furthermore, Chalmers’ claim does not seem to adequately capture and explain our experience of virtual objects, and it is in regard to this aspect that his argument does not seem to hold up. When I dodge, say, a storm trooper’s blaster fire headed my way, I am not responding to the data structure or digital object but the perceptual visual and spatial properties of the virtual object the digital object manifests. 

\subsection{Virtual Blueness vs. Digital Blueness}

An adept and experienced programmer fluent in the coding language may be able to look at the segment of code and understand that the object will be a lightsaber with such and such properties, but the experience of the virtually rendered object is in no way identical with the experience of observing the digital object on which it depends. Just because a virtual object covaries with\footnote{For you cannot make a change in the virtual object without making a change in the digital object on which it depends.} and depends on the digital object does not necessitate that the two objects—and, especially, our experience of them—are identical. Even though the properties of the virtual object are related to the properties of the digital object, the virtual object has properties that the digital object does not: namely, the perceptible properties the user experiences when encountering a virtual thing. You cannot hear the crackling sound of the lightsaber as it moves through the air or see the slight halo of light that radiates from it like heat waves coming off a hot sidewalk by merely examining the digital object on which it depends. You must experience the virtual object in and of itself. 

The fact that my virtual lightsaber is virtually blue is dependent on a certain value input in the underlying data structure which generates it, but that data structure or digital object is not blue in any perceptual sense; for the only thing perceived when observing the actual digital object (code) is a series of numbers, letters, and other various signs. At most one might, as Chalmers does, argue that the data structure of the blue lightsaber is \textit{functionally} blue in that it produces or causes a visual experience of the virtual lightsaber as being blue (\cite{chalmers2017} 321). Thus, we can think about the specific input value or segment of code which \textit{functionally} produces the experience of blueness as a property of the digital object: digital blueness. Because Chalmers’ argument from causal power leads him to identify the virtual object as the digital object which constitutes it, he seems to suggest that the digital object’s property of digital blueness functions as a causal explanation for our experience of the virtual lightsaber’s virtual blueness. But to do so is to conflate two different kinds of properties of two different kinds of objects: the perceptible visual property of the lightsaber’s virtual blueness with the specific value input or digital property (digital blueness) of a digital object that generates or causes that perceptible property. 

In regard to our perceptual experience of a virtual object, Chalmers invokes an argument from perception to support his claim.

\begin{adjustwidth}{1in}{0in}
(1) When using virtual reality, we perceive (only) virtual objects. 

\noindent(2) The objects we perceive are the causal basis of our perceptual experiences.

\noindent(3) When using virtual reality, the causal bases of our perceptual experiences are digital objects. 

\noindent–—

\noindent(4) Virtual objects are digital objects. (\cite{chalmers2017} 318)
\end{adjustwidth}

While I agree with his first and second premise, his third premise, though, is problematic. As he continues, a virtual object is a digital object because “many people can see the same digital object by experiencing virtual reality with different displays, because that digital object is the causal basis for all the images” (\cite{chalmers2017} 319). 

However, to use his own words, “a moment's reflection suggests that this cannot be right” (\cite{chalmers2017} 318-19). 

Let us consider if the same digital object (\textit{d}), the data structure of our virtual lightsaber, is mediated through two different HMDs. One of them is working normally and projects the virtual lightsaber in a particular shade of virtual blue (\textit{b1}), while the other has a slight defect or perhaps is a different brand, causing the virtual blueness of the lightsaber to be projected in a different shade of virtual blue (\textit{b2}). If the blueness of the lightsaber is a functional property of a digital object, this implies that there is a relation between inputs and outputs: the same input will always produce the same output. However, in this example the same input produces two different outputs: \textit{\( d \to b_1 \land b_2 \)}. So, while we may have the same kind of digital object we have two different instances: \textit{b1} and \textit{b2}; and, if the objects we perceive are the causal basis of our perceptual experiences, then, in this case, there are two distinct perceptual experiences, similar though they may be. Because we have, ostensibly, two distinct perceptual experiences of the same digital object suggests that the digital object is not the causal basis of our perceptual experience. The only thing left, therefore, that can occupy such a causal role is the virtual object.

Because, as I mentioned above, our actual experience of the digital object’s digital blueness (code or data structure) and the virtual object’s virtual blueness (our virtual blue lightsaber) is so drastically different, I believe it is more useful to take a naïve realist stance which suggests that the blueness of the virtual lightsaber is an intrinsic feature or property of the virtual object while the digital object’s digital blueness is a functional property. Therefore:

\begin{adjustwidth}{1in}{0in}(1) When using virtual reality, we perceive (only) virtual objects.

\noindent(2) The object we perceive is the causal basis of our perceptual experiences.

\noindent(3) When using virtual reality, the causal bases of our perceptual experiences are virtual objects

\noindent(4) Digital objects are the causal basis of the virtual objects we perceive

\noindent–—

\noindent(5) Virtual objects are not Digital Objects.
\end{adjustwidth}

\noindent If this is the case, then it follows that we have two entirely different kinds of properties of two different kinds of objects both of which have their own causal power: the digital object’s property of digital blueness functions to cause the veridical blueness of the virtual object that causally explains our experience of the blueness of the virtual lightsaber. Therefore, the relation between the virtual object and our perceptual experience, and the relation between the virtual object and the digital object on which it ontologically depends is \textit{causal} or \textit{explanatory}. 

\subsection{What Is The Difference Between A Digital Event And A Virtual Event?}

In his attempt to explain why virtual objects are as real as non-virtual objects and how virtual objects exert causal powers which affect users, Chalmers grounds his explanation in the identity claim that virtual objects are digital objects. In so doing, Chalmers seems to be working to maintain what Kim calls ``\textit{the principle of explanatory exclusion}": there can only be one complete explanation of any individual event (\cite{kim1988} 233). In a sense, when first thinking about a virtual event we are presented with two potential causes for the perceptual event, say, when we first activate the virtual lightsaber and experience its virtual blueness: either the virtual object and its perceptible properties or the digital object on which it depends and generates such. 

As Kim notes, there are six possibilities when we seemingly have two causal explanations—the digital object and the virtual object—for the occurrence of one event—my perceptual experience of the lightsaber’s virtual blueness.\footnote{For more on the six possibilities, see \cite{kim1988} 233-35.} Chalmers adopts the stance that the two causal explanations are identical; I, on the other hand, see two different events with two different causal explanations. One of the upsides to Kim’s explanatory realism is that it helps individuate explanations: “explanations are individuated in terms of the events related by the explanatory relation (the causal relation, for explanations of events)” (\cite{kim1988} 233). Thus, in the moment I experience the event, the virtual blueness of the virtual lightsaber is explained by—or stands in causal relation to—the veridical, perceptible property of the visual object; and, the virtual blueness of the virtual lightsaber is explained by—or stands in causal relation to—the property of digital blueness of the digital object. In other words, when I turn on the virtual lightsaber and experience its virtual blueness two events are taking place almost simultaneously. (1) A digital event: the computer reads and activates the digital object (code or data structure) and digital property (digital blueness) which causes the corresponding virtual object (virtual lightsaber) and virtual property (virtual blueness) to perceptibly appear mediated through the HMD; and (2) the virtual object’s veridical blueness causally explains my perceptual experience of the lightsaber’s virtual blueness. 

\subsection{Virtual Objects, Properties, and Events}

My distinction between a virtual object and the digital object on which it depends does not invalidate Chalmers’ argument that virtual objects are real, but it does, I believe, offer us a more useful philosophical conception of such by allowing us to more fully pin down a virtual object’s causal powers which affect the user. Accordingly, I would define a virtual object as the virtually rendered output of a digital object; a virtual property, then, is a perceptible property that is the virtually rendered output of a digital property; and, lastly, a virtual event is the perceptual experience of a virtually rendered digital event. Furthermore, this distinction is useful because it allows us to clarify and individuate between two different explanations for two different events. \section{What is a Phantasmatic Thing}

We must now consider other types of virtual artifacts that are revealed in a particularly capacity of the human animal: namely, Aristotle's conception of \textit{phantasia}. 

While this concept will be addressed in greater detail later on, it is necessary to discuss it briefly here as it will help lay the metaphysical grounds needed to get at the nuances between virtual, non-virtual, and, what I will call, \textit{phantasmatic} things. \hl{(Differentiate between \textit{phantasmic} and \textit{phantasmatic} linguistically)}. 


\subsection{On What Does a Phantasmatic Thing Ontologically Depend?}

To reiterate Dorthea Frede's understanding, \textit{phantasia}, as appearance, is the faculty which enables us to experience an appearance, the continuing appearance in and of itself, and ``what appears" (\cite{frede1992cognitive} 279). In this sense, I believe vivid mental images generated externally through \textit{ekphrasis} or internally through conscious recollection or imagination can also be considered as virtual things.

Aristotle states that \textit{phantasia} ``does not exist apart from sense perception" (\cite{aristotle2014soul} 428b, 431a). It is initiated when perception occurs, and it continues to function even when direct sensory input is absent. This is why \textit{phantasia} enables one to recall, reconfigure, or anticipate things not currently being perceived. Aristotle further describes \textit{phantasia} as ``some kind of movement" produced by sensory activity (\cite{aristotle2014soul} 428b, 431a). This implies that sensory input generates mental images which persist in the mind even after the external stimulus disappears. In this sense, \textit{phantasia} serves as a bridge between perception and cognition. He distinguishes \textit{phantasia} from both thought (\textit{nous}) and perception, emphasizing that it is fallible: ``\textit{Phantasia} is an affection [\textit{pathos}] which lies in our power whenever we choose" (\cite{aristotle2014soul} 427b). This suggests that--unlike direct perception, which is always true in its apprehension of its proper objects--\textit{phantasia} can produce false or distorted images based on prior sensory experiences. Logically, then, \textit{phantasia} does not function independently of perception, but instead relies on perception as its ontological ground. In other words, in order to recall an apple we must have \textit{already} experienced an apple either directly or conceptually and have that experience embedded in the mind as a discrete chunk of data. Without this chunk of data, it would not be possible to recall an apple. 
This residual trace of a perceptible object, which I will refer to as a \textit{phantasmatic thing} or \textit{object}, \hl{is the discrete residual trace of the apple that exists latently, or \textit{potentially}} within the mind and serves as the ontological ground for the recollected apple. If that \textit{phantasmatic object} did not exist, it would be impossible for the recollected apple to exist. Or, if a person suffers traumatic brain injury and falls into a persistent vegetative state, despite being alive and still possibly containing the necessary \textit{phantasmatic} object in their mind, they will be unable to exercise the cognitive processes needed for active recollection. Lastly, the \textit{phantasmic} apple only exists when the person is actively recalling or imagining it, whereas the \textit{phantasmatic} apple exists \hl{as a potentiality} regardless of whether the person is actualizing it. In other words, the \textit{phantasmatic} object only depends on the brain of the person where it is stored and which \hl{actualizes} and renders it as a perceptible object, whereas the \textit{phantasmic} object ontologically depends on a particular \textit{phantasmatic} object and the brain of the person that stores, renders, and mediates the perceptible experience. 

In fact, I believe there are clear similarities between virtual and \textit{phantasmic} things. To reiterate, within the context of a digitally rendered object, entity, or event, Do we not feel before we form an opinion? or, can not our emotion influence opinion formation? \textit{virtual} refers to the final rendered output of the digital object. Thus, ontologically, the virtual object depends on the digital object which is grounded in computational processes which are themselves grounded in physical processes on a computer. Analogously, \textit{phantasmic} things are mental objects (the ``perceptible" image) that depend on prior perceptual traces (\textit{phantasmatic} things) which are grounded in \textit{phantasmatic} processes (the functions of \textit{phantasia}) which are themselves grounded in neurological processes in the brain (actual physical processes of the human brain). \hl{Seems like their are several major differences. One could be phantasmatic thing is subject to degradation. The other, so long as the hardware and code do not change, the digital object will always produce the exact same visual results. This is not necessarily the case with the phantasmatic thing.}

Just as our virtual lightsaber in \textit{Trials of Tatooine} depends on lines of code that determine its perceptible properties, a \textit{phantasmic} thing depends on a mental trace that derives from direct perception (\textit{aisthēsis}) which I will call a \textit{phantasmatic} thing. The mental equivalent of a digital object is the stored perceptual trace, the residual imprint of past sensory experience. These traces are not perceptible as discrete objects in and of themselves, \hl{just as a digital object is not perceptibly identical to the virtual object rendered from it. Now, while one might be able to open the file and view the digital code on which a specific virtual object depends, our current technology does not let us actively view residual perceptual traces stored in the brain. Even if one day we are able to see exactly where a specific perceptual trace or \textit{phantasmatic thing} is stored, it will be perceptibly distinct from the \textit{phantasmic thing} that we perceive in our mind. Conceptually, however, I believe we can understand the functioning of \textit{phantasmatic objects} as similar to that of digital objects.} Much like how a virtual object only exists when computational processes actively read and project the digital object as a rendered image, a \textit{phantasmic thing} is only experienced when the mind actively \textit{renders} it through \textit{phantasia}. The mediation of a \textit{phantasmic thing} occurs through the mind's cognitive faculties rather than an external technological device like an HMD. However, similar to VR, consciousness serves as a mediating apparatus that enables the perception of what is imagined, recalled, or projected. Thus, just as a virtual object requires a functional technological infrastructure to appear, a \textit{phantasmic thing} requires a functional perceptual and cognitive \hl{(??phantasmatic??)} infrastructure. The virtual object \textit{depends} on the digital object, and the \textit{phantasmic thing} \textit{depends} on the residual perceptual trace, the \textit{phantasmatic thing}. It is crucial to note that the perceptual trace can exist without being actively rendered into a \textit{phantasmic thing}: even when one is not consciously recalling a memory, the perceptual trace that allows for recall remains embedded in the mind. This is akin to a digital object being stored in a computer's memory even when the virtual object it generates is not being actively rendered. \hl{Unlike a digital object, which is stored externally and remains relatively stable, perceptual traces are stored in an embodied, mutable form within \textit{Dasein's} lived experience.} \textit{Phantasmic things} are, therefore, ontologically dependent entities: they require a ground (perceptual traces pr \textit{phantasmatic things}), an active rendering process and a mediating apparatus (\textit{phantasia}) in order to be and be consciously experienced (the perceptible object that appears within the mind). 

Accordingly, a memory is not a direct imprint of the past but, in the moment it is recalled, a rendered reconstruction (\textit{phantasmic thing}) of past sensory data (\textit{phantasmatic things}). It does not exist independently of the mind's storage and retrieval processes in the same way that a digital object exists even when not being rendered as a virtual object. An imagined object (\textit{phantasmic thing}) could then be seen as a reconfiguration of \textit{phantasmatic things}: a combination of past perceptual experiences into a perceptible composition. Like a virtual object rendered from code, it is an active manifestation that requires \textit{phantasia} for mediation. Similarly, projected hopes or fears into the future are future-oriented renders constructed from previous experiences (perceptual traces) and future expectations. They exist within the mind's capacity for simulation, extending perception beyond the immediate moment into an imagined future. 

Lastly, just as virtual objects possess causal powers that serve as an indication of their reality (they interact with other virtual objects, affect people, and produce real-world outcomes), \textit{phantasmic things}, similarly, are causally operative. For instance, a traumatic memory can affect our emotional responses and decision-making in the future. One can act based on imagined outcomes: a fear of a future wrought by climate change can move someone to act more environmentally responsible in the present. Thus, \textit{phantasmic things} exert real causal force on the subject, making them ontologically significant beyond mere subjective illusions. Just as is the case with virtual objects where we do not experience the data structure itself (digital object) but the rendered virtual object, so too with our experience of \textit{phantasmic things}. We do not engage with the \textit{phantasmatic things} or perceptual traces themselves, but with their reconstructed, rendered forms as \textit{phantasmic things}. The \textit{phantasmic thing} may appear directly within the mind's awareness, but it is mediated by cognitive structures in the same way that a virtual object appears within a VR headset but is mediated by computational structures. \textit{Phantasmic things}, therefore, are fundamentally and experientially distinct from their perceptual traces or \textit{phantasmatic things} on which they ontologically depend.

\textit{Phantasmatic Event vs Phantasmic Event}

A \textit{phantasmic event} is the dynamic process by which a \textit{phantasmic thing} (a memory, an imagined object, or a projected hope/fear) is generated, rendered, and experienced in consciousness. This event unfolds in an ontological hierarchy wherein different layers of reality interact. So, for example, when I am actively recalling turning on the virtual lightsaber for the first time two events are taking place almost simultaneously. (1) A \textit{phantasmatic event}: \textit{phantasia} reads and actualizes the \textit{phantasmatic object} (residual perceptual traces) and \textit{phantasmatic property} (residual \textit{phantasmatic} blueness) which causes the corresponding \textit{phantasmic object} (rendered recollection of lightsaber) and \textit{phantasmic property} (recollected blueness) to perceptibly appear in our consciousness mediated through \textit{phantasia}; and, (2), the \textit{phantasmic thing's} \textit{phantasmic} blueness causally explains my perceptual experience of the recollected lightsaber's blueness. 

A \textit{phantasmic event}, then, is a temporally situated moment in which a latent \textit{phantasmatic thing} (residual perceptual trace) and its \textit{phantasmatic properties} is actualized by \textit{phantasia}, generating a \textit{phantasmic thing}, which is experienced through its \textit{phantasmic properties}, resulting in a cognitive-affective response. In other words, a \textit{phantasmic event} is the perceptible experience of a cognitively rendered \textit{phantasmatic event}. 

\begin{adjustwidth}{1in}{0in}(1) \textit{Phantasmatic Thing} (Ground): The perceptual trace existing latently in the mind that serves as the causal basis for the \textit{phantasmic thing}.

\noindent(2) \textit{Phantasmic Thing} (Rendered Object): The constructed mental representation. 

\noindent(3) \textit{Phantasmatic Properties} (Ground): The specific property of a \textit{phantasmatic thing} that serves as the causal basis for a \textit{phantasmic property}. 

\noindent(4) \textit{Phantasmic Properties} (Perceptible Quality of Rendered Object): The rendered experience of the stored trace, the \textit{phantasmatic property}, actively mediated by \textit{phantasia}. 

\noindent(5) \textit{Phantasmatic Event} (Ground, Structural Process???): Actualization of \textit{phantasmatic thing} and \textit{phantasmatic properties} (latent perceptual traces) that serves as the causal basis for the \textit{phantasmic event}. 

\noindent(5) \textit{Phantasmic Event} (Perceptual Experience): The perceptual experience of the rendered \textit{phantasmatic event}. 
\end{adjustwidth}

\noindent Temporally speaking, a \textit{phantasmic event} unfolds in four distinct phases, corresponding to the actualization, rendering, and experience of the \textit{phantasmic thing}. Phase 1: Actualization, begins when the subject capacity for \textit{phantasia} is engaged and a perceptual trace (\textit{phantasmatic thing} and \textit{properties}) is retrieved from the mind and is analogous to a computer reading the digital object before rendering a virtual object. This process may be intentional (e.g., deliberately recalling a past event) or spontaneous (e.g., a memory being triggered by an external sensory stimuli). Phase 2: Rendering, \textit{phantasia} renders the perceptual trace (\textit{phantasmatic thing} and \textit{properties}) into a perceptible thing (\textit{phantasmic thing} and \textit{properties}). This process may involve recombination, distortion, or augmentation (e.g., imagining a new scenario based on past memories), and the \textit{phantasmic thing} is a rendered mental virtuality. Phase 3: Phenomenological Presence, the \textit{phantasmic thing} enters consciousness, where it is perceived with \textit{phantasmic properties} (e.g., color, shape, emotional tone). The experience is veridical within consciousness even if it does not correspond to present external reality. Phase 4: Causal Effects, the \textit{phantasmic event} influences cognition, emotion, and behavior. For instance, the subject may react emotionally after recalling a nostalgic memory of a deceased loved one, perhaps leading the subject to contacting family members who they may not have spoken to in some time. 

A \textit{phantasmic event}, therefore, is not merely an internal illusion; rather, it possesses real causal power, structuring how an individual engages with the world. like virtual events, \textit{phantasmic events} can alter perception, shape emotional states, and influence future actions and/or how we interpret past events. Thus, a \textit{phantasmic event} operates as mental simulations that actively shape one's understanding of the past and experience of the present and future. 

With this in mind, let us now consider the differences and similarities between a non-virtual thing, a virtual thing, and a \textit{phantasmic} thing. A non-virtual apple exists as a physical object in the ``objective" world. Its continued existence does not depend on anything once it has come to be; it simply \textit{is}--even when one is not perceiving it. A virtual apple, on the other hand, a virtual apple depends on (i) specific lines of code (the digital object), (ii) the physical hardware that store and render that code (a computational device and computational processes), and (iii) the user's mediating device (e.g. a display or HMD) that renders the virtual apple perceptible. The virtual apple only exists for a subject while the computer program is running and actively rendering the apple in a virtual environment. If the computer is turned off, or the program is closed, the virtual apple ceases to exist as a perceptible object (even if the underlying data structure--the digital object--remains in the computer's memory). 

Similar to the virtual apple, the \textit{phantasmic} apple ontologically depends on (i) a prior experience with a real apple in at least some capacity (directly experienced or perceived through other representations) to create the necessary perceptual trace (\textit{phantasmatic object}), (ii) the perceptual trace in an of itself, and (iii) the mind of the person that stores and capacity that renders that perceptual trace (a human mind and \textit{phantasia} as the capacity or process which reconstructs stored traces into \textit{phantasmic things} that is perceptually experienced.) The \textit{phantasmic} apple only exists for the subject who is actively imagining it. If that person were to become suddenly distracted by external stimuli or suffer amnesia and lose the necessary perceptual trace, then the \textit{phantasmic} apple will cease to exist as a perceptible object (even if the underlying perceptual trace of \textit{phantasmatic} object still exist within the mind of the subject). Metaphysically speaking, then, the difference between the virtual, \textit{phantasmic}, and non-virtual apple comes down to a the difference between causal and ontological dependence. A non-virtual apple and the tree it comes from have a causal relationship, but once the apple is formed it remains a physically independent entity. The apple does not need the continuing, real-time existence of the tree to remain an apple. In this sense, the non-virtual apple is not ontologically dependent on its tree, but rather is \textit{causally} derived from them. Whereas the virtual apple and its digital object or the \textit{phantasmic} apple and its \textit{phantasmatic} object have an ontological relationship: neither the virtual apple or \textit{phantasmic} apple \textit{cannot exist} at any point in time without the active presence (and operation) of the digital code \textit{and} hardware or the \textit{phantasmatic} object and the mind of the subject which actively render the perceptual events. Therefore, even if the human experience of the virtual or \textit{phantasmic} apple are identical to the experience of the non-virtual apple, the difference in their hierarchy of being dictates that, why they are all very much \textit{real} entities, they are distinct kinds of entities. Whereas the non-virtual and virtual apples are externally experienced, the \textit{phantasmic} apple exists \textit{internally} within the mind of the thinking subject that perceives it. Another major difference between, specifically, the virtual and \textit{phantasmic} apple is that the \textit{phantasmic} apple requires a perceptual trace or \textit{phantasmatic} object which in turn requires the person to have actually experienced or learned about apples (through direct perception, testimony, or conceptual learning). In other words, the subject would not have that particular apple-memory or apple-image \textit{unless} they had encountered an apple (or the concept of such) at some point in their life. Once their mind encodes the apple as a \textit{phantasmatic object} or memory, the existence of that memory no longer requires the \textit{continuing} existence of the actual apple in the real world. You can remember apples that have long since been eaten or decayed. So, the memory or image is \textit{causally} dependent on the real apple having existed in the past (you needed an encounter or some conceptual input), but it is \textit{not} ontologically dependent on the apple still existing now.

## I. Virtual Objects: The Digital Substrate

The distinction between virtual objects and digital objects is one that I believe to be central to understanding how phantasmatic objects relate to phantasmic ones; for just as virtual objects ontologically depend on digital objects which constitute and causally explain them, phantasmatic objects—the retained kinetic traces impressed upon the soul—similarly depend upon and causally explain the phantasmic images that manifest to consciousness when memory or imagination is activated. The parallel is not accidental, nor is it merely analogical; rather, it reveals a structural homology that operates across both computational and psychic domains, illuminating the soul itself as a kind of rendering medium through which retained content is transformed into experiential presence.

When we speak of virtual objects—a rendered scene in a video game, a simulated environment, a digital avatar—we are confronting entities that possess a peculiar mode of being. A virtual tree in a game world appears green, rustles in simulated wind, casts shadows; yet none of these perceptible properties exist in the machine code itself, which consists only of binary states, data structures, and algorithmic processing operations. An adept and experienced programmer fluent in the coding language may be able to look at the segment of code and understand that the object will be a tree with such and such properties, but the experience of the virtually rendered object is in no way identical with the experience of observing the digital object which constitutes it. Just because a virtual object covaries with and depends on the digital object does not necessitate that the two objects—and, especially, our experience of them—are equivalent or identical.

This raises the question that will orient our entire investigation: How does the transition from digital substrate to experiential presentation occur? And what does this computational architecture reveal about the more ancient problem of how phantasmatic traces in the soul give rise to the phenomenal images that appear to consciousness? I argue that the virtual-to-digital relationship provides an ontological template for understanding the phantasmatic-to-phantasmic relationship; by analyzing how virtual objects emerge from their computational ground, we can illuminate how phantasmatic objects emerge from their psychic ground. Both exhibit what we might term ontological dependence upon a substrate that does not itself possess the properties manifested in experience—the digital substrate does not itself possess the color, texture, or spatial extension that appears in the rendered virtual scene, and similarly, the phantasmatic substrate does not itself possess the phenomenal qualities of the image that appears to consciousness when memory or imagination is activated.

## II. The Rendering Problem: From Data to Experience

Consider a concrete example from contemporary game design which I believe can help us get at the distinction between virtual and digital objects. In *Red Dead Redemption 2* (Rockstar Games, 2018), the player encounters a virtual campfire that exhibits multiple perceptible properties: it emits orange-yellow light that flickers realistically, produces heat that distorts the air above it, generates smoke particles that rise and disperse, and responds dynamically to environmental conditions such that rain extinguishes it and wind alters its shape. None of these properties exist in the game's source code; at the digital level, the campfire consists of numerical values representing position coordinates in three-dimensional space, state variables indicating whether it is lit or extinguished, property arrays specifying light intensity and particle emission rate, and procedural algorithms that calculate lighting effects and simulate particle physics following fluid dynamics equations.

The *rendering problem* is precisely this: how does the transition from abstract computational substrate—data structures and algorithms that possess no perceptible qualities—to concrete experiential presentation occur? The rendered campfire is not identical to its digital substrate; the orange glow is not "in" the hexadecimal color value, the flickering motion is not "in" the procedural noise function, the sense of warmth is not "in" the temperature variable. Yet the campfire is entirely dependent upon these computational structures, possessing no existence apart from them; change the data, and the campfire changes, halt the processing, and the campfire vanishes.

The fact that my virtual campfire is virtually orange is dependent on a certain value input in the underlying data structure which generates it, but that data structure or digital object is not orange in any perceptual sense; for the only thing perceived when observing the actual digital object (code) is a series of numbers, letters, and other various signs. At most one might argue, as David Chalmers does in "The Virtual and the Real," that the data structure of the orange campfire is *functionally* orange in that it produces or causes a visual experience of the virtual campfire as being orange (321). Thus, we can think about the specific input value or segment of code which functionally produces the experience of orange as a property of the digital object: digital orange. This distinction between virtual orange (the perceptible property of the rendered object) and digital orange (the functional property of the data structure) is crucial; for to conflate the two is to conflate two different kinds of properties of two different kinds of objects.

This dependency relationship is ontological, not merely epistemological; the virtual object does not represent some independently existing campfire that could persist without its digital ground, but rather the virtual campfire *is* the manifestation-in-experience of computational processes that do not themselves possess experiential qualities. The rendering pipeline—the sequence of transformations that convert abstract data into pixel arrays on screen through vertex processing, rasterization, texture mapping, shader application, and final frame buffer output—is the ontological bridge between two radically different domains of being: the domain of computational abstraction (symbolic manipulation of binary states) and the domain of perceptual concreteness (phenomenal qualities presented to consciousness).

What makes this rendering possible? I believe we can identify three conditions that must be satisfied. First, *substrate persistence*: the digital substrate must maintain stable states over time, for without this temporal persistence, no coherent object could be rendered, as each frame would present a random configuration unrelated to previous states. Second, *algorithmic transformation*: procedural functions must systematically transform substrate states into output values according to rule-governed operations that are deterministic or pseudo-random following specified probability distributions, such that the same input state reliably produces the same output state. Third, *experiential interface*: the transformed output must be transduced into a medium accessible to perceptual consciousness—photons on screen, sound waves from speakers, haptic vibrations in controllers—for without this final transduction, the computational process remains closed within the machine, never manifesting as experience.

These three conditions—persistence, transformation, interface—constitute what I call the *architecture of rendering*; they describe not merely the technical implementation of virtual objects, but the ontological structure that makes such objects possible. And as we shall see, this same tripartite architecture structures the emergence of phantasmatic objects from their psychic substrate, revealing phantasia as the soul's rendering faculty.

## III. Phantasmatic Objects: The Soul's Substrate

Aristotle's account of phantasia in *De Anima* III.3 provides the foundational framework for understanding what I term phantasmatic objects—the retained perceptual traces that persist in the soul independently of current perception. When Aristotle defines phantasia as "a movement resulting from actual perception" (*De Anima* III.3, 429a1-2), he is articulating an ontological thesis about the conditions that make retained experience possible; the thesis has three components which we can extract from his analysis of perception and imagination.

First, perceptual contact is instantaneous: "Actual sensation is of particulars, while knowledge is of universals, and these are in a sense within the soul itself" (*De Anima* II.5, 417b22-23). Perception (*aisthēsis*) occurs only when the sense organ is in direct contact with the perceptible object; the eye sees the red apple only while light from the apple strikes the retina, and once contact ceases, perception ceases. There is no temporal gap, no persistence beyond the moment of contact; thus perception exhibits what we might call temporal punctuality, bounded by the actual presence of the stimulus.

Second, traces persist after contact: "Phantasia is that in virtue of which an image occurs for us" (*De Anima* III.3, 428a1-2). But what enables this image to occur when the object is no longer present? Aristotle's answer invokes *kinēsis*—motion or change; when the sense organ receives the sensible form, it undergoes a modification that is not instantaneous in cessation but persists as a residual motion after the external cause has been removed. The technical term Aristotle employs is *emphasis*: an imprint, stamp, or impressed trace that remains in the perceptual faculty as a kinetic residue of past sensory encounter.

Third, retained motion constitutes substrate for image: "The soul never thinks without a *phantasma*" (*De Anima* III.7, 431a16-17). The retained motion (*kinēsis*) serves as the material substrate from which the image (*phantasma*) is constructed when memory or imagination is activated; just as the digital substrate—data structures stored in memory—serves as the material from which the virtual object is rendered, so too does the phantasmatic substrate—retained motions impressed upon the soul—serve as the material from which the phenomenal image is rendered when phantasia is activated.

The parallel to virtual objects is now explicit, and I believe it illuminates both phenomena. The phantasmatic substrate—the network of retained kinetic traces impressed upon the soul's faculties—does not itself possess the phenomenal qualities that appear when a *phantasma* manifests to consciousness; the retained motion of "redness" is not itself red, the kinetic trace of "middle C" does not itself sound. Rather, these traces are formal patterns, structural configurations that encode information about past perceptual encounters without replicating their qualitative content, much as digital data structures encode information about virtual objects through abstract symbols without possessing the perceptible properties that will appear when the virtual object is rendered.

Consider Aristotle's famous example of the sun appearing "only a foot in diameter" (*De Insomniis* 2, 460b16-20). When one sees the sun, perception delivers a certain size-appearance; but judgment (*doxa*) contradicts this appearance, for we know the sun is vastly larger than it appears. What persists in phantasia? Not the veridical size (which was never perceived) nor the illusory size (which perception delivers only during direct contact); rather, what persists is a structural relationship: the sun-as-appearing-small, a configuration that retains the form of past perception without manifesting as current phenomenal content until reactivated by memory or imagination. This suggests that the phantasmatic substrate operates through formal encoding rather than qualitative replication—the pattern of retained motions corresponds to the pattern of perceptual relations without replicating the phenomenal presentation, just as digital encoding preserves structural information through abstract symbols that bear no perceptual resemblance to the virtual objects they will generate when rendered.

## IV. The Parallel Rendering: From Trace to Image

If the phantasmatic substrate consists of formal traces rather than experiential qualities, how does the phenomenal *phantasma*—the image that appears to consciousness in memory, dream, or deliberation—emerge from this substrate? The question is precisely analogous to the rendering problem for virtual objects: how does experiential presentation emerge from non-experiential substrate? Aristotle's answer, though never stated systematically, can be reconstructed from passages across *De Anima*, *De Memoria*, and *De Insomniis*; the process involves three stages that parallel the architecture of rendering we identified for virtual objects.

First, *substrate persistence* (*De Memoria* 1, 450a25-32): "Memory belongs to that part of the soul to which imagination belongs; and things are memorable in their own right which are objects of imagination, while things which do not exist without imagination are memorable incidentally." The retained motions must persist across time, maintaining their formal structure despite the passage of temporal duration; without this persistence, no coherent image could be reactivated, as each act of remembering would produce disconnected fragments unrelated to past experience. This is precisely analogous to the persistence of digital data structures in memory, which must maintain stable configurations across processing cycles to enable the rendering of coherent virtual objects.

Second, *algorithmic transformation* (*De Anima* III.3, 429a4-8): When the soul activates phantasia—through voluntary recollection, involuntary memory, or imaginative recombination—the retained traces undergo a process of transformation whereby they are not simply "replayed" like a recording but are processed according to the soul's current state, context, and aims. In deliberative phantasia, traces from different perceptual episodes are combined to form novel configurations (imagining a golden mountain by combining retained impressions of gold and mountain); in recollective memory, traces are sequenced according to temporal or causal associations (remembering one's childhood home triggers associated memories of family, events, emotions). The transformation is rule-governed—not arbitrary—but flexible rather than mechanically deterministic, operating through what we might call psychic algorithms that process retained content according to associative patterns, affective valences, and deliberative needs.

Third, *experiential interface* (*De Anima* III.3, 428b10-17): The transformed patterns must finally manifest as phenomenal presentation—as *to phainomenon*, the appearing that consciousness experiences as image. Aristotle is frustratingly brief on this final step, but the implication is clear: the kinetic substrate cannot remain merely kinetic but must cross the threshold into presentation, into that which shows itself to the perceptive soul. This is analogous to the final transduction in virtual rendering, where computational output must be converted into photons, sound waves, or haptic vibrations that can be perceived by human sensory organs.

This tripartite structure—persistence, transformation, interface—precisely mirrors the architecture of rendering we identified for virtual objects, and I believe this parallel is not superficial but reveals a deep ontological similarity. Both virtual and phantasmatic rendering involve the same fundamental transition: from non-experiential substrate (digital code, kinetic traces) to experiential presentation (rendered scene, phenomenal image) through a process of formal transformation that preserves structural information while generating perceptible qualities that did not exist in the substrate itself.

Consider a concrete example that I believe illustrates this parallel. I remember my grandmother's kitchen from childhood; what persists in my phantasmatic substrate is not a literal visual recording—not pixels or photons captured and stored—but rather a network of formal relationships: spatial configurations (the table was near the window, the stove against the far wall), qualitative associations (warmth, brightness, the smell of baking bread), temporal sequences (entering through the side door, sitting at the table, watching her cook), affective tones (comfort, safety, anticipation). These traces are not themselves experiential—they are kinetic patterns, structural information encoded in the soul's fabric, much as digital data structures encode spatial coordinates, property values, and relational information about virtual objects.

When I voluntarily recall this memory, the traces undergo transformation: they are activated, combined, sequenced according to associative patterns and affective valences; spatial relationships are reconstructed into a quasi-visual schema, qualitative associations are mapped onto this schema, temporal sequences unfold in imagination's time. And finally—through whatever mysterious interface connects substrate to presentation—a phenomenal image appears: I "see" the kitchen, not with eyes but with imagination; I "smell" the bread, not with nose but with memory; I "feel" the warmth, not through skin but through phantasia's presentational power. The image is not identical to past perception—details are vague where perception was sharp, schematic where perception was rich, stable where perception was dynamic—yet the image is not arbitrary either, for it is constrained by and dependent upon the phantasmatic substrate from which it emerges. Change the substrate (through trauma, neurological damage, or temporal decay), and the image changes; destroy the substrate, and no image can manifest. The image is the *rendering* of traces, not their replication, just as the virtual object is the rendering of digital data, not its replication in some perceptible form.

## V. Synthesis: Virtual and Phantasmatic as Ontological Twins

The parallel between virtual and phantasmatic objects is now explicit, and I argue that they exhibit the same ontological structure—what we might call the structure of *dependent being through rendering*. Both manifest the same fundamental pattern: a stored substrate that possesses no experiential qualities undergoes transformation through a mediating faculty (computation, phantasia) to produce an experiential object that appears with perceptible properties but exists only through and during the rendering process.

The schema of ontological dependence can be articulated as follows:

**Virtual Objects**: Digital substrate (binary states, data structures, algorithms possessing computational but not experiential properties) → Rendering process (algorithmic transformation, pixel output, perceptual transduction) → Virtual object (possessing perceptible qualities: color, shape, motion, sound; existing only during processing/display)

**Phantasmatic Objects**: Phantasmatic substrate (kinetic traces, formal patterns possessing structural but not phenomenal properties) → Rendering process (trace activation, formal combination, phenomenal presentation) → Phantasmic image (possessing perceptible qualities: visual, auditory, affective; existing only during psychic activation)

What makes this parallel ontologically significant is not merely structural similarity, but the revelation of a shared principle: *rendering as ontological bridge*. In both cases, we confront the same philosophical problem: how does experiential presentation emerge from non-experiential substrate? The rendering process is not epiphenomenal—it is constitutive of the object's mode of being; the virtual campfire does not exist "behind" or "beneath" its rendered appearance, awaiting discovery, but exists *as* rendered, or not at all. Similarly, the phantasmatic image does not exist as a hidden entity within the substrate, awaiting retrieval, but exists *as* manifested to consciousness through the activation and transformation of traces, or it does not exist at all.

This ontological structure challenges two common misconceptions that I believe have plagued both philosophy of mind and digital ontology. First, the *storage misconception*: it is tempting to think of virtual objects as "stored" in memory (hard drives, RAM, cache) awaiting display, and phantasmatic objects as "stored" in the soul awaiting recall; but this reifies both, treating them as complete entities that merely need to be retrieved rather than as processes that must be actively constituted through rendering operations. What is stored is not the object itself, but the substrate-information from which the object can be rendered—the digital substrate stores data structures, not the rendered scene; the phantasmatic substrate retains kinetic traces, not the phenomenal image. The object itself exists only in and through the rendering process, coming-to-be rather than being-revealed.

Second, the *replication misconception*: it is tempting to think of rendering as merely "displaying" or "presenting" what already exists in complete form, as if the virtual scene pre-exists its rendering and simply needs to be made visible, or as if the phantasmatic image pre-exists its psychic activation and simply needs to be recalled. But rendering is ontologically productive, not merely presentational; it does not reveal a hidden reality but constitutes a manifest reality from formal conditions. The virtual scene does not pre-exist its rendering but comes-to-be through rendering; the phantasmatic image does not pre-exist its psychic activation but manifests through the soul's synthetic operation, combining and transforming retained traces into phenomenal presentation.

If this analysis is correct, then virtual and phantasmatic objects are not merely similar—they are *ontological twins*, instantiating the same structure of dependent being, the same architecture of rendering, the same relationship between formal substrate and experiential manifestation. The difference lies not in their ontological structure, but in their substrate-type: computational (virtual) versus psychic (phantasmatic); yet the logic of their being is identical, revealing that both computational systems and psychic faculties operate according to the same fundamental principle of rendering-based ontology.

This synthesis enables us to deploy terminology from digital ontology to clarify phantasmatic ontology, and vice versa; we can speak of the soul's "rendering pipeline" as the sequence of operations that transform kinetic traces into phenomenal images, we can speak of "data structures" in the soul as formal configurations that encode information about past perception, we can speak of "processing" as the activation and combination of traces. And conversely, we can speak of digital substrates as "impressed" with formal patterns, of algorithms as "activating" potentials, of virtual objects as "manifesting" to perceptual consciousness. The terms are not metaphorical—they describe the same ontological operations in different domains, revealing rendering as a universal structure of dependent being that operates wherever substrate and manifestation are ontologically distinct yet causally connected.

## VI. Implications for Temporal Experience

The recognition that virtual and phantasmatic objects share ontological structure has profound implications for understanding temporal experience, for both Aristotle and contemporary philosophy of time struggle with the same problem: how does consciousness apprehend temporal extension when perception itself is confined to the present instant? Aristotle formulates the problem explicitly in *Physics* IV.11: "It is also worth considering how time can be related to the soul, and why time is thought to be in everything... One might raise the puzzle of whether, if soul did not exist, time would exist or not" (*Physics* IV.11, 218b21-219a1). If perception delivers only the present moment, and the present moment is extensionless—a mathematical point, not a duration—then how do we experience the *passage* of time, the succession of events, the distinction between before and after?

The answer, I argue, lies in phantasia's function as temporal medium. Temporal experience requires the simultaneous presence to consciousness of what-has-been and what-is-now; but past and present cannot be simultaneously present to perception, which is confined to the instantaneous and ceases when sensory contact ceases. They can, however, be simultaneously present to phantasia, which retains traces of past perception while receiving new impressions from ongoing perception, thereby creating what we might call the *extended present*—a temporal field in which retained past and incoming present are held together in consciousness. As Aristotle writes in *De Memoria*: "Memory is of the past... but there is no perception of the past, but only of the present" (*De Memoria* 1, 449b15-17); memory is possible only because phantasia bridges the temporal gap, holding past-as-retained alongside present-as-perceived.

This is precisely the function of rendering in virtual environments, and I believe the parallel illuminates both phenomena. A game engine cannot display temporal succession directly—each frame is a static configuration, a frozen instant captured at a single moment of computational processing; yet the player experiences smooth motion, continuous change, unfolding narrative rather than a flickering succession of isolated instants. How? Through the rendering of sequential frames that persist in perceptual consciousness just long enough to overlap with subsequent frames, creating the *impression* of continuous motion from discrete static states. The frame rate—typically 30-60 frames per second—is calibrated to the temporal threshold of human perception, ensuring that the discreteness of computational processing is concealed beneath the phenomenal continuity of experienced motion; if the frame rate drops too low, the illusion breaks down and the discrete character of rendering becomes perceptible as stuttering or lag.

The parallel to phantasia is exact, revealing what I call the soul's *frame rate*: the speed at which it processes and integrates perceptual input with retained traces determines the temporal grain of experience. Too slow, and experience fragments into disconnected instants; too fast, and distinctions blur into undifferentiated flux; the soul's temporal processing must operate at a rate that maintains both continuity (past and present integrated into extended temporal field) and distinctness (past recognized as past, not confused with present). Accordingly, we can articulate a tripartite temporal schema that applies equally to virtual and phantasmatic experience: (1) *Substrate persistence*—digital memory states and phantasmatic kinetic traces maintain stable configurations that persist through temporal duration, providing the material from which successive moments can be rendered; (2) *Sequential processing*—rendering pipeline cycles through successive states, and the soul processes successive perceptual inputs integrated with retained traces, creating the flow of temporal experience from discrete computational or psychic operations; (3) *Phenomenal continuity*—discrete computational frames appear as continuous motion, and discrete perceptual moments appear as unfolding temporal experience, because the rendering process produces overlapping presentations that conceal their discrete origins.

This schema reveals the soul as operating according to an *algorithmic logic of temporal synthesis*, processing, storing, retrieving, and rendering experiential content through operations that parallel the architecture of digital rendering. The soul is not a passive receptacle that merely records perceptual input, nor a mysterious substance that spontaneously generates images *ex nihilo*; rather, the soul is a computational medium—in the ontological sense, not the reductive mechanistic sense—that transforms retained substrate into experiential presentation through rule-governed operations that constitute temporal experience itself.

The implications extend to affective experience, for emotions, as Aristotle argues throughout the *Rhetoric* and *De Anima*, are not instantaneous responses to present stimuli but temporally extended modifications of the soul that involve both retained past (memory of injury grounds anger) and projected future (anticipation of harm grounds fear). Emotion requires phantasia because emotion requires temporal synthesis: the holding-together of what-has-been, what-is, and what-may-come in a unified field of affective orientation. When the rhetorician activates fear in the audience by depicting future catastrophe, when the epideictic orator evokes pride by recalling past glory, when the deliberative speaker generates hope by projecting future prosperity—in each case, the rhetorical effect depends upon phantasia's capacity to render past, present, and future as simultaneous objects of consciousness, creating the temporal field within which affective judgment can occur.

## VII. Conclusion: The Soul's Computational Medium

We began by asking how virtual objects emerge from computational substrates that do not themselves possess the perceptible properties manifested in rendered experience; through analysis of the rendering problem, I established that virtual objects depend upon a tripartite architecture consisting of substrate persistence, algorithmic transformation, and experiential interface. We then turned to phantasmatic objects, and I argued that Aristotle's account of phantasia describes an identical structure: kinetic traces persist in the soul, undergo formal transformation through memory or imagination, and manifest as phenomenal images to consciousness. The synthesis revealed virtual and phantasmatic objects as ontological twins—different substrate-types instantiating the same logical structure of dependent being through rendering.

The dialectical progression can be summarized as follows: *Thesis*—Virtual objects exhibit dependent being through rendering, whereby non-experiential computational substrate is transformed into experiential presentation through algorithmic processing; *Antithesis*—Phantasmatic objects exhibit dependent being through psychic rendering, whereby non-phenomenal kinetic traces are transformed into phenomenal images through phantasia's synthetic operations; *Synthesis*—Virtual and phantasmatic objects instantiate the same ontological structure, revealing the soul as a computational medium that processes, retains, and renders experiential content according to an algorithmic logic of temporal synthesis.

What emerges from this analysis is a conception of the soul as *temporal rendering engine*—the living medium through which temporal experience is synthesized from disparate elements: past traces retained, present perceptions received, future possibilities projected. The soul renders time itself, not in the sense of creating temporal passage *ex nihilo*, but in the sense of making temporal passage experientially manifest through the integration of retention, presence, and protention into what phenomenology calls the *extended present* but which I argue is more accurately understood as the *rendered temporal field*.

This account has several advantages over both ancient and contemporary alternatives. Against Platonic dualism, the soul need not be conceived as a separate immaterial substance but can be understood as the formal organization of material processes—just as software is not a separate substance from hardware but the formal configuration through which hardware performs computational operations. Against reductive materialism, the soul cannot be eliminated in favor of pure neurophysiology, for just as virtual objects cannot be reduced to binary code (the phenomenal scene is not identical to its digital substrate), phantasmatic images cannot be reduced to neural firing patterns; the rendered object is ontologically distinct from—though entirely dependent upon—its substrate. Against Humean skepticism, the continuity of temporal experience is not a mysterious projection of habit or custom but the necessary consequence of the soul's architecture as a rendering medium that synthesizes discrete elements into phenomenal continuity through algorithmic integration. Against Kantian transcendentalism, temporal synthesis need not be attributed to a transcendental ego operating outside of natural causality but can be understood as immanent to the soul's processing operations, which are themselves natural (though not reducible to mechanistic causation).

The soul, then, is neither supernatural nor eliminable; it is the computational substrate of experience itself—the medium through which information about world, body, and self is processed, retained, transformed, and rendered as phenomenal presentation. Phantasia is the name Aristotle gives to this rendering function, the capacity through which kinetic traces manifest as experiential images, and I believe this conception illuminates not only the structure of human temporality but also the rhetorical forces that seek to manipulate it.

The implications for rhetoric are immediate and, I believe, profound. If persuasion operates through phantasia—as Aristotle repeatedly insists in the *Rhetoric*—then rhetorical discourse must engage the soul's rendering operations; the rhetorician does not merely transmit information or logical arguments but provides inputs to the soul's computational medium, formal patterns that will be integrated with existing traces, transformed according to the soul's current state and context, and rendered as emotionally salient images that orient deliberation and action. Rhetorical invention is thus a kind of *substrate programming*: the strategic provision of inputs designed to produce specific rendered outputs when processed through the audience's phantasia, much as a game designer provides digital inputs (data structures, algorithms) designed to produce specific virtual outputs (rendered scenes, interactive experiences) when processed through the computational medium.

But this raises further questions that will guide our subsequent analysis: What formal structures are most effective for engaging the soul's rendering operations? How do different types of discourse (forensic, deliberative, epideictic) modulate phantasia differently? And how does the temporal architecture of the soul constrain the possibilities of persuasive intervention? These questions point toward the need for a rhetorical theory grounded in the ontology of rendering, one that understands discourse not as the transmission of content but as the programming of psychic substrates to generate desired phenomenal outputs.

Whether this computational conception of the soul can fully account for the richness of human experience—particularly self-awareness, intentionality, and the qualitative character of phenomenal consciousness—remains an open question that I do not claim to have fully resolved. But as a framework for understanding how phantasmatic objects emerge from psychic substrates through formal rendering operations, it offers what I believe to be a powerful synthesis of ancient insight and contemporary ontology, revealing the soul as both temporal medium and rendering engine for experiential presentation, and illuminating the structural homology between computational and psychic processes that makes virtual and phantasmatic objects ontological twins in the most profound sense.
