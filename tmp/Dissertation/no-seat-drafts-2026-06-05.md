It is tempting---and the long tradition of faculty psychology has seldom resisted the temptation---to picture the soul as a federation of powers, each lodged in its own seat: a perceptive faculty here, a desiderative there, an imaginative and a noetic faculty elsewhere, severally housed and severally exercised. Aristotle's own procedure forbids the picture. The powers of soul are not parts that compose a whole, nor organs that occupy distinct regions of it; they are differentiated modes of a single actuality, distinguished in account and in being yet one in substrate, and unified precisely as so many ways of being-moved and being-affected.

That the soul admits of no definition standing over and above its forms Aristotle states in the analogy of figure:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
It is now evident that a single definition can be given of soul only in the same sense as one can be given of figure. For, as in that case there is no figure apart from triangle and those that follow in order, so here there is no soul apart from the forms of soul just enumerated. (\textit{De Anima} II.3, 414b21--23)
\end{adjustwidth}
The point is exact, and easily passed over. There is no \textit{soul} that the nutritive, perceptive, appetitive, locomotive, and noetic forms subsequently divide, as a genus is divided into its species; the forms are not partitions of an antecedent whole but the only items there are to call soul. As triangle, quadrilateral, and the rest exhaust what figure is---no further figure standing behind them---so the forms of soul exhaust what soul is. To ask which seat houses the appetitive as against the perceptive is thus to ask what the analogy refuses: it is to seek a figure behind the triangle.

If the forms are not seats, by what are they told apart? Not by location, but by activity, and by the object upon which the activity bears:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
But if we are to express what each is, viz.\ what the thinking power is, or the perceptive, or the nutritive, we must go farther back and first give an account of thinking or perceiving; for activities and actions are prior in definition to potentialities. If so, and if, still prior to them, we should have reflected on their correlative objects, then for the same reason we must first determine about them, i.e.\ about food and the objects of perception and thought. (\textit{De Anima} II.4, 415a16--22)
\end{adjustwidth}
A power is what it is through its activity, and an activity through its object. The perceptive is not a thing that, among its other properties, happens also to perceive; it is the capacity-to-be-actualized-by-the-perceptible, and it possesses no content apart from that relation. The appetitive is the capacity-to-be-moved-by-the-apparent-good; the nutritive, the capacity-to-be-altered-by, and to assimilate, nourishment. Each form is constituted by a determinate manner of standing open to a determinate range of objects---a determinate way of being-approached, and of answering---so that to enumerate the forms is already to enumerate so many modes of being-affected and acting, and not so many tenants of a single house.

This is not to dissolve the forms into bare potentialities. The soul is no portion of the body's matter but its form, and form is actuality:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
Now matter is potentiality, form actuality; and actuality is of two kinds, one as e.g.\ knowledge, the other as e.g.\ reflecting. (\textit{De Anima} II.1, 412a9--11)
\end{adjustwidth}
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
[T]he soul must be a substance in the sense of the form of a natural body having life potentially within it\ldots\ Now there are two kinds of actuality corresponding to knowledge and to reflecting. It is obvious that the soul is an actuality like knowledge; for both sleeping and waking presuppose the existence of soul, and of these waking corresponds to reflecting, sleeping to knowledge possessed but not employed. (\textit{De Anima} II.1, 412a19--26)
\end{adjustwidth}
The distinction between the two actualities is what permits the forms to be called, without contradiction, both \textit{forms} and \textit{powers}. Each form of soul is a \textit{first} actuality---a standing, realized condition, like knowledge possessed by one who sleeps---which grounds a corresponding \textit{second} actuality, the operation itself, like the knowing of one awake. Sight is the first actuality of the eye; seeing, its second. The perceptive form is therefore not potential in the sense in which matter is potential; it is an achieved condition of the living body, ordered toward an exercise it does not yet perform. The faculties belong, that is, on the side of actuality even as they are named for the operations they enable---which is why Aristotle may call them \textit{dynameis}, powers, without thereby consigning them to the merely potential.

What holds these first actualities together is no container but a single causal office. The soul, Aristotle says, is at once ``the source of movement,'' ``the end,'' and ``the essence of the whole living body'' (\textit{De Anima} II.4, 415b9--12): not one thing among the body's things, but that in virtue of which the body lives, moves, and is for the sake of anything whatever. And the office is two-poled from the outset. For \textit{dynamis} itself, as the \textit{Metaphysics} has it, is not bare receptivity but names at once a source of change in another and the capacity to be changed by another (\textit{Metaphysics} V.12, 1019a15--23)---the active and the receptive belonging to one field, not to two unrelated kinds. The forms of soul are accordingly not unified by passivity alone, as though the soul were merely that in us which suffers the world. They are arrayed along a single polarity of \textit{paschein} and \textit{poiein}, of undergoing and bringing-forth: the perceptive and the receptive intellect toward the receptive pole, affected by sensibles and intelligibles; the appetitive between, affected by the apparent good and \textit{therein} become pursuit or avoidance; the nutritive receiving nourishment and \textit{producing} growth; the locomotive moved by the foregoing and \textit{moving} the body in place; and, at the productive extreme, the generative power and the making intellect.

Nowhere is the polarity sharper, nor its unity more instructive, than in the intellect, where Aristotle sets both poles within a single process of actualization:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
Since in every class of things, as in nature as a whole, we find two factors involved, a matter which is potentially all the particulars included in the class, a cause which is productive in the sense that it makes them all\ldots\ these distinct elements must likewise be found within the soul. And in fact thought\ldots\ is what it is by virtue of becoming all things, while there is another which is what it is by virtue of making all things: this is a sort of positive state like light; for in a sense light makes potential colours into actual colours. (\textit{De Anima} III.5, 430a10--16)
\end{adjustwidth}
The receptive intellect \textit{becomes} all things---it is the noetic mode of being-affected, stood toward the intelligible as perception toward the sensible---while the productive intellect \textit{makes} all things, standing to the receptive as light to colour: adding no further object, but first rendering objects intelligible at all. Here the active pole declares its peculiar character: thought in this sense is ``separable, impassible, unmixed, since it is in its essential nature activity'' (\textit{De Anima} III.5, 430a17--18). This is the limit case of the whole structure, and must be named as such: the making intellect is the one form not, like the rest, entangled with body and alteration---the purely \textit{poietic} pole, and for just that reason ``impassible'' and ``separable.'' Yet it does not stand over against the receptive intellect as a second thing. It is, rather, the active condition under which the receptive is possible at all---``without this nothing thinks'' (\textit{De Anima} III.5, 430a25)---so that even at the summit we find not two faculties side by side but the two moments, receptive and productive, of one event of disclosure.

It is this reading of the soul's powers as moments of a single happening, and not as a roster of mental organs, that Heidegger recovers and presses. Thinking, \gk{νοεῖν} [\textit{noeîn}], he observes, is itself a manner of being-reached:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
Just as \gk{αἴσθησις} [\textit{aisthēsis}] is, for the Greeks, a \textit{being approached} by the world---something comes toward---so also is \gk{διανοεῖσθαι} [\textit{dianoeîsthai}, ``to think through''] a \gk{δέχεσθαι} [\textit{déchesthai}, ``to receive''], a ``perceiving''---the world is encountered by me. \gk{Νοεῖν} [\textit{Noeîn}] is, in a certain sense, a \gk{πάθος} [\textit{pathos}], a \textit{being-approached} by the world. (\textit{BCAP} 220)
\end{adjustwidth}
And the receptive and productive poles he reads, with Aristotle, as inseparable:
\begin{adjustwidth}{0.5in}{0in}
The \gk{νοῦς παθητικός} [\textit{nous pathētikós}] is possible only through the \gk{νοῦς ποιητικός} [\textit{nous poiētikós}], through a \gk{νοεῖν} that uncovers the world. The determinations of \gk{ποίησις} [\textit{poíēsis}, ``bringing-forth''] and \gk{πάθησις} [\textit{páthēsis}, ``being-affected''] reach into the genuine center of the Greek consideration of world and living. Therein lies the fact that all understanding of how the Greeks conceived being depends upon how one understands \gk{κίνησις} [\textit{kinēsis}]. (\textit{BCAP} 220--221)
\end{adjustwidth}
The receptive openness Heidegger will name disposedness (\textit{Befindlichkeit}), and the disclosive making, understanding (\textit{Verstehen}); but the sentence that bears most on the present argument is the last. That the soul's powers compose one structure of \textit{poíēsis} and \textit{páthēsis}---of letting-be-manifest and being-affected---is intelligible, he says, only through \textit{kinēsis}, motion; for it is as ways of being-moved and of moving that the powers are at once differentiated and held together. The affective and the productive, he concludes, point ``into the being of being-there as such'' (\textit{BCAP} 265).\footnote{The reading of \gk{νοῦς ποιητικός} as the disclosive, world-opening pole---rather than as a separate substance or as the divine intellect---is Heidegger's, and it bears on the most contested passage in the corpus; I adopt it as the construal most faithful to Aristotle's own coupling of the productive and the receptive within ``the soul'' (\textit{DA} III.5, 430a13), without resting the present argument on the further question of the active intellect's separability or origin.}

Here, then, the principle for which I have been arguing shows itself as more than a feature the soul's activities happen to share. To say that being-in-motion-and-time holds the soul's life together is to say that the soul's powers are not a collection of faculties but the differentiated modes of one being-in-motion: one structure of \textit{dynamis} actualizing itself, along the polarity of \textit{paschein} and \textit{poiein}, in the perceiving, desiring, imagining, and thinking of a living body. \textit{Kinēsis}---itself \textit{energeia atelēs}, the actuality of the potential \textit{qua} potential---is the medium of this differentiation, and time, its number, the measure in which the modes succeed one another, persist, and sediment. The faculties do not first exist and then fall into motion; they \textit{are} the soul's determinate ways of being-moved and being-affected, and they are one because the motion is one. It is on this ground that the chain of actualization traced in what follows must be read. Each of its moments---the actualizing of \textit{aisthēsis}, the residual motion of \textit{phantasia}, the completed cognitive actuality, the \textit{doxastic} taking-as-true---is not the operation of a separate organ passing its product to the next, but one being-affected differentiating itself, under the form of \textit{logos} where \textit{logos} is present, into the modes the analysis will distinguish.

Two consequences of this reckoning bear directly on the \textit{seat} of the emotions, and both tell against the very question. The first is that, strictly, there is no seat. The powers of soul are not organs lodged in distinct regions but differentiated modes of one actuality---there being ``no soul apart from the forms of soul just enumerated,'' as there is no figure apart from triangle and the rest (\textit{De Anima} II.3, 414b21--23)---and they are told apart not by their location but by their activity and its object, ``for activities\ldots\ are prior in definition to potentialities'' (II.4, 415a16--22). The \textit{aisthētikon}, \textit{orektikon}, and \textit{phantastikon} accordingly name not three tenants of the soul, capable severally of this and incapable of that, but three ways one living being is affected and moved---by the perceptible, by the apparent good, by its own retained motion---one in substrate and differing only in being (\textit{De Anima} III.7, 431a13--14). And the structure is two-poled throughout, since \textit{dynamis} is at once the capacity to be affected and the source of change (\textit{Metaphysics} V.12), so that even the intellect holds together a receptive moment that ``becomes all things'' and a productive one that ``makes all things'' (\textit{De Anima} III.5, 430a14--15). To ask whether ``the part'' in which the passions reside is or is not capable of \textit{doxa}---the wedge on which the \textit{phantastic} reading turns---is therefore to ask a malformed question. \textit{Doxa} is no resident of a part; it is the mode this same being-affected takes when it is gathered into \textit{logos}, the articulating moment that belongs to the one structure wherever \textit{nous} is present and is wanting only where, as in the beasts, \textit{logos} is. The passions are not shut out of belief by their lodging in a belief-incapable organ, for there is no such organ: the higher emotion is the orectic mode of the one field articulated through its \textit{doxastic} mode. The second consequence preserves the chain: that these modes are one in substrate does not collapse the actualizations the analysis tracks, for one substrate may be many in being---and it is precisely as differing-in-being that the \textit{aisthētikon} is actualized at $A_1$, the \textit{phantastikon} at $A_2$, and the \textit{doxa}-bearing rational mode at $A_3$.
