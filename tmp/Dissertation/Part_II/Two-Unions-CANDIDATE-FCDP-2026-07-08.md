We take the mechanism-vocabulary for the world-facing pole of the union from Calleja's incorporation, which in Calleja's account names ``the absorption of a virtual environment into consciousness, yielding a sense of habitation, which is supported by the systemically upheld embodiment of the player in a single location, as represented by the avatar'', and whose double axis we should keep in view from the start. The virtual environment is absorbed into the player's consciousness, and the player is embodied within the environment through the avatar, so that each side supports the other and neither alone suffices. Calleja states the two-way structure directly — ``incorporation occurs when the game world is present to the player while the player is simultaneously present, via her avatar, to the virtual environment'' — and the world thereby becomes present to the player as a place while the player becomes present to the world as a located body within it. This double axis is the structure the applications will read as world-disclosedness: the union of player and disclosed world, dwelling in a place, the first of the two poles an incorporation can reach. Calleja's word for the achieved state, habitation, may carry a distant echo of dwelling in Heidegger's sense, though we gesture at that echo without building on it. What we build on is the mechanism itself, already integrated into the dissertation's game-analysis methodology, where incorporation gives us the working vocabulary for how a virtual world comes to count as a place the player inhabits.

We take the mechanism-vocabulary for the other-facing pole from Burke's identification, since in Burke's account to identify with another is to become ``both joined and separate, at once a distinct substance and consubstantial with another'', and the formula holds a deliberate tension we should not smooth away. The player who identifies with another remains a distinct substance, a separate locus of interest and act, and yet is joined with that other in a shared substance, so that the two act as one without ceasing to be two. The union does not dissolve the difference it bridges, and the difference does not undo the union. This joined-and-separate structure is what the applications will read as co-disclosedness, the union of player and others, the second of the two poles — an acting-together rather than a mere co-presence, since what consubstantiality names is acts genuinely shared rather than bodies merely adjacent. Burke does not treat this union as something persuasion occasionally achieves; he holds instead that ``A doctrine of consubstantiality, either explicit or implicit, may be necessary to any way of life''. Consubstantiality is a standing condition of living with others at all, and we take it in exactly that register: the mechanism, already integrated into the dissertation's game-analysis methodology, gives us the working vocabulary for how a game's others come to count as a ``we.''

Taken together, these are not, on our account, two unions of different kinds but the two operative poles of one union. World-disclosedness names its world-facing pole, co-disclosedness names its other-facing pole, and the applications work between them, with the paired case studies showing that which pole a work reaches is, we argue, a contingency of design and not of the medium. The medium shapes how a union is approached, but design settles whether it is reached. The virtual world, \textit{Gnomes \& Goblins}, reached world-disclosedness in full while holding co-disclosedness at its threshold: the goblins were disclosed, and they looked, but acting-together was designed out, and the consubstantial ``we'' was withheld. The flat screen consummated the collective ``we'' the headset withheld, for \textit{Red Dead Redemption 2} admits the player to acts genuinely sourced by a collective — the heist, the raid, the rescue — and the ``we'' the virtual world showed only at its edge is there made whole. The deeper pole was reached on the screen, not in the headset; the virtual case is a preview that withholds on purpose, and that, we take it, is the point: the medium sets the route but not the destination, and neither pole holds a monopoly on the end.
