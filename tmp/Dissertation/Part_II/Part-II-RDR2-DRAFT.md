## 2.1 — The Tutorial: A Disposition Built on Rails

Where *Gnomes & Goblins* throws the player into a wordless forest and lets the dwelling accrete from free play, *Red Dead Redemption 2* does the opposite. It opens not on a world to be explored but on a cutscene to be watched, and it keeps the player on a tight rein for hours before the world opens — a long, controlled introduction in which, under the cover of story, the design installs everything the player will later be free to do. The early game is in this sense a *paideia*, a formation of character: not a threshold to be crossed but a disposition (*hexis*, ἕξις) to be built, and built twice over — a skill (*technē*, τέχνη) of horsemanship, gunplay, and tracking, and, underneath it, an ethical disposition of loyalty, belonging, and a morally-scored sense of one's own deeds.

It begins with the player as spectator. The opening cinematic withholds agency entirely — the player watches a blizzard-stranded gang grieve its dead and rally to its leader, named "Arthur" before ever holding the controller — a baseline of pure patiency, the agency the cinematic form withholds, against which the later handover of control will register as a release. The cutscene's work is not embodiment but loading: the catastrophe that drove the gang into the mountains is never shown, only spoken, so that the world's whole prior history is supplied by the player's own image-making (*phantasia*, φαντασία) out of dialogue. The high-phantasia pole of the medium announces itself at the outset — the setting built in the imagination before a single thing is rendered to be touched, the player named before being given a body to inhabit.

Then the leash. When control comes, it comes corralled: the first thing the world does, as the player tries to strike off through the whiteout, is rebuff the stray vector — "I think you're going the wrong way" (clip 2, 00:11) — and funnel the player back onto the trail. The snow is doing covert work here. Because the screen withholds any visible wall, the spoken line *stands in* for the boundary, and a hard corral reads as friendly counsel; the high phantasia-load that the thin white image forces is exactly what lets an invisible leash pass for diegetic help. This is the world's resistance turned to a soft force (*bia*, βία) — pushing back against free movement and bending it to a path — and what it cultivates, beat by beat, is a disposition of *being-led*: obedience to the route, compliance with the corridor. The on-rails stretches that follow only deepen it, scripting the player's position from cover to cover while the controls are taught.

For that is what the rails are for. Held to the path, the player is walked through the entire verb-set of the game — riding, looking, shooting, looting, skinning, fighting — each introduced not as an item on a menu of controls but as a thing the story needs done now, so that the mechanics are naturalized in the doing rather than declared. The hunt makes the method plainest: a companion voices each stage of the act aloud, "get your eye in" and then "Good tension on the string before releasing… Now, Arthur" (clip 4), so that the chain from trained perception to released arrow is learned as a piece of the world's business and not as a lesson. By the time the leash slackens the hand knows the systems; the tutorial has been not offered but *compelled*, the linearity the technical means of ensuring that no one reaches the open country without first having been made competent in it. And because the controls are symbolic — a stick for a stride, a button for a punch — the felt body of all this riding and shooting is supplied by phantasia; the surest sign the supply has taken is the involuntary flinch, the player's real body ducking a threat that exists only as a flat image on a screen.

The constraint is not only pedagogy; it is rhetoric. A disposition of being-led, laid down across hours, is the privative ground against which freedom, when it finally comes, registers *as* freedom: the open range opens onto a player taught to feel its openness as release, the new home-camp received as "perfect" (clip 5, 65:00) because the corridor of the mountains came first. The linear introduction and the open world are thus not two phases but one device — the leash cultivating the want of the open road, the open road answering a want the leash produced. What in the virtual forest was a single wordless throw-in is here a long, deliberate withholding of freedom, calculated so that its eventual granting will be felt.

And all the while, beneath the skill-curriculum, the deeper disposition is being seeded. The cinematic's grief-and-rally installs gang-loyalty as the orienting good before the player has done anything to earn it (clip 1, 04:11–05:01); the camp that follows builds belonging not through goals but through sheer repetition — chores, coffee, the hail-by-name "Morning, Mr. Morgan" — long dialogue-empty minutes whose very triviality is the point, dwelling rather than pursuit, until the campfire is, in a companion's words, "in our skin now" (clip 6c, 02:52). Threaded through it runs a moral overlay the virtual forest had no equivalent for: an externalized honor meter that scores some of the player's deeds and not others, a designed cue to the player's own motive that registers public, witnessed virtue while staying mute over the hardest intimate choices. The thing being cultivated, across all of this, is a desiring, dwelling, morally-scored agent with a felt stake in the gang's world — a self the design spends some six hours building. It builds it, in the end, so that it can be broken: the disposition laid down across this long tutorial is precisely what the later rupture will seize upon, and that rupture will carry ontological force only because the cultivation came first.
