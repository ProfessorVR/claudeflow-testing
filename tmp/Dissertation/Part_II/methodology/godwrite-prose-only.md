# Part II: The Justification Layer — Method, Union, and the Grammar of Motives

## 1. Rhetorical-Ontological Diachronic Analysis: The Mechanism of a Reshaping

The preceding metaphysics chapter discharged the reality-burden: virtual and phantasmic things are real, distinct in kind from the physical things alongside which they appear, and they possess the ontological status of a potency rendered into act. What that chapter deferred — deliberately, because the question required its own methodological apparatus — was the further problem of how such rendered worlds come to work upon, and subsequently reshape, the one who engages them. A virtual world, once granted reality, does not merely sit alongside the player as a spectacle; it solicits perception, orients cognition, and mobilizes desire. The method that answers this deferred question is what we call Rhetorical-Ontological Diachronic Analysis (RODA). RODA reads a stretch of play as the progressive actualization of desire and traces how a designed world drives that actualization toward a substantial union — rhetorical incorporation — that sediments into the player's *hexis*, the settled dispositional character through which one encounters future situations. The method is diachronic because it follows the temporal arc of engagement; it is ontological because it concerns the being-structure of the player-world relation, not merely its psychological intensity; and it is rhetorical because the world that moves the player does so through the persuasive structures — ἦθος, πάθος, λόγος — that Aristotle first identified as the available means of pistis, and that Heidegger subsequently re-read as existential structures of Dasein's Being-with-one-another. Indeed, the rhetorical dimension of RODA draws its warrant from Heidegger's own insistence, articulated in his 1924 lectures, that rhetoric is not a merely instrumental supplement to politics but rather "inside politics" as an ontological position — that is, rhetoric names a fundamental power (*dynamis*) of being-human-with-others (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 121–122). Heidegger's assertion reframes the classical trivium: rhetoric is not the handmaiden of politics, nor a technique appended to an already-constituted subject, but a constitutive disclosure of Being-with, of *koinōnia*. As Daniel Gross observes, reading Heidegger's Marburg lectures, "The determination of Being-with-one-another in politike founds that which rhetoric brings to language" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 45). This founding relation between political Being-with and rhetorical disclosure is what RODA inherits when it treats the virtual world as a rhetorically structured environment: the world does not merely present information to a disengaged subject but constitutes the very situation in which a player's being-with-others — and being-with-the-world — is given form. The mechanism, then, is threefold. First, RODA identifies the *orekton* — the desirable thing, the final cause that the designed world holds out — and reads the entire stretch of play backward from it, since desire, for Aristotle, is always desire-for-something, and the chain of actualization is intelligible only from its end. Second, RODA reads the same stretch forward, tracing how perception gives way to phantasia, phantasia to judgement, and judgement to movement — the stages we formalize as A₀ through A₄ — and identifies the specific persuasive structures (the six channels of involvement, the pentadic ratios) that the world deploys at each stage. Third, RODA attends to the sedimentation that follows: how the completed act, once actualized, does not simply vanish but leaves a dispositional trace, a modification of the player's *hexis*, which subsequently conditions future engagement. Thus the method is not a snapshot but a temporal analysis; it tracks the diachronic reshaping of the player by the world the player inhabits. The rhetorical character of this reshaping is perhaps best understood through Heidegger's retrieval of Aristotle's account of ἦθος. In the *Rhetoric*, Aristotle insists that ἦθος as πίστις must be produced by the speech itself, not by the speaker's prior reputation (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 7). Heidegger re-reads this in existential terms: ἦθος names the settled disposition of Dasein in its everyday being-with, the character that both conditions and is conditioned by rhetorical encounter (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 100–102). RODA takes this insight as methodological ground: the virtual world produces its own ἦθος — its characteristic way of addressing the player, of soliciting trust, of establishing the terms of engagement — and this produced ἦθος works upon the player's own dispositional character, reshaping it through the very act of play. The method, accordingly, does not ask whether a player was "persuaded" in the narrow sense of having adopted a propositional belief, but rather whether and how the player's *hexis* has been modified through sustained rhetorical encounter with the designed world. 

## 2. The Producing Art and the Using Art: What the World Supplies and What the Player Does

RODA inherits two load-bearing features from the ontological analysis conducted in Part I, and it is sufficient here to state them without re-deriving the argument that established them. The first is the causal unity of designing and playing. Aristotle's account of the four causes in the *Physics* insists that all four belong to a single change and cannot be distributed between two agents as though each contributed its own independent causal stream. Designing a virtual world and playing within it are indeed two distinct acts, but they are joined at what Aristotle calls the relation between the producing art and the using art (*Phys.* II.2 194a36–b8): the helmsman does not build the rudder, but the helmsman's use is the end (*telos*) for which the shipwright builds it (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 7). The designer's end-product — the rendered environment with its rules, affordances, spatial configurations, and narrative structures — is thus the player's given means, the material and formal causes of the change that play enacts. What the player does, in turn, supplies the efficient and final causes: the player initiates movement within the world and directs that movement toward the *orekton*, the desirable object that the world has placed before desire. The four causes thus remain unified within a single change — the change that is gameplay — even though two agents are involved, because the producing art and the using art stand in a determinate relation that Aristotle himself specifies. Heidegger's retrieval of this Aristotelian structure deepens it in a crucial respect. In his 1924 lectures, Heidegger reads Aristotle's account of *logos* as going "beyond what is simply given," the beings that are there in the given case, and identifies in *legein* "a basic phenomenon of being-there itself" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 94–96). This going-beyond is precisely what happens when the player takes up the designer's world as material for action: the world as given — its spatial layout, its rule-systems, its narrative prompts — is not merely received but surpassed in the direction of the player's own project. The world supplies the totality of involvements within which the player finds herself already engaged; what the player does is articulate, through action, a specific path through that totality. Indeed, Heidegger's reading of Aristotelian *praxis* and *poiēsis* makes clear that the user's art does not simply consume the producer's product but transforms it: the helmsman's handling of the rudder is not a repetition of the shipwright's crafting but a new actualization, one that brings the rudder's *dynamis* — its capacity-for-steering — into *energeia* (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 94–96). Similarly, the player's engagement with the virtual world is not a repetition of the designer's creative act but a distinct actualization that completes the world by inhabiting it. The second load-bearing feature concerns the union-structure that RODA inherits for describing the player-world relation at its deepest point. Aristotle's formula in *De Anima* III.2 — "one in substrate, different in being" (425b26–27) — characterizes the relation between the sense faculty and its object at the moment of actual perception: when one sees a color, the seeing and the color are one actuality, yet they remain different in being, different in their *logos* (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 30). This structure is not a metaphor but an ontological determination: two things that are distinct in kind become a single actuality without either ceasing to be what it is. RODA inherits this union-structure for the player-world relation: at the moment of full engagement, the player and the virtual world are one in substrate — one actualization, one lived event — yet different in being, since the player has not become virtual and the world has not become physical. Rickert's ambient rhetoric extends this point by insisting that rhetorical being is emergent and systemic; as he observes, the new arrangement "cannot necessarily be predicted from the previous arrangement," so that rhetorical understanding itself alters "how we inhabit and practice rhetoric in the world" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 10). The union of player and world is thus genuinely emergent: it is not deducible from the properties of either party taken separately but arises only in the act of their coming-together, and it transforms the conditions of its own possibility. ¹ Aristotle's example of the helmsman and the shipwright is the canonical illustration of the producing-art/using-art relation. The helmsman prescribes the form (the rudder's shape) to the shipwright, because the helmsman's use is the final cause for which the rudder is produced. 

## 3. Rhetorical Incorporation: The Substantial Union of Player and World

The actualization-of-desire chain that RODA traces — A₀ through A₄ — is read forward, from perception through phantasia to desire and finally to movement, but it is understood backward from the *orekton*, the desirable object that organizes the entire sequence. At A₀, the player perceives the world: spatial forms, other agents, rule-governed affordances present themselves to the senses (and to the senses' virtual analogues). At A₁, phantasia renders these perceptions into images that persist beyond the moment of contact and become available for deliberation. At A₂, deliberation occurs: the player weighs alternatives, calculates means to ends, and forms a judgement about what is to be done. At A₃, desire is actualized in a specific direction — not as a brute impulse but as an *orexis* shaped by the phantasmic image and oriented toward a particular good that the world has disclosed. And at A₄, movement occurs: the player acts within the world, and the world responds, feeding back into perception and initiating the cycle anew. The chain is thus not linear but recursive; each completed cycle modifies the conditions for the next, and the cumulative effect is the progressive sedimentation of a dispositional orientation — a *hexis* — that is neither the player's original character nor the world's original design but something that has emerged from their sustained encounter. The central event of RODA occurs at the transition from A₃ to A₄, where the actualization of desire culminates in what we call a substantial union with two correlative poles. The first pole is the world-pole, which we name *incorporation*: the player is taken into the world, absorbed into its totality of involvements, so thoroughly engaged that the boundary between self and environment ceases to function as a barrier and begins to function as a permeable membrane through which intentional energy flows in both directions. The second pole is the with-others pole, which we name *consubstantiation*: the player enters into a communal "we" with the other agents — human and non-human, present and absent — who populate the world, and this "we" becomes itself a source of action, a locus from which motives can originate and to which responsibility can be attributed. These two poles are held as one event: incorporation into the world is simultaneously consubstantiation with its inhabitants, and consubstantiation with its inhabitants is simultaneously incorporation into the world. The poles are analytically distinguishable but experientially inseparable. We must state plainly that this fusion, grounded in the actualization of desire at A₃→A₄, is the dissertation's own theoretical contribution; neither Calleja's incorporation nor Burke's consubstantiation, taken alone, contains both poles. Calleja supplies the world-pole — the player's absorption into the game environment — but does not account for the communal "we" that arises among agents within that environment. Burke supplies the with-others pole — the consubstantial identification that makes cooperation and division possible — but does not address the specific structure of absorption into a designed virtual world. The synthesis we propose is warranted by the fact that both thinkers converge, independently and from different disciplinary traditions, on the same structural insight: that rhetorical engagement produces a union that is more than mere proximity but less than identity. Burke's own formulation is instructive here: he understands consubstantiality as the condition in which agents who remain distinct in their particular interests nevertheless achieve a shared substance through identification, and it is likely that this formulation echoes, perhaps unconsciously, the Aristotelian structure of "one in substrate, different in being" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 166–168). A crucial guard must be placed around this claim. The substantial union we describe is an experiential convergence, not a change of kind. A wholly incorporated player has not made the virtual non-virtual; the gnomes, goblins, and rendered landscapes of the virtual world remain ontologically distinct from the physical body that sits before the screen. What has changed is the player's mode of being-in-the-world: the player now inhabits the virtual world as a world, engages its affordances as genuine equipment, and encounters its inhabitants as genuine others. The union is real — it is an actualization, not a fiction — but it is real as an experiential event, not as a metaphysical transformation of substances. The player who logs off does not cease to exist; the world that is closed does not cease to be real. What ceases is the specific actuality of their union, though its traces persist in the player's *hexis*, in the dispositions and orientations that the encounter has sedimented. Hence RODA's diachronic character: it tracks not only the event of union but the sedimentation that follows, the way in which the player carries the world forward into subsequent encounters, both virtual and physical. 

## 4. Incorporation, Re-Grounded: Calleja and the Channels of Involvement

The actualization-of-desire chain shows that a designed world engages the player's perception, cognition, and desire — but it gives no vocabulary for *where* within the world's design that engagement happens, nor a test for *whether* the player has been truly taken into the world. Calleja's Player Involvement Model supplies both. In *In-Game: From Immersion to Incorporation*, Calleja identifies six dimensions — or channels — of player involvement: kinesthetic, spatial, shared, narrative, affective, and ludic. Each channel names a distinct site at which the designed world solicits the player's engagement; together, they constitute what we might call the architecture of involvement, the structured totality through which the world addresses the player (Calleja, *In-Game*, pp. 87–89). Players themselves testify to the salience of these channels: spatial involvement, for instance, depends on the player's capacity to internalize the world's geography, and when that capacity fails — when "most of the landscape isn't too different on a continent" — a "sense of disconnection from the spatial dimensions of the game" ensues (Calleja, *In-Game*, pp. 87–89). Similarly, ludic involvement can be disrupted when the game's designers alter the characteristics of an object to make it less powerful — what players call "nerfing" — thereby undermining the player's investment in the rule-system that had previously solicited her engagement (Calleja, *In-Game*, pp. 201–202). The sublation of Calleja's model proceeds in three beats. First, we *preserve* incorporation as the world-pole of RODA's substantial union, and we preserve the six dimensions as designed channels through which the world solicits the player along the actualization-of-desire chain. What the producing art supplies — the material and formal causes of the gameplay change — is distributed across these six channels: spatial design is one mode of formal causation; narrative structure is another; ludic rules constitute still another. Calleja's model thus gives RODA a fine-grained vocabulary for describing what the world supplies at each stage of the chain. Second, we *transform* Calleja's incorporation from a psychological intensification of involvement into an ontological union. Calleja's own account treats incorporation as the culminating degree of involvement: when all six channels are simultaneously active and mutually reinforcing, the player crosses a threshold and becomes incorporated into the game world. But Calleja himself acknowledges that this description remains ultimately metaphorical — incorporation is, in his own framing, "ultimately a metaphor" for a threshold that his experientialist framework cannot fully articulate (Calleja, *In-Game*, pp. 201–202). There is a gap between the phenomenological vocabulary Calleja reaches for — absorption, inhabitation, world-constitution — and the experientialist ground from which he operates. This gap is precisely what our Heideggerian bridge fills. The "absorption of concern in its equipmental world" — Heidegger's formulation for the way Dasein loses itself in the referential totality of the ready-to-hand — provides the ontological structure that Calleja's metaphor gestures toward but cannot supply. Heidegger's *circumspective concern* (*Umsicht*) names precisely the mode of engagement in which the user is so thoroughly absorbed in the totality of involvements that the world ceases to be an object of contemplation and becomes the transparent medium of action. This bridge connects explicitly to each of the preceding subsections. With respect to RODA (Section 1), Heidegger's reading of Aristotelian ἦθος as the settled disposition of Dasein re-reads the *hexis* that RODA tracks not merely as a habit but as a fundamental way of being-in-the-world, one constituted through rhetorical encounter with others; accordingly, the sedimentation that RODA traces is not psychological conditioning but ontological formation (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, pp. 121–122). With respect to the producing-art/using-art relation (Section 2), Heidegger's re-reading of *legein* as going "beyond what is simply given" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 94–96) re-reads the player's taking-up of the designer's world not as mere consumption of a product but as the actualization of a *dynamis* inherent in the world's design — the same going-beyond that transforms the rudder from a shaped piece of wood into a means of steering. With respect to the substantial union (Section 3), Heidegger's retrieval of Aristotle's formula "one in substrate, different in being" grounds the union in a specific ontological structure: the perceiver and the perceived are one actuality in the moment of perception, yet they remain irreducibly distinct in their *logos* (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 30). Calleja's incorporation, re-grounded in this Heideggerian-Aristotelian structure, is no longer a metaphor for intense involvement but an ontological determination of the player-world relation at the moment of full engagement. Third, we *elevate* incorporation into union with the other pole — consubstantiation — by showing that the world-pole and the with-others pole are not merely compatible but mutually constitutive. The player who is incorporated into the world thereby enters into consubstantial relations with the world's other inhabitants; the player who is consubstantiated with others thereby deepens her incorporation into the world they share. The six channels become the loci at which this double union is tested: for each channel, we formulate a criterion (R1 through R7) that specifies what incorporation-as-ontological-union looks like in that dimension and what consubstantiation-as-communal-identification adds to it. Why Calleja over alternatives? The experiential literature on player engagement offers several competing models — flow, presence, transportation, immersion — but none provides the dimensional specificity that RODA requires. Flow (Csikszentmihalyi) identifies a threshold state but says nothing about the structural features of the world that produce it; presence (Lombard and Ditton) attends to the perceptual conditions of mediated experience but lacks a vocabulary for social and narrative involvement; immersion, as Calleja himself demonstrates, conflates spatial, narrative, and psychological senses under a single metaphor. Calleja's model alone offers the six-channel architecture that maps onto RODA's actualization chain and provides testable criteria for the union-event. Furthermore, Calleja's candid acknowledgment that his own ground is insufficient — his admission that incorporation remains "ultimately a metaphor" — makes his model uniquely amenable to the ontological re-grounding we perform (Calleja, *In-Game*, pp. 201–202). Accordingly, it is Calleja's framework, transformed but preserved, that supplies RODA with its diagnostic apparatus. 

## 5. Consubstantiation and the Grammar of Motives: Burke at the Other Pole

Two things that the actualization-of-desire chain and incorporation leave unaccounted for demand a further theoretical resource. The first is a consubstantial bond among several agents — a "we" that can itself *source* an action, a communal identification that is not merely the aggregate of individual desires but a genuine locus of motive and agency. The second is a grammar by which a finished act is given its motive, and the true source of that motive is displaced or concealed. Burke supplies both. Two agents who remain distinct in their particular interests nevertheless achieve a shared substance — a consubstantiality — through the rhetorical process of identification, and this shared substance becomes a genuine locus of action. Burke's formulation is striking for its explicit engagement with Aristotelian substance-theory: he reads Plato's doctrine of Forms as a search for the verb — "to-be-a-house" — as "the universal in which every particular 'being-a-house' would participate" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 166–168). This search for the verbal, active dimension of substance is precisely what Burke's own rhetoric of identification pursues: substance is not a static substrate but a dynamic participation, a shared act that constitutes the agents who perform it. The parallel with the Aristotelian union-structure — "one in substrate, different in being" — is thus more than analogical; Burke is working within the same ontological tradition, even if he transforms it in characteristically dramatistic directions. The sublation of Burke proceeds in the same tripartite structure. First, we *preserve* consubstantiation as the with-others pole of RODA's substantial union. Burke's insight that identification produces a shared substance — a "we" that is more than the sum of its parts — gives RODA the vocabulary it needs for describing how players, non-player characters, and the narrative agents of the virtual world enter into communal relations that generate motives irreducible to any individual agent's desire. The crisis psychology that Burke identifies in *The War of Words* — a psychology "deliberately engineered by political and journalistic hacks" (Burke, *The War of Words*, pp. 89–91) — finds its analogue in the designed world's capacity to engineer shared emotional responses, to mobilize a collective pathos that moves the player-community as a whole. Second, we *transform* consubstantiation from a process of symbolic persuasion into the desire-sourced "we" ratified at A₃ in the actualization chain. In Burke's own account, consubstantiation arises through the symbolic resources of language — through naming, narrative, and the identification of interests. In RODA, by contrast, the "we" arises not (or not only) through symbolic address but through the actualization of shared desire within a common world. When several players pursue the same *orekton* — when they coordinate their actions toward a shared goal within the virtual world — they undergo a convergence of desire that constitutes them as a communal agent prior to and more fundamental than any explicit act of symbolic identification. Burke's consubstantiation, thus transformed, becomes the name for this desire-grounded communal constitution. Furthermore, the suggestion that "the 'desire to kill' a certain person is much more properly analyzable as a desire to transform the principle which that person represents" (Unknown, *Blakesley_Edward_Schiappa_2009_Elements_of_Rhetoric_and_Dramatism*, p. 78) — Burke's own remark, cited by commentators — reveals that desire in Burke's account is always already rhetorical: it is directed not at brute objects but at principles, at the symbolic identifications that give objects their significance within a shared world. Third, we *elevate* consubstantiation into the with-others pole of RODA's substantial union. The "we" that emerges at A₃ is not merely a psychological phenomenon — a feeling of togetherness — but an ontological event: a new locus of agency, a shared substance from which motives can originate and actions can be sourced. This elevation connects Burke's rhetoric to Heidegger's existential analytic through the structure of *Mitsein*: Dasein is always already Being-with, and the communal "we" that consubstantiation produces is not an addition to Dasein's solitary existence but a fulfillment of its constitutive sociality (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 45). The pentad — act, scene, agent, agency, purpose — functions within RODA not as a general method of rhetorical analysis but as a diagnostic for motive-attribution. When a player (or a critic, or a designer) gives an account of why an action was performed within the virtual world, the pentad reveals which term has been foregrounded and which has been suppressed. If the account emphasizes scene ("the world made me do it"), the player's own agency is concealed; if it emphasizes purpose ("I wanted to win"), the world's causal contribution is concealed. The pentad thus functions as what we might call a concealment diagnostic: it reveals not that every account hides its ground — that would be too sweeping a claim — but that every account *selects* among the five terms, and the selection is itself a rhetorical act, an act of identification that produces a particular distribution of motive and responsibility. Burke himself grounds this diagnostic in Aristotle's causal vocabulary, noting that the four causes map onto the pentadic terms (*Grammar* 292), and this mapping provides the bridge between RODA's Aristotelian ontological structure and Burke's dramatistic vocabulary (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 166–168). Hence the convergence between Burke and Calleja — a convergence that is blind in the sense that neither thinker was aware of the other's work, yet remarkably precise in its structural parallels. Calleja tracks the player's progressive absorption into the virtual world through six channels of involvement and names the culminating event "incorporation." Burke tracks the rhetorical production of shared substance through identification and names the culminating event "consubstantiation." Both describe a union that is more than proximity but less than identity; both insist that the union is produced through engagement with a structured environment (a game world, a symbolic order); and both acknowledge that the union transforms the agents who undergo it. The fact that two thinkers, working from different traditions and addressing different objects, converge on the same structural insight is perhaps the strongest evidence that the structure they describe is not an artifact of either theory but a genuine feature of the phenomenon itself. Accordingly, RODA holds incorporation and consubstantiation as the two poles of a single event — the substantial union of player and world at A₃→A₄ — and uses the vocabulary of each to illuminate what the other leaves unsaid. Calleja tells us *where* the union happens (in the six channels); Burke tells us *with whom* and *to what motive-distributing effect*. Together, they give RODA the diagnostic apparatus it needs to trace how a designed world reshapes the one who plays within it — not as a metaphor, not as a psychological intensification, but as an ontological event grounded in the actualization of desire. ---
