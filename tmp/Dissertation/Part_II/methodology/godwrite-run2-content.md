# Rhetorical-Ontological Diachronic Analysis: The Mechanism of a Reshaping

The metaphysical groundwork discharged in Part I established that virtual worlds and the phantasmic entities populating them possess reality as a distinct kind—a potency rendered into act through the player's engagement; that section, however, deferred the question of precisely *how* such rendered worlds come to work upon, and ultimately reshape, the one who engages them. The present section answers that deferred question by introducing the method through which the dissertation traces this transformative process: Rhetorical-Ontological Diachronic Analysis, hereafter RODA. This method reads a given stretch of play as the actualization of desire, tracing how a designed world drives that desire toward a substantial union—what will be termed rhetorical incorporation—which subsequently sediments into the player's *hexis*, the stable disposition that persists beyond any single session. The actualization-of-desire chain that RODA traces is not a sequence of discrete psychological states but rather a unified movement in which perception, cognition, and desire are progressively engaged and transformed by the virtual world's designed structure; indeed, the chain's terminal moment is a fusion of player and world so complete that the player's concern is absorbed into the equipmental structure of the virtual environment, and the player acts *with* others in a consubstantial bond that can itself source action. The justification for this method, and for the conceptual apparatus it employs, requires establishing four things: first, the ontological frame inherited from Part I, which specifies the distinct causal contributions of designer and player; second, the structure of the substantial union that forms the chain's terminus, understood as two correlative poles—incorporation and consubstantiation—of a single event; third, the re-grounding of Gordon Calleja's Player Involvement Model, which supplies the vocabulary for the world-pole and the test for whether incorporation has occurred; fourth, the integration of Kenneth Burke's consubstantiation and pentadic grammar, which supplies the vocabulary for the with-others pole and the diagnostic for detecting displaced motive. Each of these four justificatory movements will be developed in turn, establishing the theoretical warrant for the method and the concepts it deploys.

# The Producing Art and the Using Art: What the World Supplies and What the Player Does

The ontological frame that RODA inherits from Part I rests on two load-bearing features, both derived from Aristotle's causal analysis, which must be stated here to clarify the division of labor between designer and player without re-deriving the entire metaphysical apparatus. The first feature concerns the unity of the four causes within a single change: Aristotle's analysis of the producing art and the using art (*Phys.* II.2 194a36–b8) establishes that the four causes belong to a single change and cannot be split between two agents in such a way that each agent possesses all four causes of their respective acts; rather, designing and playing are two acts, joined at the relation Aristotle articulates between the art that produces an artifact and the art that uses it, such that the designer's end-product becomes the player's given means. Thus, what the world supplies—the material and formal causes of the player's engagement—is precisely what the designer has actualized as the final cause of the design act; what the player does—the efficient and final causes of the engagement—takes the designed world as the material substrate and formal structure within which desire is actualized. This division is not arbitrary but follows necessarily from the structure of production and use: the designer cannot play the game for the player, nor can the player alter the formal structure the designer has instantiated, yet the two acts are not independent, for the player's act takes the designer's product as its necessary condition, and the designer's act is incomplete—remains merely potential—until the player actualizes it through engagement.

The second feature concerns the structure of the union that forms when player and world converge in the actualization of desire: Part I established that the agent–world relation is best understood as "correlative poles of a single actualization," and that the union formula governing this relation is "one in substrate, different in being" (*DA* III.2 425b26–27), the same structure Aristotle uses to describe the actualization of the sensible and the sense. This formula specifies that player and world, while remaining distinct in being—the player does not become the virtual world, nor does the world become the player—nonetheless achieve a unity in substrate such that the actualization of the world's potency to engage and the actualization of the player's potency to be engaged are one and the same event, differing only in account. The significance of this formula for RODA is that it licenses the method's central move: reading the actualization chain backward from the *orekton*, the object of desire, to trace how the world's designed structure drives the player's desire toward a union in which the player's concern is absorbed into the world's equipmental structure, and the player acts with others in a bond that can itself source action; indeed, the union is not a relation *between* two independent entities but the actualization of a potency that belongs to both, each contributing what the other lacks, such that the whole is prior to the parts in the order of actuality, even as the parts remain distinct in the order of being.

# Rhetorical Incorporation: The Substantial Union of Player and World

The actualization-of-desire chain, read forward from the player's initial perception of the virtual world and understood backward from the *orekton* that draws the player's desire, traces a progressive engagement in which perception, cognition, and desire are successively actualized by the world's designed structure; the chain's terminus, designated A₃→A₄, is the central event that RODA seeks to identify and analyze—a substantial union with two correlative poles that must be understood as a single occurrence, not two independent processes that happen to coincide. The world-pole of this union is incorporation, the absorption of the player's concern into the virtual environment such that the player comes to inhabit the world rather than merely perceive a representation of it; the with-others pole is consubstantiation, the formation of a bond among several agents such that a "we" emerges that can itself source an action, and such that the motive for an act may be attributed to this collective rather than to any individual locus. These two poles are correlative in the strict sense: each is intelligible only in relation to the other, for incorporation without consubstantiation would be a solipsistic absorption in which the player's concern is directed toward equipment but never toward being-with-others, while consubstantiation without incorporation would be a social bond lacking the shared equipmental world that makes cooperative action possible; hence, the dissertation treats the fusion of these two poles as one event, grounded in the actualization of desire, which is the method's proper object.

It must be stated plainly that this two-pole structure is the dissertation's own construction, assembled from sources that do not themselves conjoin both poles in a single account: Calleja's incorporation, as will be shown, is developed as the world-pole but does not extend to the consubstantial bond with others; Burke's consubstantiation, conversely, is developed as a rhetorical bond among agents but is not grounded in the absorption of concern in an equipmental world. The dissertation's claim is that these two poles track a single structure, and that the actualization-of-desire chain provides the ontological ground that neither source supplies; indeed, the convergence of Calleja and Burke on the vocabulary of "incorporation" and "consubstantiation," despite their disciplinary distance and mutual unawareness, is evidence that both are describing aspects of the same phenomenon, which RODA renders explicit as the substantial union of player and world. A crucial guard must be placed here against a misreading that would take this union to be a change of kind: incorporation, however complete, does not make the virtual world non-virtual, nor does it make the player a virtual entity; the union is an experiential convergence in which player and world achieve unity in substrate while remaining different in being, such that a wholly incorporated player is one whose concern is absorbed into the world's equipmental structure, not one who has crossed an ontological boundary and become something other than a player.

The actualization chain itself, which will be traced in detail in the application chapters, moves through four transitions: A₀→A₁, in which the player's perception is engaged by the world's designed spatial and kinesthetic structure; A₁→A₂, in which cognition is engaged by the world's ludic and narrative structure, such that the player comes to understand the world's rule-governed affordances and the narrative context that frames action; A₂→A₃, in which desire is engaged by the *orekton*, the good or apparent good that the world presents as the object of pursuit, and the player's affective architecture is oriented toward that object; A₃→A₄, the substantial union in which the player's concern is absorbed into the world (incorporation) and the player acts with others in a consubstantial bond (consubstantiation), such that the two poles converge in a single actualization. This final transition is the chain's terminus and the event that RODA seeks to identify through the union-test criteria developed in the next section; it is also the moment at which the actualization, having reached its completion, begins to sediment into *hexis*, the stable disposition that persists beyond the session and reshapes the player's subsequent engagements, both within the virtual world and in the world beyond it.

# Incorporation, Re-Grounded: Calleja and the Channels of Involvement

The actualization-of-desire chain, as articulated thus far, shows that a designed world engages the player's perception, cognition, and desire in a progressive movement toward substantial union; however, the chain as developed from Aristotelian resources alone gives no vocabulary for specifying *where* that engagement happens—through which designed channels the world acts upon the player—nor does it provide a test for determining *whether* the player has achieved the incorporation that marks the chain's terminus, or has merely engaged the world in a more superficial mode that does not rise to the level of substantial union. Gordon Calleja's Player Involvement Model, developed in *In-Game: From Immersion to Incorporation* (2011), supplies both the vocabulary and the test, and thus warrants integration into RODA's conceptual apparatus; however, this integration requires a sublation in three beats—preserve, transform, elevate—that re-grounds Calleja's incorporation in the dissertation's ontological frame and extends it to the union's second pole, which Calleja does not address.

The preservation moment retains incorporation as the world-pole of the substantial union and adopts Calleja's six dimensions of player involvement as the designed channels through which the world engages the player: the kinesthetic dimension, encompassing "all modes of avatar or game piece control… ranging from learning controls to the fluency of internalized movement"; the spatial dimension, encompassing "engagement with the spatial qualities of a virtual environment… the sense that they are inhabiting a place, rather than merely perceiving a representation of space"; the shared dimension, encompassing "engagement derived from players' awareness of and interaction with other agents… cohabitation, cooperation, and competition"; the narrative dimension, encompassing "the narrative that is scripted into the game and the narrative that is generated from the ongoing interaction"; the affective dimension, encompassing "various forms of emotional engagement… either purposefully designed into the game or precipitated by an individual player's interpretation"; and the ludic dimension, encompassing "players' engagement with the choices made in the game and the repercussions of those choices."[^1] These six dimensions map onto the actualization chain's transitions: kinesthetic and spatial engagement correspond to A₀→A₁, the engagement of perception; ludic and narrative engagement correspond to A₁→A₂, the engagement of cognition; affective and shared engagement correspond to A₂→A₃ and A₃→A₄, the engagement of desire and the formation of the consubstantial bond. Calleja's canonical definition of incorporation—"the absorption of a virtual environment into consciousness, yielding a sense of habitation, which is supported by the systemically upheld embodiment of the player in a single location, as represented by the avatar" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169)—is likewise preserved as the definition of the world-pole, with the crucial specification that incorporation "operates on a double axis: the player incorporates (in the sense of internalizing or assimilating) the game environment into consciousness while simultaneously being incorporated through the avatar into that environment," and "the simultaneous occurrence of these two processes is a necessary condition for the experience of incorporation" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169).

The transformation moment re-grounds incorporation from a psychological intensification of involvement into an ontological union, and it is here that the dissertation makes its most significant interpretive move, one that Calleja himself does not make and that requires honest acknowledgment of provenance. Calleja grounds incorporation in "their experientialist ontology" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 168), citing Lakoff and Johnson's embodied cognition framework, and in "a view of consciousness as an internally generated construct (Dennett, 1991; Damasio, 2000; Lakoff and Johnson, 2003)" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169); this ground treats incorporation as a cognitive-experiential phenomenon, a matter of how consciousness assimilates and organizes sensory input from the virtual environment. The dissertation's re-grounding, by contrast, ties incorporation to the Heideggerian vocabulary of concernful absorption that was established in Part I: the "characteristic absorption of concern in its equipmental world" and "circumspective concern" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, 405), in which equipment withdraws into the activity it enables—the hammer "disappears into the hammering" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, 98)—such that the player's concern is directed not toward the virtual world *as* a representation but toward the equipmental structure *within* the world, the network of tools and affordances that enable action. This re-grounding is the dissertation's construction, not Calleja's; Heidegger is absent from Calleja's incorporation chapter, and the experientialist ground Calleja adopts is incompatible with the phenomenological vocabulary he nonetheless reaches for when describing incorporation's double axis and the sense of habitation it yields. The warrant for this re-grounding is twofold: first, Calleja himself concedes that "incorporation is ultimately a metaphor like presence or immersion…" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 5), acknowledging that his own theoretical ground does not fully capture the phenomenon he is describing; second, the gap between Calleja's experientialist ground and the phenomenological vocabulary he employs—particularly the language of "absorption," "habitation," and "embodiment"—suggests that incorporation requires an ontological account that his cognitive-scientific framework cannot supply, and that the Heideggerian account of concernful absorption provides.

The elevation moment extends incorporation beyond the world-pole to encompass the union's second pole, consubstantiation, which will be developed in the next section; for the present purpose, it suffices to note that Calleja's shared dimension, while it names "engagement derived from players' awareness of and interaction with other agents… cohabitation, cooperation, and competition" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 43), does not theorize the consubstantial bond as such, nor does it provide a grammar for attributing motive to the collective "we" that emerges from such engagement. The elevation of incorporation into a two-pole union thus requires supplementation from Burke, as will be shown; however, the incorporation-pole's own test for whether substantial union has occurred is supplied by Calleja's criteria, which the dissertation designates R1–R7: R1, assimilation of the game environment into consciousness; R2, embodiment of the player in a single location via the avatar; R3, simultaneity of the two processes (the strictest gatekeeper, for incorporation cannot occur if assimilation and embodiment are sequential rather than concurrent); R4, the spatial-kinesthetic cornerstone, for "two particular dimensions—spatial and kinesthetic—form the cornerstone of the incorporation experience," and "without them, incorporation cannot take place" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169–170); R5, blending of player and avatar such that the distinction between "I" and "my avatar" becomes phenomenologically inoperative; R6, temporal apex, the moment at which incorporation reaches its fullest intensity; R7, intrusion-integration, the criterion that distinguishes incorporation from immersion by requiring that the player integrate intrusions from the world beyond the virtual environment rather than being "broken out" of the experience by them.[^2] These seven criteria, applied to a given stretch of play, constitute the test for whether the player has achieved incorporation or has merely engaged the world in a more superficial mode; they are, accordingly, the operational definition of the world-pole of substantial union, and they will be applied in the analysis chapters to identify the A₃→A₄ transition in each case study.
