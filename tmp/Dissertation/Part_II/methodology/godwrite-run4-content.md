# Part II, Chapter [N]: The Justification of the Method

## Rhetorical-Ontological Diachronic Analysis: The Mechanism of a Reshaping

The metaphysical section that precedes this one discharged a single and substantial burden, namely the reality-burden; it argued that the virtual and the phantasmic are not lesser counterfeits of some firmer order of being but are themselves real, distinct in kind, and best understood as a potency rendered into act. That argument, having secured what such rendered worlds *are*, deliberately deferred a second and quite different question: not what the virtual world is, but how it comes to work upon, and subsequently to reshape, the one who engages it.[^1] This deferral was not an evasion; it was a division of labour, for the question of reshaping is not metaphysical but rhetorical-ontological, and it requires a method rather than a further ontology. That method is what this chapter names Rhetorical-Ontological Diachronic Analysis, hereafter RODA.

RODA is best characterised by what it does to a stretch of play. It reads such a stretch—a bounded run of engagement, motion by motion—as the actualization of desire; it traces, across that stretch, how a designed world drives the player toward a substantial union with it; and it shows how that union, far from evaporating when the play concludes, sediments into the player's *hexis* (ἕξις, the settled disposition or state, *NE* 1105b25). Indeed, the central wager of the method is that engagement and reshaping are not two events but one, the latter being the temporal residuum of the former; hence to trace the actualization is already to trace the reshaping.

Three features of this characterisation warrant emphasis at the outset, since each governs a movement to follow. First, RODA is *diachronic*: it does not photograph a state of involvement but follows a motion through time, and it is for this reason that the analysis is read forward, motion by motion, even where it is understood backward from its end. Second, RODA is *ontological* before it is psychological: the union it traces is not, in the first instance, a heightened feeling but a convergence in being, which is why the method must borrow an ontology rather than a vocabulary of affect. Third, RODA is *rhetorical*: the world does not merely present itself to the player but moves the player, and the grammar of such moving—the attribution of an act to its motive, and the displacement of that motive's true source—belongs to rhetoric as classically conceived. Accordingly, the justification that follows proceeds in five movements: the ontological frame inherited from Part I; the actualization chain that forms the method's spine; the re-grounding of incorporation through Calleja at the world-pole; and the re-grounding of consubstantiation through Burke at the with-others pole.

## The Producing Art and the Using Art: What the World Supplies and What the Player Does

Part I derived, at length, the ontological frame on which RODA stands; that derivation will not be repeated here, and only two load-bearing features need be stated, since the whole subsequent argument rests upon them.

The first concerns the unity of the change. It is tempting to suppose that, because a virtual world has a designer and a player, there are therefore two changes—one of making, one of using—each with its own complement of causes. This supposition was rejected in Part I, and rightly so, for the four causes belong to a single change and cannot be parcelled out between two agents. The relation that joins designing and playing is the one Aristotle marks between the producing art and the using art (*Phys.* II.2 194a36–b8): the art that makes the rudder is not the art that uses it, yet the rudder is one thing, and the maker's end-product is precisely the user's given means.[^2] Designing and playing are thus two acts joined at this relation; what follows for RODA is a clean partition of the four causes across the joint. What the world supplies—its matter and its form, the material and formal causes—is the designer's end-product handed over as a given. What the player does—the bringing-about and the for-the-sake-of-which, the efficient and final causes—is the using art exercised upon that given. To *engage* a world, on this account, is for the player's efficient-final activity to take up the world's material-formal supply as its means; the relational verb names neither a mere encounter nor a metaphor but this precise causal handing-over.

The second feature concerns the structure of the union toward which the change tends. Part I appropriated, for this purpose, Aristotle's formula for the unity of the perceiver and the perceived: the activity of the sensible object and the activity of the sense are one and the same activity, though their being is not the same—"one in substrate, different in being" (*DA* III.2 425b26–27). This formula is the union-structure that RODA inherits, and it must be stated with care, since everything in the third movement turns on it. It does not say that perceiver and perceived collapse into a single substance; it says that one actuality is, at once, the actuality of both, while what it *is to be* the one differs from what it is to be the other. The agent and the world, similarly, are "correlative poles of a single actualization" (Part I)—joined in act, separate in being. This is the formula that licenses RODA to speak of a substantial union without thereby committing the absurdity of saying that player and world become numerically one thing.

## Rhetorical Incorporation: The Substantial Union of Player and World

The spine of the method is the actualization-of-desire chain, hereafter the actualization chain. It is read forward, motion by motion, yet it is understood backward from the *orekton* (ὀρεκτόν, the object of desire), which draws the whole toward itself as its terminus and its intelligibility; the later nodes explain the earlier as an end explains a means.[^3]

The chain begins at **A₀**, the ontological horizon: a sensible object and the sense-faculty given together within motion and time—the designed *Umwelt* (the surrounding world) into which the player is already thrown. From A₀ the perceptual motion M₀→A₁ yields **A₁**, completed perception, which deposits a dual residue, the *resonant kinēsis* (the persisting after-motion, *DA* 425b25 ff.): namely the *resonant aisthēma*, the persisting eidetic trace, and the *resonant epithymia*, the persisting hedonic-orectic tone. The phantastic motion M₁→A₂ generates from this residue the **A₂** node, the *phantasma* proper—the determinate, available, charged image, the pivot on which every later operation works. The cognitive motion M₂→A₃ then yields **A₃**, completed cognition: the *phantasma* engaged by the three orientational modes—intellection, memory, and the discursive-deliberative—together with the orthogonal committal *doxa* (δόξα, the taking-as-true) and the concretized emotion. Finally the orektikon motion M₃→A₄ yields **A₄**, completed action, whether *praxis* or *poiēsis*; and this completed action sediments into *hexis*. This cross-pass build-up is *temporal sedimentation*, and it must not be confused with the within-pass residue named above.[^4]

The central event of the chain—rhetorical incorporation proper—occurs at the **A₃→A₄** transition. Here the committed, charged cognition of A₃ passes into action; and it is precisely in this passage that a substantial union is consummated. The union has two correlative poles, held not as two events but as one. The first is the world-pole: in acting into the world, the player is incorporated into it, and it into the player's consciousness—a single actuality, one in substrate, different in being. The second is the with-others pole: the same act is an acting-together, a consubstantiation with one's fellows, a "we" that the act both presupposes and ratifies. These two poles are not successive and not separable; the player who is taken into the world is, in the same motion, taken into a being-with-others within it.

This fusion is the dissertation's own. It must be said plainly that neither source from which the method draws supplies both poles together: one source furnishes the world-pole, the other the with-others pole, and the welding of the two into a single event grounded in the actualization of desire is an interpretive construction for which the author, and not the sources, is answerable.

One guard is indispensable. The union here described is an experiential and ontological convergence in act; it is emphatically *not* a change of kind. The player who has been wholly incorporated into a virtual world has not thereby rendered that world non-virtual, any more than the perceiver who is one-in-substrate with the perceived has turned perception into its object. The reality-burden discharged earlier secured the virtual as a distinct kind; rhetorical incorporation does not undo that distinctness but presupposes it.

## Incorporation, Re-Grounded: Calleja and the Channels of Involvement

The actualization chain demonstrates that a designed world engages the player's perception, cognition, and desire; what it does not supply is a vocabulary for *where* such engagement occurs, nor any test for *whether* the player has in fact been taken into the world rather than merely positioned before it. Calleja's Player Involvement Model supplies both, and it is for this reason that it is appropriated here.

The appropriation proceeds as a sublation in three beats: a preserving, a transforming, and an elevating. What is preserved is incorporation itself, understood as the world-pole, together with the six dimensions of the model as so many designed channels through which a world reaches the player.[^5] Calleja's own definition is preserved verbatim: incorporation is "the absorption of a virtual environment into consciousness, yielding a sense of habitation, which is supported by the systemically upheld embodiment of the player in a single location, as represented by the avatar" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169). Its double-axis is likewise preserved: the player "incorporates… the game environment into consciousness while simultaneously being incorporated through the avatar into that environment," and "the simultaneous occurrence of these two processes is a necessary condition" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169).

What is transformed is the *standing* of incorporation. In Calleja's hands incorporation is a psychological intensification of involvement, grounded in an experientialist cognitive science—"their experientialist ontology" after Lakoff and Johnson, and "a view of consciousness as an internally generated construct" after Dennett and Damasio (Calleja, *In-Game*, 168–169). The present method transforms this into an ontological union, and it does so by tying incorporation to the dissertation's own Heideggerian vocabulary, already established in Part I: "the characteristic absorption of concern in its equipmental world" and "circumspective concern" (BT 405), wherein the equipment withdraws—the hammer "disappears into the hammering" (BT 98). The world-pole of rhetorical incorporation simply *is* this absorption of concern, redescribed for the virtual *Umwelt*.

It must be stated, honestly and without softening, that this Heideggerian re-grounding is the author's construction and not Calleja's. Heidegger is absent from Calleja's incorporation chapter; Calleja reaches instead for cognitive science. Yet the re-grounding is warranted, and warranted by Calleja's own text in two ways. First, Calleja concedes that "incorporation is ultimately a metaphor like presence or immersion" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 5); a metaphor, however illuminating, leaves its ontological cash-value unpaid, and the present method pays it. Second, there is a gap between Calleja's experientialist ground and the phenomenological vocabulary—habitation, inhabiting a place "rather than merely perceiving a representation of space"—that he nonetheless reaches for; the Heideggerian re-grounding does not import a foreign vocabulary so much as supply the ground that the borrowed vocabulary already presupposes.

What is elevated is incorporation's relation to the other pole: no longer the whole of the union but one of its two correlative poles, joined to the with-others pole within a single event. Here the seven criteria R1–R7 are pressed into a new service: they become the union-test that the chain itself could not provide.[^6] R3, the simultaneity criterion, is the strictest gatekeeper, since it is Calleja's own necessary condition; R4, the spatial-kinesthetic cornerstone, marks the dimensions "without [which] incorporation cannot take place" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169–170).

Why Calleja, and not a rival account of immersion or presence? The answer is routed through Calleja's own corrective moves. He excises the magic circle as a clean boundary between the virtual and the actual; this excision is precisely what the reality-burden requires, since a world that is real in kind cannot be sealed behind such a membrane. Moreover, he corrects the unidirectional metaphors of immersion and presence, which figure the player as merely *placed within* a world; his double-axis restores the reciprocity—the world taken into consciousness as much as consciousness taken into the world—that the union-structure "one in substrate, different in being" demands.

## Consubstantiation and the Grammar of Motives: Burke at the Other Pole

Two things the actualization chain and incorporation leave unaccounted for; neither is a residue, in the locked sense of that term, but each is a real lacuna. The first is a consubstantial bond among several agents—a "we" that is not merely an aggregate of players but can itself *source* an action, such that the act issues from the bond rather than from any single locus. The second is a grammar by which a finished act is furnished with its motive, and by which the true source of that motive may be displaced or concealed. Burke supplies both.

The appropriation again proceeds as sublation. What is preserved is consubstantiation: "In being identified with B, A is 'substantially one' with a person other than himself. Yet at the same time he remains unique, an individual locus of motives. Thus he is both joined and separate" (Burke, *Rhetoric*, 21). This "joined and separate" is, strikingly, the very grammar of "one in substrate, different in being," and its act-basis is explicit: "substance… was an act; and a way of life is an acting-together" (Burke, *Rhetoric*, 21). What is transformed is the *engine* of this bond. For Burke, consubstantiation is achieved through symbolic persuasion, and is compensatory: "Identification is affirmed with earnestness precisely because there is division" (Burke, *Rhetoric*, 22). The present method transforms this into a desire-sourced "we"—the bond ratified at A₃, where the committed cognition of co-acting agents constitutes a shared *orekton*. What is elevated is its place: consubstantiation becomes the with-others pole, correlative to incorporation, and folded into what Part I called "the affective architecture of bodily-being-in-the-world-with-others."

The grammar of motives is appropriated on Burke's own warrant. Burke "refers human actions to seven causes (*aitia*): chance, nature, compulsion, habit, reason, anger, and desire" (Burke, *Grammar*, 292), and this licenses the reading of *Rhetorica* I.10 (1369a–b) as a table of motive-categories.[^7] Burke's own explicit map runs from the *four* causes to the pentad; the crosswalk from the *seven* causes to the pentad is the dissertation's construction, built upon Burke's own invocation, and is so declared.

The pentad—Act, Scene, Agent, Agency, Purpose, with Attitude as the sixth term—is framed here precisely as motive-*attribution*: a grammar of how a motive is assigned to a finished act, not a psychology of the drives behind it. "Strictly speaking, we mean by a Grammar of motives a concern with the terms alone" (Burke, *Grammar*, xvi).[^8] To this attaches the concealment diagnostic, which must be framed narrowly: not the blanket claim that every account hides its ground, but the precise one that "in banishing the term, far from banishing its functions one merely conceals them… we may detect its covert influence even in cases where it is overtly absent" (Burke, *Grammar*, 21). The diagnostic detects a *displaced* motive-locus—a source covertly imported under the name of another term—and nothing more sweeping.

There remains a final piece of evidence that the two appropriated sources track one and the same structure. Calleja, grounding incorporation in cognitive science, and Burke, grounding consubstantiation in symbolic action, arrive—each blind to the other, neither citing the other—at the identical grammar of joined-yet-separate: Calleja's reciprocal double-axis and Burke's "both joined and separate." This blind convergence is not proof, but it is strong corroboration: where two independent inquiries, proceeding from incompatible grounds, deposit the same formal structure, it is likely that the structure is in the matter and not in the eye of either inquirer.
