# PART II: METHODOLOGY

## Rhetorical-Ontological Diachronic Analysis: The Mechanism of a Reshaping

The prior section discharged the reality-burden, establishing that virtual and phantasmic things possess genuine reality, distinct in kind from material substances, and that the designed world constitutes a potency rendered into act; it deferred, however, the question of how such rendered worlds come to work upon, and reshape, the one who engages them. The present section answers that deferred question by articulating the method through which the dissertation will trace this mechanism of reshaping: Rhetorical-Ontological Diachronic Analysis, hereafter RODA. The method reads a stretch of play as the actualization-of-desire chain and traces how a designed world drives that actualization toward a substantial union—a rhetorical incorporation—that sediments into the player's *hexis*, the dispositional character that persists beyond the session and shapes subsequent action. RODA thus operates diachronically, following the temporal unfolding of a single actualization from the perceptual horizon through completed action; it operates ontologically, attending to the being-structure of each phase and the transitions that join them; and it operates rhetorically, insofar as the designed world functions as a persuasive environment that solicits, shapes, and completes the player's desire. The chain is read forward, motion by motion, but understood backward from the *orekton*, the object of desire that draws the whole sequence into actuality; the analysis thereby recovers both the mechanism by which the world acts upon the player and the structure of the union that results. This section articulates the method in four movements: first, the ontological frame inherited from Part I; second, the actualization-of-desire chain and the substantial union at its apex; third, the re-grounding of incorporation through Calleja's Player Involvement Model; fourth, the integration of Burke's consubstantiation and pentadic grammar at the with-others pole.

## The Producing Art and the Using Art: What the World Supplies and What the Player Does

The ontological frame that RODA inherits from Part I rests on two load-bearing features, both drawn from the analysis of change and the agent–world relation established in the metaphysics section. First, the four causes belong to a single change and cannot be split between two agents; designing and playing are therefore two acts, joined at the relation Aristotle articulates in *Physics* II.2 (194a36–b8) between the producing art and the using art. The designer's end-product—the virtual world as a structured, rule-governed environment—becomes the player's given means; what the world supplies corresponds to the material and formal causes of the player's action, and what the player does corresponds to the efficient and final causes. The designed world furnishes the matter (the sensible objects, the spatial configuration, the available actions) and the form (the rule-structure, the genre conventions, the narrative scaffolding), while the player furnishes the efficient cause (the initiation of motion, the control inputs, the deliberative choices) and the final cause (the end toward which the action is directed, the desire that draws the whole chain into actuality). This division does not fracture the unity of the change; rather, it articulates the correlative poles of a single actualization, in which the world's potency and the player's potency are brought into act together. The union-structure that RODA inherits from this analysis is the formula articulated in *De Anima* III.2 (425b26–27): "one in substrate, different in being." The agent and the world are distinct in their being—the player remains a human substance, the world remains a virtual environment—but they become one in the substrate of the actualization itself, the single motion in which perception, cognition, and desire are completed. This formula governs the substantial union that RODA traces; it ensures that the union is an experiential convergence, not a change of kind, and that the player who is wholly incorporated into the world has not thereby made the virtual non-virtual, nor ceased to be a bodily agent situated in the material order.

## Rhetorical Incorporation: The Substantial Union of Player and World

The actualization-of-desire chain unfolds across five nodes and four motions, each transition marking a distinct phase in the player's engagement with the designed world. **A₀**, the ontological horizon, is the sensible object and the sense-faculty given together within motion and time; it is the designed *Umwelt* into which the player is already thrown, the perceptual field that solicits attention and invites action. The first motion, **M₀→A₁**, is perceptual motion, which completes in **A₁**, the completed perception that deposits a dual residue, the *resonant kinēsis*: the *resonant aisthēma* (the persisting eidetic trace, the formal imprint of the perceived object) and the *resonant epithymia* (the persisting hedonic-orectic tone, the affective charge that accompanies the perception). This dual residue is the material on which the second motion operates. **M₁→A₂**, the phantastic motion, generates **A₂**, the *phantasma* proper: the determinate, available, charged image, the pivot on which every later operation works. The *phantasma* is not a mere copy of the sensible object but a synthetic product, shaped by the formal and affective residues of perception and available for cognitive engagement; it is the image that persists when the sensible object withdraws, the representation that the player carries forward into deliberation and action. The third motion, **M₂→A₃**, is cognitive motion, which completes in **A₃**, the completed cognition that engages the *phantasma* through three orientational modes—intellection, memory, and discursive or deliberative reasoning—plus the orthogonal committal *doxa*, the taking-as-true that ratifies the cognitive operation, and the concretized emotion that accompanies it. At this node, the player has not merely perceived and imagined but has cognized, deliberated, and committed to a course of action; the *phantasma* has been taken up into a structured, emotionally charged, truth-committed cognitive act. The fourth motion, **M₃→A₄**, is orektikon motion, the motion of desire, which completes in **A₄**, the completed action, whether *praxis* (action for its own sake) or *poiēsis* (action directed toward a product). This completed action sediments into *hexis*, the dispositional character that persists beyond the session and shapes subsequent perception, cognition, and desire; this cross-pass build-up is temporal sedimentation, the mechanism by which a single actualization reshapes the agent who performs it.

The central event that RODA traces occurs at the **A₃→A₄** transition, the moment at which cognition and desire converge in completed action. This transition is a substantial union with two correlative poles: the world-pole, which the dissertation terms incorporation, and the with-others pole, which it terms consubstantiation. These poles are held as one event, not as successive or separable phases; the player who acts within the designed world is simultaneously incorporated into that world and consubstantiated with the other agents who share it. The union is grounded in the actualization of desire, the motion by which the *orekton*, the object of desire, draws the player's perception, cognition, and affective orientation into a completed act; it is a fusion of the player's efficient-final causality with the world's material-formal causality, a convergence in which the designed environment and the desiring agent become one in substrate, different in being. This fusion is the dissertation's own construction; neither Calleja nor Burke articulates both poles of the union, and the integration of incorporation and consubstantiation into a single substantial event is the interpretive contribution that RODA makes. It is crucial to guard against a misreading: the union is an experiential convergence, not a change of kind. A wholly incorporated player has not made the virtual non-virtual, nor has the player become a virtual entity; the player remains a bodily agent situated in the material order, and the world remains a designed environment distinct in its mode of being. The union is substantial in the sense that it actualizes a potency shared between agent and world, not in the sense that it collapses the ontological distinction between them.

## Incorporation, Re-Grounded: Calleja and the Channels of Involvement

The actualization-of-desire chain shows that a designed world engages the player's perception, cognition, and desire, drawing each into a structured motion that completes in action; it does not, however, supply a vocabulary for *where* that engagement happens, nor a test for *whether* the player is truly taken into the world. Calleja's Player Involvement Model supplies both, and the dissertation re-grounds incorporation through a sublation that preserves, transforms, and elevates Calleja's original formulation. The preservation: Calleja's incorporation operates on a double axis, in which "the player incorporates (in the sense of internalizing or assimilating) the game environment into consciousness while simultaneously being incorporated through the avatar into that environment," and "the simultaneous occurrence of these two processes is a necessary condition for the experience of incorporation" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169). The canonical definition follows: "We can thus conceive of incorporation as the absorption of a virtual environment into consciousness, yielding a sense of habitation, which is supported by the systemically upheld embodiment of the player in a single location, as represented by the avatar" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169). Incorporation is thus the world-pole of the union, the process by which the player is taken into the designed environment and comes to inhabit it; the six dimensions of the Player Involvement Model—kinesthetic, spatial, shared, narrative, affective, and ludic[^1]—are the designed channels through which this incorporation occurs. Each dimension names a distinct mode of engagement: kinesthetic involvement encompasses "all modes of avatar or game piece control… ranging from learning controls to the fluency of internalized movement"; spatial involvement is "engagement with the spatial qualities of a virtual environment… the sense that they are inhabiting a place, rather than merely perceiving a representation of space"; shared involvement is "engagement derived from players' awareness of and interaction with other agents… cohabitation, cooperation, and competition"; narrative involvement is "the narrative that is scripted into the game and the narrative that is generated from the ongoing interaction"; affective involvement is "various forms of emotional engagement… either purposefully designed into the game or precipitated by an individual player's interpretation"; ludic involvement is "players' engagement with the choices made in the game and the repercussions of those choices" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 43–44). Calleja identifies two dimensions—spatial and kinesthetic—as "the cornerstone of the incorporation experience," asserting that "without them, incorporation cannot take place" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169–170).

The transformation: Calleja grounds incorporation in "their experientialist ontology" (Lakoff and Johnson) and "a view of consciousness as an internally generated construct" (Dennett, Damasio, Lakoff and Johnson) (Calleja, *In-Game*, 168–169), a psychological intensification of involvement that remains at the level of subjective experience. The dissertation re-grounds incorporation as an ontological union, tying it to the Heideggerian vocabulary of concernful absorption that Calleja's own phenomenological language reaches for but does not theoretically ground. Heidegger's "characteristic absorption of concern in its equipmental world" and "circumspective concern" (*Being and Time*, 405) name the structure in which the agent's attention is taken up into the world's equipmental totality, such that the equipment itself withdraws—the hammer "disappears into the hammering" (*Being and Time*, 98)—and the agent's action becomes transparent to its end. This is the ontological structure that Calleja's incorporation gestures toward when it describes "the absorption of a virtual environment into consciousness, yielding a sense of habitation"; the dissertation's re-grounding supplies the missing ontological frame, transforming incorporation from a psychological metaphor into a genuine union of agent and world. This re-grounding is warranted by Calleja's own admission that "incorporation is ultimately a metaphor like presence or immersion" (Calleja, *In-Game*, 5), a concession that signals the gap between his experientialist ground and the phenomenological vocabulary he employs. The dissertation bridges that gap by reading incorporation as the world-pole of the substantial union articulated in the actualization-of-desire chain, the process by which the player's efficient-final causality is taken up into the world's material-formal causality and the two become one in substrate, different in being.

The elevation: incorporation, re-grounded as concernful absorption, is elevated into union with the with-others pole, consubstantiation, which the next section will articulate. The test for whether this union has occurred is supplied by Calleja's own criteria, which the dissertation designates R1 through R7[^2]: R1, assimilation (the player internalizes the environment into consciousness); R2, embodiment (the player is incorporated through the avatar into the environment); R3, simultaneity (the strictest gatekeeper, requiring that assimilation and embodiment occur together); R4, the spatial-kinesthetic cornerstone (without spatial and kinesthetic involvement, incorporation cannot take place); R5, blending (the player's action and the avatar's action are experienced as a single motion); R6, temporal apex (incorporation reaches its peak when the player's attention is wholly absorbed in the equipmental world); R7, intrusion-integration (external interruptions are either rejected or integrated into the incorporative flow). These criteria, drawn from Calleja's account of incorporation (Calleja, *In-Game*, 169–173, 178), function as the operational test for the substantial union at the world-pole; a player who satisfies R1 through R7 has achieved the concernful absorption that RODA traces.

[^2]: The seven criteria (R1–R7) are detailed in Appendix C, which reproduces the verbatim formulations and theoretical context for each criterion.

The choice of Calleja over alternative accounts of player engagement is routed through Calleja's own excision of the magic circle and his correction of the unidirectional metaphors of immersion and presence. Calleja rejects the notion that play occurs within a bounded, separate domain insulated from ordinary life, arguing instead that involvement is a graduated, multi-dimensional phenomenon that extends across the boundary between game and world; similarly, he corrects the unidirectional metaphor of immersion, in which the player is passively submerged in the game environment, replacing it with the double-axis structure of incorporation, in which the player both takes the world into consciousness and is taken into the world through the avatar. These corrections align with the dissertation's own ontological commitments: the substantial union is not a crossing into a separate domain but a convergence of agent and world within a single actualization, and the player's agency is not dissolved but rather completed in the incorporative act. Calleja's account thus supplies the vocabulary and the test that the actualization-of-desire chain requires, and the re-grounding through Heideggerian concernful absorption supplies the ontological depth that Calleja's experientialist ground lacks.

## Consubstantiation and the Grammar of Motives: Burke at the Other Pole

The actualization-of-desire chain and the re-grounded account of incorporation leave two things unaccounted for, both essential to the substantial union that RODA traces. First, a consubstantial bond among several agents, a "we" that can itself source an action, such that the player's completed act is not merely individual but shared with one's fellows; second, a grammar by which a finished act is given its motive, and its true source displaced, such that the player attributes the action to the world, or to the avatar, or to the collective, rather than to the desiring self. Burke's account of consubstantiation and the pentadic grammar of motives supplies both, and the dissertation sublates Burke's formulation through the same preserve-transform-elevate structure applied to Calleja. The preservation: Burke defines consubstantiation as the process by which "in being identified with B, A is 'substantially one' with a person other than himself," yet "at the same time he remains unique, an individual locus of motives," such that "he is both joined and separate" (Burke, *Rhetoric*, 21). This identification is grounded in act: "substance… was an act; and a way of life is an acting-together" (Burke, *Rhetoric*, 21); it is compensatory to division, arising precisely where separation exists: "Identification is affirmed with earnestness precisely because there is division. Identification is compensatory to division" (Burke, *Rhetoric*, 22). Consubstantiation is thus the with-others pole of the substantial union, the process by which the player who acts within the designed world is joined to the other agents who share that world, not by dissolving individual agency but by establishing a shared substrate of action in which each remains distinct in being.
