## A. The full statement (six paragraphs — use all six where space allows)

The two published studies asked whether an instrument could tell the three films apart. That
question has an answer, and the preceding tests give it. It is not, however, the question the
laboratory was built to settle. The instruments were adopted because the participants' own
reports were treated as the criterion, and each channel was scored by whether it agreed with
what the participant said afterward. Restated in the form the reprocessing can test: does a
physiological channel move with a person's own account of their boredom, episode by episode?
Neither test substitutes for the other, and the two can disagree — as they do here.

Both sides of the comparison are converted to within-subject standard scores before anything
is correlated. Each participant's value for one film is expressed as its distance from that
participant's own three-film mean, in units of that participant's own standard deviation. The
step is not cosmetic. Absolute pupil diameter, habitual gaze range and rating style vary
enough between twelve people that an uncentered correlation would chiefly measure who has a
wide-ranging eye or a generous hand with a nine-point scale. After centering, each participant
contributes only the shape of their three films relative to themselves, which is the quantity
the question is about. The association is then computed across all thirty-six
participant-by-film cells.

The reported coefficient is Spearman's rank correlation. It correlates the order of the values
rather than the values themselves, which suits this material for two reasons. No theory
predicts that self-reported boredom rises in proportion to millimeters of pupil or degrees of
gaze deviation, so a statistic that assumes a straight line assumes something the design does
not license; and with twelve participants a single unusual episode can bend a linear
coefficient in a way it cannot bend a rank one. Pearson's product-moment correlation is
computed alongside and reported in full in the appendix, both because the two coefficients
should be compared rather than one quietly chosen, and because their agreement is itself
informative. Across every channel and both criteria the median absolute difference between
them is 0.032 and the largest is 0.117. Where a monotonic relationship is also close to
linear, the rank statistic loses almost nothing, so the choice of Spearman costs little.
Nothing in it flatters the result: on every association that carries the finding the Pearson
coefficient is the *larger* of the two in magnitude, so the reported figure understates the
association rather than inflating it.

The probability attached to each coefficient is obtained by permutation rather than from the
closed-form distribution, and the reason is a property of the design that the standard formula
cannot accommodate. Thirty-six paired observations are not thirty-six independent
observations: they are twelve participants contributing three films each. Within-subject
centering, which the comparison requires for the reasons given above, makes the dependence
structural rather than incidental — constraining three values to a mean of zero and a standard
deviation of one means that knowing two of them very nearly fixes the third. Both the rank and
the linear coefficient carry analytic p-values that assume independent observations, so both
are anti-conservative here. The correction is to build the null distribution from the data's
own structure: the three film labels are shuffled within each participant, never across
participants, the coefficient is recomputed, and the procedure repeats twenty thousand times
with a fixed seed. The proportion of shuffles producing an association at least as strong as
the observed one is the reported probability. Analytic values are retained beside the
permutation values throughout, so the size of the correction is visible rather than absorbed.

That correction is consequential, and it is applied to both coefficients, which shows it to be
a feature of the design rather than an artifact of ranking. Where an association is weak the
two probabilities barely differ; where it is strong the analytic value runs three to eight
times too small, so the distortion is largest exactly where a reader would lean on it hardest.
Three associations that clear the conventional threshold on the analytic value do not clear it
on the permutation value, and they are reported as not clearing it. What survives is stated
without hedging. On the per-participant baseline, the one assigned to this question, gaze
deviation moves with self-reported boredom at rho = +0.59 and against self-reported engagement
at rho = −0.59; the per-file baseline stands next at +0.52 and −0.53 and points the same way,
so the result does not turn on which of the two is used. Eye closure moves with reported
boredom at rho = +0.46 and against reported engagement at −0.42. All of these survive the
permutation test. Pupil dilation, which separates the three films more powerfully than any
other channel, does not move with either report.

Two limits bound what any of this can be taken to show. The cohort is twelve, so these
coefficients describe this group and support no estimate of a population value; they are
reported for their direction and their relative ordering, not as parameters. And the
comparison is episode-level throughout — each physiological figure is an aggregate over a
whole seventeen-minute film and each rating is given in the break immediately afterward — so
nothing here time-locks a moment of experience to a moment of signal. What the analysis
establishes is that two of the eye channels track a person's own retrospective account across
their three films, and that the channel which best distinguishes the films is not among them.

## B. Short form (two paragraphs, if the method module runs tight)

The instruments were adopted because the participants' own reports were the criterion, so each
channel is scored twice: once for whether it separates the three films, and once for whether it
agrees with what the participant said. Both sides are converted to within-subject standard
scores first, so that differences in absolute pupil size, habitual gaze range and rating style
cannot drive the association and each participant contributes only the shape of their own three
films. The reported coefficient is Spearman's rank correlation, chosen because no theory
predicts a linear relation between self-reported boredom and millimeters of pupil, and because
one unusual episode among twelve participants can bend a linear coefficient more than a rank
one. Pearson's coefficient is computed alongside and reported in the appendix; the two agree
closely, with a median absolute difference of 0.032, and on every association that carries the
finding the linear coefficient is the larger, so the reported figure is the more conservative.

Probabilities are obtained by permutation rather than from the closed-form distribution,
because thirty-six paired observations are twelve participants contributing three films each,
and within-subject centering makes that dependence structural. Film labels are shuffled within
each participant only, twenty thousand times, and the proportion of shuffles matching or
exceeding the observed association is the reported value; analytic values are retained beside
them. The correction runs three to eight times where associations are strong, and three
associations that clear the conventional threshold analytically do not clear it under
permutation and are reported accordingly. Gaze deviation and eye closure both track the
participants' own reports on both items — gaze most strongly on the per-participant baseline,
at +0.59 and −0.59 — while pupil dilation, the channel that best separates the films, tracks
neither.

## C. Anticipated objections, with the answer already in the text

Keep these in reserve for the defence rather than in the prose. Each is answerable from the
statement above without new analysis.

**"Thirty-six observations from twelve people is pseudo-replication."** Conceded and corrected,
which is the point of the permutation scheme. The null distribution is built by shuffling only
within participants, so it reproduces exactly the dependence structure the objection names. The
analytic values are printed beside the permutation values so the reader can see what the
uncorrected analysis would have claimed.

**"Within-subject z-scoring induces the dependence you then correct for — why do it at all?"**
Because the alternative is worse. Uncentered, the correlation is dominated by between-person
differences in absolute level that have nothing to do with boredom. Centering is required by the
question; the induced constraint is then handled by the resampling scheme rather than ignored.
Both choices are stated, and the appendix shows the analytic and permutation values side by
side so the cost of the centering is legible.

**"Spearman was chosen because it gave the answer you wanted."** The appendix answers this
directly: both coefficients are reported for every test, they agree to a median of 0.032, and
on every association carrying the finding Pearson is the larger — +0.623 against +0.590 and
+0.574 against +0.524 and +0.577 against +0.460 on boredom, −0.609 against −0.585 and −0.590
against −0.527 and −0.530 against −0.416 on engagement. Choosing the rank statistic makes the
reported association smaller, not larger, and its probability larger, not smaller. One
borderline case runs the other way and is reported as such: gaze in degrees from center on the
device-forward baseline, against engagement, sits at permutation p = 0.052 under Spearman and
0.048 under Pearson, so it falls just outside the conventional threshold on the reported
statistic and just inside on the unreported one. It is treated as not significant, which is the
conservative reading, and the appendix prints both values rather than the convenient one.

**"Twenty thousand permutations is arbitrary."** With twelve participants and three films there
are six orderings per participant and 6¹² ≈ 2.2 × 10⁹ distinct arrangements, so twenty thousand
draws sample the null adequately for a p-value reported to three decimal places, and the seed
is fixed so the value is reproducible. The number is recorded in the run manifest with every
other parameter. The per-round splits are coarser and are labelled accordingly: Round 1 admits
6⁸ ≈ 1.7 × 10⁶ arrangements and Round 2 only 6⁴ = 1,296, which is why the Round 2 split is
descriptive.

**"Your pooled association could be produced by one round rather than by both."** Answered by
computing it inside each round separately. Every association that carries the finding points
the same way in both rounds, so none of them is carried by one round; the coefficients are
smaller within rounds than pooled, which is what fewer participants produce. Gaze is markedly
stronger in Round 2, where the feature was sampled at the rate its definition assumes, and that
asymmetry is reported as support for the sampling-rate account rather than as a second finding.

**"You corrected for clustering but not for multiple comparisons."** Correct, and stated. The
criterion tests are reported as a family of descriptive associations, not as a set of
independent hypothesis tests, and the ordering of channels rather than the individual
probability is what the argument uses. Where a multiplicity-corrected claim is made in this
study it is made explicitly with Holm correction, and those are the stimulus-separation tests
reported earlier. `****** CONFIRM AT DRAFTING (O-14): whether to add a Holm or FDR column here;
my recommendation is not to, and to say why in a footnote rather than to compute it and then
argue against it.`

**"Why not a mixed-effects model?"** It would be the conventional choice with more
participants, and it would estimate a population-level slope this design cannot support at
n = 12. The permutation scheme makes fewer assumptions — no distributional form, no variance
components to estimate from twelve clusters — and answers the question actually asked, which is
whether the observed association exceeds what within-participant reshuffling produces.
