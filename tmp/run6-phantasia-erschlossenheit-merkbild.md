# Pre-Theoretical Disclosure and the Living Being: Aristotle's φαντασία, Heidegger's Erschlossenheit, and Uexküll's Merkbild as Three Articulations of a Single Structural Insight

The question that animates this chapter is deceptively simple: how does a living being find itself already immersed in a world that matters to it prior to any act of reflection, judgment, or theoretical cognition? Three thinkers, separated by centuries and disciplinary formations, converge upon a remarkably unified answer—namely, that the living being is never enclosed within itself as a mental cabinet or sealed container of representations but is always already outside itself, in the midst of a meaningful environment that is disclosed pre-theoretically. Aristotle's account of φαντασία in *De Anima* III.3 and III.8, Heidegger's analysis of Erschlossenheit (disclosedness) in *Being and Time* §28, and Jakob von Uexküll's concept of the Merkbild (perceptual image) in his *Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans* each articulate, in distinct but structurally analogous vocabularies, the thesis that disclosure precedes and conditions all subsequent theoretical apprehension. What follows is an attempt to read these three articulations together—not as identical doctrines, but as convergent insights into a single structural feature of living being. 

## 1. Aristotle: φαντασία as Pre-Theoretical Disclosure

The standard translation of φαντασία as "imagination" or "mental imagery" has long obscured the structural role this faculty plays in Aristotle's psychology. Far from being a mere capacity for producing pictures in the mind's eye, φαντασία names a kind of movement (κίνησις) that arises from actual sense-perception and persists beyond the immediate presence of the sensible object, thereby enabling desire, locomotion, and thought itself. As Papachristou observes, "the Stageirite philosopher asserts that phantasia (φαντασία) is a kind of motion (κίνησις) in the soul that cannot exist apart from sensation (αἴσθησις) —it is caused by the activity of sensation" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, p. 12)[1]. This characterization is crucial, for it situates φαντασία not as a representational faculty sealed within a mental interior but as a dynamic movement that extends sensation beyond its immediate actuality, thereby opening the organism to a world that persists and solicits action even in the absence of the object that first occasioned the movement. Aristotle's famous dictum that the soul never thinks without a phantasma (Aristotle, *De Anima*, 431a17) thus expresses not a deficiency of thought—as if thinking required a crutch—but rather the constitutive dependence of all cognitive activity upon a prior disclosure of the world through sensation-derived movement. The phantasma is not a degraded copy of an object; it is the medium through which the world remains available to the living being as meaningful. Indeed, the distinction between sensitive (αἰσθητική) and deliberative (βουλευτική) φαντασία, which Aristotle draws at [18]*De Anima* 434a5–10, functions as what we may call a proto-phenomenological differentiation between two grades of pre-theoretical engagement. Sensitive φαντασία belongs to all animals and governs the immediate, pre-thematic engagement with the environment: the prey animal that flees the approaching predator does so not on the basis of a syllogism but through a φαντασία that presents the predator as fearful, as to-be-fled. Deliberative φαντασία, by contrast, belongs only to rational animals and involves the capacity to compare and compose images in the service of practical reasoning—yet even this more sophisticated grade of disclosure remains pre-theoretical in the sense that it operates in the service of desire and action rather than disinterested contemplation. O'Gorman underscores this point when he notes that Aristotle calls choice (προαίρεσις) "deliberative desire" (βουλευτικὴ ὄρεξις), and that "Phantasia, Aristotle argues in De Anima, is indispensable to desire, in that images of a good or desirable object move even the most brute animals" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 9)[2]. Thus, the sensitive/deliberative distinction does not mark a boundary between the pre-theoretical and the theoretical; rather, it differentiates two levels within the pre-theoretical itself—one oriented by immediate sensory solicitation, the other by the temporally extended deliberative weighing of possible goods. The full implications of this distinction for the question of belief (δόξα) and the voluntariness of phantasia will be addressed in greater detail in the following chapter; here it suffices to note that both grades share the structural feature of disclosing the world as already meaningful, already charged with practical significance, prior to any act of propositional judgment. Specifically, Heidegger himself recognized this Aristotelian insight as foundational, noting the intimate connection between δόξα and προαίρεσις: as he puts it, they "approach each other precisely when one takes δόξα in the narrow meaning of being directed at 'that which can be otherwise,' the ἐνδεχόμενον ἄλλως, insofar as it is a συμφέρον" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 116–117)[3]. In this way, φαντασία emerges as Aristotle's name for the pre-theoretical disclosure that makes any subsequent theoretical apprehension possible. 

## 2. Heidegger's Radicalization: Erschlossenheit as Pre-Thematic Disclosure

If Aristotle's φαντασία names the movement through which the living being finds itself already engaged with a meaningful world, Heidegger's Erschlossenheit—the disclosedness of Dasein—radicalizes this insight by stripping away the residual hylomorphic framework within which Aristotle articulated it. For Aristotle, φαντασία still bears the imprint of form-reception: the phantasma is a residual movement in the sense-organ that retains the form of the sensed object without its matter. Heidegger, however, insists that Dasein is not a substance that first exists and then enters into relation with a world; rather, Dasein is always already outside itself, in the midst of entities that are disclosed within a clearing (Lichtung)[14] of significance. The fundamental structure of this disclosure is what Heidegger calls Erschlossenheit, which he analyzes in *Being and Time* §28 as constituted by three equiprimordial moments: Befindlichkeit (state-of-mind or disposedness), Verstehen (understanding), and Rede (discourse). Heidegger explicitly links this analysis to the Aristotelian tradition, as the editors of *Heidegger and Rhetoric* note: the 1924 lectures represent "an extraordinary opportunity for the historian of rhetoric" precisely because Heidegger reads Aristotle's πάθη not as static mental states but as modes of disclosure, as Befindlichkeiten (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 110). Moreover, Heidegger observes that Aristotle's notion that passions alter judgments reveals "where passions intrude on logos—here articulated judgements, although frequently translated by historians of philosophy as 'reason'" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115)[4]. How, then, does Heidegger re-read the specific Aristotelian concepts we have examined? With respect to φαντασία as pre-theoretical disclosure—the first sub-move of our Aristotle section—Heidegger's concept of circumspective concern (Umsicht)[15], developed in *Being and Time* §15, offers a direct parallel. Just as the phantasma discloses the world as already practically significant, circumspective concern reveals the ready-to-hand (Zuhandenes)[16] as equipment within a totality of involvements; in both cases, the entity is encountered not as a bare present-at-hand object but as something charged with practical meaning. With respect to the sensitive/deliberative distinction—our second sub-move—Heidegger's analysis of Befindlichkeit and Verstehen recasts this differentiation existentially: Befindlichkeit corresponds to the immediate, pre-thematic attunement through which Dasein finds itself always already in a situation (analogous to sensitive φαντασία), while Verstehen corresponds to the projective capacity through which Dasein discloses possibilities for action (analogous to deliberative φαντασία). The internal distinctions within phantasia—its grades, its relation to δόξα, its voluntariness—are thus transposed by Heidegger into the existential analytic of Dasein, where the question is no longer about a faculty of the soul but about the structural moments of being-in-the-world. Rickert captures this ambient dimension when he notes that "even the earliest Heidegger, prior to formulating our 'being there' as Dasein, was aware of this problem, referring to a person as an 'I-situation'" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 31)[5]. The parallel between φαντασία and Erschlossenheit thus lies precisely in the shared insistence that disclosure is always already operative prior to theoretical reflection—that the living being, whether conceived as ψυχή or as Dasein, never stands outside the world in a posture of detached contemplation but is always already in the midst of things that matter. 

## 3. The Affective Dimension: Stimmung, πάθος, and the Merkwelt

Disclosure is never affectively neutral. This is a point upon which Aristotle, Heidegger, and Uexküll converge with striking unanimity. For Aristotle, φαντασία presents objects as already colored by desire and aversion; the φαντασία of the hungry animal presents food as pleasant, as to-be-pursued, while the φαντασία of the frightened animal presents the predator as painful, as to-be-fled. This affective coloring is intimately bound up with what Aristotle calls πάθος—the passions or affections that are not mere feelings appended to cognition but constitutive dimensions of how the world shows up for the living being. Heidegger, in his 1924 lectures on Aristotle, explicitly interprets πάθος as Befindlichkeit, and thus recognizes in Aristotle's account of the passions an anticipation of his own analysis of Stimmung (mood) in *Being and Time* §29 (H.134–140). As the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* document, "Passion itself is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115)[6]. Heidegger re-reads Aristotle's φαντασία as pre-theoretical disclosure by showing that the phantasma is never affectively inert but is always already attuned—always already colored by the πάθη that shape the organism's engagement with its environment. Similarly, Heidegger's re-reading of the sensitive/deliberative distinction transforms it into the relationship between Befindlichkeit (the thrownness into a mood that determines what can appear at all) and Verstehen (the projection upon possibilities that extends disclosure into the future). Heidegger's Erschlossenheit, examined in the previous section, is thus not merely cognitive but fundamentally affective: Stimmung is not a subjective feeling overlaid upon an objective world but the very manner in which the world is disclosed as mattering in this or that way. Uexküll's concept of the Merkwelt (perceptual world) offers a biological analogue of this affective disclosure. As Uexküll writes, observers "located in the Umwelt are looking 'from afar' at the world of the poet" (von Uexküll, *A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans*, p. 225); this remark, though made in a different context, captures the essential insight that the Umwelt is always perspectival, always structured by the organism's own perceptual and effector capacities. The Merkwelt thus functions as a filter: it determines what can be encountered, what can show up as significant, and what remains invisible. Just as Heidegger's Stimmung tunes what can appear within Dasein's disclosedness, and just as Aristotle's φαντασία presents objects as charged with desire and aversion, the Merkwelt encloses the organism within a species-specific affective world in which only certain features of the environment are disclosed and others are structurally excluded. The internal distinctions within phantasia—between its sensitive and deliberative grades—find a partial analogue here in the different degrees of complexity exhibited by the Merkwelten of different species: the tick's Merkwelt, for instance, is structured by only three carrier-of-significance features (butyric acid, temperature, and the tactile quality of skin), whereas the human Merkwelt admits of the deliberative weighing of multiple possible meanings. The relation between φαντασία and δόξα, and the question of phantasia's voluntariness, will be taken up more fully in a subsequent chapter; for present purposes, the salient point is that the affective dimension of disclosure—whether articulated as πάθος, Stimmung, or the Merkwelt's filtering function—is constitutive rather than supplementary. Gibson's ecological approach to perception resonates here, for he notes the duality of visual information, observing that "the observer is never quite sure how to answer the question, 'What do you see?'" (Gibson, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, p. 303)[7]—a duality that reflects the pre-theoretical entanglement of perception with significance and affect. 

## 4. Uexküll's Biological Reframing: Merkbild and Suchbild

With Uexküll, the trajectory of pre-theoretical disclosure reaches its biological consummation. The Merkbild (perceptual image) is not a passive reception of form from the environment but an active, species-specific construction through which the organism projects meaningful structure onto its surroundings. This constitutive character of the Merkbild is most vividly illustrated by Uexküll's concept of the Suchbild (search image): the organism that searches for a particular kind of food, mate, or shelter does so not by scanning an undifferentiated environment but by projecting a perceptual template that renders certain features salient and others invisible. The Suchbild thus completes the trajectory we have been tracing: from Aristotle's φαντασία, which retains the form of the sensed object as a movement in the sense-organ; through Heidegger's Erschlossenheit, which discloses entities within a clearing of significance; to Uexküll's Merkbild, which actively constitutes the perceptual field in accordance with the organism's species-specific capacities and needs. As Uexküll himself insists, technology that ignores the organism's own meaning-world has "lost all sense of Nature; indeed, it boldly presumed to solve the deepest questions of life, such as the relation of human beings to God-Nature, with its wholly inadequate mathematics" (von Uexküll, *A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans*, p. 200). This passage reveals the depth of Uexküll's critique: the reduction of the organism to a mechanism that passively receives stimuli from an objective environment is not merely a scientific error but a fundamental misunderstanding of the nature of life itself. Burke's dramatistic vocabulary helps us articulate what is at stake in this biological reframing. For Burke, the "scene" is never merely a backdrop to action but a constitutive element of the act itself; accordingly, "when we confront a simplicity we must forthwith ask ourselves what complexities are subsumed beneath it" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 126)[8]. The Merkbild, like Burke's scene, is not a simple container but a complex, actively constituted medium through which the organism encounters its world. Heidegger, too, recognized the importance of the biological dimension, interpreting Aristotle's κίνησις not merely as physical motion but as a fundamental way of being. As the contributors to *Heidegger and Rhetoric* note, "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 27)[9]. The Merkbild thus represents the biological instantiation of a structural insight that Aristotle articulated in psychological terms and Heidegger in existential-ontological terms: pre-theoretical disclosure is not an optional supplement to living being but its very condition of possibility. The organism does not first exist as a bare biological entity and then acquire a perceptual world; rather, to be an organism at all is already to inhabit a Merkwelt, already to project Merkbilder and Suchbilder that constitute the meaningful environment within which life unfolds. 

## 5. The Key Divergence: Form-Reception versus Meaning-Constitution

Despite the structural convergence we have identified, it would be misleading to collapse the three accounts into a single doctrine. The key divergence lies in the question of whether pre-theoretical disclosure is fundamentally receptive or fundamentally constitutive. For Aristotle, the phantasma is a residual movement in the sense-organ—a kinetic trace left by the activity of sensation that retains the form of the sensed object without its matter. Aristotle's account thus remains committed to a form-reception model: the sensible form is received from the external object, and the phantasma is a derivative movement that preserves this form in the absence of the object. As Papachristou notes, φαντασία "is a kind of motion (κίνησις) in the soul that cannot exist apart from sensation (αἴσθησις)" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, p. 12)[10]. The phantasma, however dynamic its role in guiding desire and enabling thought, remains ontologically dependent upon a prior act of reception. Uexküll's Merkbild, by contrast, is constitutive through and through. The organism does not receive meaningful forms from the environment; it projects perceptual templates—Suchbilder—that actively structure what can appear. There are, as the editors of Uexküll's *Foray* note, "equally striking parallels between Uexkull's epistemology of perceptual significance—that the subject encounters and is affected by its projections—and the basic epistemology of early quantum theory with its assurance that 'modern man confronts only himself'" (von Uexküll, *A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans*, p. 241). This is a radical departure from Aristotle's form-reception: the Merkbild does not preserve a form that was received from without but constructs the very field of significance within which forms can appear at all. Heidegger occupies the pivot between these two positions. On the one hand, Heidegger inherits from Aristotle the insight that disclosure is always already operative prior to theoretical reflection—that Dasein, like the ensouled animal, never stands outside the world but is always already alongside entities that matter. With respect to φαντασία as pre-theoretical disclosure, Heidegger's circumspective concern (Umsicht)[17] recasts the phantasma's function of making the world practically available as an existential structure of being-in-the-world. With respect to the sensitive/deliberative distinction, Heidegger's differentiation of Befindlichkeit and Verstehen transposes the two grades of φαντασία into the equiprimordial moments of disclosedness. With respect to the affective dimension, Heidegger's Stimmung recaptures Aristotle's πάθος as the attunement that tunes what can appear. With respect to Uexküll's biological reframing, Heidegger's reading of κίνησις as a fundamental way of Dasein's being anticipates the biosemiotic turn. On the other hand, Heidegger moves beyond Aristotle by insisting that Dasein's disclosure is not the reception of forms but the projection of possibilities upon a horizon of significance. As Heidegger himself indicates through his treatment of the hylomorphic framework in the *Physics*, the matter (ὕλη) that must be present "if a house or another being is to be in its being-completed" is not simply raw material awaiting form but is always already disposed toward a τέλος (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 162–163). The projection of significance is thus not a subjective imposition but an ontological structure of Dasein's being-in-the-world—a point that brings Heidegger remarkably close to Uexküll's constitutive Merkbild while retaining the Aristotelian insistence on the priority of the world itself. Aristotle's own framework, while centered on form-reception, already contains the resources for this move, as reflected in the *Physics* where being is shown to resist reduction to a single sense: "even if what is not cannot be without qualification, there is no reason why it should not be something or other" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 7)[11]. The divergence between reception and constitution thus marks not an unbridgeable gulf but a spectrum along which the three thinkers are differently positioned—with Heidegger occupying the crucial pivot that renders the trajectory from Aristotle to Uexküll intelligible. 

## 6. Synthesis: The Rejection of the Cabinet Model

What, then, unites these three articulations of pre-theoretical disclosure? The answer lies in their shared rejection of what we may call the "cabinet model" of mind—the assumption, pervasive in modern philosophy from Descartes onward, that the living being is first and foremost enclosed within a mental space, a sealed interiority from which it must somehow reach out to make contact with an external world. On this model, perception is a matter of representations being deposited in the cabinet of the mind, and the problem of knowledge is the problem of matching these inner representations to outer reality. Aristotle, Heidegger, and Uexküll all reject this picture, though in different ways and with different vocabularies. For Aristotle, the ensouled being is not a container of phantasmata but a living being that is always already engaged with its environment through the kinetic movement of φαντασία. The phantasma discloses the world as practically significant—as something to be pursued or fled—and in doing so reveals the organism as always already outside itself, always already alongside the objects of its desire and aversion. O'Gorman captures this practical dimension by noting that phantasia "is indispensable to desire, in that images of a good or desirable object move even the most brute animals" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 9)[12]. For Heidegger, Dasein is constitutively being-in-the-world; the very idea of a subject enclosed within itself is a derivative abstraction from the more primordial phenomenon of disclosedness. As Rickert observes, situatedness is not "the tame version of situatedness with which we are well familiar, wherein we are all understood as 'coming from somewhere'" but rather something more radical: "'somewhere' is itself an entangled situation, only partially present at any given time and open to new disclosures" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 53)[13]. For Uexküll, the organism's "soap bubble"—its Umwelt—is not a prison that encloses the organism within a sealed perceptual space but the very medium through which the organism encounters its world as meaningful. The soap bubble is not a barrier between organism and environment but the condition of possibility for any encounter whatsoever. Burke's dramatistic perspective helps us articulate the deeper logic at work in this shared rejection. Burke insists upon the constitutive role of the scene in shaping action and motive, recognizing that even "a purely technical or 'disciplinary' reason" can produce "much disrelated featuring of motives, ranging from the smallest circumference, local in time, place and purpose" to the widest scenic horizons (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 106). The point is that motive, meaning, and disclosure are never the private possessions of an isolated subject but are always already distributed across the scene of engagement—a scene that, in Aristotle's terms, is opened by φαντασία, in Heidegger's terms by Erschlossenheit, and in Uexküll's terms by the Merkbild. The living being is not a cabinet; it is, as Heidegger might say, a clearing—a site at which entities show themselves as what they are and as mattering in this or that way. Similarly, Gross reminds us that the affective and situational dimensions of rhetoric are not mere subjective overlays but constitutive features of how discourse and experience are structured, as reflected in experimental work on immersion where one is invited to "be there" in the situation described (Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations*, p. 128). Accordingly, the trajectory from Aristotle through Heidegger to Uexküll does not describe a simple progression from error to truth, or from naivety to sophistication. Rather, it describes the gradual explication of a single structural insight: that the living being is always already outside itself, in the midst of a meaningful world that is disclosed pre-theoretically. Aristotle articulates this insight in the language of ψυχή and κίνησις; Heidegger radicalizes it in the language of Dasein and Erschlossenheit; Uexküll instantiates it biologically in the language of the Umwelt and the Merkbild. The soap bubble is not a prison but the very medium of disclosure—the condition under which anything can show up as meaningful for a living being at all. What these three thinkers share, despite their considerable differences, is the conviction that to be alive is already to be engaged, already to be outside oneself, already to be in the midst of a world that matters. The task of philosophy, on this view, is not to explain how we escape from the cabinet of the mind into the world, but to describe the structures of the disclosure that always already places us there. ---
