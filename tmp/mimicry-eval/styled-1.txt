Heidegger's elaboration of boredom (*Langeweile*) as a fundamental attunement (*Grundstimmung*) in the 1929–30 lecture course marks a decisive departure from any merely psychological account of the phenomenon, and it insists that profound boredom (*tiefe Langeweile*) discloses, in a way no ordinary affective state can, the whole of Dasein's situation at once. In its deepest form — the impersonal "it is boring to one" — boredom does not attach itself to any particular object or occasion but rather empties the world of relevance indifferently, and in doing so it brings the temporal structure of existence, the binding of past thrownness and future possibility, into a peculiar, suspended relief. Thus boredom is perhaps the mood in which Dasein is most nakedly confronted with its own finitude and its unchosen freedom, and accordingly it issues, however silently, in a call toward authentic resolve.
