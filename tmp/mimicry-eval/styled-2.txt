In the third book of the *De Anima*, Aristotle situates *phantasia* in a relationship of derivative dependence upon perception, and yet insists upon the irreducibility of the one faculty to the other. Perception (*aisthēsis*) is actualized only in the presence of its proper object and is, for Aristotle, invariably veridical with respect to its special sensibles; *phantasia*, by contrast, persists in the absence of the object and is susceptible to falsity, and these two asymmetries together mark its distinct character. Aristotle describes *phantasia* as a movement produced by perception in its exercise — a kind of residual kinesis that the perceptual act leaves behind — and it is likely that this genetic account is intended to explain both its dependence on and its divergence from sense. The persistence of the *phantasma* accordingly bridges the gap between immediate sensory contact and the higher operations of memory, desire, and discursive thought.
