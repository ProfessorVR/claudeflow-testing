In the *De Anima*, Aristotle carefully distinguishes *phantasia* from perception (*aisthēsis*) while nonetheless grounding the former in the latter. At *DA* III.3 (428a1–5), he argues that *phantasia* is neither identical to perception nor wholly independent of it; rather, it is a derived capacity that arises when perceptual activity leaves behind a kind of residual movement or trace in the soul. Because *phantasia* depends on the prior exercise of a sense-organ, it cannot occur in beings entirely devoid of perception, yet it persists when the object is no longer present — precisely the feature that differentiates it from actual *aisthēsis*, which requires the real presence of its object. Aristotle further notes that while perceptions are generally reliable regarding their proper objects, *phantasmata* can be false even in a waking subject (428b2–4), revealing *phantasia* as a cognitively intermediate faculty that mediates between immediate sensory contact and the higher operations of intellect and desire.
