In the third book of the *De Anima*, Aristotle situates *phantasia* in a position of constitutive dependence upon, yet irreducible difference from, perceptual activity: *phantasia* arises only in beings capable of *aisthēsis*, and yet it persists where perception has lapsed, producing representations in the absence of the objects that originally occasioned them. This asymmetry is philosophically consequential, for it suggests that *phantasia* retains the form of a sensible object without retaining its matter, much as perception itself does, but under conditions no longer governed by the causal presence of the external thing. Aristotle is careful, moreover, to distinguish *phantasia* from both belief and perception proper, noting that appearances may persist even when the perceiver knows them to be false — a dissociation that perception, bound as it is to its present object, cannot itself exhibit. Hence *phantasia* occupies an intermediate and mediating function within the soul's cognitive economy, bridging the deliverances of sense and the operations of intellect.
