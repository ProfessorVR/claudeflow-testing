# Aristotle on Motion, Time, and Temporal Awareness: Aisthēsis, Phantasia, and Heidegger's Interpretation

## 1. Articulate Aristotle's Understanding of Motion, Time, and Perception

Aristotle's philosophical architecture rests upon a deeply integrated account of motion, time, and perception—three concepts that, far from operating in isolation, mutually constitute the very fabric of natural being and human experience. To articulate Aristotle's understanding of these intertwined phenomena is to enter into a discourse wherein the categories of physics, metaphysics, and psychology converge, revealing the fundamental structures through which beings disclose themselves in the world. Indeed, the Aristotelian framework insists that motion (*kinēsis*) is not an accidental feature appended to substances from without but rather an intrinsic principle that governs the manner in which beings come into presence and undergo change; time, correspondingly, is not an empty container within which events merely occur but is instead the very measure and number of motion with respect to the before and after; and perception (*aisthēsis*) furnishes the living soul with the capacity to apprehend these unfolding processes, thereby making possible the awareness of temporal succession and the discrimination of qualitative change.

Central to this framework is the recognition that motion, in Aristotle's broad sense, encompasses not merely locomotion but also alteration, growth, and coming-to-be—modes of change that pervade the natural world and that constitute the subject matter of *physis*. As Heidegger's close reading of Aristotle makes explicit, passion itself, as one mode of being affected, "is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). This formulation reveals the depth of Aristotle's insight: motion is not only something that happens to a being but something through which a being exercises and reveals its fundamental capacities. Similarly, Aristotle's treatment of motion in the *Physics* establishes that *kinēsis* is not something separate from the things that are, a point underscored by Heidegger's interpretation, wherein "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 27). Motion, in this sense, gives shape to the very being of beings.

Accordingly, we must attend to the way in which Aristotle positions time as that which is measured through motion, and motion as that which is perceived through the senses. The *Metaphysics* insists upon the priority of substance and actuality over potentiality, and it is within this ontological hierarchy that time and motion find their proper place. Aristotle observes that "being has two forms—it exists not only in fulfillment but also as matter" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 156), and this duality is essential for understanding how motion is the transition from potentiality to actuality, and how time measures this transition. Thus, the perception of time is not a merely passive reception of external stimuli but an active, discriminating process that involves the soul's capacity to hold together the before and after—a capacity that, as we shall see, depends intimately upon both *aisthēsis* and *phantasia*. It is to these interconnected themes that we now turn, beginning with Aristotle's account of motion.

## 2. Aristotle on Motion

Aristotle's account of motion stands as one of the most consequential contributions to Western philosophical thought, for it establishes that motion is neither a mere logical abstraction nor an accidental predicate of substances but rather a fundamental mode in which beings exist and manifest themselves. In the *Physics*, Aristotle delineates motion (*kinēsis*) as the fulfillment or actualization of what exists potentially insofar as it is potential—a definition that resists any reduction of change to a static category. The *Metaphysics* reinforces this point by insisting that the principles of natural philosophy must account for *kinēsis* and what is co-given with it; specifically, the basic modes of being include "ὄν δυνάμει and ἐντελεχείᾳ," that is, being in potentiality and being in fulfillment, and motion is identified as not something existing "παρὰ τὰ πράγματα"—not apart from the things themselves—but rather as a "how of the being of beings" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, pp. 262–263). This formulation is of paramount significance, for it means that motion is ontologically inseparable from substance; it characterizes the very way in which things are.

Thus, when Aristotle identifies motion as the actualization of potentiality, he is not describing a merely mechanical process but an ontological event through which the nature of a being comes to expression. The *Metaphysics* further elucidates this by attending to the interplay of the four causes; as Aristotle notes, "when the causes are being looked for, either all four must be sought thus or they must be sought in one of these four ways" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 14). Motion is intelligible only when we grasp the material, formal, efficient, and final causes that together account for a being's coming-to-be and passing-away. Hence, the study of motion is never separable from the study of causation.

Indeed, the Aristotelian concept of motion bears directly upon the rhetorical and existential dimensions of human life, a connection that Heidegger recovers with particular force. As noted in the scholarly literature, Heidegger interprets Aristotle's passions (*pathē*) as themselves modes of motion; passion "is both motion and a cause of motions; it is a capacity for altering" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). Passions, on this reading, are not static psychological states but dynamic affective movements that alter the soul's disposition and, consequently, its judgments. Heidegger observes that this is precisely where "passions intrude on logos—here articulated judgements, although frequently translated by historians of philosophy as 'reason'" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). This insight reveals that motion, for Aristotle, is not confined to the physical domain but permeates the entire structure of human engagement with the world, including the rhetorical situation in which speakers and audiences find themselves moved and altered. Subsequently, this understanding of motion as a fundamental ontological category prepares the ground for Aristotle's treatment of time, since time is, in his account, inseparable from the perception of motion.

## 3. Aristotle on Time

Aristotle's investigation of time, undertaken primarily in the *Physics* but informed by the broader metaphysical framework of the *Metaphysics* and supplemented by his psychological writings, proceeds from a deceptively simple observation: we perceive time whenever we perceive motion or change, and when we perceive no change, we judge that no time has elapsed. This observation anchors Aristotle's celebrated definition of time as the number of motion with respect to the before and after—a definition that locates time neither in the external world alone nor in the soul alone, but in the relation between the two. Specifically, Aristotle argues that time is not identical with motion but is rather something that belongs to motion; time is what we discern when we mark out the before and after in a continuous process of change.

The precision of this account becomes evident when we consider the role of measurement. As John Bowin elucidates, "Aristotle says that we measure time by counting units of time (e.g., days) which are determinate because they are marked out by motions that are determinate in time (e.g., celestial motions)" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 7). Counting, Bowin further notes, "is the type of measurement that is most exact because the measure that it involves is absolutely indivisible, rather than 'indivisible in relation to perception'" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 7). This passage illuminates a critical feature of Aristotle's temporal philosophy: time is measured through determinate motions, and the act of counting—of discriminating discrete units within the continuous flow of change—is itself a cognitive achievement that presupposes the soul's perceptual and intellectual capacities.

Accordingly, the question of time in Aristotle is never merely cosmological but always also psychological. Time's reality as a measure depends upon the existence of a soul capable of perceiving and numbering motion. The *Metaphysics* reinforces this by emphasizing that being exists both in fulfillment and as matter (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 156); similarly, time exists both as an objective feature of change and as something that is constituted through the soul's discriminating activity. Aristotle's rhetorical works, moreover, implicitly presuppose a temporal awareness on the part of speaker and audience, as the effective use of example and narrative—such as the telling of a fable—depends upon the audience's capacity to follow a temporal sequence and to draw inferences from past to present. Aristotle illustrates this principle with Aesop's fable of a fox swept into a hole in the rocks, who "not being able to get out, suffered miseries for a long time through the swarms of fleas that fastened on her" (Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, p. 71). Thus the rhetorical situation itself rests upon a shared temporal awareness that is grounded in the perception of motion and change.

## 4. The Relation Between Motion and Time

The relationship between motion and time in Aristotle's philosophy is one of mutual implication rather than mere correlation: time cannot be apprehended without the perception of motion, and motion cannot be fully understood without reference to the temporal framework within which it unfolds. This interdependence has profound consequences for both physics and psychology, for it means that any account of temporal awareness must simultaneously be an account of how the soul perceives and measures change. As Bowin explains, the measurement of time depends upon counting units that are "marked out by motions that are determinate in time," and this act of counting requires a cognitive faculty capable of distinguishing the before and after within a continuous process (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 7). Hence, motion and time are co-given: they arise together in the soul's experience of the natural world, and neither can be reduced to the other.

This co-givenness is illuminated further when we consider how the soul's situatedness shapes its apprehension of temporal change. Thomas Rickert argues that our understanding of situatedness must go beyond the familiar notion that we all "come from somewhere"; rather, that "somewhere" is itself an entangled situation, "only partially present at any given time and open to new disclosures" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 53). This insight, while developed in the context of contemporary rhetorical theory, resonates with the Aristotelian account of time and motion: the perceiving soul is always already situated within a dynamic environment, and its awareness of time is shaped by the motions that disclose themselves within that environment. Specifically, the perception of time is not a detached, contemplative act but an ambient, embodied engagement with the world's ongoing changes.

Similarly, the connection between motion and time bears upon the concept of *kairos*—the opportune moment—which Rickert notes "originally had a spatial dimension, though this early usage was gradually lost in favor of meanings such as 'opportunity' and 'decorum'" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 108). The kairotic moment is precisely that point at which motion and time converge in a way that calls for responsive action; it is the intersection of the soul's temporal awareness with the unfolding motions of the world. Accordingly, the relation between motion and time is not a peripheral topic in Aristotle's philosophy but stands at the very center of his account of natural being and human experience, mediating between the physical world and the soul's awareness of change.

## 5. Aisthēsis and Temporal Awareness

The faculty of *aisthēsis*—sense perception—occupies a pivotal position in Aristotle's account of temporal awareness, for it is through the senses that the soul first encounters the motions and changes that give rise to the experience of time. Without perception, there could be no discrimination of the before and after, no marking of the units by which time is measured, and thus no temporal consciousness at all. Aristotle's treatment of perception in the *De Anima* establishes that the senses are not passive receptacles but active capacities that discriminate among qualities, magnitudes, and incidental features of the objects they encounter. Indeed, the discriminating power of perception and the time of its exercise must be understood as one and undivided, for the soul grasps temporal succession only through the continuous activity of sensing.

Kenneth Burke's rhetorical theory, while not explicitly Aristotelian in its orientation, illuminates the relation between temporal awareness and the structures of identification that govern human communication. Burke discusses the "temporizing of essence," wherein "the logical idea of a thing's essence can be translated into a temporal or narrative equivalent by statement in terms of the thing's source or beginnings" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). This formulation reveals a deep structural parallel with Aristotle's account of *aisthēsis*: just as perception translates the continuous flow of change into determinate units of awareness, so rhetoric translates logical essences into temporal narratives. The rhetorical act of identification, on Burke's account, proceeds by "inducing the auditor to participate in the form, as a 'universal' locus of appeal, and next by trying to include a partisan statement within this same pale of assent" (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, p. 73). This process of identification is itself a temporal phenomenon, for it unfolds over time and depends upon the audience's capacity to perceive and follow the speaker's argument—a capacity that is, at root, perceptual.

Accordingly, *aisthēsis* is not merely a biological function but a condition of possibility for both temporal awareness and rhetorical engagement. The soul's ability to perceive motion is what enables it to measure time, and it is this same perceptual capacity that enables the audience to participate in the temporal unfolding of discourse. Burke's emphasis on the incipient or dispositional character of action reinforces this point: (Hawhee 2011) elsewhere regarding sentiment and incipient action, the active dimension of perception positions it as not merely receptive but preparatory for response (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 290). Hence, the connection between *aisthēsis* and temporal awareness is essential not only for Aristotle's physics and psychology but also for any account of rhetorical persuasion that takes seriously the temporal structure of human experience. Subsequently, we must consider how *phantasia*—the faculty of imagination—further elaborates and unifies the temporal data that perception initially provides.

## 6. Phantasia and Temporal Unification

If *aisthēsis* furnishes the soul with discrete temporal impressions—the perception of this change, followed by that change—then *phantasia* is the faculty by which these impressions are unified into a coherent temporal experience. Aristotle's account of *phantasia* in the *De Anima* presents it not as a mixture of sensation and judgment, as Plato had supposed, but as a distinct faculty that preserves and re-presents sensory images, thereby enabling the soul to retain the past and anticipate the future. Phantasia, importantly, is itself a kind of motion in the soul: it is the afterimage or residue of actual perception, and it operates even in the absence of the original sensory stimulus. As Ned O'Gorman notes, "in De Anima, phantasia is intimately connected to the sense of sight, preserving visual perceptions in a sort of after-image, and is itself a sort of psyche-sight, the source of mental images or visual impressions" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4). This characterization reveals that *phantasia* mediates between perception and thought, providing the soul with an internal representational capacity that is essential for temporal unification.

The significance of *phantasia* for temporal awareness becomes especially clear when we consider its role in deliberation and rhetorical persuasion. The speaker who employs vivid imagery—what Aristotle calls *energeia* or bringing-before-the-eyes—activates the audience's *phantasia*, causing them to see as present what is absent, past, or future. This capacity to make absent things appear present is not merely an ornamental feature of discourse but a fundamental condition of temporal consciousness: it is only because the soul possesses *phantasia* that it can hold together the before and after, the remembered and the anticipated, in a unified experience of time. O'Gorman  although phantasia depends on sense perceptions, "there is no need to assume any precise correspondence between a phantasma and that which it is a phantasma of" (O'Gorman, *Aristotle's Phantasia in the Rhetoric*, p. 4), which underscores the creative, synthetic character of this faculty and its relative autonomy from immediate sensory input.

We  Aristotle distinguishes between grades of phantasia—sensory phantasia, which is shared with animals and arises directly from perception, and deliberative phantasia, which involves the rational comparison of images and is proper to human beings. The latter is closely related to belief (*doxa*), yet phantasia differs from belief in that it is more voluntary: one can summon images before the mind at will, whereas belief is constrained by evidence and argument. These internal distinctions within the faculty of phantasia are essential for understanding its role in rhetorical persuasion and temporal unification; a more detailed treatment of these grades and their rhetorical implications will be developed in the subsequent chapter. Burke's account of identification further illuminates this point, for the process of "temporizing essence" depends upon the audience's phantastic capacity to translate logical structures into narrative sequences, thereby participating in the temporal form of the speaker's argument (Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives*, pp. 27–28). Thus, *phantasia* serves as the bridge between isolated sensory impressions and the continuous, unified experience of time that is necessary for both practical deliberation and rhetorical engagement.

## 7. Heidegger's Interpretation of Aristotle

Martin Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle constitutes one of the most consequential philosophical re-readings of the twentieth century, for it recovers dimensions of Aristotelian thought that had been obscured by centuries of scholastic and modern reception. Heidegger approaches Aristotle not as a precursor to the natural sciences but as a thinker who grasps the fundamental structures of being, and his reading transforms each of the major Aristotelian concepts we have examined—motion, time, perception, phantasia, and their interrelations—into existential categories that disclose the being of Dasein.

With respect to Aristotle's understanding of motion and perception more broadly , Heidegger reads Aristotle's account of *physis* as disclosing the self-showing character of beings, such that the principles of natural philosophy are not merely physical but ontological. Heidegger notes that Democritus was "borne by the matter itself," and thereby led to the recognition that "beings must be apprehended not merely with regard to ὕλη, but also with regard to their σχῆμα" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 176). This observation reveals Heidegger's conviction that Aristotle's natural philosophy already harbors an insight into the way beings present themselves—an insight that Heidegger will radicalize in his own ontology.

Regarding motion specifically , Heidegger insists that *kinēsis* is not a category applicable only to physical objects but is a fundamental way of being. As we have seen, Heidegger maintains that "kinesis should be understood as a fundamental way that Dasein is" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 27). This re-reading transforms Aristotle's physics into an existential analytic: motion is not something that merely happens to beings but is the very manner in which Dasein exists. The existential appropriation of *kinēsis* means that Dasein's being is essentially characterized by movement—by being underway, by projection and thrownness.

Concerning Aristotle's account of time , Heidegger re-reads the Aristotelian definition of time as the number of motion with respect to the before and after as an indication of Dasein's ecstatic temporality. For Heidegger, the Greek understanding of being is fundamentally temporal: being is grasped as presence (*Anwesenheit*), and this means that the Greek experience of time is always already an experience of the presencing of beings. The measurement of time through counting, which Bowin describes as "the type of measurement that is most exact because the measure that it involves is absolutely indivisible" (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 7), becomes in Heidegger's reading an index of Dasein's finite, ecstatic relation to time.

With respect to the relation between motion and time , Heidegger's  this co-givenness is not a cosmological fact but an existential structure. The entangled situatedness that Rickert describes—wherein "somewhere" is itself "only partially present at any given time and open to new disclosures" (Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric*, p. 53)—finds its Heideggerian counterpart in the notion of thrownness (*Geworfenheit*): Dasein always finds itself already in a situation that is temporally and kinetically structured.

Turning to *aisthēsis* and temporal awareness , Heidegger re-reads Aristotle's account of perception as a disclosure of being rather than a mere registration of sensory data. The passions (*pathē*), which Aristotle treats as modes of being affected, are interpreted by Heidegger as *Befindlichkeiten*—fundamental modes of attunement or disposition. As the scholarly literature notes, Heidegger observes that passions alter judgments, and "this is where passions intrude on logos—here articulated judgements, although frequently translated by historians of philosophy as 'reason'" (Multiple Authors, *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, p. 115). This re-reading transforms perception from a cognitive function into an existential attunement, a way of being disposed toward the world that is prior to any explicit act of judgment or reflection.

Finally, regarding *phantasia* and temporal unification , Heidegger's reading recovers the ontological significance of imagination as a faculty that is not merely reproductive but constitutive of the soul's relation to time. The specious present—what Bowin describes as the immediate, incessant duration of which we are sensible (Bowin, *Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time*, p. 17)—is for Heidegger an indication of Dasein's ecstatic unity, its capacity to hold together past, present, and future in a single, articulated experience. Heidegger thus reads *phantasia* not as a psychological curiosity but as a clue to the temporal structure of Dasein's being-in-the-world.

In sum, Heidegger's interpretation of Aristotle transforms the entire constellation of Aristotelian concepts—motion, time, perception, and imagination—into existential structures that disclose the being of Dasein. This transformation is not a distortion of Aristotle but, as Heidegger insists, a recovery of what was already implicit in the Greek understanding of being, even if it was never fully articulated by Aristotle himself. The Heideggerian reading thus opens up new possibilities for understanding the interplay of motion, time, and temporal awareness, and it prepares the ground for a richer account of rhetorical being that draws upon both Aristotelian and phenomenological resources.

## Conclusion

The foregoing investigation has traced the intricate web of connections that Aristotle draws among motion, time, perception, and imagination, and it has shown how these connections are deepened and transformed by Heidegger's existential interpretation. We have seen that motion, for Aristotle, is not a mere physical phenomenon but an ontological category that pervades the natural world and the human soul alike; that time is the measure of motion and depends for its apprehension upon the soul's perceptual capacities; that *aisthēsis* furnishes the discriminating power through which temporal succession is first encountered; and that *phantasia* unifies these discrete perceptual moments into a coherent temporal experience, thereby making possible both practical deliberation and rhetorical persuasion. Heidegger's re-reading of these Aristotelian concepts reveals their existential significance, transforming motion into a fundamental way of Dasein's being, time into the horizon of ecstatic temporality, perception into attunement, and imagination into a constitutive feature of Dasein's relation to the world. The rhetorical implications of this analysis are considerable, for if temporal awareness is not a merely cognitive achievement but an embodied, situated, and affectively attuned engagement with the world, then the task of rhetoric is not simply to persuade a detached rational subject but to attune the audience to the unfolding motions and temporal rhythms of the situation itself. The concept of *kairos*, which bridges the spatial and the temporal, the opportune and the ambient, emerges as a focal point for future inquiry—one that demands the integration of Aristotelian physics, Heideggerian phenomenology, and contemporary rhetorical theory. Accordingly, the chapters that follow will pursue this integration, attending to the ways in which the ambient, entangled character of situatedness shapes both the experience of time and the possibilities of rhetorical invention.
