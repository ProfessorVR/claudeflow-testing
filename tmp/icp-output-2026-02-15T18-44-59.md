Aristotle's definition of motion (kinesis) as the actualization of potentiality qua potential establishes a distinctive ontological category that resists reduction to conventional classifications, positioning motion as an intermediate mode of being that exists between pure potentiality and complete actuality (Aristotle, Physics III.1, p. 32). Indeed, Aristotle explicitly acknowledges the difficulty of situating motion within traditional genera, observing that "One could not easily put motion and change in another genus—this is plain if we consider where some people put it: they identify" motion with categories that fundamentally fail to capture its unique ontological status (Aristotle, Physics III.1, p. 32). The precision of the Greek formulation—"the actualization of the potential qua potential" (hē tou dunamei ontos entelecheia)—distinguishes motion from static actuality by emphasizing its character as ongoing realization rather than completed achievement, thus preserving the tension between what is and what is becoming (Aristotle, Physics III.1, p. 32). Heidegger's interpretation illuminates this dynamic structure by noting that "if a being has the character of σπέρμα, it is already δυνάμει ἄνθρωπος, for the seed must still pass over into another" state, thereby illustrating how potentiality inherently requires movement to achieve actualization and cannot remain in stasis without negating its essential character (Heidegger, 2009, p. 219)[9].

This definition captures motion's paradoxical nature as simultaneously actual—insofar as it is occurring—and potential—insofar as it remains incomplete, a duality that distinguishes kinesis fundamentally from energeia, or complete actuality (Aristotle, Physics III.1, p. 32). By characterizing motion as the actualization of what is potential precisely as potential, rather than as the mere transition from potentiality to full actuality, Aristotle establishes kinesis as a fundamental category of natural being that resists reduction to static states and cannot be adequately understood through the binary of complete potentiality and complete actuality alone. Heidegger clarifies this distinction by emphasizing that "kinesis is the actualization of the potential as such," meaning that motion preserves the character of incompleteness even while actualizing, whereas energeia represents a being that has achieved its telos and no longer possesses the dynamic character of becoming (Heidegger, 2009, p. 214)[10]. This interpretation represents the consensus reading of Physics III among contemporary scholars, though substantive debates persist about whether kinesis should be understood primarily as a temporal process unfolding through successive stages or as an ontological state characterized by constitutive incompleteness. The resolution of this debate bears directly on whether Aristotle's account privileges the kinetic dimension of change or the structural condition of being-in-motion as a distinctive mode of existence.

Aristotle's treatment of time in Physics IV establishes chronos as fundamentally relational, defining it as "the number of motion with respect to before and after" (arithmos kinēseōs kata to proteron kai husteron), a formulation that positions time as dependent upon both kinetic processes and the soul's capacity to enumerate or measure (Aristotle, Physics IV.14). This definition crucially distinguishes time from motion itself, indicating that temporal structure emerges not from movement per se but rather through the cognitive act of numbering motion's successive phases, whereby consciousness apprehends and differentiates the before and after inherent in change (Bowin, p. 16)[11]. Subsequently, Aristotle poses a fundamental aporia that exposes the problematic status of time's ontological independence: "One might find it difficult question, whether if there were no soul there would be time or not," a query that suggests time's existence may be inextricably bound to a counting consciousness capable of performing the requisite measurement (Aristotle, Physics IV.14). This concern extends to the structure of temporal succession itself, as Aristotle interrogates whether "time then always different or does the same time recur," revealing his preoccupation with the relationship between time and the continuity or discreteness of "nows" that constitute temporal experience (Aristotle, Physics IV, p. 64). The cumulative effect of these investigations positions time as neither a purely objective feature of physical reality nor a wholly subjective construction, but rather as an emergent phenomenon requiring both the material substrate of motion and the formal principle of psychic enumeration.
