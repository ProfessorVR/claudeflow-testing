# A₀: Motion and Time as First Actuality

## 1. A₀ as First Actuality

The architectonic of Aristotle's actualization chain begins not with perception, not with the rational soul's encounter with sensible objects, but with something more fundamental still: the sheer ontological fact that there is motion, and that there is time. We designate this ground-level stratum A₀ — the first actuality whose prior existence is the necessary condition for everything that follows in the chain of perceptual actualization. Without motion there can be no alteration, no locomotion of animal bodies toward objects, no transmission of sensible forms through media; without time there can be no before and after in which perception unfolds, no succession by which the soul moves from potentiality to actuality. A₀ is thus not merely one element among others in the chain but the ontological substratum that licenses the very possibility of a chain at all. To grasp the sense in which motion and time constitute a "first actuality," we must attend to the peculiar ontological status that Aristotle assigns to them. As Aristotle insists, "it is impossible that movement should either come into being or cease to be; for it must always have existed," and similarly, "nor can time come into being or cease to be; for there could not be a before and an after if time did not exist" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 144). Motion and time are accordingly eternal; they are not generated by any prior process but are always already underway. This eternity marks them as first actualities in a precise sense: they are the actualized conditions without which no further actualization — of substances, of qualities, of capacities — could take place. Burke captures a cognate principle when he observes that "one actuality always precedes another in time right back to the actuality of the eternal prime mover" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, p. 280). The chain of actualities is, for Aristotle, never a chain that begins from nothing; it is anchored in the perpetual actuality of cosmic motion and the time that accompanies it. The latter belongs to A₁, the level at which living beings hold their capacities in readiness. A₀ is prior to A₁ precisely because it supplies the world in which such capacities can be exercised. Indeed, Heidegger's reading of Aristotle emphasizes that motion is not simply a being among beings but rather "a how of being-there, such that the how of this there is something already present: look" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 266). Motion is the way in which beings are there at all — the manner of their presence — and thus constitutes the most basic stratum of actuality upon which all subsequent determinations depend. 

## 2. In Physics III.1, Aristotle defines κίνησις as the actuality (ἐντελέχεια) of the potential qua potential — a formulation that has generated centuries of interpretive difficulty precisely because it appears to locate motion in an ontological space between pure potentiality and completed actuality. Heidegger, commenting on the passage at Physics 201a9–15, characterizes it as the "genuine definition of movement" and notes that it rests upon the recognition that the categories themselves admit of a determinate "twofoldness" (διχῶς), a capacity to be "thus-and-so" that constitutes "the ontological condition of the fact that the beings that are determined by these categories are possible beings in movement" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 210). The twofoldness to which Heidegger draws attention is the dual character of every being that falls under one of the relevant categories: it is both what it actually is and what it is capable of becoming, and motion is the actualization of the latter aspect precisely as that aspect. Subsequently, this definition demands careful unpacking if we are to see why motion constitutes an energeia ateles — an incomplete actuality. The crucial point is that motion, unlike a complete actuality such as seeing or thinking, does not possess its end simultaneously with its exercise. As Aristotle notes in the Metaphysics, "where the result is something apart from the exercise, the actuality is in the thing that is being made, e.g. the act of building is in the thing that is being built and that of weaving in the thing that is being woven, and similarly in all other cases, and in general the movement is in the thing that is being moved" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 110). Motion is always directed toward an end it has not yet achieved; it is energeia, certainly, but an energeia that is constitutively incomplete, always on the way toward a terminus it does not yet possess. Hence it is "incomplete" not in the sense of being deficient but in the sense of being structurally open: its very being consists in the not-yet-having-arrived. This structural incompleteness is what makes motion so foundational for the actualization chain. If motion were simply a completed state, it could not serve as the ongoing medium through which potentialities are progressively actualized. Burke captures the temporal dimension of this point when he describes "the continuity of motion (or 'duration') which we instinctively sense" as something that "is analyzed by intellectual concepts into a succession of disconnected steps or stages, as the movement of the actors in a motion picture is photographed by a series of stills, with an unphotographed hiatus between each exposure and the next" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 431–433). Burke's analogy illuminates the challenge inherent in conceptualizing motion: it is continuous, but our conceptual apparatus tends to break it into discrete moments. Aristotle's definition resists this discretization by insisting that motion is a single, continuous actuality — the actuality of the potential as such — and thus cannot be reduced to a series of completed states. Furthermore, Heidegger's reading deepens the ontological significance of this incompleteness. 213–215). The point is that ἐνέργεια was originally at home in the domain of motion before it was extended to designate the fully completed actualities of substance and form. Motion is thus, in a genetic sense, the primary locus of actuality — the domain from which the very concept of actuality was drawn. For the purposes of our actualization chain, this means that A₀ is not merely temporally prior but conceptually foundational: the notion of actuality that governs the entire chain from A₁ through A₂ has its origin in the incomplete actuality of motion itself. Indeed, it is precisely because motion is an incomplete actuality that it can serve as the ground for a chain — it is the ongoing process within which further actualities emerge. 

## 3. Priority of Actuality and the Chain Structure

The chain structure of Aristotelian actualization depends upon a rigorous doctrine of the priority of actuality over potentiality. This doctrine, articulated most fully in Metaphysics Theta, holds that actuality is prior to potentiality in three senses: in formula (λόγῳ), in time (χρόνῳ), and in substance (οὐσίᾳ). Each of these senses of priority is essential for understanding how A₀ — the actuality of motion and time — serves as the ground from which A₁ can emerge. Burke provides a particularly illuminating gloss on this Aristotelian principle. 280–281). A stage that follows another in time may nonetheless be prior in form and substantiality; accordingly, "man is 'prior' to boy because man has already attained its complete form whereas boy has not" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 280–281). This double priority — temporal and formal — is precisely what structures the relationship between A₀ and A₁. Motion and time are temporally prior to the actualization of perceptual capacities because they must already be underway before any sense-organ can be activated; but they are also formally prior insofar as they provide the very medium of actuality through which form is realized. The chain structure, moreover, is not merely a linear sequence but a nested hierarchy in which each level presupposes all those beneath it. A₁ — the level at which rational souls, sensible objects, and sense-organs possess their first actualities as capacities — cannot exist without A₀ because the very capacities that define A₁ are capacities for processes that unfold in time and through motion. Aristotle makes clear that motion exists in four categorical domains: "Οὐσία corresponds to the mode of movement of γένεσις and φθορά, for τόπος there is φορά, for ποσόν there is φθίσις, for ποιόν there is ἀλλοίωσις" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 234). Perception, as an instance of ἀλλοίωσις — qualitative alteration — thus falls under the domain of motion; and if motion itself were not actual, no qualitative alteration, and hence no perception, could occur. Furthermore, the priority of actuality has implications for how we understand the relationship between the mover and the moved. Aristotle argues that "everything that is in motion must be moved by something, and by something either moved by something else or not," and that the chain of movers cannot proceed to infinity but must terminate in "some first mover that is not itself moved by anything else" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 116). This unmoved mover is pure actuality — the apex of the chain whose eternal actuality guarantees the perpetuity of cosmic motion. A₀ thus derives its own permanence from this first principle, and it is this derivative yet eternal actuality that in turn grounds the possibility of all subsequent levels. 

## 4. The Relational Structure of Kinesis

Motion, for Aristotle, is never an isolated event but always a relational phenomenon. This relational character is essential to understanding A₀ as a ground that conditions not merely individual processes but the totality of involvements within which perception takes place. Heidegger, in his reading of the Physics, emphasizes that "the being-context about which I say, 'the being is in movement,' is determined by the category of the πρός τι, the 'being-in-relation-to-an-other,' namely, the moving thing characterized by ποίησις, the moved thing by πάθησις" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 232). Accordingly, there are "two modes of being-present in relation to movement: ποίησις and πάθησις" (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 232). The mover and the moved are not self-subsistent entities that accidentally enter into relation; rather, their very being as mover and moved is constituted by the relation itself. This relational structure is of profound importance for the transition from A₀ to A₁. The sensible object and the sense-organ, at the level of A₁, stand in precisely the relational configuration that Heidegger identifies: the object exercises its capacity to act (ποίησις) upon the organ, which in turn undergoes (πάθησις) the reception of the sensible form. But this relational structure is not something that emerges de novo at A₁; it is already present at A₀, in the general character of motion as such. Indeed, Aristotle argues in Physics V that "motion can belong neither to substance nor to relation nor to agent and patient," but rather that "there can be motion only in respect of quality, quantity, and place; for with each of these we have a pair of contraries" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 70). The relational character of motion is not itself a species of motion but is instead the form that all species of motion necessarily take — every kinesis involves a mover, a moved, and the passage between contraries. Similarly, Burke's dramatistic framework captures this relational quality in its insistence that "relations are 'determinations'" that "assign borders (termini) to a thing," a notion he equates with "conditions," since "conditions are likewise contextual, as with the conditions of an organism's existence" (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 214–215). The conditions under which something moves are always contextual; motion does not occur in a void but in a web of relations that determine what moves, what is moved, and the direction of change. Hence A₀ is not merely the bare fact that motion exists but the totality of involvements that the relational structure of motion establishes — a context within which the more specific relations of perception can subsequently unfold. Moreover, the relational structure of kinesis anticipates the relational structure of perception at A₁. Just as the mover and the moved are constituted by their relation, so too the sensible object and the perceiver are constituted by their perceptual relation. The colour is not a sensible quality apart from its capacity to affect a sense-organ, and the eye is not a perceptual organ apart from its capacity to be affected by colour. This parallel between kinetic relation and perceptual relation is what makes A₀ the genuine ground of A₁: the form of the relation is already established at the level of motion; perception merely specifies it within the domain of ἀλλοίωσις. 

## 5. Chronos as the Number of Motion

Time, for Aristotle, is not an independent substance but is constitutively bound to motion. The canonical definition at Physics IV.11 holds that χρόνος is "the number of motion in respect of the before and after." This definition binds time inextricably to the structure of motion: where there is no motion, there is no time, and where there is time, there is motion. Aristotle makes the connection explicit when he asserts that "movement also is continuous, then, in the sense in which time is; for time is either the same thing as movement or an attribute of movement" (Aristotle, *Metaphysics*, p. 144). This passage is instructive because it establishes not merely a correlation but an ontological dependence: time's continuity is parasitic upon the continuity of motion, and its existence is inseparable from the existence of movement. Furthermore, the characterization of time as a "number" (ἀριθμός) introduces a crucial ambiguity. Time is not a number in the abstract sense but a number that is numbered — that is, it is motion insofar as motion admits of enumeration. This enumeration requires something that counts: a soul capable of distinguishing the before from the after. Here we encounter a tension that Aristotle himself acknowledges, albeit obliquely. At Physics IV.14, he raises the question of whether time could exist without soul, since numbering requires a numberer; but motion, the substrate of time, can exist without soul. This tension — between time as an objective feature of motion and time as dependent upon a counting soul — is one that reverberates through the entire actualization chain, for it suggests that A₀ is not entirely independent of A₁ but is, to some extent, completed by it. We shall return to this point in the following section. The continuity of time is grounded in the continuity of magnitude and motion. Aristotle observes that "motion, as was said, goes with magnitude, and time, as we maintain, with motion" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 60). The "now" (νῦν) functions as the boundary between past and future; "in so far as it is in succession, it is different (which is just what its being now was supposed to mean), but its substratum is the same" (Aristotle, *Physics*, p. 60). The now is thus both a limit and a connector: it divides time into segments while simultaneously maintaining the continuity of the temporal flow. This dual function of the now is essential for understanding how time conditions perception, for perception requires both continuity — the ongoing receptivity of the sense-organ — and succession — the before-and-after ordering that allows the soul to distinguish one sensible impression from another. Subsequently, the relationship between time and the before-and-after in motion provides the formal structure within which the actualization chain unfolds. If time is the number of motion in respect of the before and after, then every actualization that occurs within time is ordered — there is a before in which potentiality obtains and an after in which actuality is achieved. This ordering is precisely what makes it possible to speak of a chain of actualities: A₀ is before A₁ not merely in a logical sense but in the temporal sense that is grounded in the very nature of time as the number of motion. 

## 6. The Soul's Constitutive Role and the Motion–Time Parallel

The question of whether time depends upon soul introduces a remarkable circularity into the relationship between A₀ and A₁. If time is the number of motion in respect of the before and after, and if numbering requires a soul to perform the act of counting, then it would seem that time — which we have designated as part of A₀ — presupposes the existence of soul — which belongs to A₁. This apparent circularity is not, however, a vicious one; it is rather an expression of the deeply intertwined character of the Aristotelian cosmos, in which the conditions for perception are themselves conditioned, in part, by the existence of perceivers. Aristotle's treatment of the soul's role in constituting time must be read against the broader background of his psychology. As he notes, "certain living beings — a small minority — possess calculation and thought, for (among mortal beings) those which possess calculation have all the other powers above mentioned, while the converse does not hold — indeed some live by imagination alone, while others have not even imagination" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 22). The hierarchy of psychic capacities — from nutrition through perception to thought — mirrors the hierarchy of actualities in our chain. But it is perception, not thought, that is most directly relevant to the constitution of time, for it is through perception that the soul encounters the before and after in motion and thereby constitutes time as numbered. Papachristou's analysis of phantasia in Aristotle is pertinent here, for she notes the importance of leaving the term φαντασία untranslated in order to preserve its specific Aristotelian sense, observing that "even though the Greek word 'phantasia' is usually translated it as 'imagination', I prefer to leave the word untranslated" (Papachristou, *Three Kinds or Grades of Phantasia in Aristotle's De Anima*, pp. 9–10). This terminological precision matters because phantasia — the capacity to retain and manipulate sensory images — is precisely the faculty through which the soul bridges the gap between immediate perception and temporal awareness. The φάντασμα, as a memory-image, functions simultaneously as an object of present contemplation and as a likeness (εἰκών) of a thing past; it thereby enables the soul to hold the before and the after together in a single act of awareness. Without this capacity, the soul could not number motion and time would remain, as it were, unnumbered. Furthermore, the motion–time parallel extends into the domain of deliberative imagination. Aristotle observes that "deliberative imagination" is found "only in those that are calculative: for whether this or that shall be enacted is already a task requiring calculation; and there must be a single standard to measure by, for that is pursued which is greater" (Aristotle, *On The Soul (De Anima)*, p. 52). The act of measuring — whether it concerns practical deliberation or the numbering of motion — requires a standard, and this standard is itself a form of actuality that the soul brings to the world. Hence the soul's constitutive role in the motion–time structure is not that of a passive mirror reflecting an independent reality but that of an active principle that completes the actuality of time by providing the numbering function. This is perhaps the most striking instance of the mutual dependence between levels in the actualization chain: A₀ grounds A₁, but A₁ in turn completes A₀. Burke's dramatistic vocabulary offers a productive frame for this circularity. 240–242). The ambiguity between motion and action is precisely the ambiguity at stake in the soul's relationship to time: time is, on the one hand, an attribute of motion that exists independently of any action; but on the other hand, it is constituted as numbered time only through the soul's active engagement with motion. The "orbit of mechanical motion" becomes a "field of action" only when a soul enters the picture, transforming mere succession into measured temporality (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 240–242). Thus A₀ and A₁ are not strictly sequential but mutually implicating: the world of motion and time is the condition for the existence of souls, but souls are the condition for the full actualization of time. This mutual implication does not, however, undermine the priority of A₀. Even if time qua numbered requires a soul, motion itself does not; and it is motion, not numbered time, that is the most fundamental stratum of A₀. The soul completes A₀ by numbering motion, but A₀ makes the soul possible by providing the motion through which perceptual capacities are activated. The priority is thus asymmetric: A₀ can exist in an incomplete form — as unnumbered motion — without A₁, but A₁ cannot exist at all without A₀. 

## 7. Synthesis: A₀ as Ground of A₁

We are now in a position to articulate the full sense in which A₀ — the first actuality of motion and time — serves as the ground of A₁, the level at which rational souls, sensible objects, and sense-organs possess their first actualities as capacities. The totality of involvements that constitutes A₀ is thus not a bare, featureless substratum but a richly structured ontological ground. It includes the eternal motion of the cosmos, maintained by the unmoved mover; the time that accompanies this motion as its number; the relational structure of mover and moved that prefigures the relational structure of perceiver and perceived; and the continuity and succession that make possible the before-and-after ordering of the actualization chain. Burke's insight that relations are "conditions" and "contextual" applies with full force here: A₀ is the totality of conditions — cosmic, temporal, relational — within which A₁ can emerge (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, pp. 214–215). Accordingly, every element at A₁ — the sense-organ holding its capacity as a hexis, the sensible object exercising its power to affect, the medium through which sensible forms are transmitted — presupposes the prior actuality of motion and time. The architectural function of this section within the dissertation is thus clear: it licenses everything downstream. If A₀ is not first established as the ground, then the actualization chain from A₁ through A₂ hangs in the air, lacking the ontological support that makes it intelligible. By demonstrating that motion and time are eternal, incomplete, relational, and constitutively bound to the soul, we have secured the foundation upon which the subsequent analysis of perception can be built.\footnote{A fuller account of the relationship between A₀ and Heidegger's existential-temporal analytic in \textit{Being and Time} is deferred to the Development Note below, where we outline the lines along which Heidegger's re-reading of Aristotle transforms the ontological ground we have here established.}

## Development Note

Heidegger's engagement with Aristotle's natural philosophy — pursued most intensively in the 1924 lecture course published as *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* and in the existential-temporal analytic of *Being and Time* — offers a systematic re-reading of every concept we have established in the preceding sections. The purpose of this Development Note is to outline, in a preliminary fashion, how Heidegger's interpretation transforms the Aristotelian ground of A₀ and thereby opens the path toward a deeper understanding of the actualization chain. With respect to A₀ as first actuality, Heidegger reinterprets the concept of ἐνέργεια not as a static property of the cosmos but as a mode of being-there — a way in which beings are present. 168–169). First actuality, in this reading, is not an eternal fact about the cosmos but a phenomenological structure of presence — the way in which beings show themselves at each moment. This reorientation from cosmology to phenomenology is decisive for the entire chain. With respect to kinesis as energeia ateles, Heidegger's reading emphasizes that motion is "a how of being-there" rather than a being among beings (Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, p. 266). The incomplete actuality of motion becomes, in Heidegger's hands, the existential structure of Dasein's own temporal projection — the way in which Dasein is always underway toward possibilities that it has not yet realized. This connection between Aristotelian kinesis and Dasein's existential temporality constitutes one of the most fertile lines of inquiry opened by *Being and Time*. With respect to the priority of actuality and the chain structure, Heidegger challenges the traditional metaphysical reading by insisting that the priority of actuality is not a temporal priority of one entity over another but a structural priority of presence over absence — the priority of the way in which beings are disclosed over the way in which they are concealed. Dasein's Being, he contends, "is essentially not a Being-present-at-hand" and "its 'spatiality' cannot signify anything like occurrence at a position in 'world-space'" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 139). The chain structure is thus reinterpreted not as a series of cosmological events but as a series of disclosive structures. With respect to the relational structure of kinesis, Heidegger's concept of Mitsein — Being-with — transforms the Aristotelian πρός τι into an existential category. 154–156). The relational structure of motion is thus not merely a physical relation between mover and moved but an existential relation between Dasein and the world it inhabits. With respect to chronos as the number of motion, Heidegger's re-reading in *Being and Time* §78 directly engages Aristotle's definition, arguing that it captures only the most derivative level of temporality — what Heidegger calls Innerzeitigkeit, or within-time-ness. Authentic temporality, by contrast, is grounded in Dasein's anticipatory resoluteness and cannot be reduced to the counting of motion. Yet the Aristotelian definition remains indispensable as the starting point from which Heidegger's own analysis of temporality proceeds (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, p. 139). With respect to the soul's constitutive role and the motion–time parallel, Heidegger's account transforms the Aristotelian soul into Dasein — the entity for which its own Being is an issue. The φάντασμα as memory-image, which bridges perception and temporal awareness in Aristotle, finds its Heideggerian counterpart in the ecstatic temporality of Dasein, which holds past, present, and future together in a single structure of care (Sorge). Heidegger's rethinking thus deepens the circularity we identified between A₀ and A₁: it is not merely that the soul completes time by numbering it, but that Dasein constitutes temporality as the horizon within which all beings — including those in motion — can show themselves at all (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, pp. 154–156). With respect to the synthesis of A₀ as ground of A₁, Heidegger's contribution is to show that the ontological ground is not a cosmological substrate but a phenomenological horizon. The totality of involvements that we identified as A₀ becomes, in Heidegger's terms, the world — not in the sense of the physical cosmos but in the sense of the referential totality within which entities are encountered. It is this transformation — from cosmos to world, from substance to Dasein, from chronos to existential temporality — that the subsequent chapters of this dissertation will pursue in detail. ---
