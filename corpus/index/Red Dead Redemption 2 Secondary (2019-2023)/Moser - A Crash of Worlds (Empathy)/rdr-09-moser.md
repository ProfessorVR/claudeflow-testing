# Moser, "'A Crash of Worlds'" (2022) — Empathy through Character Performance

**Citation.** Moser, Heather Rose. "'A Crash of Worlds': How *Red Dead Redemption II* Creates a World Where Players Experience Empathy Through Character Performance." MA thesis, Brigham Young University (Department of Theatre and Media Arts), 2022. (ProQuest no. 29222749; BYU ScholarsArchive, Theses and Dissertations 9444.)

**Cluster ID.** rdr-09 · slug `moser-crash-of-worlds-empathy` · discipline: theatre/media arts (performance studies + phenomenology + game studies) · depth: deep-long.

---

## Thesis

The article argues that open-world role-playing games, and *Red Dead Redemption II* specifically, do not merely entertain but actively cultivate empathy in the player. The mechanism is threefold: **character attachment**, **duration (length) of play**, and **identification in a safe experimental space**. The load-bearing move is to recast the player as a *performer*—an actor who researches, inhabits, and embodies the protagonist Arthur Morgan rather than a spectator who watches a narrative unfold. Because the game forces decisions that materially shape the story and its characters, the player's choices become entangled with Arthur's, producing an emotional bond that the article claims transfers outward: empathy practiced on a virtual "other" becomes a "bridge" toward empathy for real people who are different from the player. The whole case is built by synthesizing Husserlian phenomenology (the *lifeworld*), Stanislavskian acting theory ("the method"), and a body of empathy/affect scholarship (Manney, Wulansari et al., Morrison & Ziemke, Klimmt et al., Bowman et al.).

## Structure

The thesis is explicitly tripartite, each section building on the last:

1. **Phenomenology and Ludology** — establishes the conceptual ground. Subsections: *Game Mechanics* (open-world rules, the RDR2 map, the honor scale, the four endings, John Marston epilogue); *The Lifeworld* (Husserl/Føllesdal on subjective worlds colliding and overlapping); *The Experience of Play* (paidia vs. ludus, possibility space, Nacke's immersion phases, Jenkins on guilt).
2. **Players as Performers** — maps acting theory onto play. Subsections: *Research for the Role* (Stanislavski's magic "if"; the snowy-mountain tutorial; first-time player as audience vs. replayer as actor); *Differing Approaches and Emotional Connection to the Character* (objectives/super-objective; Schechner's "not-not-me"; spectator-to-participant transfer).
3. **Developing Empathy** — the payoff. Subsections: *Embodying Character and Experience* (character attachment; Morrison & Ziemke's "user-character empathy"); *Living Vicariously Through the Character* (moral intuition, honor-driven guilt, mirror neurons, the horse's death); *Empathizing With the Other* (storytelling as social-empathy generator; NPC stories; the limits and real-world transfer).

A short Conclusion restates the threefold claim and flags future work: how player **race and gender** inflect the performance of Arthur; online/multiplayer dimensions; and the need for quantitative data on how players actually distribute across the honor spectrum.

## Key concepts

- **Empathy** — treated as a vital, trainable interpersonal skill (understanding and sharing another's feelings); the article leans on Manney's claim that storytelling is the key to building it, and on Wulansari et al. for its social value. Empathy is distinguished from mere *sympathy* via Stanislavski's spectator-to-participant account.
- **Character performance / acting** — the central frame. The player is an actor: given a "script" (the game's parameters), the player researches the role, uses imagination to fill gaps, pursues objectives, and produces an interpretation of Arthur. Stanislavski's "the actor does not live, he plays" anchors the claim that play, like acting, is real feeling within acknowledged artifice.
- **The lifeworld** (Husserl, via Føllesdal) — each person's subjective, first-person world. The article's signature image: the player's lifeworld "collides" with Arthur's and—unlike ordinary intersubjective contact—achieves a *full overlap*, a "doubled structure" in which the player steps inside Arthur's world while bringing their own. Title phrase "a crash of worlds" names this collision.
- **Identification** — Klimmt et al.'s "automatic shift of self-perceptions": the audience member imaginatively replaces their own identity with the character's. The article argues games *intensify* this beyond film/novels because the player acts rather than watches.
- **Character attachment** (Bowman et al.; Lewis, Weber & Bowman in bibliography) — a gradual "melding of minds" formed over hours of play, not at first contact.
- **User-character empathy** (Morrison & Ziemke) — empathy the player develops specifically for the avatar they control; grounded neurologically in **mirror neurons** that map observed movement/emotion onto the observer's own body.
- **Not-not-me** (Schechner) — the double-negative realm of restored behavior: the player is *not* Arthur but also *not not* Arthur. Evidenced by players' slippage between "Arthur did X" and "I/we did X" in speech.
- **Possibility space / paidia–ludus** (Jensen; Frasca) — RDR2 blends rule-bound *ludus* with free, experimental *paidia*; meaning and pleasure reside in the bounded-but-free "possibility space." Jensen's **narrauthor vs. simauthor** distinguishes designers who dictate paths from those who only legislate rules.
- **Moral choice / honor** — the honor system makes ethics legible and consequential; the article reads it through Jenkins's "ethical testing grounds" and Hartmann et al.'s finding that *unjustified* virtual violence produces guilt in empathetic players.
- **Magic "if"** (Stanislavski) — the imaginative lever ("if this were happening, how would the character act?") that the article equates with the player's experimentation in the world.

## Readings (interpretive claims)

- Open-world RPGs differ in kind from non-narrative genres (sports, FPS, battle royale), which the article suggests may *dampen* rather than build empathy; the empathy claim is scoped to character-and-story-driven simulation games.
- RDR2's restrictions (clouded map, impassable rock walls, instant death west of Blackwater, the "invisible sniper") are read not as failures of openness but as necessary narrative scaffolding—rules that "make the game possible."
- The player occupies a *triple* position—performer, character, and audience simultaneously—and a "special empathy connection" is established across all three at once (extending Schechner's performer/text/environment/audience quadrangle).
- First-time players are more like spectators (discovering the story live), which paradoxically yields a more "sincere" performance; replayers align more closely with the trained actor who "knows the end from the beginning" (illustrated with the Othello analogy).
- Guilt over fictional characters' fates is, per Jenkins, nearly unique to games because the player *chooses* what happens; this is the article's strongest lever connecting choice, responsibility, and felt emotion.
- The honor system carries an "implicit ideology" (Jensen's implicit/manipulation rules): without a mandated honor goal, the game still tacitly rewards the notion that "good returns to those who put good out in the world."

## Game-elements treated (precise)

- **Arthur Morgan's redemptive character arc** — central. Arthur, Dutch's lieutenant and "surrogate son" in the Van der Linde gang (fleeing the failed Blackwater heist, saving for Tahiti), naturally moves toward recognizing guilt for his past and resolving to "do better"; the article stresses the arc bends with player input (honorable/dishonorable portrayal).
- **The Downes encounter + tuberculosis** — read as the narrative's rigid, choice-free hinge. As debt collector for the moneylender Herr Strauss, Arthur is *forced* to beat the farmer Mr. Downes, who coughs blood on Arthur's face; Downes had TB and infects Arthur. Arthur's advancing tuberculosis and impending death drive the mortality/morality reckoning that fuels the arc. The article notes this scene removes player choice precisely so the plot can cohere.
- **The honor system / honor scale** — extensively analyzed as RDR2's distinctive, empathy-relevant mechanic. A red-to-white gradient bar with a tracking cowboy figure; skull-faced red icon (with negative sign/sound) for dishonor, white cowboy (positive sign/sound) for honor. Consequences: store discounts up to 50% / raised prices; friendlier or more hostile NPCs; honor-gated special items (e.g., an ebony revolver grip at a dishonor threshold); altered dialogue, cutscenes, and vision-dreams (black coyote in a lightning storm = low honor; stag in sun rays = high honor).
- **The four endings + spirit-animal visions** — honor and final choices yield four outcomes. The endgame: Arthur discovers Micah Bell is a Pinkerton mole; Dutch goes "beyond reason"; a shootout with Dutch/Pinkertons follows. Player chooses to help John return to his family or grab the camp loot. Dishonorable → Micah kills Arthur, coyote vision plays; honorable → Arthur succumbs to TB, stag vision appears. The "spirit animals" function as a final reminder that player action shaped the *kind of man* Arthur became, even if the basic plot is fixed.
- **Arthur's grave (epilogue as John Marston)** — after Arthur's death players control John; visiting Arthur's grave shows the consequence of honor: high-honor grave adorned with flowers ("Blessed Are Those Who Hunger and Thirst for Righteousness"); low-honor grave bare ("Blessed Are Those Who Mourn for They Will Be Comforted"). Cited via Metz (TheGamer).
- **The Saint Denis "pet" shack (implied rape)** — a hidden, optional dark event triggered only by exploring an unvisited building outside Saint Denis: a "friendly" man knocks Arthur unconscious; a black screen, a belt unbuckling, the man calling Arthur his "pet"—implying Arthur is raped; Arthur wakes in a clearing with drained vitals. The article uses it to mark a key player/character lifeworld asymmetry: the player can *reload a save* to "undo" the event for Arthur, but the player's own lifeworld still remembers it. **diss-hook:** the unbuckling-belt / drained-vitals scene and its reload-to-undo option is a precise screen-side companion to the dissertation's RDR2 rape-scene/disruption analysis—the player's irreversible memory vs. the avatar's erasable experience dramatizes the actualization-of-desire chain's break between deed, consequence, and recollection (anticipatory-application).
- **The horse's death** — near Arthur's end his horse is shot and killed; the article makes this a touchstone for genuine grief over a virtual loss ("that horse was also theirs"), illustrating user-character empathy and the affective stakes of attachment.
- **The snowy-mountain tutorial** — the opening hideout teaches movement, shooting, hunting, fistfighting, riding, scavenging, the weapon/item wheel, and the satchel—framed as the actor being handed "techniques and tools," with deliberately thin backstory at first.
- **Magic-"if" experiments** — concrete examples of paidia-style testing: robbing the Valentine general store (and the escalating bounty/lawmen if the owner is killed or escape fails); the countryside snakebite rescue (do nothing/he dies; give a cure or suck out the venom—earning honor and a free gun from the grateful man in town).
- **Morally weighted incidents** — the KKK meeting (killing members is honor-*neutral*); the prisoner-coach rescue (killing the two officers earns dishonor, freeing the innocent prisoner restores some honor); the "justified violence" logic from Hartmann et al. that lets players bypass guilt.
- **Map / containment mechanics** — clouded fog-of-war revealed by travel (with Arthur's hunting notes); yellow main-mission markers and white side-quest dots (some time-gated); impassable rock walls; the "wanted dead or alive" red zone east of Blackwater patrolled by Pinkertons; instant death on crossing the river west of Blackwater (the "invisible sniper" of the designers).
- **NPC stories / Arthur's journal** — optional NPC conversations and the readable in-game journal are treated as empathy-builders that let players "listen" to the other and access Arthur's most private self (singing/whistling alone, hunting), the condition the actor most desires.

## Themes

- Storytelling as the engine of empathy and potential social/cultural change (Manney).
- Phenomenological intersubjectivity: worlds colliding, overlapping, and reshaping one another through play.
- Performance/acting as a model of ethical and emotional participation.
- The "safe experimental space" / ethical testing ground where consequences stay (mostly) in-game.
- Responsibility and guilt as evidence of attachment; care-labor toward the avatar (feeding, dressing Arthur).
- Real-world transfer and its limits (the geisha example; "learning a language" analogy—empathy must finally be practiced on real people).
- Reflexive call to industry: design more honor-scale-like systems that encourage empathy.

## Debate-axis positioning

- **Player as performer/actor vs. player as spectator/operator** — Moser plants firmly on the *performer* side, importing acting theory wholesale; this is her distinctive axis-shift relative to cluster-mates who read RDR2 as text, ideology, or historical artifact.
- **Empathy-affirmative vs. empathy-skeptical (and the "empathy machine" debate)** — strongly affirmative: games as empathy generators. She concedes limits (games are no substitute for real intercultural contact; the field is split between "games make players violent" and "games teach empathy"), positioning her work in the prosocial camp against the violence-effects tradition (Anderson et al.).
- **Phenomenological/experiential vs. structural/ideological reading** — Moser is experiential/first-person (lifeworld, immersion, felt emotion), contrasting with ideology-critique approaches in the cluster (e.g., Tuominen).
- **Honor/morality as designed ethics** — she reads the honor system as a tacit moral pedagogy ("implicit ideology"), aligning with the morality-focused strand (Hoffmann, Hartmann et al., Jenkins) while staying descriptive rather than critical.

## Tensions / limits (article-internal)

- **Transfer claim under-evidenced.** The central leap—from in-game user-character empathy to real-world empathy for different people—rests on theory and analogy (geisha; language-learning), not on the empirical/quantitative data the conclusion admits is missing.
- **Race/gender bracketed.** The thesis concedes that how a player's own race and gender inflect performing Arthur is unexamined (the "Black woman vs. White man" example), a significant gap for a project about empathizing across difference.
- **Choice vs. constraint friction.** The performer/agency frame sits uneasily with the many forced or predetermined beats (the Downes beating; fixed plot spine; four pre-authored endings)—the article repeatedly has to reassure that rigidity is necessary, slightly straining the "actor with creative freedom" analogy.
- **Honor reload paradox.** The save-reload "undo" both enables the safe-space thesis and undercuts the stakes/consequence logic on which felt responsibility depends (acknowledged via the Saint Denis case but not fully resolved).
- **Single-player, single-game scope.** Online/multiplayer and cross-game generalization are deferred to future work.
