# Crowley, Silk & Crowley (2021) — "The Educational Value of Virtual Ecologies in *Red Dead Redemption 2*"

- **ID:** rdr-04-crowley
- **Citation:** Crowley, E. J., Silk, M. J., & Crowley, S. L. (2021). The educational value of virtual ecologies in *Red Dead Redemption 2*. *People and Nature*, 3(6), 1229–1243. (British Ecological Society / Wiley; special issue "Nature on Screen," ed. Jamie Lorimer.)
- **Discipline:** conservation science / environmental education / human–nature relations (empirical, mixed-methods)
- **Depth:** deep

---

## Thesis

The article argues that a big-budget entertainment game not designed for teaching can nonetheless carry genuine *educational value*: through its detailed open-world simulation of late-19th-century North American ecosystems, *Red Dead Redemption 2* (RDR2) measurably improves players' ability to identify real-world wildlife and indirectly teaches them about animal behaviour, habitats and ecological interactions. The authors frame this around "vicarious experiences" of nature — encounters mediated through representation rather than direct physical contact — and contend that such virtual experiences, while different in quality from embodied ones, are not necessarily less informative or affecting. Their core empirical claim is that playing RDR2 is consistently associated with higher species-identification skill, an effect strengthened by completing the main storyline, playing more recently, and playing the online "Naturalist" role. The broader programmatic claim is that ecologists, educators and conservationists should take popular commercial games seriously as a "communicative force," not dismiss them as the antithesis of engaging with nature.

## Structure

The piece follows a standard empirical-article arc. The **Introduction** has three movements: (1.1) video games and conservation education — surveying the underused potential of games for ecological learning and the Pokémon precedent (Balmford et al.); (1.2) vicarious experiences — building, after Clayton et al. (2017), a broadened account of "experiences of nature" that includes virtual ones; (1.3) a description of RDR2 as a AAA title with a realistic open world, plus *Red Dead Online* (RDO) and its "Naturalist" role. **Methods** detail an online survey (n = 586) combining a 15-species wildlife-identification quiz with gameplay/demographic questions and two open-ended qualitative prompts, analysed via GLMMs (lme4, glmmTMB) and inductive NVivo coding. **Results** report quantitative findings (players identify more species; effects strongest for high-utility ungulates and fish; recency, completion and Naturalist-role effects) and qualitative findings coded into subject-area themes (species awareness; distributions/habitats; natural history/behaviour; human–animal interactions; environmental change; personal reflection) and memorable-experience themes (human–animal interactions; observing wildlife; aesthetic scenes). The **Discussion** interprets the learning mechanisms, the realism-versus-entertainment compromises, the game's frontier ideology, and conservation implications, before a brief **Conclusion** affirming games as providers of both education and vicarious nature experience.

## Key concepts

- **Virtual ecology / virtual ecologies** — the simulated, dynamic ecosystem of the game treated as an object of study and a site of learning; the central organising concept and title term. Notably, the article speaks of how player action influences "the virtual ecology of the game" (e.g., scavengers eating player-left carcasses).
- **Vicarious experience(s) of nature** — understanding of the living world formed not through direct interaction but through representation (art, story, and now digital media); the framing the authors adopt and extend.
- **Environmental / ecological education via entertainment** — the question driving the study: "if educational games are unpopular, can popular games be educational?" RDR2 is the test case.
- **Virtual biophilia / "gamers like it green"** — borrowed from Truong et al. (2018, *World of Warcraft*): an attraction to healthy vegetated landscapes accessible virtually; a cluster of prior work the article positions itself within.
- **"Videophilic" vs "biophilic" societies** — after Pergams & Zaradic (2006); the worry that screen time displaces nature contact, which the authors complicate rather than simply endorse.
- **Natural history** as "description of nature" (Schmidly) — the declining-then-reviving subject the game incidentally teaches.
- **Recency effect** — knowledge gained appears to decay over time, mirroring retention of basic science knowledge (Custers, 2010).
- **Poetic licence / realism–entertainment compromise** — the tension between the developers' realistic rendering and dramatised choices (especially exaggerated predator aggression) that shape what players (mis)learn.

## Game-elements treated

The study is unusually precise about RDR2's systems because they are the very mechanisms of learning:

- **Hunting & fishing** — named "key components of gameplay." Players learn simplified versions of tracking, baiting, shooting, and skinning; ungulate and fish species (high "in-game utility value" as quarry) showed the largest identification gains. The hunting mechanic is described as not skimmed over: wounded animals are visibly distressed and skinning is "brief but graphic," producing guilt in some players (e.g., the bison shot with the wrong weapon).
- **Animal / wildlife systems (AI behaviour)** — ~200 species programmed to "appear, sound and behave in realistic ways," interacting with environment, other species and the player. Players reported learning behaviours: opossums feigning death, foxes pouncing into snow, bears bluff-charging, egrets riding alligators, and — reported independently by six participants — grey wolves appearing to mourn killed pack members. The "player-independent" liveliness (animals interacting with each other) was a recurrent memorable feature.
- **Plant / flora systems & pollution effects** — the game depicts unhealthy flora and fauna where toxic mining run-off pollutes a water body (Elysian Pool), and the now-extinct Carolina Parakeet appears in-world.
- **Biomes / regions / territories** — RDR2's five territories "broadly reflecting different North American biomes"; wildlife "spawns" in appropriate territories and habitats, so players learn species–region associations. Settings are inspired by real locations (Yosemite Valley, California Redwoods, the Great Plains, the Mississippi Bayou). Players named preferred biomes/regions ("bayou," "Big Valley") and features ("Window Rock," waterfalls).
- **The animal compendium** — the in-game catalogue displaying species facts (e.g., diets). The authors use it as a contrast case: players seemed to learn more through *immersion in the dynamic simulation* than by reading compendium facts.
- **Weather & dynamic environment** — thunderstorms, dust storms, sunsets; weather affects Arthur and his horse, and is a major source of "aesthetic" memorable experiences (one player tied an in-game dust storm to Arizona). The environment is "more than a backdrop": it acts as both "resource and antagonist."
- **The honour system** — noted as a mechanic through which players "experience different consequences depending on their actions."
- **The Naturalist role (RDO)** — an online specialisation rewarding non-violent interaction (photography, tracking, sample collection, protection from poachers); a key explanatory variable, associated with broader species-identification, especially smaller non-quarry species.
- **Story missions / scripted quests** — completing the storyline (proxy for ~40–60 hr) raised scores; the article notes you cannot finish the story without killing some animals, and flags a hidden quest that drives a bird species to extinction for no reward.
- **Photo mode / studying animals** — photographing and "studying" species, especially under the Naturalist role.

## Themes

- **Education without intent** — entertainment products can teach incidentally; learning is a side-effect of immersion and active engagement (hunting, exploring, observing).
- **Immersion and affect as learning mechanisms** — the most memorable experiences are tied to immersion and emotional provocation (awe at a moose, fear of a cougar, guilt at over-hunting); affect is presented as a vehicle, not noise.
- **Realism vs. dramatisation** — developers' realism invites players to trust depictions, so dramatised distortions (aggressive predators) risk mis-teaching real-world risk.
- **Nature as resource vs. nature as lively co-agent** — RDR2's frontier ideology frames wildlife as a harvestable resource, yet the world is "always lively, risky and to be negotiated with, rather than mute, benign and there to be observed."
- **Vicarious access and conservation ethics** — virtual nature could widen access and relieve pressure on protected areas (echoing Wilson's "Half-Earth"), but risks "flattening" — standardising and sensationalising — wild experience.
- **Critical reflexivity about "reconnection" discourse** — the authors resist nostalgic, culturally specific "reconnect with nature" narratives that ignore class, race and gender (Dickinson; Fletcher).

## Debate-axis positioning (nature-as-resource ↔ nature-as-ecological)

Crowley et al. sit deliberately *across* the axis rather than at one pole. They acknowledge the game's dominant **nature-as-resource** logic — the frontier "living off the land" ideology in which wildlife is a harvestable resource, reinforced by hunting/fishing mechanics whose "added value" drives the strongest learning for quarry species. Yet their finding and argument pull toward **nature-as-ecological**: the virtual ecology is dynamic and agentive (animals interact independently of the player, predators pose real risk, scavengers respond to carcasses), and the Naturalist role and storyline completion broaden players' attention beyond quarry to ecological relations and behaviours. Following Clayton et al., they read the game's antagonistic wildlife as a salutary reminder that biodiversity "is not designed to satisfy human interests." Net positioning: the resource frame is the game's surface ideology, but the simulation's ecological depth — and the Naturalist counter-frame — is what produces the article's claimed educational value. They neither celebrate the game uncritically nor reduce its nature to mere resource extraction.

## Tensions / limitations the article flags

- **Self-selection / framing bias** — the survey was advertised as a "Wildlife Quiz for Gamers," possibly attracting wildlife-interested gamers, and the quiz itself may have primed identification responses.
- **Realism's double edge** — exaggerated predator aggression may teach inaccurate risk perceptions precisely because the game is trusted as realistic.
- **Limits of simulation** — repetition of the same handful of behaviours in open-text responses (bluff-charging, canids playing, horses rolling) "hints at the limits of RDR2's capacity to fully replicate real-world ecology"; programmed behaviour reflects developers' selective choices.
- **Knowledge decay** — the recency effect implies gains may be transient without reinforcement.
- **Scope caveat** — RDR2 is an adult (18+) game; the authors explicitly do not claim educational value for children, and gesture to gentler alternatives (Abzû, Viva Piñata) for younger or classroom audiences.
