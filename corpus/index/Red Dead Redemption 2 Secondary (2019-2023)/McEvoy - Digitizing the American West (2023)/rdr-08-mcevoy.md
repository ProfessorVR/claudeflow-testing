# McEvoy, Amalia (2023), "Digitizing the American West: Analyzing Rhetoric in *Red Dead Redemption 2*"

**Venue:** Seton Hall University MA thesis (Department of English; adviser Dr. Mary Balkun, second reader Dr. John Wargacki), South Orange, NJ, May 2023. ETD #3098.
**Discipline:** literary studies / rhetoric / game studies (English Literature).
**Depth tag:** deep.

---

## Thesis

McEvoy argues that the long-form, narrative-heavy video game can be "read" much as a literary text is read, and that *Red Dead Redemption 2* (RDR2) is best approached through **reader-response theory** supplemented by Ian Bogost's **procedural rhetoric**. Her core claim is that RDR2's *rhetoric* — though delivered differently from prose — persuades players toward moral choices while teaching them about a compressed slice of American history (1899–1900). The argument runs along two coupled tracks: (1) a *theoretical bridge* between literature and games, whereby the experiences gained from reading and the experiences gained from interacting with a digital narrative are treated as continuous (the article says reading and play both yield "frames of reference" for life); and (2) a *rhetorical-historical* reading of how RDR2's systems — above all the **Honor system** and its reward/punishment structure — function as persuasion that "stifles" the expansion of the discriminatory ideologies it depicts. The recurring synthesis is that RDR2 blurs the line between "teaching history and romanticizing a virtual recreation" of post-Civil War America, and that its persuasive force lies less in its plot than in its coded, reactive systems.

## Structure

The thesis proceeds through named sections rather than numbered chapters:

- **Opening / framing** — long-form games (Amy Green via Johansen Quijano) as texts demanding prolonged engagement; immersion and realism as preconditions for empathy; reader response as the lens; *The Last of Us* as precedent for games delivering social critique.
- **Game Summary** — a full plot synopsis of the Van der Linde gang's arc from the Grizzlies to Arthur's death.
- **Worldbuilding** — RDR2's "fourth dimension" (time/a world that changes without the player), drawn from developer interviews (Goldberg/Vulture, MacDonald/*The Guardian*); contrast with *GTA V*'s consequence-free mayhem.
- **Critical Context** — the theoretical core: Bogost on procedural rhetoric; Denning on "deep play" and the historical imaginary; Bourgonjon on game literacy; Rosenblatt's "invisible reader"; Fish's interpretive communities.
- **"Playing" History** — enemy-reduction, moral conflict, work-vs-play, and how RDR2 "plays with" history (Gilded-Age economic stand-ins).
- **Immigrant Discrimination** — historical grounding via Dinnerstein & Reimers and Menchaca (the 1896 Rodriguez naturalization case), keyed to the game's "greaser" slur and Javier.
- **A Return to Rhetoric** — synthesis: realism as the carrier of rhetorical/educational merit; the "completionist" player who misses the rhetoric; Ostenson on games in the English classroom; the Honor system and the two deaths as the game's central persuasive device; closing plea to *experience* the game rather than merely complete it.

## Key concepts and the rhetorical framework used

McEvoy's **primary rhetorical framework is reader-response theory** (Louise Rosenblatt; Stanley Fish), repurposed as a *reading method for games*. Her **secondary and most operationally important framework is Ian Bogost's procedural rhetoric** — the claim that authoring code that enforces rules to generate a representation (rather than authoring the representation directly) is itself a mode of persuasion, "the principal value of the computer." She explicitly adopts Salen & Zimmerman's definition of play (via Bogost) — play as the "free space of movement within a more rigid structure" — and maps it onto RDR2: the open-world map is the "free space of movement," while the game's coded social systems (law enforcement, NPC reactions, the morality meter) are the "rigid structure" that fences players toward moral conduct.

Other load-bearing concepts:

- **Realism / the "fourth dimension"** — Dan Houser's phrase (via Goldberg) for a world that "unfolds around you" and persists without the player; McEvoy treats realism as the *enabling condition* of the game's rhetoric (a man builds a house over an in-game week, corpses decay, animals migrate).
- **Deep play / the historical imaginary** (Andrew Denning) — historical games as a form of public history that shapes understanding "before and oftentimes in lieu of" professional historians' input; "gamification" of the past; "edutainment."
- **Enemy reduction** — the stripping of humanity from "stock villains" to ease the player's conscience (Denning's Wolfenstein analysis), which McEvoy finds partially operative in RDR2's mandatory shootouts but *not* extended to passive NPCs.
- **Media / game literacy** (Bourgonjon) — games as deserving scholarly study but not readable "like traditional text forms."
- **The frontier myth / the death of the Wild West** — RDR2's setting as the close of the outlaw era amid encroaching modernity and law enforcement; McEvoy frames the gang's story as "the beginning of the downfall of the lower class."
- **The Other** — RDR2's representation of Native American, Mexican, African American, and European-immigrant characters as groups "systematically disrespected" by the digitized American government.
- **Manifest Destiny / Western genre** — present implicitly: the encroachment of modernity, the Gilded-Age robber barons (Cornwall as a Vanderbilt/Rockefeller stand-in), and the frontier's economic closure. (Note: McEvoy does not use the explicit term "Manifest Destiny"; the frontier-myth content arrives through the "death of the Wild West," class collapse, and the Gilded-Age framing.)

## Readings (interpretive moves)

1. **The Honor system as the seat of the game's rhetoric.** McEvoy's central reading: be polite, spare lives, minimize crime → the game reads you as high-honor; rob, murder, harm animals → low-honor. The system "mitigates how players can go about experiencing the game." Its basic moral commentary: "goodness is rewarded with peace while misdeeds are punished with suffering."

2. **The two deaths of Arthur as rhetorical payoff.** Arthur dies regardless, but *how* he dies is honor-contingent — a peaceful sunrise versus a bullet from Micah in the rain. McEvoy reads this as the game delivering "the death the player deserves," and as a demonstration of the gap between Arthur-the-character and Arthur-the-player-avatar.

3. **Consequence as anti-discriminatory persuasion.** Because rude/discriminatory player dialogue toward immigrants triggers "immediate consequences," McEvoy argues Rockstar uses narrative structure to comment on period bigotry and to "stifle the expansion of those ideologies held today."

4. **RDR2 contra GTA V.** Both Rockstar open worlds let NPCs act autonomously, but GTA's star-rating mayhem carries "few to no consequences," enabling escapist idealization of violence; RDR2 "forces" the player to watch society react, raising the moral stakes.

5. **Economic Othering / good-vs-evil framing.** Cornwall and the Pinkertons are Gilded-Age robber-baron stand-ins; the poor gang is positioned as "good" and wealthy enemies as "evil," with the "good" characters themselves framed as Other.

6. **Reader-response as the player's better mode.** Against the "completionist" who treats the game as something "to overcome" (Fish's "poetry-seeing eyes" transposed to "gamers"), McEvoy urges players to gather meaning from the *experience* of play, the way Rosenblatt wants students to attend to felt response rather than formula.

## Game-elements treated (precise)

- **Plot spine / towns and regions:** the Grizzlies (post-Blackwater flight); **Valentine** (New Hanover cowboy town); **Rhodes** (Southern states; Grays vs. Braithwaites feud); **Saint Denis** (New Orleans/Louisiana analogue; Angelo Bronte; the bank robbery where Hosea is executed); **Guarma** (Haiti analogue, revolutionary/sugar-plantation warfare); **Beaver Hollow** (final camp, army-payroll-train plan); **Annesburg** (dying coal-mining town as figure for industrial decline).
- **The Valentine saloon / Tommy fight (opening tutorial brawl):** Arthur fights Tommy to save Javier, is thrown through a window; the player may release the grip and let Tommy go, or beat him into "rag-dolling" (cranial damage seen later) with an honor penalty. McEvoy reads its "dual purpose" — combat tutorial + establishing Arthur's potential dishonor.
- **The Honor system:** binary reward/punishment morality meter; coyote = negative, buck = positive (image captioned in the thesis); governs endings.
- **Arthur's two deaths (the endings):** peaceful sunrise vs. Micah's gunshot; honor-contingent.
- **Dead Eye:** combat system that slows time for precise aim; McEvoy's worked example of "work-vs-play" mastery — also usable to shoot an innocent horse, costing honor.
- **The epilogue (John Marston):** noted as the segment where the player no longer controls Arthur (the article flags this rather than analyzing it at length).
- **Charlotte Braithwaite / suffragette mission (Rhodes):** scripted event in which Arthur chaperones suffragettes to town hall; a Saint Denis suffragette recurs daily in the city square, harassed by default NPCs regardless of player conduct.
- **Leviticus Cornwall & the Pinkertons:** oil tycoon and his private police force; the train heist; Gilded-Age stand-ins.
- **Discrimination content:** the prologue slur "darkie" toward a Black character; "greaser" used by NPCs and Bill toward Javier (tied to Menchaca/Dinnerstein history); the coins-over-the-eyes burial of Davey in the prologue (Gaelic/Irish heritage cue, sourced to YouTuber RealPixels).
- **Diverse cast:** Javier (Mexican immigrant), Charles (Native American and African American), Lenny (father enslaved), Tilly (African American) — read as the game exposing players to period injustice.
- **Mandatory shootouts (e.g., vs. the Pinkertons):** nameless, endlessly aggressive enemies → "enemy reduction"; contrasted with the preserved humanity of passive NPCs.

## Themes

History as compressible and "playable"; realism as the medium of persuasion; morality and consequence as the game's argument; the porous boundary between teaching and romanticizing the past; immigration, nativism, and the Other; class collapse at the close of the frontier; the player's freedom as both ethical opportunity and risk; the continuity between literary and ludic experience; the danger that for young or non-U.S. players "Rockstar's American history is the only American history they may know."

## Debate-axis positioning

- **Education vs. entertainment.** McEvoy occupies a *both/and* position: RDR2 is primarily entertainment ("mud, blood, and gore," per Houser) yet carries genuine educational/rhetorical merit through its realism and systems. She concedes, with Denning, that developers chase revenue, not the historian's "why did it occur / why is it significant," but insists the game still functions as public history.
- **Realism: pedagogy vs. romanticization.** She holds the tension open rather than resolving it — "teaching history" and "romanticizing a virtual recreation" are presented as simultaneously true; for some audiences the game's history may be the *only* history they encounter (a cautionary note shared with Denning and Bourgonjon).
- **Narrative vs. systems as locus of meaning.** McEvoy shifts the locus of RDR2's rhetoric *away from plot/dialogue and toward procedure* — the Honor system and reactive NPC code — aligning with Bogost against purely narratological readings.
- **Player freedom vs. moral steering.** She argues the open world's freedom is bounded by a moral "fence" (law, NPC reaction, honor), so the game both empowers and disciplines the player.

## Tensions / soft spots

- **Framework hybridity.** Reader-response (experiential, reception-side) and procedural rhetoric (systems/production-side) sit somewhat uneasily together; McEvoy bridges them via "meaning gained from experience" but does not fully theorize the seam.
- **Honor binarism.** The reading leans on a high/low honor binary and a "deserved death" moral economy that may flatten the game's gradations and the considerable player effort that decouples Arthur-the-character from Arthur-the-avatar.
- **Persuasion-efficacy claim.** The assertion that consequences "stifle the expansion of those ideologies held today" is asserted rather than evidenced (no player-reception data).
- **Methodological status.** Largely single-player close reading plus developer-interview and historical sources; no empirical reception study despite a reader-response frame that invites one.

---

**diss-hook:** *(anticipatory-application — single)* McEvoy's systems-first relocation of RDR2's rhetoric to the Honor system's reward/punishment loop, framed via Salen & Zimmerman's "free space of movement within a more rigid structure," offers a directly cognate procedural-rhetoric counterpart to the dissertation's Aristotelian–Heideggerian actualization-of-desire chain: the honor meter and its honor-contingent endings can be read as a coded apparatus that channels *orexis* toward (or away from) virtuous action — a rhetorical-ontological steering of desire that the RODA method could analyze scene-by-scene (e.g., the Valentine/Tommy tutorial brawl as the game's first staged choice between completed and arrested moral action).
