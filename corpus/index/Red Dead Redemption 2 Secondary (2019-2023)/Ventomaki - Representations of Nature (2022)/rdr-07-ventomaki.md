# Ventomäki, Hanna (2022) — "Representation of Nature in *Red Dead Redemption 2*"

- **ID / slug:** rdr-07 / ventomaki-representations-nature-2022
- **Citation:** Ventomäki, Hanna (2022). *Representation of nature in Red Dead Redemption 2*. Master's thesis, English, Language and Literature, Faculty of Humanities, University of Oulu (Spring 2022).
- **Discipline:** English literary studies / ecocriticism / game studies
- **Depth:** deep-long (~20,500-word MA thesis)
- **Debate-axis position:** straddles the spine but tilts toward **nature-as-aesthetic/ecological**, while insisting the game holds aesthetic-Romantic, ecological-conservationist, AND resource-exploitative registers in unresolved tension; final verdict is anti-reductionist ("no single form or role").

## Thesis

The article argues that *Red Dead Redemption 2* (Rockstar, 2018) represents nature and civilization as **separate, opposed entities**, with civilization (industrialization, modernization, capitalism) seeking to overcome nature — a structure mirroring the late-19th-century United States. Nature is coded with respite, freedom, frontier spirit, and survival; industrialized space is coded as restrictive, hectic, inhospitable, polluted. The thesis demonstrates this opposition across the game's **spatial narratives, mechanics, and themes**, and reads it through the genre conventions of the Western (nostalgia for the Old West; man-vs-nature struggle). Crucially, Ventomäki resists collapsing the game into a single position: nature is simultaneously **Romanticized, exploited, antagonistic, and an autonomous entity**, and the player's own choices (especially around hunting) co-author which register dominates. The holistic claim is methodological — that only a *multifaceted* reading capturing photographs, maps, the compendium, NPC dialogue, missions, and spatial narrative can register the game's "complexities, ambiguities, and contradictions."

## Structure (sections)

1. **Introduction** — three research questions: (a) how RDR2's nature-related virtual spaces hold dynamic features (NPCs, narratives, interactions); (b) what these communicate about Western themes; (c) how themes/narratives are constructed in-game.
2. **Literature review** — nature-in-videogames scholarship (environmentalism, conservation, "indoorisation," exergames, anthropocentrism, player landscape-preference/biophilia, the eco-conundrum of an exploitative medium carrying eco-content); Western-genre scholarship; nature-in-Westerns.
3. **Analytical framework** — (3.1) realism and space in virtual worlds + (3.1.1) spatial narrative; (3.2) themes/stories of the contemporary Western; (3.3) multimodality; (3.4) subjective vs. objective experience.
4. **Materials and methods** — single-analyst close reading of the six chapters (Arthur Morgan arc; epilogue/John Marston largely set aside), screenshots under fair use.
5. **Analysis** — (5.1) physical spaces/environments + (5.1.1) New Austin vs. Annesburg; (5.2) maps and Arthur's journal; (5.3) hunting and survival; (5.4) animals; (5.5) Western-theme narratives + (5.5.1) loading screens + (5.5.2) Old West narratives in nature/space; (5.6) close analysis of the mission "Exit Pursued by a Bruised Ego."
6. **Discussion** — nature-culture as separable opposites; Arthur as embodiment of Old West values; the New West's victory symbolized by his death.
7. **Conclusion** — RDR2 gives a "mainly Romantic" portrayal of nature as mirror to the authentic self, while conservation messaging and player-elective exploitation coexist.

## Key concepts

- **Nature representation as multimodal construction.** Following Elleström's four media modalities (material, spatiotemporal, sensorial, semiotic), Ventomäki treats nature as something *built* through visual, textual, auditory, and kinesthetic cues — the compendium, journal sketches, NPC speech, music, and controller feedback together "communicate what nature is."
- **Spatial narrative / environmental storytelling.** Drawing on Domsch (and Jenkins via Domsch), she distinguishes *sequence narrative* (films, literature) from *spatial narrative*, which privileges spatial exploration over plot and asks the player to deduce story from environmental clues. Sub-types deployed: **evocative spaces**, **embedded narratives** (letters, NPC conversations, diaries), and **cause-and-effect** environmental response.
- **Nature defined as the uncontrolled.** She adopts Öhman et al.'s fluid definition — nature as "the uncontrolled aspects of elements and processes" — so that in-game gravity, fatigue, weather, and predator attacks count as nature, supporting a reading where consequence and "collateral learning" are nature-effects.
- **The nature-as-safe-space thesis.** Her signature interpretive move: in RDR2, nature recurrently becomes a *safe space* where NPCs open up emotionally and bonding occurs without outside disturbance — fishing trips, campfires, traveling between mission points.
- **Wilderness ↔ civilization opposition.** The game-world is split into wilderness (freedom, respite, adventure) and civilization (cramped, polluted, unequal); the player can move between, but both player and protagonist are "made to feel out-of-place in cities."
- **Anthropocentrism and the eco-conundrum.** Via Saxton Brown, she carries the tension that a game can advocate environmental consciousness while being a high-energy electronic product that consumes resources and may deepen "declining contact with the natural world."
- **Old West / New West (frontier nostalgia).** Via Carter, McVeigh, Walsh, McFarlane: the Western's nostalgic remembrance of "simpler times" and the man-vs-nature struggle as the "simplest" form of conflict, alongside the revisionist current (e.g., *Unforgiven*) that reads Westerns as critical of the industrial-capitalist present.
- **Romanticism of nature.** The conclusion frames the Old West as adhering to **Romantic views** of nature — nature as mirror to one's authentic, individual self.

## Readings (interpretive set-pieces)

- **New Austin vs. Annesburg — the Old West and the New West.** The iconic arid desert of New Austin (Arizona/New Mexico/Texas analog) is gated off for the Arthur playthrough and reserved for John Marston in the epilogue; Ventomäki reads this **inaccessibility** as authored commentary — dissociating Arthur from the most iconic Western landscape redirects focus to eastern industrialization (the disappearing Old West, the growing New West). Annesburg (sooty coal town, dead fish, sickly population, hostile mine guards) embodies industrialization's cost to environment *and* people; Butcher Creek's "curse" is revealed as lead/arsenic mine poisoning.
- **Maps and Arthur's journal as meta-narrative.** Arthur's hand-drawn map markers and journal entries humanize a mechanical interface and grant the player privileged interior access to Arthur. Reading: the journal's overwhelming preference for **animals/plants/landscapes over factories/streets** characterizes Arthur as one who "prefers nature over culture" — and, historically, the old world over the new.
- **Hunting as contested ethics.** Against Saxton Brown's blanket claim that hunting mechanics are exploitative, Ventomäki argues hunting-for-survival is "natural" and the methodical kill (binoculars to rate the animal, Eagle Eye tracking, correct-caliber weapon) can be read as **humane/conservationist**; the exploitation charge is *contextual* (food vs. trophy). Yet the game never forbids needless killing.
- **The bison/poacher mission (Charles Smith).** Charles's account of his mother's tribe "moving with the bison," his admiration for the herd, and the discovery of slaughtered bison left to rot position the player on the **side of nature and conservation**; the mission commentates directly on 19th-century bison near-extinction and on the colonization of indigenous peoples (poachers paid to frame Native Americans).
- **The honor mechanic as ecological feedback.** Conservative hunting and animal care yield honor; excess killing yields dishonor and a worse ending — a buck in sunlight (high honor) vs. a black coyote in the rain (low honor). The "Manifest Destiny" achievement in the first game (hunt the buffalo to extinction) is cited as the cynical counter-pole.
- **Horses (revisionist reading).** Via McMahon and Tompkins: horses are the emotional parallel to the protagonist yet "marginalized," roped and ridden until they drop; the link between man and nature *in controllable form*. Taming the elite white Arabian is read as an act of conquest/mastery. The thesis concedes this section is one-sidedly revisionist.
- **Loading screens.** Twelve sepia "photographs" (collapsed cottage, snowy Grizzlies, a pioneer in Big Valley, a bird on a stump) set wistful nostalgia before play; the bird-on-a-stump image is read ambivalently as either nature's decline under construction or nature reclaiming a ruin.
- **"The New South" fishing trip.** Arthur, Dutch, and Hosea fish at a mirror-clear lake at sunset and sing rowing back — the "last good moments before the fall"; the early sunset symbolizes the gang's coming decline.
- **Campsites as barometer.** Six campsites track the gang's wellbeing; each successive site sits closer to industrialized areas where nature recedes, ending at Beaver Hollow near Annesburg's pollution. Horseshoe Overlook (spring valley) is the peaceful Old West ideal.
- **Close analysis — "Exit Pursued by a Bruised Ego."** The legendary-bear (Bharati) hunt with Hosea: tutorialized horse-saddle-switching folded into narrative; cinematic transition from Valentine's cityscape to untouched wilderness (man's "fragility and insignificance"); realistic skinning animation that may unsettle and prompts reflection on the meat industry's hidden production chain; Dead Eye combat; the scarred, antagonized bear as nature's deadliness given form. Arthur's wounds wash away while his impact on the bear is final — the asymmetry of man over nature.

## Game-elements treated (precise)

- **States/regions (biomes):** Ambarino (rugged snowy mountains, glaciers, geysers, Grizzlies, Calumet Ravine), New Hanover (plains/forests; Annesburg, oilfields; Roanoke Ridge/Butcher Creek), Lemoyne (meadows, southern climate, Bayou Nwa marsh, Saint Denis), West Elizabeth (Blackwater, Strawberry lumber town, Big Valley, white-Arabian snowfields), New Austin (arid SW desert, Armadillo — gated for Arthur), Guarma (restricted tropical island).
- **Cities/industrial space:** Saint Denis (industrial port city — factories, crowds, crime, inequality), Annesburg (coal town — black smoke, polluted/oil-and-garbage water, dead fish, no stable), Blackwater (rapid modernization), Butcher Creek (lead/arsenic mine poisoning), Appleseed Timber Company deforestation site.
- **Wildlife/animals:** ~80 species + 15 legendary animals; bison (Charles mission), whitetail deer, American pronghorn doe (compendium exemplar), bears (legendary Bharati grizzly), wolves/wolfpacks, cougar/panther (legendary panther near Saint Denis), alligators (Bayou Nwa, red glowing eyes), hawks/eagles fishing, oxpecker-type birds on bison, dogs (Australian Shepherd at Emerald Ranch; Cain the stray), horses (white Arabian elite; war horses; abused Murfree mounts), fox/rat/stork (journal sketches).
- **Hunting mechanic:** binocular study (1–3 star quality rating), Eagle Eye tracking (wind direction, tracks, clues), correct-caliber weapon for clean kill, skinning + carcass harvesting, pelts→satchels/garments/talismans, legendary-pelt prestige from trapper, predator bait crafting.
- **Survival/weather:** dynamic weather; cold-rain shivering in Annesburg; warmth-rated clothing vs. draining health core; location-specific events (New Austin dust storms, snowy-glacier extremes); 48-minute in-game day; compressed map distances.
- **Honor system:** care/conservation → honor; cruelty/excess killing → dishonor; ending and self-image (buck vs. black coyote) keyed to honor.
- **Interface/meta artifacts:** in-game map with Arthur's (then John's) distinct handwriting/markers; personal journal (drawings of plants/animals/people, private doubts); species **compendium** (habitat, hunting method, kill-count, uses).
- **Mechanics-in-narrative:** Dead Eye targeting; horse bonding (ride/feed/groom) and customization (mane, saddle, saddlebags, provisions); saddle-switching tutorial; fishing and photography as recreational (quasi-exergame) activities.
- **NPCs as nature/conservation vectors:** Charles Smith (indigenous conservation ethic), Malcolm MacIntosh (Scottish scientist protesting mining; sells "Hopes for the Future"), Mary-Beth (Arthur's confession about needless killing), Hosea (paternal hunting mentor), Dutch (Old-West-freedom ideology), Murfree Brood / Night Folk (wilderness antagonists).

## Themes

- Nature vs. civilization as separable opposites; industrialization's environmental and human cost.
- Frontier nostalgia and the decline of the Old West (Arthur's death as the New West's victory).
- Man-vs-nature as the "simplest," pre-political struggle; predators as nature's deadliness personified.
- Nature as safe space / site of emotional disclosure and authentic selfhood (Romanticism).
- Player agency and moral ambiguity (hunting freedom vs. conservation messaging; honor feedback without prohibition).
- Conservation and indigenous land-relations (bison, colonization).
- The autonomy of nature — animals interacting independently of the player.

## Debate-axis positioning (nature-as-resource ↔ nature-as-aesthetic/ecological)

Ventomäki refuses a single pole. She **shifts the axis** from either/or to *both/and-in-tension*: hunting and pelt-economy instantiate nature-as-resource (the anthropocentric, exploitative pole, per Saxton Brown), but she **contests** the reading that this exhausts the game — survival-hunting is "natural," the methodical clean kill reads as humane, and the conservation mission, MacIntosh's protest, the honor feedback, animal autonomy, the journal, and the loading screens pull strongly toward nature-as-aesthetic/ecological. Her net placement leans **aesthetic/ecological** (Romantic nature, conservation valorized, exploitation displaced onto antagonists), while explicitly holding that "representations of nature shift depending on the context" and player choice co-determines the outcome.

## Tensions / unresolved points (article-internal)

- **Conservation message vs. unbounded killing.** The bison mission and the Mary-Beth confession condemn excess hunting, yet the game never prevents it (and the prequel even rewards buffalo-extinction with an achievement) — "the dimensions of commentary and player freedom collide."
- **Hunting as exploitative vs. necessary.** The mechanic is exploitative in itself; the game frames it as survival-necessary; the matter is contextual (food vs. trophy).
- **Romanticized vs. exploited nature.** Nature is both safe haven and resource; both antagonist (man-vs-nature) and autonomous entity indifferent to humans.
- **Single-analyst subjectivity.** The thesis flags its own subjectivity (one interpreter) while defending describable, designed player responses; the horse section concedes it is one-sidedly revisionist.
- **Protagonist-dependence.** It raises but leaves open whether the nature-culture juxtaposition would hold if the game were played through someone other than Arthur.

## diss-hook (anticipatory-application)

- diss-hook: Ventomäki's honor mechanic — conservation rewarded, excess killing penalized, yet never prohibited — is a clean exhibit of an *actualization-of-desire* chain where the apparatus shapes (but does not compel) the player's orexis toward a hunting telos; her "contextual exploitation" distinction (food vs. trophy) and her "nature-as-safe-space" finding could seed the RODA reading of how RDR2's environment solicits, rewards, and morally inflects desire without removing player agency. (Tagged anticipatory-application; the thesis itself makes no Aristotelian-Heideggerian claim.)
