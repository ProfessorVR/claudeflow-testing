# Vanderhoef & Payne — "Press X to Wait: The Cultural Politics of Slow Game Time in *Red Dead Redemption 2*" (2022)

**Citation.** John Vanderhoef & Matthew Thomas Payne, "Press X to Wait: The Cultural Politics of Slow Game Time in *Red Dead Redemption 2*," *Game Studies* 22, no. 3 (August 2022). Open-access, peer-reviewed (the international journal of computer game research).

**Discipline.** Media studies / game studies — cultural-political and critical-theoretical, blending discourse analysis with textual (formal/mechanical) analysis.

**Depth treated here.** Deep.

---

## Thesis

The article introduces **"slow game time"** as a concept that explains why some players reacted negatively to the animation, gameplay, and storytelling tempo of *Red Dead Redemption 2* (Rockstar Studios, 2018). The authors argue that RDR2, unusually for a commercially and critically successful multi-platform title, repeatedly employs slow game time, thereby breaking from the mainstream design norm they name **"hegemonic game time"** — a privileging of player action, efficiency, and optimization. Their central claim is dialectical: whether players embrace or resent it, RDR2's slow game time *reveals* the otherwise unmarked hegemonic temporal regime governing action-adventure conventions, and in doing so it transforms what would be *subversive* play in another context (process, stillness, unproductive waiting) into a *dominant* mode of engagement inside a blockbuster. The authors are careful to qualify that RDR2 is no political panacea — it remains enmeshed in the capitalist game industry's design principles, ideological projects, and exploitative labor practices (citing Schreier on Rockstar's crunch) — yet its considerable use of slow time "complicates its own relationship to hegemonic play" and demands critical attention.

## Structure

The essay moves through six titled movements, each pun-laden in the western register:

1. **"Once Upon Some Times in the West"** — frames RDR2 as a game fundamentally *about time* (and slow time in particular); introduces the Van der Linde gang, Arthur Morgan, the closing frontier, and Arthur's terminal tuberculosis as nested narrative, epochal, and existential clocks. Establishes the "cowboy simulator" reception and player complaints about slowness.
2. **"The Critical Potentiality of Slow Game Time"** — situates the argument in scholarship on game temporality (Hanson, Jayemanne, Ruberg, Scully-Blaker) and in critical theory of neoliberal time and gamification (Harvey, Deterding et al., Rey, Dyer-Witheford & de Peuter).
3. **"Slow Your Horse There, Cowboy"** — pivots from a verisimilitude-blaming explanation to a *temporal* one; argues frustration springs not from realism but from ruptures that let capitalist chrononormativity "bleed through." Introduces the authors' tripartite framework.
4. **"Action and Stasis"** — the mechanics/animation analysis (canned animations, looting, skinning, hunting, Eagle Eye, Dead Eye).
5. **"Travel and Stillness"** — the traversal-systems analysis (horseback, train, fast-travel gating, auto-run, stamina).
6. **"Narrative and Prolongment"** — the story-structure analysis (109 missions, cyclical hope/defeat, slow death).
7. **"Press 'X' to Wait"** — the conclusion, which generalizes to other AAA cases (*The Witcher 3*, *Death Stranding*) and reads RDR2's quiet moments through Galloway's "video games are action."

## Core concepts

- **Slow game time.** The article's signature term: ludic time that "eschews" efficiency, optimization, and productivity to explore process, stillness, waste, and "the pregnant potential of a null set." Distinct from mere player choice — RDR2 produces it through aesthetic, narrative, *and* mechanical means.
- **Hegemonic game time.** The proposed foil: prevailing relations and rhythms of play that bind pleasure to power and speed to control; insists on immediacy and the player's "supreme role" in mastering the system. Built atop Fron/Fullerton/Morie/Pearce's "hegemony of play," Fiske's "hegemonic pleasures," and Ruberg's reading of chrononormativity.
- **Chrononormativity** (Freeman, via Ruberg). The dominant temporal mode demanding that time be used toward productive ends; the article maps it onto big-budget design and neoliberal capitalism (Harvey). RDR2's slow time "betrays" this regime.
- **Stasis and stillness** (Scully-Blaker). Stasis = inaction created by mechanics (e.g., slow movement); stillness = inaction invited by sublime aesthetics (vistas to pause and view). The authors borrow both.
- **Prolongment.** The authors' *own* added third category: slow game time cultivated through an "excessively extended narrative" — stretching out the last moments of a dying era.
- **Radical slowness** (Scully-Blaker). A deliberate failure "to 'keep up'" with capitalist tempo as a political act. The article's twist: RDR2 makes radical slowness the *dominant* mode rather than a player-initiated subversion.
- **Deliberate friction / waiting.** The lived texture of the above — "Press X to wait," contemplative inaction, the "null set" of unproductive play.

## Readings

- The **drawer-by-drawer cabin search** (Figure 1) is the article's keystone close reading: after the gang murders a rival gang at a mountain cabin, Dutch instructs the player to search for supplies, and the player watches Morgan open *every* cupboard and drawer one by one — converting a momentary pickup into a multi-beat process. Read as paradigmatic stasis.
- The **legendary grizzly-bear hunt** (Chapter 2 tutorial) is read as the systematic deployment of slow time during optional activities: locating paw prints, activating Eagle Eye, following the vapor trail to a fish carcass then to droppings, baiting, waiting, then a scripted ambush — designed to thwart "hurried, determined players."
- **Dead Eye** receives a nuanced reading: it looks like "hegemonic play par excellence" (player control over time, multi-target killing) but acquires "fresh meaning" amid RDR2's broader slowness — exemplifying the game's *complex* oscillation between hegemonic and slow time. Crucially, the authors distinguish this mastery-slowness from the *radical* slowness of slow game time.
- The **opening horseback trek** through snow behind Dutch is read for its diegetic signal — Dutch's line that the loot can't be collected "any time soon" — underscoring that "nothing will happen in the game 'anytime soon.'"
- The **narrative's cyclical structure** (renewal → tragedy, repeated each chapter) is read as "the false hope of meritocracy": amassing weapons and abilities cannot avert the inevitable, foregrounding Morgan's impotence.
- **Arthur's tuberculosis** (coughing from Ch. 2; diagnosis in Ch. 5, mission 65; decline over ~30 missions) is read as narrative refusal of "economy" — the game denies any expedited path to the grave *and* any means of avoiding it.
- The **fireside moment** (Figure 3) anchors the conclusion via Galloway: even in quiet stillness "the player is still playing; the software is still running," so slow time challenges what counts as *action* in AAA games.

## Game-elements treated (precise)

- **"Press X" prompts** — the title device; the authors contrast pressing X "to shoot, or to punch an enemy, or leap to a ledge" (instrumental, known ends) with pressing X "to wait" or electing not to press it, which "opens a contemplative play space." Treated as the conceptual emblem of waiting/deliberate friction.
- **Drawer-by-drawer looting/searching** — opening and closing furniture drawers one by one; reaching into every cupboard, items placed in the satchel individually (Figure 1). Treated as the prime instance of *stasis* extending resource-gathering across "unavoidable beats."
- **Looting bodies & skinning felled animals** — named alongside mounting a horse as repetitive canned animations whose sheer cumulative volume "adds up to considerable wait time."
- **Canned animations generally** — "nearly every in-game action is linked to canned animations" that leave the player waiting for the game to "catch up to their intentions."
- **Optional/side activities** — fishing, treasure-hunting, hunting legendary wildlife, campfire cooking; each "produce their own wait times" requiring patience and meticulous movement.
- **Eagle Eye** — tracking system that slows time, mutes screen color, and illuminates a vapor trail of the animal's path; central to the legendary-hunt deceleration.
- **Dead Eye** — gunslinger ability that dramatically slows game time ("bullet time," a term from *The Matrix*) for lining up shots; the series' signature mechanic since the first entry; read as the hinge between hegemonic and slow time.
- **Slow traversal / horseback** — the primary travel mode across a 29-square-mile world; ~16 minutes to cross by horseback, "three-plus hours at a walking pace" (citing a player measurement); treated as designed *stillness* (cinematic vistas, journey over destination).
- **Horse stamina + horse death** — sprint is tempered by a stamina system; stamina grows with use/care, but if the horse dies (gunfire, falling), all progress is lost and the player must walk out on foot until reaching camp/town/victim.
- **No horseback movement through camp** — players must dismount and walk between tents and wagons, forcing a "more methodical" pace; camp acts as a between-missions hub.
- **Auto-run feature** — auto-guides Morgan's horse to a selected map destination and wrests the camera for cinematic framing; treated as *removing player agency* while injecting contemplative inaction (a "bathroom break" or "traveling vignette").
- **Train system** — loops the world but is restricted to fixed tracks and schedules; contributes to simulation but "does not greatly assuage" travel time.
- **Gated/obscured fast-travel** — warping between base camp and discovered townships is locked behind story missions and optional camp upgrades, so "some players will never discover or make use of it"; read as RDR2 refusing "modernity's logic of acceleration" (Virilio).
- **Extended horseback NPC dialogues** — riding with companions for exposition "suture[s] the game's mechanics to its enormous narrative content."
- **Narrative scale as mechanic of prolongment** — 109 missions (94 central story), six chapters plus a two-part epilogue, a boasted 2,000-page script ("eight feet high"), ~40–60 hours; treated as *prolongment*.

## Themes

- Temporality and pace as the game's true subject (and as a politics, not just an aesthetic).
- Neoliberal/capitalist colonization of time; gamification, productivity, and the guilt of "wasting" time.
- Productivity vs. process; efficiency vs. stillness; speed vs. contemplation; economization vs. "potential waste."
- Mastery, meritocracy, and "gaming capital" (achievements, leaderboards, speedruns) as the affective substrate players are reluctant to surrender.
- Gendered/normative assumptions about who "real" play is for (Consalvo & Paul; the "non-game" charge from Paul).
- Mortality, decline, and the closing frontier mirrored in mechanics (Arthur's tuberculosis; the gang's dissolution).
- The paradox of a $200M, 3,000-worker, crunch-built product nonetheless enacting a critique of capitalist tempo.

## Debate-axis positioning (player-agency ↔ scripted-constraint)

The article sits firmly toward the **scripted-constraint** pole, and self-consciously so. Its recurring move is to show RDR2 *removing* or *foreclosing* agency to manufacture slowness: the auto-run feature "removing agency from players"; fast-travel "gated" and "obscured"; horse death imposing forced walking; canned animations that make the player "wait for the game to catch up." Most strikingly, the authors *invert* Scully-Blaker's player-side category of "radical slowness": where radical slowness is a chosen, player-initiated act of resistance, RDR2 makes slowness "the dominant mode of play" by design — "not necessarily as a subversive activity contra to the game's intentions." Agency is not erased but *channeled*: Galloway's coda preserves the player as co-actor even in stillness ("the player is still playing"), so the article's settled position is that RDR2's *designed* constraint is what produces the critical, contemplative opening — a constraint-first reading of where temporal politics live.

> **diss-hook:** This constraint-produced "contemplative play space free from the worry over productive ends" maps directly onto the dissertation's actualization-of-desire chain — slow game time as a designed *withholding* of desire's efficient discharge, where the prompt to "wait" (or to refrain from pressing X) suspends the orectic motion toward an end and exposes the friction between the player's *epithymia*/impulse to optimize and the game's deliberate deferral. (Tagged: anticipatory-application.)

## Tensions / internal qualifications

- **Critique vs. complicity.** The authors repeatedly hedge: RDR2 is "not some radical, political panacea" and is "inextricably enmeshed" in capitalist industry practice (crunch labor, $200M budget) — yet they still claim genuine critical potentiality. The argument lives in this productive ambivalence.
- **Verisimilitude vs. temporality.** They explicitly *reject* the popular "too much realism" explanation, relocating the cause of frustration in temporal rupture ("reality bleed[ing] through") rather than excess simulation — a deliberate displacement of the obvious reading.
- **Designed slowness vs. chosen slowness.** Borrowing Scully-Blaker's player-side "radical slowness" but reassigning it to *design* creates a conceptual strain the authors acknowledge by distinguishing Dead Eye's mastery-slowness from radical slowness.
- **Vocal minority vs. commercial triumph.** The negative player discourse is conceded to be "perhaps a vocal minority" against record-breaking sales, raising the question of how representative the analyzed frustration is — the authors lean on its diagnostic rather than statistical value.
