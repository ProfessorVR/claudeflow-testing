# Westerside & Holopainen, "Sites of Play: Locating Gameplace in *Red Dead Redemption 2*" (2019)

**Citation.** Andrew Westerside & Jussi Holopainen (2019), "Sites of Play: Locating Gameplace in *Red Dead Redemption 2*," *Proceedings of DiGRA 2019*, Digital Games Research Association. (Conference paper; both authors, University of Lincoln.)

**Discipline.** Performance studies × games design research (with a human-computer-interaction bias) — a deliberately interdisciplinary "analytical conversation." **Depth: deep.**

---

## Thesis

The article argues that *RDR2* makes **place qua place** its central compositional resource, and that the critical vocabulary developed in **site-specific performance studies** can sharpen our account of how place is *experienced* (and therefore *designed*) in open-world videogames. To name the object of that account, the authors coin the term **gameplace**: the affective relationship between place, experience, and play. The headline claim is that *RDR2*'s design primacy of place — functioning as both **topopoetic storyteller** and **structural ballast** — is the compositional fulcrum through which the game generates affect, emotional response, and player identification.

## Method

The study proceeds by **close-reading of gameplay experience** (after Bizzocchi and Tanenbaum, 2011). Each author individually played 30+ hours of *RDR2*, taking notes, recording, and capturing screenshots; the two then met periodically to compare notes, discuss notable moments, and share analytical insights. This is qualitative, autoethnographic-adjacent first-person play-criticism rather than empirical user study — a point worth flagging against the cluster's HCI/UX-survey members.

## Section structure

1. **Introduction — Site-Specific Performance.** Establishes the genealogy of site-specific performance (land art of the 1960s–70s; Smithson's *Spiral Jetty*, Turrell, Christo & Jeanne-Claude; collectives Brith Gof, Lone Twin, Wrights and Sites) and the place/space distinction that grounds the whole argument.
2. **Players & Spectators.** Maps the videogame *player* onto the performance *spectator*, drawing on Pearson's triangulation of activity / audience / place and on Soraya Murray's reading of *GTA: San Andreas*.
3. **Go West, Young Man.** Applies Jenkins's four modes of environmental storytelling to *RDR2*; situates the game against its predecessor *RDR* and Humphreys's neoliberal-Western critique.
4. **Site as Storyteller, Site as Structure.** The analytic core: a three-moment "geographic narrative" tracing Chapter 1 (the blizzard mountains) → Valentine (Chapter 2) → Saint Denis (Chapter 4), theorized via Casey's *topopoesis* and spatial compression.
5. **Home, Identity, and Self-Motivation.** Reads the moving **camp** as home, the dual mode of player agency, NPC believability, and Nitsche's three indicators of placeness.
6. **Conclusion.** Restates gameplace as design fulcrum; gestures toward future comparative work (*This War of Mine*, *Papers, Please*, *Everybody's Gone to the Rapture*).

## Key concepts

- **Gameplace.** The article's signature coinage: "the affective relationship between place, experience and play." Defined against the merely navigable **gameworld** — most pointedly in the closing distinction that a gameworld is "not just a space for traversal, but a world for being in." The authors explicitly refine Ali's (2016) prior use of "gameplace" (a meaning-evoking location within gamespace) by adding how *ludic* elements — player activities, goals, narrative structures — manufacture that meaning.
- **Placeness / sitedness.** Borrowed from Nitsche's (2009) three indicators of placeness — **identity**, **self-motivated and self-organised action**, and **traces of memory** — which the authors read as overlapping with site-specific performance's notion of *site*.
- **Place vs. space (phenomenology).** Following Tuan (1977), the place/space difference is "a fundamentally human one": space becomes place when filled or "woven through" with human connections. This phenomenological premise licenses treating games-that-occur-in-places as **site-specific encounters**.
- **Placeevent.** McLucas's term (via Kaye 2000): "a place and what is built there bleed into each other." Supplies the ontological place/space differentiation the authors transpose to games.
- **Topopoesis.** Casey's (2002) "poetic truth of place" rather than topographical representation. The authors claim *RDR2* is "arguably the most explicit and abundant example of topopoesis in videogames."
- **Site as symbol / storyteller / structure.** Wilkie's (2002) taxonomy, used to argue *RDR2* "engages with site as symbol, site as story-teller, [and] site as structure."
- **Pearson's stances toward visitation.** A taxonomy of player-as-visitor figures — **nomad**, **wayfarer**, **derivist**, **flâneur** — mapped onto *RDR2*'s traversal and encounter systems.
- **Dual mode of agency.** A "slight friction" between free-roaming (Arthur as "vessel of the player's own objectives") and story quests (which "take away the player's agency" and impose Arthur's fictional agency), producing identification *with* Arthur vs. *as* Arthur.
- **Compression (topographic → topological).** After Wark (2007): regions are "compressed and elaborated, becoming caricatures," not accurate representations (contrast Schwartz 2006); the *order* of encounter is itself an affective device.

## Readings of *RDR2*

- *RDR2* deploys Jenkins's **four modes of environmental storytelling** — evocative spaces, enacting stories, embedded narratives, emergent narratives — whose interweaving builds a believable gameworld saturated with a mediated "Wild West."
- Where the predecessor *RDR* used the Western as a "powerful conduit" of neoliberal ideology (Humphreys 2012), *RDR2* makes the frontier itself the political ideal — "a place for dreamers, rebels and reactionaries." The new protagonist Arthur Morgan, a bona fide outlaw, escapes the recuperation-into-neoliberalism critique aimed at John Marston; the authors even wonder aloud whether scholarly critiques of *RDR* shaped *RDR2*'s development.
- The three-place "geographic narrative" stages a "wrenching dissonance between three orders of existence" — blizzard wilderness, frontier-town Valentine, industrial Saint Denis — and that dissonance is an *effect* of the iterative, compressed relationship to landscape.
- Arthur's "man out of time" sentiment ("we're more ghosts than people"; "thieves, in a world that don't want us no more") is keyed to place; the authors note its "curious parallels to the post-truth era."
- *RDR2*'s realism, after Galloway (2004), resides "in the pedestrian, domestic details of everyday life" — NPC background chatter, market activity — rather than in graphical fidelity. The world "appears to exist in spite of us, rather than for us."

## Game-elements treated (precisely)

- **Regions / map geography.** The game's regions evoke "archetypical environments of the southern states from Louisiana swamps to Texan oilfields" (evocative spaces). The authors stress *compression*: it takes Arthur "less than 10 minutes to ride from the mountain ranges of the Grizzlies to the coastal swamps of Lemoyne," yet the affect comes precisely from that quasi-real spatial compression and its ordering. Lemoyne's swamps invoke Louisiana; Saint Denis stands in for New Orleans / the Deep South (endnote 4).
- **Chapter 1 blizzard mountains (Grizzlies).** Low visibility, scarce food and warmth; the gang reads as "nomadic," even refugee/migrant-coded. Site functions as "story-teller-in-chief." On first playthrough it takes "well in excess of an hour" to leave the mountains, making safety inconceivable.
- **Valentine (frontier town, Chapter 2: Horseshoe Overlook).** "Replete with a saloon, jail, stables, a hotel and a train station," a "quintessential 'frontier town'" drawing on Leone, *Once Upon a Time in the West*, HBO's *Westworld*, John Ford's *The Searchers*, and Hudson River School vistas; character art mimics John Singer Sargent. Valentine is where the player gets the "first meaningful window" into Arthur's character — precisely because it looks exactly as the Old West is expected to look.
- **Saint Denis (industrial city, Chapter 4).** A "newly industrial steel and steam town" with streetlights, cobbled streets, theatres, photography studios, butchers, barbers, doctors, chimneys, factories, public transport — "an emerging, civilized, and emboldened industrial America." Its emergence feels "almost magical / inconceivable" given the surrounding wilderness; arrival is the "empathy-driven climax of feeling."
- **The camp (moving home).** The gang's base of operations "moves across the map as the story progresses"; it is "the place of care, maintenance, and familial social interaction" and "acts as a home for the player" despite relocating. Anchors the article's expanded sense of **home** (political home as much as bricks-and-mortar), read through Will Wright's cowboy-myth "open frontier."
- **Traversal & encounter systems.** Two ways of meeting content — the "dot on the mini-map" (player as **wayfarer**) vs. random encounter via an NPC's call or a gathering crowd (player as **derivist**). Continuous control over Arthur (outside cut-scenes) builds a "strong kinaesthetic connection with the avatar."
- **NPCs & settlements.** Camps, towns, and the city "teem with NPCs" with apparent aims and desires; background chatter (e.g., a Saint Denis marketplace) signals "meaningful activity carried out in the place regardless of the player's actions." NPCs have "different levels of believability" (Mateas 1997; Tanenbaum 2008). The player here is Pearson's **flâneur**.
- **Free-roam activities & long-term goals.** Hunting (big game; "hunt as many different animals as possible"), poker in the saloon, theatre shows, exploration; the **compendium / achievement systems** and Arthur's **personal journal** (in which he "proves himself a talented artist") scaffold self-set long-term goals.
- **Honor system (endnote).** *RDR2* runs on an honor system: positive/negative actions shift NPC behavior, vendor prices, and dialogue trees; robbery and murder are most "dishonorable." The notable exception — punishing a randomly encountered **Ku Klux Klan** meeting yields *positive* honor — is the "only instance" where a crime is judged "through the lens of an ethical politics one-hundred years in the future."
- **Prequel haunting (endnote).** As a prequel to a game in which the Van Der Linde gang is hunted down, and given Arthur's absence from *RDR* (later confirmed by his tuberculosis diagnosis in Chapter 5), *RDR2* is "haunted by the prospect of Arthur's death," which both galvanizes and diminishes the drive to impose self-motivated desires on emergent narrative.

## Themes

Phenomenology of place; affect and emotional response; player identification (with/as avatar); environmental storytelling; the mythic American frontier and the Western genre; neoliberalism and its partial disavowal; home and belonging; realism as everyday minutiae; spatial compression and "unreal geographies"; interdisciplinarity (performance ↔ games).

## Debate-axis positioning

- **Phenomenology / experience-of-place vs. ideology-critique.** The article occupies a distinctive seat: it takes ideology critique seriously (engaging Humphreys at length) but **shifts the primary axis** from *what the game represents/legitimates* to *how place is affectively experienced*. It treats *RDR2* as partly escaping the neoliberal recuperation pinned on *RDR*, siding with affect/phenomenology over straight ideology-critique while conceding the critique's force.
- **Methodological axis: qualitative close-reading vs. empirical user study.** By grounding claims in 30+ hours of reflective first-person play (Bizzocchi & Tanenbaum), the paper stakes out the interpretive-humanities pole against the cluster's HCI/UX-measurement members.
- **Disciplinary import axis.** Its signature move is to import *performance studies* (site-specific theory) into game analysis — a methodological transplant few cluster-mates attempt, positioning place/site as the bridge concept.
- **Gameworld vs. gameplace.** It sharpens an internal games-studies distinction (mere traversable space vs. lived, affect-bearing place), extending Nitsche's placeness toward an explicitly *ludic-affective* account.

> diss-hook: *RDR2*'s **dual mode of agency** (free-roam Arthur as "vessel of the player's own objectives" vs. story quests that "impose his fictional agency") maps cleanly onto an actualization-of-desire chain — free-roam corresponds to player-originated *orexis* terminating in self-chosen action, while scripted quests reroute desire through the protagonist's settled disposition; the camp-as-moving-home and Pearson's nomad/wayfarer/derivist stances offer ready RODA coordinates for tutorial-vs-disruption analysis. *(anticipatory-application)*
