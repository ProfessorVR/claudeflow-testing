# Donald & Reid — "The Wild West: Accuracy, Authenticity and Gameplay in *Red Dead Redemption 2*"

**Citation:** Iain Donald and Andrew Reid, "The Wild West: Accuracy, Authenticity and Gameplay in *Red Dead Redemption 2*," *Media Education Journal* 66, pp. 15–23 (n.d.; post-2019 given the legal disputes it cites).

**Discipline:** Media/history education; game studies (applied, pedagogical).
**Depth handled:** deep.

---

## Thesis

The article advances a tripartite analytic vocabulary — **account, accuracy, authenticity** — for reading video games as historical "text," and it argues that *Red Dead Redemption 2* (RDR2) succeeds historically not because it is accurate but because it *feels* authentic. The authors say that historical accuracy ("contextualisation and alignment with the established historical record") and historical authenticity (a more "personal and subjective" matter rooted in "communal and collective memory") are genuinely distinct concepts, and that games — more than film or literature — foreground the gap between them. RDR2 is, on their reading, "not historically accurate" yet "firmly rooted in the early 20th century": Rockstar "weaves facts and fiction so expertly" that players "easily forgive discrepancies." The deeper purpose is pedagogical: the authors aim to encourage teachers to use popular games as classroom artefacts, treating RDR2 as a worked example of "games as containers of historical text" that can stimulate inductive reasoning, source evaluation, and inquiry into American Expansionism.

A single anchor-claim governs the whole: authenticity "does not have to be accurate but it has to feel authentic." The game's value lies in inviting players to "play with history to understand the world of the protagonist," not in teaching history directly.

## Structure

The piece moves from concept-definition to a structured "Historical Analysis" applied across three game-design areas:

1. **Framing** — RDR2's commercial success, the "sandbox" genre, and the Red Dead series' tangled continuity (Revolver 1880s; Redemption 1911; RDR2 1899).
2. **Account** — defines "historical account" as the evidentiary basis games draw on; introduces Tracy Fullerton's split of game "layers" into **formal elements** (rules, boundaries, procedures, objectives, conflicts, resources, outcomes) and **dramatic elements** (premise, challenge, play, story, dramatic arc). Adds Clara Fernández-Vara's "game analysis" as a content-analysis method for treating games as text.
3. **Accuracy** — defines "material accuracy" (organisations, events, buildings, outfits, objects); argues material detail grounds immersion and believability, while games deliberately avoid binding *characters'* thought and action to the historical record.
4. **Authenticity** — defines authenticity as feeling/atmosphere rooted in collective memory; this is where Rockstar excels.
5. **Historical Analysis of RDR2** — a three-part applied analysis, each subsection structured as *Account → Accuracy → Authenticity → Application to Learning*:
   - **Characters:** Van der Linde Gang; Pinkerton Detective Agency; Murfree Brood.
   - **Environment:** the Declining Wild West; Encampments; Saint Denis.
   - **Events:** the Union Pacific Overland Flyer Robbery; the Spanish-American War (newspapers); Civic Unrest / the Braithwaite–Gray feud.
6. **Next Steps** — concedes the three concepts are "at best nebulous"; reaffirms RDR2 as a teaching gateway rather than a history lesson.

## Key Concepts

- **Account / Accuracy / Authenticity (the tripartite frame).** Account = the evidentiary, interpretive basis a game depicts; accuracy = alignment with the established record (timeline + material detail); authenticity = subjective feeling of period, grounded in communal/collective memory. The article's central methodological contribution.
- **Material accuracy.** A narrowed sense of accuracy restricted to "solid, objective facts" — organisations, events, buildings, outfits, objects — which the authors say is "fundamental to constructing a gameworld that is believable." Crucially, accuracy is *not* extended to characters' thought and action, which remain "an exaggerated version" filtered through "present day attitudes and standards."
- **Formal vs. dramatic elements (Fullerton).** The "layers" distinction that lets analysts separate rule-systems from aesthetic/emotional components without "misrepresenting" the game.
- **Game analysis as content analysis (Fernández-Vara).** Treating games "as containers of text," applying literary/film/art content-analysis methods to identify, critique, and evaluate.
- **Collective / cultural memory.** Authenticity is "deeply rooted in" shared perceptions of the past; RDR2 is praised for handling "the multiple layers of cultural memory associated with the wild west."
- **Weaving fact and fiction.** The recurrent mechanism: storytellers "find the space" in "gaps in the historical record" to blend fact and fiction into "a believable account."
- **Immersion / believability.** The payoff concept: accurate material detail lets players "feel that the world is believable and 'real,'" so they "can be immersed."
- **Games as text / pedagogy.** The applied frame: games as classroom artefacts that develop observational, evaluative, and reasoning skills.

## Readings (interpretive claims about RDR2)

- **The Western genre as authenticity-generator.** The authors read RDR2 as a "sprawling cowboy epic" whose power is atmospheric: "carefully considered historical atmosphere," "one of gaming's best examples." Authenticity here is explicitly *film-inflected* — the Murfree Brood, for instance, are read as more obviously indebted to *Deliverance*, *The Hills Have Eyes*, *Ravenous*, and *Bone Tomahawk* than to the historical record. The Western, on this reading, is mediated through cinema before history.
- **Characters as composites, not portraits.** Dutch Van der Linde is read as drawing on the "mythos and subjectivity" of Butch Cassidy as "a framework — rather than an accurate account." Leviticus Cornwall is read as a "parodic caricature" amalgamating Vanderbilt, J.P. Morgan, and Andrew Carnegie. The reading stresses that Rockstar "stop short of making any social commentary," giving "authentic feeling" without critique.
- **Environment as fictionalised place rooted in real time.** RDR2 is "set in a fictional place but it is firmly rooted in the early 20th century." Saint Denis is read as a "fictional characterisation of New Orleans," its segregated design possibly echoing *Plessy v. Ferguson*, and even a layered allusion to St. Denis of Paris.
- **Newspapers as authenticity-effect, not record.** The in-game press is read as "not accurate portrayals" yet conveying "an aura of the journalistic standards of 1899" — jingoistic, "yellow journalism" in tone but "simplified and modernised."
- **Play with history, not history taught.** The governing interpretive stance: RDR2 "is not a game that teaches history," but invites players to "play with history."

## Game-Elements Treated (precise)

The article's applied analysis is unusually granular; it treats game-elements through the *Account → Accuracy → Authenticity* lens:

- **Historical settings / towns — Saint Denis.** The largest urban area; modelled on New Orleans (Jackson Square → "Harris Square"; the David Hennessy murder → in-game Henry Jenkins murder + New Orleans lynchings; an optional saloon vermin-control mission; a vampire side-encounter based on *Nosferatu*'s Count Orlok). Read as the authentic-feeling fictionalisation of a real port city.
- **The open world / environment — the Declining Wild West (1899).** The "Gilded Age → progressivism" transition as a "template of conflicting beliefs"; environmental change (new properties, deforestation, completed railway) reflecting ideological change. Influenced by paintings — Bierstadt's *Among the Sierra Nevada* and N. C. Wyeth's *Hands Up!* — for lighting and composition.
- **Encampments (e.g., Horseshoe Overlook).** A gameplay/strategic system (camps near towns for supplies; near rivers for food/water; fortresses for defence). Horseshoe Overlook is read as reflecting Wyoming's Hole-in-the-Wall Pass (Butch Cassidy's Wild Bunch); the transitory movement of camps "projects the feeling of insecurity, paranoia."
- **Factions / characters — the Van der Linde Gang; Pinkertons; Murfree Brood.** The gang composited from the Dalton, Doolin-Dalton, and Wild Bunch gangs. The Pinkertons are the load-bearing legal case: their real-world trademark prompted a cease-and-desist; Take-Two counter-filed on fair-use grounds ("the game emphasises historical authenticity"), settled out of court — the article's clearest instance of authenticity having real-world stakes.
- **Missions / set-pieces — the Union Pacific Overland Flyer Robbery** ("Who the Hell is Leviticus Cornwall?", the 5th main mission). Plays "the Western trope of train robbery"; modelled on the 1899 Wilcox Robbery by the Wild Bunch (timeline tie-in called "largely fortuitous"); the in-game leaping-aboard diverges from the real flag-down + dynamite-on-mail-cars.
- **Events / period detail — the Spanish-American War via newspapers.** Fictional President Alfred MacAlister ↔ William McKinley; Colonel Thaddeus Waxman ↔ Theodore Roosevelt Jr.; Battle of San Juan Hill relocated to fictional "Guarma" (a fictitious Cuba); references to the Treaty of Paris and the Philippine Insurrection. Newspapers as the chief authenticity-vector.
- **Events — Civic Unrest / the Braithwaite–Gray feud (Chapter 3).** Composited from the Liddell–Jones and Hatfield–McCoy feuds; the player situated as "an external force" whose intervention drives the dramatised, blood-soaked resolution.
- **Weapons / material objects (period detail).** Treated under "material accuracy" generally — "the factual use of organisations, events, buildings, outfits and objects" — as the believability-anchoring layer rather than as a standalone analysis.

## Themes

- **Atmosphere over accuracy / feel over fact** — the spine of the argument.
- **Cinema mediates the historical** — Westerns reach the player through film tropes (cannibal-clan horror, the train robbery) before reaching the record.
- **Collective memory and "who owns history?"** — the Pinkerton dispute frames intellectual property and the contested ownership of the past.
- **Selective realism / depoliticised authenticity** — Rockstar evokes industry, labour, and colonialism but "stop short" of social commentary (the anti-imperialist movement is conspicuously absent).
- **Games as inquiry pedagogy** — the over-arching purpose: history-classroom use to cultivate sense-making and source evaluation.
- **Modern lens on the past** — material detail is period-true, but thought, action, and journalism are "modernised for the audience."

- **Tracy Fullerton**, *Game Design Workshop* (2014) — the formal/dramatic-elements scaffolding.
- **Clara Fernández-Vara**, *Introduction to Game Analysis* (2015) — games-as-text content-analysis method.

**Cluster-mates cited:** *None.* The article cites no member of the RDR2 secondary-literature cluster (Bagnoli, Westerside & Holopainen, Tuominen, Crowley, Wright, Vanderhoef & Payne, Ventomäki, McEvoy, Moser, Ruffino). Its bibliography is game-design pedagogy + American/Scottish history + film. This makes it a *conceptual* rather than *citational* cluster-mate: it supplies the authenticity-vs-accuracy axis that other cluster members presuppose or contest.

## Debate-Axis Positioning (authenticity-as-accuracy ↔ authenticity-as-feel)

This article is the cluster's clearest **authenticity-as-feel** statement, and it does the work of *naming* the axis explicitly. It separates "accuracy" (record-alignment) from "authenticity" (subjective feeling rooted in collective memory) and then argues that RDR2's achievement lies entirely on the feel side: "authenticity is about creating a feeling for the gameplay experience that feels correct in order to be immersed." It treats accuracy as *instrumental to* feel — material accuracy exists to make the world "believable" so immersion (feel) can occur — rather than as an end in itself. It thereby positions accuracy as the servant of authenticity, not its measure.

Within the cluster, this makes Donald & Reid a useful *foil and foundation*: realist/representational readings (e.g., critiques of RDR2's frontier ideology or labour politics) can be staged as contesting the article's willingness to "forgive discrepancies" for the sake of feel. The article itself concedes the depoliticisation point (Rockstar "stop short of making any social commentary") without treating it as a flaw — a concession other cluster members may weaponise.

**diss-hook:** *(anticipatory-application)* The account→accuracy→authenticity progression maps onto the dissertation's Aristotelian–Heideggerian actualization-of-desire chain as a *staging of believability*: "material accuracy" functions as the equipmental/ready-to-hand layer that secures a world (the *account* as the given), while "authenticity-as-feel" names the affective attunement (mood/*Stimmung*) through which a player is drawn into desiring within that world — i.e., the move from a depicted world to a felt, desire-soliciting one. RODA can borrow the article's insistence that feel, not fact, is what binds the player to the gameworld's solicitations.

## Tensions / Internal Strains

- **Concept-instability admitted.** The authors close by calling all three concepts "at best nebulous," partly undercutting the analytic rigour the frame promises.
- **Accuracy quietly subordinated.** Despite naming accuracy as distinct, the analysis repeatedly treats it as merely the substrate for authenticity, leaving "accuracy" with little independent evaluative force.
- **Authenticity ≈ cinematic familiarity.** Authenticity is grounded in collective memory, but the worked examples (cannibal-clans, train robbery) trace that memory to *film*, risking circularity: the game feels authentic because it matches our movies, not the past.
- **Depoliticisation noted but not problematised.** The observation that Rockstar evokes colonialism/labour "without complexity" is registered approvingly (as authentic restraint) rather than critically — a tension a politically-minded reading would press.
- **Pedagogical aim vs. fictional content.** The article wants RDR2 as a history-teaching gateway while conceding it "is not a game that teaches history," leaving the classroom payoff resting on critical comparison rather than on the game's own content.
