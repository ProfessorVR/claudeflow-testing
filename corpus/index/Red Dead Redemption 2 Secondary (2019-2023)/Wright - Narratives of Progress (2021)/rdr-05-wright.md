# Wright, Esther (2021), "Rockstar Games, Red Dead Redemption, and Narratives of 'Progress'"

**Citation:** Esther Wright, "Rockstar Games, Red Dead Redemption, and Narratives of 'Progress'," *European Journal of American Studies* 16-3 (2021), Special Issue: Video Games and/in American Studies. DOI: 10.4000/ejas.17300.
**Discipline:** American Studies / game history / historical game studies / industry & paratext studies.
**Depth:** deep.

---

## Thesis

Wright argues that Rockstar Games deploys narratives of "progress" on three interlocking levels — to maintain its developer-brand image, to structure the Red Dead franchise's *promotional surround*, and within the textual content of the games — and that this practice is internally contradictory. The same Frontier-mythic, civilization-versus-savagery discourse that Rockstar uses to *sell* its games and brand identity is precisely what the games claim to *critique*. The article's central move is to read the games' professed critical, "authentic," satirical engagement with turn-of-the-century US history against the promotional and branding apparatus that frames them. The verdict: the critique is selective and surface-level, undercut by Rockstar's reliance on the very ideologies (the Frontier myth, white supremacy, settler colonialism, capitalist "progress") it gestures at condemning. The article-says position is that any criticism which merely scratches the surface of structural violence while elsewhere profiting from the discourses bound up with it deserves scrutiny rather than celebration.

A second, recursive thread runs in parallel: Rockstar's brand narrative of *industrial* "progress" — pushing the "frontiers" of open-world game development, making bigger and "better" worlds — mirrors and reinforces the *historical* narrative of "progress" inside the games. Wright connects this to a critique of games-industry historiography itself (drawing on Murray, Nooney, Therrien, Litherland) as a "patrilineal," teleological, technologically determinist "fairy tale" — and aligns those cautions with revisionist Western historiography's own warnings (Limerick, Trachtenberg) about the violence of progress-narratives.

## Structure

The article moves through four named parts after an introduction:

1. **Introduction** — frames the October 2019 PC re-release of RDR2 and its "enhanced"/"progress" promotional discourse; sets up the three-level argument (brand, paratext, text); introduces the "irony as no-lose gambit" frame (Kline, Dyer-Witheford & de Peuter) and the "have your cake and eat it too" structure of self-cancelling critique.
2. **The Rockstar Brand and Open-World Development** — the "Outlaws for Life" brand, "problem child"/rebellious-outsider identity intersecting with "industry leader" status; Take-Two press releases; "Rockstar World" replacing "open world"; games-industry obsession with "progress" and digi-Whiggish/teleological game history.
3. **The "True West" and an "Evolving" America** — the paratextual *historical-authenticity* network: the "True West" Newswire blog series, Rockstar as "developer-historian," faux-historical advertisements (Wheeler & Rawson catalogue, Cattleman Revolver ad), the civilization/savagery binary, the Homestead Act / "safety valve" myth versus historical reality, and the labor-irony of the revolver ad against Rockstar's own "crunch" controversies.
4. **"It's 'reverse Integration'…or 'regressive acculturation'…"; or, "Eugenics for Beginners"** — the racial/colonial content: Reservations (Cochinay in RDR, Wapiti in RDR2), Nastas and MacDougal, race science, the Saint Denis eugenics-pamphlet encounter, the "I don't see color" response, and the false-equivalence problem.
5. **Conclusion: The Outlaw Brand's "Frontier" Narrative(s)** — synthesizes: progress-narratives as "moral disengagement" device; muddled politics; branding frame undermining textual critique; the franchise as "conflicted in their possible meanings."

## Key Concepts

- **Narratives of "progress"** — the organizing concept. "Progress" (bound to "civilization"/"savagery," "development," "improvement") functions simultaneously as historical content, marketing rhetoric, and brand self-description. Wright treats it as ideologically loaded, traceable to Turner's Frontier Thesis and Roosevelt's *Winning of the West*.
- **The paratextual / promotional surround** — Wright's signature analytic. Rockstar builds a wide network of pre-play content (trailers, Newswire blogs, gameplay videos, faux advertisements) that "preloads" players with historical "truths" and manages expectations — a "discourse of historical authenticity." The argument insists analysis of paratext must not be separated from text, because paratext casts a "harsher light" on ideological resonance.
- **Authenticity as a constructed discourse** — authenticity here is *not* accuracy but a manufactured, marketed claim; Rockstar adopts a "pedagogical role" and a "developer-historian" posture (Chapman's term) to commodify research and stake authenticity claims.
- **Rockstar's historiography / "developer-historian"** — the company writes history twice: the history *in* the games and the history *of* its own brand/industry "progress." Both are teleological.
- **Irony as "no-lose gambit"** — the "have your cake and eat it too" structure (Kline et al.; Dyer-Witheford & De Peuter on GTA's "self-cancellation") whereby simultaneous affirmation/negation gives the appearance of critique while retracting it.
- **The "F-Word" (Frontier) / civilization-savagery binary** — the pervasive, useful myth (Slotkin, Limerick) structuring both the games' narratives and Rockstar's "new frontier" of development rhetoric.
- **Moral disengagement** — progress-narratives operate as a "moral disengagement factor" (Hartmann & Vorderer; Hartmann) justifying the ludonarrative imperative to fight/eliminate antagonists who represent modernizing "civilization."
- **False (and damaging) equivalence** — the article's recurring critical lever: the suffering of Native Americans is repeatedly aligned with the threatened "extinction" of white outlaws; and satirical NPC encounters (eugenicist, suffragette, get-rich-quick schemer) receive equivalent comic weighting.
- **"Playing Indian"** — Deloria's concept, applied to the Van Der Linde gang's selective adoption of Native identity.

## Readings (interpretive claims about the games)

- The franchise structures both narrative and gameplay around a lament for the passing of the age of outlaws and the loss of "free" Western space for white male outlaws (Arthur Morgan, John Marston, Dutch).
- Domestic/"chores" gameplay (camp upkeep, providing for the gang; RDR's final act; RDR2's epilogue) dramatizes the lost yeoman-homesteader ideal — but the outcome is "always unavoidably tragic," denying the ideal "Western" life. Wright reads this against the historical reality that only ~10% of 1860–1900 Western land went to individual homesteaders (Trachtenberg citing Shannon).
- The critique of "civilization" is a "superficial repurposing" of revisionist-Western themes (*The Wild Bunch*, *Unforgiven*); the games' politics "read as hopelessly muddled."
- Native American characters function only as a "device" to position white outlaws as oppositional to "the Law"; their narrative value is "directly proportional to their otherness."
- The greatest sympathy and screen time go to white male protagonists *as victims*, not to Indigenous populations — a false equivalence that confines genocide to "the past."
- The Saint Denis eugenics encounter and the MacDougal subplot reduce race science to something "idiotic" and "of the past," aligning with individual-bad-racist framing (Anderson's *White Rage*) rather than structural critique (Saini).

## Game-Elements Treated

Wright is unusually precise about specific in-game and paratextual artifacts:

1. **The RDR2 PC announcement/launch trailer** (paratext/trailer) — Arthur Morgan voiceover ("More and more civilization, what a damn mess we're making of things") over Western-landscape shots; used as epigraph and recurring anchor.
2. **"The True West" Newswire blog series** (paratext) — Jan–Sept 2010 posts ("Frank James," "Western Weaponry," "New Inventions," "Patent Medicines, Drink and Drugs," "The Brands of Red Dead Redemption") positioning Rockstar as developer-historian and historicizing the 1899-era world.
3. **Faux-historical in-game advertisements / Wheeler & Rawson Co. mail-order catalogue** (in-game UI / store interface + paratext) — the player's purchasing interface for provisions, weapons, clothing; the **Cattleman Revolver advert** ("gateway to the riches of the frontier"; factory in Worcester, Mass.; "skilled laborers who work tireless hours…for little pay") used to expose the labor-irony against Rockstar's "crunch" culture.
4. **RDR2 "Official Gameplay Video" non-diegetic voiceover** (paratext/trailer) — historicizes the late-1800s "evolving" America, "civilization spreading rapidly into the huge, wild and lawless frontier," "capturing this pivotal moment when the age of outlaws was ending."
5. **Cochinay Reservation, RDR** (game space / mission) — inaccessible until third-act missions "And You Will Know the Truth" / "And the Truth Will Set You Free," where Marston must storm the Reservation with the US Army; "wiping out every last man" is a predetermined mission objective — a micro-scale recreation of genocidal violence.
6. **Wapiti Reservation, RDR2** (game space) — populated largely by "the old and infirm"; Eagle Flies's dialogue (mission "Favored Sons") that the young have been "shipped off to reform schools"; reinforces the "vanishing American" trope (Dippie).
7. **MacDougal & Nastas, RDR** (characters / cutscene + mission "At Home with Dutch") — disgraced Yale professor performing race science ("reverse Integration"/"regressive acculturation," "Eugenics for Beginners" register), played off against the rational, critical Nastas; "played for laughs."
8. **"Eugenics for Beginners" pamphlet / Saint Denis NPC encounter, RDR2** (optional interaction / satchel item) — Professor Norris Forsythe handbill; Arthur's "I've known good, bad, and dumb white people" / "I don't see color" pre-scripted response (Blake on "racism without racists"); satchel description: "a racist, idiotic eugenics handbill."
9. **Saint Denis** (game space/city) — "a representation of all that is wrong with American modernity and 'progress'"; site of multiple satirical NPC encounters (eugenicist, suffragette, get-rich-quick proto-entrepreneur) given "equivalent weighting."
10. **Law-and-order antagonists** (characters) — Federal Agent Ross (RDR), Pinkerton Agent Milton (RDR2), Colonel Favours — stand-ins for US government policy; Milton/Dutch cutscene exchange ("This is a civilized land now…" / "ain't no such thing as civilized…found only appetites") as emblematic "critique."
11. **Red Dead Online roles** (game system) — Bounty Hunter, Trader, Trapper, etc.; the player "becomes whatever kind of Westerner" satisfying the desire to exist in the virtual West (instances the exploration/mastery-of-space critique).
12. **Optional KKK rallies and "The Iniquities of History" / Jeremiah Compson side mission** (optional random encounters / side missions, RDR2, footnote 6) — slave-catcher's ledger of "recovered" slaves, cited as further ambivalent inclusions.

## Themes

- The collapse of authenticity-claims into ideology when paratext is read alongside text.
- The doubling of "progress" (industrial/technological progress of the brand ↔ historical progress in the diegesis) and its shared teleological violence.
- The "have it both ways" structure of corporate irony in the games industry.
- Settler colonialism, Indigenous erasure, and the "absent presence" of the Indian (Byrd).
- White male victimhood as the franchise's emotional center of gravity.
- Race science / eugenics rendered as comic anachronism rather than living structure.
- Labor, "crunch," and the contradiction between depicted and actual exploitation.

## Debate-Axis Positioning

- **Celebratory ↔ Critical:** firmly **critical**. Wright is among the most pointed critics of Rockstar's self-presentation, explicitly questioning "the value of any criticism that merely scratches the surface." She is careful, however, not to assign Rockstar "full brunt of responsibility" — they are "merely capitalizing on a wider capitalistic, postmodern recycling of images," which positions her as a *structural/ideological* critic rather than a moralizing one.
- **Authenticity-as-accuracy ↔ authenticity-as-feel:** Wright **reframes the axis entirely** toward **authenticity-as-constructed-discourse / authenticity-as-marketing**. Authenticity is neither accuracy nor phenomenological "feel" but a *manufactured claim* circulated through paratext. She explicitly refuses to grade the games on accuracy ("it is unrealistic to expect a video game to prioritize…historiographical debate") — but turns that concession into a sharper question: why make authenticity claims at all if exempt from historical standards?
- **Text ↔ paratext:** strongly insists the two be read together; this is the methodological axis she most distinctively occupies and pushes the cluster toward.

## Tensions / Internal Caveats

- Wright concedes it may be "unrealistic to expect a video game" to carry nuanced historiography, and that Rockstar isn't solely responsible for video games' "dubious ideological positions" — yet maintains that authenticity-claims forfeit that excuse.
- She allows that the games "suggest an awareness" of their representational stakes (non-optional dialogue, cutscenes) and that "it would be overly simplistic" to say Rockstar are *not* critical — but holds that awareness ≠ sustained critique.
- She acknowledges the *radical potential* of historical games (Chapman) even while arguing these particular titles default to a "historiographic status quo" (Uricchio).

### diss-hook (anticipatory-application)
**diss-hook:** Wright's three-level "progress" apparatus (brand / paratext / text) and her insistence that authenticity is a *manufactured discourse* rather than accuracy could anchor a RODA-method treatment of how RDR2's promotional surround pre-shapes the player's *desire* and horizon of expectation before play — i.e., paratext as a pre-actualization conditioning of the actualization-of-desire chain, with Arthur's "more and more civilization…" lament functioning as the diegetic correlate of a marketed nostalgia. *(Tagged anticipatory-application; not argued by Wright.)*
