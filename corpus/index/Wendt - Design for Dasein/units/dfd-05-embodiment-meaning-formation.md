## 1. Argument arc (overview)

Wendt's chapter moves from a *negative* clearing operation to a *positive* reconstruction and finally to design consequences. (1) He clears three "less-than-helpful" Cartesian assumptions designers carry — that users are rational subjects, that users are conscious of their own goals, and that users think-then-act (p.123 / pdf 125). (2) He installs an embodiment frame drawn from Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, Dreyfus, and the extended-mind thesis, so that what a thing *is* matters less than what it *does* (p.125 / pdf 127). (3) He develops affordances (Gibson, via Norman, Tonkinwise, Krippendorff) as the hinge concept that resists the subject/object dichotomy — affordances are "both physical and psychical, yet neither" (p.130 / pdf 132). (4) He reframes meaning as **embodied meaning**, distributed in the environment and contingent on action, and distinguishes *sense* (single use-context) from *meaning* (the sum of imaginable use-contexts) (pp.133–138 / pdf 135–140). (5) He treats the **interface** as the active mediation point of structural coupling, and critiques the design fetish for "invisibility" and "intuition," proposing *transparency* as the more attainable and preferable goal (pp.140–148 / pdf 142–150). (6) He opens **embodied design practice** (situated design, service/systemic design, embodied interaction, ethnography) as the constructive program for the rest of the book (pp.148–152 / pdf 150–154).

The single through-line: **a move from a metaphysics of design (what objects *are*) to a phenomenology of design (what objects *do* and how they mean in use)** (p.133 / pdf 135).

## 2. Section: Embodiment (pp. 123–127 / pdf 125–129)

### 2.1 The clearing operation: three Cartesian assumptions
The chapter opens by naming Heideggerian phenomenology as a reaction to Cartesian dualism: mind and body are intrinsically linked and non-hierarchical (p.123 / pdf 125). Wendt observes that Cartesianism has leaked far beyond philosophy — into the split of physical vs. mental health, theory vs. practice. He then isolates three assumptions designers "make all the time," implicit in action though not conscious in thought:
- **(A1)** users are rational subjects (p.123 / pdf 125);
- **(A2)** users are conscious of their own goals — they act on consciously sought ends (p.123 / pdf 125);
- **(A3)** users think-then-act: identify a goal, form a cognitive plan, then execute it (p.123–124 / pdf 125–126).

These assumptions are baked into design outputs — flow diagrams, personas, scenarios, use cases — which are "important and necessary," but, unrecognized as *disembodied*, lead designers "down an unnecessarily dualist path" (p.124 / pdf 126). This is a key methodological claim: the deliverables are not wrong, but their inherently abstracted, disembodied state must be acknowledged.

### 2.2 Embodied cognition
Wendt glosses **embodied cognition** as the idea that thinking is embodied in the world: we do not formulate theoretical models then act on them; we interact with things and learn, thus shaping future behavior (p.124 / pdf 126). He draws **Andy Clark**: intelligence is rooted "not in the presence and manipulation of explicit, language-like data structures" but in "the tuning of basic responses to a real world" that lets an embodied organism sense, act, and survive (p.124 / pdf 126). The education example follows: rote memorization is only one type of knowing; real understanding comes from acting out what we know — "learn by doing" rings true. *Knowledge* tells us how to classify (a tomato is a fruit); *understanding* suggests how to use it (wisdom is not putting it in a fruit salad) (p.124 / pdf 126).

### 2.3 Dreyfus: AI as the example, not the target
Wendt returns to **Hubert Dreyfus** (introduced in ch. 2): his critique of AI is not "simply a critique of AI" but a theory of embodied, contextual, praxis-based cognition that happens to use AI as a convenient example (p.125 / pdf 127). Classical AI rested on symbolic systems that ignored bodily context (p.124 / pdf 126); a computer "had no bodily experience" and could not adapt to changing conditions. Dreyfus, taking a Heideggerian line, holds that humans are bound within their context and that situational elements affect behavior and cognition more than recitable facts. The tomato-in-fruit-salad case recurs: category membership is "trumped" by implicit understanding of how things operate in combination. Wendt applies this to interaction design and information architecture: a piece of web content may fit logically under one category but behaviorally under another — the IRS filing a tax form under "Taxes > Forms > Individuals" vs. labeling it "I need to do my taxes." "There is a difference between what the content *is* and what it *allows users to do*" (p.126 / pdf 128). **DESIGN PRINCIPLE (IA):** organize by what content lets users *do*, not by what it taxonomically *is*.

### 2.4 Merleau-Ponty: the body as ambiguous third party
**Maurice Merleau-Ponty** is introduced as the French phenomenologist and Heidegger contemporary who centered the body in perception and cognition (p.125 / pdf 127). Wendt draws three moves:
- The body is "neither subject nor object, but an ambiguous third party"; perception of external reality comes about "through and in relation to a sense of the body"; a theory of the body "is already a theory of perception" (p.125 / pdf 127).
- **Anti-representationalism / solicitations:** as an agent acquires skills, those skills are stored "not as representations in the agent's mind, but as the solicitations of situations in the world"; what the learner acquires is "presented … as more and more finely discriminated situations," not represented (p.126 / pdf 128). Wendt connects this to the **extended mind** thesis: the mind's function extends into the world (hard drives storing "memory," phones removing the need to memorize numbers, maps and instructions accessed only when needed, always through the body) while remaining connected to the body (p.126 / pdf 128). **RO-hook:** Merleau-Ponty's "solicitations of situations" and "finely discriminated situations" name a *pre-representational* taking-of-the-world that maps onto the perception→*phantasma* moment in the actualization-of-desire chain — the world soliciting action before any propositional grasp.
- **The body as a system of possible actions:** "what counts for the orientation of the spectacle is not my body as it in fact is … but as a system of possible actions, a virtual body whose phenomenal 'place' [is] defined by its task and situation. My body is wherever there is something to be done" (p.126 / pdf 128). This has "major implications for experience design," especially the role of user goals and the **multistability** of technology. The key question: can we design a system that guides accomplishment of goals — some conscious, some non-conscious — within the bounds of multistability? (p.126 / pdf 128).

### 2.5 Bonsiepe and structural coupling; Ihde's amplification/reduction
**Bonsiepe** takes a literal view: all design "ultimately ends in the body," and the task of design is to "attach the artifacts to the human body" — a process named **structural coupling** (p.127 / pdf 129). The mobile phone becomes an extension of the brain as the hand-axe extends the hand; software extends the body's capacity beyond its physical limits (Google Street View lets us "see" elsewhere; communication tech extends the voice). The caveat, drawn from **Don Ihde**: technologies that enable also obscure — the telescope shows what is far but hides what is near (p.127 / pdf 129). This is the amplification/reduction structure of mediation.

## 3. Section: Affordances and Action (pp. 127–133 / pdf 129–135)

### 3.1 The persistence of dualist bias in practice
Embodied views are increasingly embedded, yet modern designers remain "afflicted with the same deep-seated dualist bias" as early AI researchers (p.127 / pdf 129). There is a gap between designers' *conscious* metaphysics (no one would say users act on pure mental representations) and the *embodied expression* of their work, where "the dualist tendencies bubble up" (p.127 / pdf 129).

### 3.2 The "earthy" site of cognition; potentiality and bodily limit
Merleau-Ponty's "my body is wherever there is something to be done" places the **body-effect** (the experience of the body) into the "earthy" realm Andy Clark calls the site of cognition (p.128 / pdf 130). Body and mind come together "most explicitly in what we call *experience*" — the site of potentialities, where the individual mediates between which actions are *possible* and which are *preferable*, reading cues from environment and body (p.128 / pdf 130). The staircase example: a healthy teenager can walk down or creatively slide down the handrail; the arthritic 80-year-old with a cane has one option foreclosed (p.128 / pdf 130). Chapter 3's emphasis on the **hands** returns: hands are "the active agents of potentiality," but the rest of the body limits the hands and thus limits **creative misuse** (p.128 / pdf 130). "Embodiment can enable or hinder creative misuse by limiting the possibilities that objects afford." Drawing **Franssen**, objects have a *for-ness* — a reason for being that "calls forth certain actions" — but potential actions are limited by bodily capacity and opened by creative misuse, yielding a constant mediation between what *can theoretically* and *can practically* be done (p.128 / pdf 130).

### 3.3 Scripts (Latour) and the affordance concept (Norman)
Via **Latour**, designers create **scripts** — intended/planned actions or purposes embedded in an object (the speed bump scripts car-slowing; Facebook's apparent script to reconnect people) (p.128 / pdf 130). Scripts are communicated by embedding clear **affordances**. **Don Norman**'s *The Design of Everyday Things* supplies the familiar definition: affordances are interface elements that communicate what can be done — doorknobs afford graspability and turnability, buttons afford pushability, chairs afford sittability (p.129 / pdf 131). Wendt insists the theory "runs deeper than communication": it points to "a fundamental blending of the physical and cognitive" (p.129 / pdf 131). He qualifies **Flusser**'s etymology of the object as "what gets in the way" (from Latin *objectum* / Greek *problema*): the object impedes as much as it improves, but Wendt is "not so sure we can take Flusser's etymological interpretation too literally," since designed objects are so ingrained in everyday being (p.129 / pdf 131).

### 3.4 Gibson: affordances against dualism
**James J. Gibson** is the origin of affordance theory — a psychologist studying how pilots interact with complex cockpit interfaces (p.129 / pdf 131). We do not first perceive objects as discrete things but "according to their possible actions": the steering wheel is perceived primarily as graspable (especially with finger grooves), secondarily as a means of pointing the car; only after that do we think "steering wheel," a name "completely arbitrary in terms of what it actually does" (p.129 / pdf 131). **RO-hook (single, flagged):** Gibson's claim that we perceive objects "according to their possible actions" *before* naming them is a near-isomorph of the perception→*phantasma*→desire moment — the world disclosed first as action-potential (a *kinēsis*-soliciting appearance) and only derivatively as categorized object. Affordance theory thus understands objects "by *what they do*," not simply "*what they are*" (p.130 / pdf 132).

Gibson uses affordance to critique dualism like Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty: a tool in use is "an extension of the hand, almost an attachment … no longer a part of the environment of the user"; when not in use it is "a detached object of the environment." This shifting boundary shows "the absolute duality of 'objective' and 'subjective' is false"; "when we consider the affordances of things, we escape this philosophical dichotomy" (p.130 / pdf 132). Wendt reads Gibson as supplying what post-phenomenology found lacking in Heidegger (attention to individual objects): the object-in-use is like Heidegger's **readiness-to-hand** (it recedes into the background); when not in use it assumes a **present-at-hand** status (p.130 / pdf 132). Embodied action or skillful manipulation "couples body and world into a system of interaction" — the expert graphic designer is coupled with the mouse/trackpad/stylus, and in deep expertise with Photoshop itself, working "without conscious thought" toward a final design (p.130 / pdf 132).

### 3.5 The categorical irreducibility of affordances
Gibson: an affordance is "neither an objective property nor a subjective property; or it is both"; it "cuts across the dichotomy of subjective-objective," is "equally a fact of the environment and a fact of behavior," "both physical and psychical, yet neither," and "points both ways" (p.130 / pdf 132). Wendt's gloss: the button's affordance is not merely that it "looks" pressable but "a result of sensory communication, interpretation, and potential action"; affordances are "pure potentiality, which both communicate and enable action" (p.131 / pdf 133). He traces the root further back to **Georg Simmel**'s study of art: the vase handle must not only perform but make its possibility "manifest in its appearance"; design, for Simmel, "is the act of communicating practical use," and the handle figures the vase's "reaching out" into the world and its "connection to human teleology" (p.131 / pdf 133).

### 3.6 Perception vs. capacity to act; the unsemiotic affordance
Gibson might say the affordance exists regardless of the perceiver's bodily capability, but practically the object's potential for action "must be considered alongside bodily capability" (p.131 / pdf 133). The handrail affords slideability for both teenager and arthritic elder, but only the former can complete it — hence the distinction between **perceiving** an affordance (near-universal, save culturally determined affordances and differing ability) and the **ability to act** on it (wholly dependent on bodily capacity) (p.131–132 / pdf 133–134). "The action itself creates the coupling." Affordances communicate the **verb** that lets a user determine an object's script (a steering wheel steers), but may also communicate potentialities for creative misuse. Drawing **Tonkinwise**, an affordance is "an 'actual possibility,' a 'promised action opportunity'" — the result of "interactional perceptions, seeing not just a feature, but a future way of making use of that feature"; its key is its "utterly unsemiotic nature" — "communications, as it were, between things and my body without the involvement of my mind" ("my hand sees that handlable … before I have even really 'seen' it") (p.132 / pdf 134). Wendt names this **forced abductive thinking**: the affordance "forces the user to think about what could be and what an object *can do*, rather than what exists now" (p.132 / pdf 134). "Everyone designs but not everyone is a designer … it is the resulting action that designates design."

### 3.7 The between-ness of affordances; toward phenomenology of design
**Krippendorff** notes Gibson recognized the need for a human-centered language that would "neither physicalize human perception … nor psychologize the terrestrial environment" (p.133 / pdf 135). The special quality of affordances is that they "resist objective or subjective categories" and "operate outside the bounds of dualism." Wendt's thesis: design can build off this resistance to "rethink the role of the verb" — instead of explaining what designed objects *are*, the designer can articulate "what it means for designed objects *to be*," a shift "from a metaphysics of design to the phenomenology of design" (p.133 / pdf 135). **DESIGN PRINCIPLE (verb-orientation):** design the *action* (the verb) an object calls forth, not the static object.

## 4. Section: Embodied Meaning (pp. 133–140 / pdf 135–142)

### 4.1 Embodied meaning defined
Continuing the move "from what things *are* to *what things do*," Wendt examines how meaning operates in the designed world (p.133 / pdf 135). Meaning is "almost the opposing force of design"; it "reflects the successes and failures of the design process," depending on designer intent and user interpretation. **Embodied meaning** holds that meaning "arises from the ways in which we engage with and act within the world" (p.133 / pdf 135) — a "highly personal" concept that is nonetheless "distributed around the environment and … contingent on interaction with things." The **brain in a vat** would have no sense of meaning: despite storing and reciting facts, it has no bodily engagement (p.133 / pdf 135). **RO-hook (single, flagged):** "meaning arises from engaging and acting within the world" is the design-theoretic counterpart of meaning-formation as the terminus of an embodied perception→action arc — sense-making as the actualization of a worldly engagement rather than the read-out of an internal representation. Sensemaking and learning depend on memory and information *and* on "the potential for action." The professor anecdote (a colleague who could recite passages and dates but could not push past "what he knew") makes the point: his sense of meaning "was not embodied; he read them many times but did not *do* anything with them" (p.134 / pdf 136).

### 4.2 Action, writing, and the Freud analogy
Action matters for designers trying to embody knowledge; sketching and prototyping enact theoretical knowledge, but Wendt argues many designers under-rate **writing** as "an intermediary between decontextualized, armchair-style thinking and full prototyping" — letting a designer articulate intentions, scenarios, and outcomes in a linear story where missing pieces and conflicts show (p.134 / pdf 136). The **Freudian psychoanalysis** analogy: therapy as making the unconscious conscious; the therapeutic environment is "designed" to surface anti-rational unconscious desires so they can be rationally examined — moving the source of neurosis "from the decontextualized, disembodied unconscious into the embodied conscious mind through acting out, repetition" (p.134 / pdf 136). "The act of doing something with knowledge carries over to the design process."

### 4.3 Meaning as systemic and contextual
Meaning must be approached "from a systems perspective" and "is never divorced from its context" (p.134 / pdf 136): a coffee shop interior is unintelligible apart from tables, chairs, coffee, customers; an automobile apart from roads, drivers, cities, highways. Phenomenology studies the world "always through the lens of the individual and their capacity to generate meaning"; that interpretation — "the finding of place, understanding the connection between self and world — is experience" (p.134 / pdf 136). Designed objects "can either help us understand the complex web of meaning … or they can obscure it, or both" (p.135 / pdf 137).

### 4.4 Krippendorff: product semantics
**Klaus Krippendorff**'s **product semantics** maps how designed objects "incorporate themselves into everyday life and become meaningful," positioned as the next evolution of design thinking (which had focused on the design process and how designers think, forgetting the *effects* of objects) (p.135 / pdf 137). Wendt draws the programmatic statement: product semantics should be concerned "not with the forms, surfaces and visual or tactile boundaries of artifacts … but with the understanding that penetrates them"; "not with material objects as such, but with how they participate in human affairs"; it should "optimize not performance, as measured by outside criteria, but meaningfulness, motivation and the centeredness of humans in their world by their own criteria" (p.135 / pdf 137). The **water bottle** runs as the worked example through 135–138: product semantics ignores physical qualities and asks what portable bottles mean for environmental concern, how the bottle mediates the human–bodily-need relationship, and how brand choice communicates the purchaser's qualities; "understanding things involves relating them to their context of use" within "circular enabling patterns" of action, perception, and intent (p.135 / pdf 137). The bottle permutations (imported water = status; refillable metal = environmental/economic/health concern; refilled cheap plastic = concern without willingness to spend) each "create a different system of meaning," for self and observer (pp.135–136 / pdf 137–138).

### 4.5 Sense vs. meaning
The key distinction (Krippendorff): "something makes **sense** when we understand the role it plays in a particular context"; the **meaning** of an object is "the sum total of all the contexts for which someone is capable of imagining some sense for it" (p.137 / pdf 139). Sense = one use case; meaning = a larger sensemaking system across multiple contexts. The bottle "makes sense" as a vessel affording portability and drinkability; its "meaning" speaks to larger communication systems and varies by context (a vessel; an object to occupy nervous hands when speaking publicly) (p.137 / pdf 139). "Meaning is also dependent on abduction" — an orientation toward potential use cases and future contexts (p.137 / pdf 139). **DESIGN PRINCIPLE (sense/meaning):** design the *interaction* for immediate sense, but fit individual sense into systemic meaning; experience design's power is "fitting individual sense to systemic meaning" (p.137 / pdf 139). Service, strategic, and systemic design "specifically call out the need for considering broad meaning systems"; the failure of experience design is practitioners' inability "to differentiate between the interaction and the experience of an interaction" (p.137 / pdf 139).

### 4.6 Use precedes sense; meaning relies on communication and language
Krippendorff: "the truth of a text is always secondary to its reading, just as the use of an artifact is always secondary to its meanings" (p.137 / pdf 139). Wendt *qualifies/contests* this: interaction precedes interpretation, so use should precede meaning; he reads Krippendorff as really claiming that "the use of an object is necessary for sensemaking, not necessarily for meaning," since meaning can be distinct from individual sense (we know the meaning of a military tank without operating one, but "without that practical perspective, the tank makes little sense") (pp.137–138 / pdf 139–140). Meaning then "has a certain reliance on communication": without language to communicate sense, design would be confined (as in craft societies, small networks with little distinction between maker, designer, user) (p.138 / pdf 140). But language is not mere information-conveying: drawing **Wittgenstein** via Krippendorff, "Language does not represent, it does something," judged by "appropriateness … as judged by the participants in the language game" (p.138 / pdf 140). Language is "descriptive but also active, and … creative" — "I do" in a wedding "literally does something" (p.138 / pdf 140). **Communicating is a design activity**; linguistic communication is "an attempt to convey a particular message or create a particular outcome," like design as an attempt to communicate preferred uses (p.138 / pdf 140). Designs "are not simply descriptive — they are creating new realities" (p.139 / pdf 141). This necessitates a **mediation between communication and usability**: artwork is "pure declaration without usability," but designed objects "must be usable" (p.139 / pdf 141). Drawing (apparently) Bonsiepe/Krippendorff, design is "related to declarative statements" — it "produces a reality," as a chairman declares a meeting open; "form expresses a function" is "the declaration that a certain object is there for a purpose" (p.139 / pdf 141). The mix of declarative/creative and usable is "a crucial goal for designers." The chapter pivots to the interface as "the point at which user and object come together — the point that enables use" (p.139–140 / pdf 141–142).

## 5. Section: Interface (pp. 140–148 / pdf 142–150)

### 5.1 The interface as point of sensemaking and structural coupling
Accepting Krippendorff's sense/meaning distinction, "the interface is the point of sensemaking between user and object and context" (p.140 / pdf 142). Sensemaking is "essentially an individual act between user and object"; sense comes "from the active use of the object," which the interface facilitates. Etymology: "inter" = between; "face" = that which enables communication and recognition between humans; a preliminary definition is "that which enables communication, interaction, and recognition between two separate entities" (p.140 / pdf 142). **Bonsiepe**: "without interface there are no tools" — the interface is the point of structural coupling through which all tool-use is enabled, allowing readiness-to-hand to let object and interface "fade into the background" (p.140 / pdf 142). Conversely, the interface is "an 'agitation' or generative friction between different formats" — a point of contention fitting two things "that do not 'naturally' fit," always causing some tension (p.140–141 / pdf 142–143).

### 5.2 The interface as a moving concentration of affordances
The interface is "a concentration of affordances," where primary and secondary affordances communicate action (p.141 / pdf 143). For the water bottle, the **primary interface** is the spout (enabling the primary function); cap, threading, and grip ridges are **secondary** (supporting "drinking"/"quenching thirst" but not necessary to it). The interface shifts with goal: after drinking, the primary interface shifts from spout to cap as the goal shifts to closing — "what we determine as the interface is always contextual to the end goal, which … is always moving," like the movement between ready-to-hand and present-at-hand (p.141 / pdf 143).

### 5.3 Contesting Bonsiepe's "central domain"; the fetishization of the interface
Wendt *contests* Bonsiepe's claim that the interface is "the central domain on which the designer focuses attention" (the interface "makes ready-to-hand [*Zuhandenheit*] as opposed to present-at-hand [*Vorhandenheit*]") (p.141 / pdf 143). He finds it "quite limiting": it focuses on the physical object and "forgets about the role of user behaviors" and the variability that goes with shifting affordances/goals; it ignores everything after the interface is designed (how it is used, whether it works) (p.141–142 / pdf 143–144). This points to a "fetishization of the interface" in market-driven experience design: designers present the interface as the object of work for clients, who become accustomed to tangible deliverables, "continuing the cycle … by focusing on what deliverables will be offered instead of approach, principles, methods" (p.142 / pdf 144). The error traces back to assuming an observable inner/outer split: presenting interface design as "the most important part" sustains "the illusion that we are becoming better connected to the 'external' world" and becomes "the tangible thing for which clients exchange their money" (p.142 / pdf 144). **Herbert Simon**'s positivist artifact-as-interface ("a meeting point … between an 'inner' environment … and an 'outer' environment") is named and criticized: "if the inner environment is appropriate to the outer environment … the artifact will serve its intended purpose" is precisely the positivist statement the book's post-phenomenological framework rejects (p.142–143 / pdf 144–145).

### 5.4 Hookway: the interface as active mediation
**Branden Hookway** supplies "one of the most comprehensive definitions": the interface is "a form of relation that obtains between two or more distinct entities … such that it only comes into being as these distinct entities enter into an active relation"; it "actively maintains, polices, and draws on the separation" while "selectively allow[ing] transmission or communication," producing "a united condition or system"; it couples "holding apart and drawing together … confining and opening up … disciplining and enabling … excluding and including" (p.143 / pdf 145). Wendt reads this as the prior definitions converging: the interface is constant movement — drawing together and pulling apart user and system — and yields an emergent "third entity: user, system, and user-system" (p.143 / pdf 145). "The interface, then, is not a passive connection but rather an active mediation point that brings together different modes" (p.143 / pdf 145).

### 5.5 The interface as "prototypical artifact of the postindustrial era"
With industrialization and the digital age, the interface became (Krippendorff) "the prototypical artifact of the postindustrial era" (p.143 / pdf 145); with personal computers it became the focal object for bridging users and complex machines, bringing the user-system entity to the fore (p.144 / pdf 146). The era of personal computing has "gone to great lengths to minimize the user's consciousness of that tension." Krippendorff: interfaces "weave human sensory-motor coordination and an artifact's reactivity into a dynamic whole … The human body is as much part of an interface as the artifact" (p.144 / pdf 146). Gestural and "natural" user interfaces rely on direct manipulation rather than representation/metaphor, "ostensibly creating a more natural way of interacting"; the overarching goal "came to instill a sense of familiarity and ease" (p.144 / pdf 146).

### 5.6 Critique of "intuition" and "invisibility"
Designers reach for "intuition" and "invisibility" as catch-alls for ease of use, smooth interaction, noninvasiveness; Wendt (citing his own prior work) argues these adjectives are "inaccurate and potentially harmful" (p.144 / pdf 146). They promise interfaces "so easy that the user will forget they are there" — attractive and "in a certain sense appropriate to a phenomenological conception of the interface" (echoing readiness-to-hand), but they cause designers to "take their implications for granted and over-simplify their own work" (p.144–145 / pdf 146–147). Striving for an "invisible" interface "takes the 'unsemiotic' nature of affordances very seriously, perhaps too seriously": almost all interactions "call upon cultural, linguistic, or learned information," so design principles that demand its eradication "are almost impossible to achieve" (p.145 / pdf 147). He notes Heidegger "did not necessarily advocate" readiness-to-hand as *better* than presence-at-hand — merely as more common — so arguments ranking smooth above conscious interaction misread him (p.145 / pdf 147).

### 5.7 Transparency vs. invisibility (the central design proposal)
From a postphenomenological perspective, "the interface need not always become transparent to be useful" (p.145 / pdf 147): a guide-dog leash may improve maneuverability without drawing attention (embodiment relation), but a ski instructor's uniform could be changed to *highlight* his body (alterity relation) and aid learning. Wendt distinguishes **transparent** from **invisible** (p.146 / pdf 148): an invisible object implies "a complete lack of awareness" and inability to enter consciousness without a mediating factor (microscopic life forms — known but not consciously interactable without aid); a **transparent** object "is something we perceive *through*," like a window, which can be brought into awareness "without the aid of other influences." "The object is free to move in and out of conscious awareness." **DESIGN PRINCIPLE (transparency):** "transparency is a much more attainable and preferable design goal than invisibility because it allows for this type of movement" (p.146 / pdf 148).

He draws **Michael May** (animals rely on direct perception of affordances; humans should rely on designed affordances — handle to remote control) but *qualifies* it: in any actual social/cultural context, "artifacts will acquire additional meaning from the network of relations and actions they are involved in"; affordances point to intuition, but no human interaction "can be free of cultural/artificial influence," and "meaning systems pervade every aspect of existence" (p.146 / pdf 148). The intuition argument falsely implies "transcendent meaning one can embed into a design," which collides with Krippendorff's claim that use is necessary for sensemaking (p.146 / pdf 148).

### 5.8 Coping, client communication, transparency of use vs. of context
Much product-development anxiety in market-driven contexts "stems from client communication"; invisibility/intuition are "convenient, mundane, and easily understandable descriptors" to reassure clients (p.146 / pdf 148). A more accurate (and harder) framing is **coping** (ch. 2): everyday Dasein is "one of coping — finding ways to adapt"; contemporary methods (rapid prototyping, continuous testing) begin to account for this variability (p.146–147 / pdf 148–149). Reframing client communication, phenomenology shifts language "from invisibility to transparency": "transparency enables people to sustain their relationship with the product, even when something goes wrong" (p.147 / pdf 149). Wendt distinguishes **transparency of use** (a perceptual "neutrality"; technologies as "a perspicuous interface between humans and world") from **transparency of context** (a cognitive dimension — "our awareness of the mediating role technologies play") (p.147 / pdf 149). With transparency we "can more closely examine the mediating role that technology … plays." "The user senses its presence without feeling unnecessarily burdened by it. Transparency is neither active nor passive, neither exhibitionistic nor voyeuristic" (p.147 / pdf 149).

### 5.9 Ihde's paradox of invisibility; the Borg and Heidegger's fear
**Don Ihde** explains the "double desire" in embodiment relations: on one side a wish for "total transparency / total embodiment, for the technology to truly 'become me'" (which would equal having no technology); on the other a desire for the power and transformation the technology makes available (capacities "always *different* from my naked capabilities") (pp.147–148 / pdf 149–150). "The desire is, at best, contradictory. I want the transformation … but I want it in such a way that I am basically unaware of its presence … that it becomes me" (p.148 / pdf 150). Wendt reads the call for invisible interfaces as harboring "its logical conclusion of complete bodily and cognitive incorporation" — visions of the Borg (p.148 / pdf 150). The desire for invisibility assumes interaction is "difficult" and that decreasing its workload is preferable; this validates "Heidegger's fears of humans assuming a passive position over and against technology": the conclusion of decreasing interaction-labor is "a docile state in which technology … could actually embed itself into Dasein" (p.148 / pdf 150). The earlier **kettle accident** recurs: invisible handle/spout interfaces caused harm; multistability made the spout "no longer a spout, but a tool to scratch." The design question becomes "how to design for the most beneficial outcomes," singular or many (p.148 / pdf 150).

## 6. Section: Embodied Design Practice (pp. 148–152 / pdf 150–154)

### 6.1 The constructive program
Returning to ch. 4's dualisms-in-practice, Wendt sketches "embodied, non-dualist design methods influenced by phenomenology," as opposed to a "rationalist, Cartesian methodology" (p.148 / pdf 150). **Design thinking**'s human-centered process is "particularly well-suited to embodied design practice," though the community has failed to pursue "deep understanding of the human experience" (p.149 / pdf 151).

### 6.2 The semantic turn (Krippendorff)
Krippendorff's **"semantic turn"** is a move "from the image of humans as having to adapt to technological progress … to the image of humans as able to influence the direction of technological development," and of designers as "finding ways to support diverse practices of living, community, and the sense needed for individuals to feel at home" — "the acknowledgement that meaning matters" (p.149 / pdf 151). It moves "from pure usability … to an extended consideration for imagining new ways of being in the world," creating new "homes" for users; design becomes "the driving activity of the postindustrial society," shifting "from being product- or production-centered to a human-centered effort" (pp.149–150 / pdf 151–152).

### 6.3 The systemic turn; situated design
Emerging practices (service design; speculative and critical design) extend the semantic turn into a **"systemic turn"** — "the complex blend of interfaces and cultural-semiotic systems" (p.150 / pdf 152). Systemic methods "zoom in and out," moving from macro service ecologies to micro product interfaces, accounting for both macro advantage and the "situated-ness of humans." Wendt distinguishes product design (individual products), service design (how products interact with humans to form a system), and experience design (the *experience* of that system) (p.150 / pdf 152). Drawing **Brereton**, a **situated approach** "where understanding or construction of the situation is core" emphasizes "creative research into the situated nature of the problem space," with theories "deriving from design investigation as much as contributing to it" (p.150–151 / pdf 152–153). It rejects the "conservative" pure problem-solving model: "solutioning" "will almost always result in a new perspective on the problem space" (p.151 / pdf 153). **Suchman/Dourish** lineage: "context isn't just 'there,' but is actively produced, maintained and enacted in the course of the activity at hand" (p.151 / pdf 153).

### 6.4 Dourish: embodied interaction; ethnography
**Paul Dourish**'s **embodied interaction** extends a theory of context based on embodied cognition into design — a "larger theory of interaction" now applicable to design practice (p.151 / pdf 153). **Ethnography** is "a key method for embodied interaction and situated design," for its "in-context probing and adaptability to change." Ethnographers "go beyond specific instances of design," resting on "the fundamental repudiation of a traditional separation between designer and user, between technology and practice" — the categories of design, user, and designer "are themselves in question" (pp.151–152 / pdf 153–154). Like actor-network theory, anthropological ethnography "tends to not separate designers from users but … views them within the same system"; "design ethnography" applies ethnography "with a greater concern for specific design problems" (p.152 / pdf 154).

### 6.5 Context and the emergence of meaning
The "main concern for embodied interaction and design ethnography is … the role of **context**" — "a framework for understanding the emergent properties of interaction," the idea that "designer intention and purposeful design cannot dictate the emergence of new variables in an interactive system" (p.152 / pdf 154). Dourish: the essential feature of embodied interaction is "allowing users to negotiate and evolve systems of practice and meaning in the course of their interaction with information systems" (p.152 / pdf 154). Embodied interaction "gives designers the building blocks to embrace the uncertainty of multistable technologies" and "calls for a deep care, in the Heideggerian sense, for embodiment as what constitutes interaction": embodied interaction takes "embodiment to be central to, even constitutive of, the whole phenomenon" (p.152 / pdf 154). The chapter closes by insisting designers must first "shed the Cartesian and positivist baggage" before articulating "a theory of design thinking informed by phenomenological philosophy" (p.152 / pdf 154).

## 7. Interlocutors and what Wendt draws from each

- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty** (*Phenomenology of Perception*) — the body as "ambiguous third party"; "a theory of the body is already a theory of perception"; skills as "solicitations of situations" not representations; the body as "a system of possible actions, a virtual body." Wendt draws the anti-representational, action-oriented account of perception and embodiment that grounds the whole chapter (pp.125–126 / pdf 127–128).
- **Andy Clark** — embodied cognition / extended mind: intelligence rooted in "the tuning of basic responses to a real world"; the "earthy" site of cognition. Wendt draws the cognitive-science warrant for embodiment and the "earthy realm" framing of experience (p.124, p.128 / pdf 126, 130).
- **Hubert Dreyfus** — Heideggerian critique of symbolic AI; humans bound within context. Wendt draws the demonstration that disembodied, rule-based cognition fails, with AI as exemplary case (p.125 / pdf 127).
- **James J. Gibson** — affordances; objects perceived "according to their possible actions"; affordance as "both physical and psychical, yet neither." Wendt draws the central dualism-dissolving concept and the by-what-they-do reframe (pp.129–130 / pdf 131–132).
- **Don Norman** (*The Design of Everyday Things*) — affordances as communicated possibilities for action (graspability, pushability, sittability). Wendt draws the working design-practice definition, then deepens it past "communication" (p.129 / pdf 131).
- **Cameron Tonkinwise** — affordance as "promised action opportunity," "utterly unsemiotic," "between things and my body without the involvement of my mind." Wendt draws "forced abductive thinking" and the unsemiotic/between-ness of affordances (p.132 / pdf 134).
- **Klaus Krippendorff** (*The Semantic Turn*) — product semantics; sense vs. meaning; language games (via Wittgenstein); the semantic turn; interface as "prototypical artifact of the postindustrial era." Wendt's principal design-theory interlocutor for meaning-formation (pp.133–138, 143–149 / pdf 135–140, 145–151).
- **Gui Bonsiepe** — all design "ends in the body"; structural coupling; "without interface there are no tools"; interface as "central domain." Wendt draws structural coupling and the interface's centrality, but contests the "central domain" overstatement (pp.127, 140–141 / pdf 129, 142–143).
- **Don Ihde** — amplification/reduction (telescope); embodiment vs. alterity relations; the "double desire" / paradox of invisibility. Wendt draws postphenomenological mediation and the contradiction in the invisibility ideal (pp.127, 145, 147–148 / pdf 129, 147, 149–150).
- **Bruno Latour** — scripts (intended actions embedded in objects); actor-network actants. Wendt draws "scripts" and the human/non-human symmetry behind embodied/ethnographic method (pp.128, 150–152 / pdf 130, 152–154).
- **Georg Simmel** — the vase handle; design as "the act of communicating practical use"; the handle's "reaching out" / human teleology. Wendt draws the pre-Gibson root of affordance-as-communicated-use (p.131 / pdf 133).
- **Branden Hookway** (*Interface*) — the interface as active relation coupling holding-apart and drawing-together. Wendt draws his comprehensive definition of the interface as active mediation (p.143 / pdf 145).
- **Herbert Simon** (*The Sciences of the Artificial*) — artifact as interface between inner and outer environments. Named as the positivist foil Wendt's framework rejects (pp.142–143 / pdf 144–145).
- **Paul Dourish** (*Where the Action Is*) — embodied interaction; context as enacted; meaning negotiated in interaction. Wendt draws the constructive design-method core of embodied interaction (pp.151–152 / pdf 153–154).
- **Vilém Flusser** — object as "what gets in the way" (*objectum*/*problema*). Wendt entertains then qualifies the etymology (p.129 / pdf 131).
- **Maarten Franssen** — objects have a *for-ness* that "calls forth certain actions." Wendt draws the action-soliciting character of objects, bounded by bodily capacity (p.128 / pdf 130).
- **Michael May** — humans should rely on designed affordances as animals rely on natural ones. Wendt draws then qualifies the intuition argument (p.146 / pdf 148).
- **Margot Brereton** — situated design vs. conservative problem-solving. Wendt draws the situated-design stance (pp.150–151 / pdf 152–153).
- **Heidegger** (background throughout) — readiness-to-hand / presence-at-hand (*Zuhandenheit*/*Vorhandenheit*); care; the fear of human passivity before technology; coping. The chapter's ontological frame (pp.123, 130, 145, 148, 152 / pdf 125, 132, 147, 150, 154).
- **Ludwig Wittgenstein** (via Krippendorff) — language games; "language does not represent, it does something." Wendt draws the performative/creative character of language (p.138 / pdf 140).

## 8. Explicit design principles (consolidated)

1. **Acknowledge the disembodiment of your deliverables.** Flow diagrams, personas, scenarios, and use cases are necessary but inherently disembodied; failing to recognize this leads designers "down an unnecessarily dualist path" (p.124 / pdf 126).
2. **Organize by what things *do*, not what they *are*** (information architecture by user action: "I need to do my taxes," not "Taxes > Forms") (p.126 / pdf 128).
3. **Design the verb (the action an object affords), moving from a metaphysics to a phenomenology of design** (p.133 / pdf 135).
4. **Design affordances that communicate potential action**, but treat them as "pure potentiality" that both communicate *and* enable, not merely as visual signals (pp.129–131 / pdf 131–133).
5. **Consider the affordance alongside bodily capacity**: perception of an affordance is near-universal; the ability to act on it is body-dependent (pp.131–132 / pdf 133–134).
6. **Fit individual *sense* into systemic *meaning***: design the interaction for immediate sense and locate it within larger meaning systems / contexts of use (p.137 / pdf 139).
7. **Treat communication/declaration and usability as a required mediation**: designed objects must be both declarative (producing a reality, "this is for a purpose") and usable (p.139 / pdf 141).
8. **Treat the interface as an active, moving mediation point**, not the "central domain" or a passive connector; design for behaviors and contexts beyond the current interface (pp.141–143 / pdf 143–145).
9. **Pursue *transparency*, not *invisibility* or *intuition***: a transparent interface lets the user move it in and out of awareness and "sustain their relationship with the product, even when something goes wrong" (pp.146–147 / pdf 148–149).
10. **Use rapid prototyping and continuous testing to design for coping, multistability, and creative misuse** rather than dictating a single outcome (pp.146–148 / pdf 148–150).
11. **Adopt situated / embodied-interaction / ethnographic methods** that put context first and treat designer/user/technology/practice as a single system; let users "negotiate and evolve systems of practice and meaning" (pp.150–152 / pdf 152–154).
