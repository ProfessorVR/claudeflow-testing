## 0. Argument arc (overview)

The chapter moves from a general re-orientation of philosophy (Heidegger's turn away from theory toward everyday practice) to a series of increasingly concrete design payoffs. Its spine: (1) Dasein names situated, embodied, future-directed being-in-the-world; (2) Cartesian dualism is rejected, dissolving the self/world and mind/body splits and grounding embodied cognition; (3) knowledge comes through *praxis* — engaged action, not detached theory; (4) the tool analysis (ready-to-hand vs. present-at-hand) describes the two poles of human–object interaction, on a continuum punctuated by *breakdown*; (5) *coping* (after Dreyfus) is the default mode of dealing with the world, so usability must be reconceived as support for coping and meaning, not mere error-elimination; (6) Heidegger's later philosophy of technology (*technē*, *poiēsis*, *physis*, standing reserve, enframing) warns that instrumental, "invisible" technology risks reducing world to a passive resource; (7) the chapter closes on *thing-ness* — objects become *things* only when users gather meaning into them through use, a "becoming" that is the proper joint concern of designers and philosophers. Throughout, Samuel Beckett's *Malone Dies* and Paul Dourish's *Where the Action Is* serve as recurring illustrations.

## 1. Dasein and the situated-ness of Being (pp. 15–18 / pdf 17–20)

Wendt opens by situating Heidegger against his teacher Husserl: Heidegger faults prior philosophy (Plato's forms, Descartes' dualism, Husserl's subject–object split) for being too theoretical and insufficiently concerned with the everyday interactions of bodily entities (p.15 / pdf 17). Heidegger "mundane-ifies" philosophy paradoxically — by asking the most general possible question, the question of the meaning of Being, described as "the most universal and the emptiest of questions" (p.15 / pdf 17).

**Dasein** (Da = here/there + Sein = being → "here/there-being," "being-there," "being-in-the-world") is the central, most complex notion. It is *situated* Being — not an abstract form that explains our embodied state but *the embodied state itself* (p.16 / pdf 18). Wendt notes the convention (which he keeps) of preserving the German word to retain the sense of situated-ness. Dasein is distinctive in that "in its being, that being is itself an issue" (paraphrasing Heidegger): self-consciousness and the capacity to reflect, analyze, and feel anxiety over one's situation mark Dasein off from other modes of being — a plant has a mode of being but lacks concern for itself as a being; only humans (and perhaps some higher primates) possess Dasein (p.16 / pdf 18).

Crucially, the "in" of being-in-the-world is **not spatial containment** but **involvement**. Wendt quotes (via secondary literature) that human and world "are not two distinct entities but only one which results from Dasein's involvement in the world," so the "in" is unrelated to Aristotelian containment and better understood as engaged, absorbed involvement in an undifferentiated world (p.16 / pdf 18). This is the chapter's first explicit design payoff (§1 below).

> **DESIGN-PRINCIPLE (context-as-involvement):** When considering user context, designers must resist defaulting to *location* as the primary contextual datum. Being-in means being-*involved* in a situation; the airport example shows that the same physical place (e.g., a security line) carries different concerns at different times, and the post-security mindset differs though the location is similar (pp.17 / pdf 19).

Wendt invokes Paul Dourish: Heidegger's reorienting question is not "How can we know about the world?" but "How does the world reveal itself to us through our encounters with it?" The world reveals itself only when Dasein manipulates it; through interaction with things we come to know ourselves and our surroundings (p.17 / pdf 19). Hence the designer's goal: understand how users interpret their *own involvement* with the world as a meaningful activity — attending not only to actions taken but to *meaning derived*. Wendt criticizes design research for over-focusing on actions (more "real," more commercially viable) at the expense of meaning; without meaning, action is "simply pantomime" (p.17 / pdf 19).

*RO-hook (clearly marked, ≤1): Wendt's insistence that interaction without derived meaning is "pantomime" gestures at a desire/meaning telos behind action — a possible touch-point for an actualization-of-desire reading.*

## 2. Being ahead of itself; thrownness and coping (pp. 18–21 / pdf 20–23)

Drawing on *Being and Time*, Wendt presents Dasein as **always ahead of itself**: Heidegger questions the "now," asserting that current focus is directed toward the future. We act toward future goals — eating to sustain ourselves and enjoy taste, working for money and satisfaction, seeking friendship for connection (p.18 / pdf 20). The design implication is "self-evident": everything designed should keep the end user's *future goals* in mind; users interact with a product not for interaction's sake but to accomplish something less tangible. He quotes Stiegler: Dasein is in the mode of "having-to-be," never yet totally is, always anticipates itself as "not yet," existence extending [Erstreckung] between "already" and "not yet" (p.18 / pdf 20). This temporal structure maps onto the design process: research methods understand past/present; ideation anticipates the future via Dasein's goal-orientation; designers use induction/deduction for the present and *abduction* to create alternate futures (p.18 / pdf 20).

Dasein is also **thrown** — dropped into a non-ideal world it must *cope* with. Coping is both dealing with broken things and the ordinary incorporation of things into life — and this incorporation "necessitates design" (p.18 / pdf 20). Wendt quotes a striking gloss tying design to thrownness via the Dutch *ontwerpen* (read as "un-throw"): design = making decisions about form to liberate us from life's arbitrariness, making order out of chaos; "Thrownness-unthrowing: there you have the human condition" (p.18–19 / pdf 20–21). As we move through life we change our environment — small (turning up the heat) to large (a new job) — asserting control and redesigning the current state to better meet our needs; coping is not sacrifice but an intentional or unintentional movement toward a more preferable state (p.19 / pdf 21).

This altering from non-preferable to preferable is "what links design to Dasein" (p.19 / pdf 21). Wendt quotes the image of Dasein as so naked (no clothes, habitat, biology) that it scarcely stands a chance of survival: a "situated blank slate," both omnipotent (existence predicated on situatedness/embodiment) and powerless (wholly dependent on bodies and objects) (p.19 / pdf 21).

**Beckett's *Malone Dies* (preliminary illustration).** The bed-ridden Malone, who cherishes a pencil and a hooked stick as his only meaningful possessions, is "Dasein as being-involved in objects" (pp.19–21 / pdf 21–23). The stick is friend *and* means of attaining other objects; stripped of creative capacities, Dasein "plays itself out" in Malone through trivial objects. Wendt generalizes: we are "all like Malone" — the forgotten mobile phone produces a felt sense of loss not merely because it is a fetishized consumer object but because it provides an almost infinite means of interaction for attaining goals; without that "cluster of affordances," we design our situations by substandard means. "Our objects define us. They shape our understanding of the world." (p.21 / pdf 23)

## 3. Connecting the poles of self and world: rejecting Cartesian dualism (pp. 21–24 / pdf 23–26)

Phenomenology's stress on things is bound up with its rejection of Cartesian dualism. Descartes' "cogito ergo sum" places the activity of the mind over the body — the mind an executive function, the body "a bag of meat that follows orders" (p.22 / pdf 24). Wendt quotes Descartes' conclusion that the "I" (the mind) is "wholly distinct from the body," a self whose essence is only thinking, needing no place and dependent on no material thing (p.21–22 / pdf 23–24).

Heidegger's "attack on Cartesian thinking" is "one of his most important contributions": knowledge and understanding come from interactions with things and each other, not from a divorced mind's detached formulations (p.22 / pdf 24). Wendt quotes Dreyfus's gloss: being-in-the-world is more basic than thinking and problem-solving and is "not representational at all"; in coping at our best we are drawn in by solicitations and respond directly, so that the distinction between us and our equipment — inner and outer — vanishes (p.22 / pdf 24). And, in another gloss: "Self and world belong together in a single entity, Dasein"; self and world are, Wendt continues, "the basic determination of Dasein itself in the unity of the structure of being-in-the-world" (p.22 / pdf 24). This lays the theoretical groundwork for **embodied cognition** and its offshoots — thinking environment-as-extension-of-mind, things-as-extensions-of-bodies (p.22 / pdf 24).

**To-do-list apps** illustrate offloading memory outside the head: memory is no longer purely cognitive; the apps acknowledge default memory is inadequate and use an "external" apparatus — which "begs the question" of whether the apparatus is truly external, or whether internal/external categories cease to apply when reliance on the app exceeds our "wetware" memory (p.22–23 / pdf 24–25). Technological devices have always been how Dasein interacts with the environment; highly personal devices are now incorporated ever more intimately into everyday existence (p.23 / pdf 25).

Wendt then diagnoses **Cartesian dualism in design**: radical distinctions between problems/solutions, strategy/tactics, designers/users, theory/practice, thinking/doing. Some are useful; others cause confusion. The designer/user split is a product of the shift from craft-based societies (where it did not exist) to post-Industrial-Revolution societies where products had to *communicate meaning* to large groups — division of labor and awareness of end goals pushed the groups apart. The theory/practice and thinking/doing splits, by contrast, are a *direct result of Cartesian thinking*: the assumption that a theoretical-thinking phase must precede practical-doing, which is easy to sell to clients but "far from how design processes actually work" — in reality the whole team is engaged in theoretical, practical, thinking, doing, problem-finding, and solution-articulating activities throughout, without clean "phases" (p.23 / pdf 25).

## 4. Praxis and situated action (pp. 24–26 / pdf 26–28)

Heidegger goes beyond merely linking self and world: he calls for a complete rejection of Cartesian thinking, asserting that everyday *engaged action* brings knowledge — we formulate what we know by interacting with objects (p.24 / pdf 26). This does *not* simply mean practice precedes theory (theory still has an important place); rather, *pure theory has less influence on how we interpret the world than practical action*. Thinking through every step of going to a concert is radically different from actually going — the former is divorced from use-context, the latter provides practical knowledge (p.24 / pdf 26).

The same holds for knowledge of objects: Wendt quotes Heidegger that the functionality going with a chair, blackboard, or window "is exactly that which makes a thing what it is" — essential qualities are not physical properties but the actions an object allows; "we know something by what we do with it"; form essentially means nothing (one could eat cereal with a baseball bat, making it a poor cereal-eating device) (p.24 / pdf 26).

Two key distinctions:

- **Acting *on* vs. acting *through*.** Acting *upon* takes the object itself as the focal point; acting *through* takes the user's end goal as the focal point (p.24 / pdf 26). Applicable from spoons and eyeglasses to military drones and transit systems.
- **Knowing-that vs. knowing-how** (via Hubert Dreyfus). To understand a hammer is not to know *that* it has such-and-such properties but, "most primordially," *knowing how* to hammer (p.24–25 / pdf 26–27). Example: a child learns to swim by being taken swimming, not given a book on fluid dynamics (p.25 / pdf 27).

**Brainstorming vs. rapid prototyping.** A corporate brainstorm yields theoretical assumptions based on current knowledge and assumes everyone shares the same base knowledge of the problem space; rapid prototyping treats outputs as *assumptions to be tested with real users in the real world*, extending theory into the spaces that matter and "creating a layer of praxical knowledge" (p.25 / pdf 27).

**Praxis** is the heart of the section. Heidegger prioritizes practice over theory but "leaves the door open" for praxis = *theoretically-informed practice*: embodied in everyday experience yet informed by theoretical reflection; "praxis takes the rigor of theory and embeds it into practical action" (p.25 / pdf 27). In the **Aristotelian sense**, praxis emphasizes the *process itself* over the final product — action over making, allowing the emergence of new ideas through process rather than executing a pre-set plan (p.25–26 / pdf 27–28). The paradigm example is **sketching**: we sketch not to create client-ready documents but to help us think — "sketching is literally externalized thought" that can be discarded, revised, or used for discussion (p.26 / pdf 28).

Wendt quotes that tools are not simply objects with qualities; what a tool *is* depends on a *use context*, and the knowledge arising in praxis is "not cognitive, Cartesian-type knowledge" but a *use knowledge* in which the tool becomes a means of accomplishment (p.26 / pdf 28). A baseball bat on the ground is meaningless; picked up and used it becomes a tool for sport, an instrument of violence, or a cereal-eating device, depending on context. Praxis lets designers explore use contexts by decreasing the "compulsive need for immediate finality"; prototypes and unfinished things let designers play and account for the variability of use contexts — a variability later flagged as a focus of *post-phenomenological* thinkers (p.26 / pdf 28).

> **DESIGN-PRINCIPLE (praxis / prototyping over premature finality):** Resist the compulsion toward immediate finality; use prototypes and unfinished artifacts to test theory-laden assumptions in real use contexts, generating praxical (use-) knowledge rather than empty pantomime.

*RO-hook: praxis-as-process and "action over making" sit close to an Aristotelian praxis/poiēsis distinction the rhetorical ontology may want to engage; Wendt himself flags the "Aristotelian sense" (p.25–26 / pdf 27–28).*

## 5. Interaction patterns: the tool analysis — present-at-hand & ready-to-hand (pp. 26–30 / pdf 28–32)

Wendt presents Heidegger's "famous tool analysis," centered on two poles of interaction with objects ("equipment"): **Vorhandenheit / present-at-hand** and **Zuhandenheit / ready-to-hand** (p.26 / pdf 28).

- **Present-at-hand (presence-at-hand):** a relationship defined by theoretical knowledge and scientific observation; the object is observed as wholly separate, factual and analyzable from afar — decontextualization and detached speculation. (The bat lying unused on the ground: the observer examines functional components but not contexts of use.) (p.26–27 / pdf 28–29)
- **Ready-to-hand (readiness-to-hand):** a relationship based on active engagement; the object is a tool for a goal, an *extension of the user's body*; the user acts *through* the object, which "fades into the background," becoming part of the background relations shaping behavior outside direct consciousness. Readiness-to-hand is the essence of Dasein existing ahead of itself (p.27 / pdf 29).

**They form a continuum, not a binary.** Typing an email when fluent, focus rests on the message (ready-to-hand, the keyboard withdrawn); a misspelling breaks the relation, forcing conscious re-typing, and the keyboard becomes present-at-hand "as an object of concern and deliberation" (p.27 / pdf 29).

Wendt quotes Dourish at length (paraphrased): the computer mouse is normally ready-to-hand (an extension of the hand); reaching the edge of the mouse pad interrupts it, making the mouse present-at-hand — an object of attention. Dourish's point is "more radical": the mouse exists for us as an *entity* only because it can become present-at-hand, and becomes *equipment* only through being ready-to-hand; in being ready-to-hand it "withdraws," and "the ready-to-hand is not grasped theoretically at all." Our everyday dealings dwell not with the tools themselves but with *the work* — as we act through ready-to-hand technology, the technology disappears; our mode of being is "absorbed coping," equipment fading into the background (pp.27–28 / pdf 29–30).

Wendt extracts three observations from the passage (p.28–29 / pdf 30–31):
1. **The ready-to-hand relationship is characterized by withdrawal** — the object becomes a transparent means to accomplishment; "equipmentality" = the *transparency* of equipment with regard to user intention.
2. **Interruptions cause a shift in interaction style** — breaking smooth interaction shifts the relationship to present-at-hand; such interruptions are *inevitable*; the contextual nature of object use "will always result in breakage," and the important question is *how users cope with these breaks*.
3. **The shift causes a change in mindfulness, which defines presence-at-hand** — from non-conscious use to recognizing the object *as* an object to be acted upon.

The vast majority of dealings are ready-to-hand, so phenomenology is "a very mundane philosophy," concerned with day-to-day experiences that shape the background of existence (the chair you sit on, the shift in consciousness about it from before to after reading the sentence) (p.29 / pdf 31). There is constant movement between present-at-hand and ready-to-hand in everyday life, and "designing with that movement in mind is the job of experience designers" (p.29 / pdf 31).

> **DESIGN-PRINCIPLE (design for the ready-to-hand/present-at-hand continuum):** Experience designers should design for the *constant movement* between absorbed coping (ready-to-hand transparency) and conscious deliberation (present-at-hand), since breakage is unavoidable; the goal is supporting how users cope with the shift, not pretending it can be eliminated.

*RO-hook: "absorbed coping" and equipmental withdrawal are candidate analogues for engaged actualization vs. reflective interruption in the rhetorical-ontology chain (one major concept).*

## 6. Reflecting on the object experience; coping, breakdown, and usability (pp. 30–37 / pdf 32–39)

Returning to *Malone Dies*, Wendt draws the Dasein–object relationship: Dasein constantly reaches out to its environment, incorporates objects into itself, and extends beyond the body while keeping the body as the primary mode of action; objects stand "in-between" self and world yet are an inherent part of the self–world system (p.29–30 / pdf 31–32). Malone's pushing/pulling of objects with his stick parallels how we move apps to background/foreground; for Heidegger "near" and "far" are about the *orientation of Dasein*, not physical space — for us the nearness/far-ness of information is *cognitive*, not proximal (p.30–31 / pdf 32–33). Malone's stick is an extension of his body (like a wrench to a plumber, brush to a painter, steering wheel to a driver) and an object of affection/ambivalence — paralleling our love/hate relationship with mobile devices: we love the connectivity but hate the burden of availability (p.31 / pdf 33).

**Usability and social learning.** Usability studies ease of use (clear labels, expected functionality, task completion); its measures are usually mechanical, but a less pragmatic side concerns whether a product communicates design intention and meets expectations against competitors (p.31–32 / pdf 33–34). User testing can be a *social learning process* (citing Tonkinwise): testing a prototype facilitates an educational goal. Apple's generational releases (iPod → iPhone → iPad) educated the public about portable music, the "pocket computer," and eventually the iPad/cloud — taking smaller steps to introduce revolutionary products without rupturing the system (p.32 / pdf 34).

**Coping (Dreyfus).** Wendt quotes that Dreyfus first articulated **practical coping** — the "mostly smooth and unobtrusive responsiveness to circumstances that enable human beings to get around in the world" — as an alternative to classical cognition (rules applied to internal representations to create plans then executed); coping is practical, skillful, *representation-free* dealing with world and technology (p.32–33 / pdf 34–35). Colloquially coping means handling something broken/non-optimal (grieving a death, wedging a wobbly table), but for Dreyfus coping is far more mundane: we cope simply by interacting with things. The **ready-to-hand = smooth coping**; the **present-at-hand = a rupture of smooth coping** producing conscious awareness; coping can be seen as an everyday design process, actively managing the environment (p.33 / pdf 35).

This implies **things do not have properties in themselves but only in relation to context of use**: Wendt quotes that a failed hammer is dealt with as too heavy, unbalanced, broken — characteristics belonging to the hammer "only as used by me in a specific situation"; being too heavy is "certainly not a property of the hammer" (p.33 / pdf 35). So user testing should observe *coping strategies* and build that insight back into design — "a more genuine way of testing" than the positivist approach of determining and eliminating "unintuitive" aspects (p.33 / pdf 35).

**Breakdown.** Wendt resists the commercial tendency to eliminate (or create the illusion of eliminating) undesirable aspects, born of a fear of instability and complexity. He quotes Bonsiepe: breakdowns are "a necessary accompaniment to design," not from designer incompetence but from the nature of the design process; a breakdown is not a negative situation to avoid but "reveals the tissue of the relations needed to fulfill the tasks." From this follows a clear design target: *anticipate the forms of breakdowns and open up possibilities for action in case they happen* (p.33–34 / pdf 35–36). The next step after a breakdown is not (only) to eradicate its cause but to learn how it affects the entire system of interaction; the anxiety from breakdowns drives designers toward pure eradication, under client pressure or a need for perfection (p.34 / pdf 36).

**Abandonment, conspicuousness, meaning.** The fear of imperfection surfaces in the word *abandonment*: we are told unusable products are abandoned because users won't invest time to learn them (p.34 / pdf 36). But Wendt's examples (Spotify's buried shuffle button; Seamless's poor taxonomy and dead-ends) show users *cope* with bad usability when *perceived value/meaning* is high enough — products are abandoned not when usability is merely sub-par but when they become *meaningless* (p.34–35 / pdf 36–37). When equipment malfunctions it becomes "conspicuous" (Heidegger): smooth coping is marked by deep involvement and action without conscious thought (jazz musicians, carpenters, craft professionals "one with their instrument"); only a major rupture (e.g., a break in rhythm) makes instruments "disembodied" / conspicuous as present-at-hand objects (p.35 / pdf 37).

Wendt quotes Krippendorff: what breaks down in a "breakdown" is "neither the artifact… nor the users' concepts or memories" but "the meaningfulness of the interface of which we are a part" — when expected and actual senses deviate significantly (p.35–36 / pdf 37–38). Therefore usability is *not* merely a functionalist mode of determining user-friendliness; *meaning* forces designers past function into culture, language, and everyday practice. Quoting further: "User-friendliness… is rooted in the confidence of being able to handle disruptions. Design can support that confidence" (p.36 / pdf 38). By facilitating *understanding* (not mere knowledge of function, which inflates expectations), designers open up possibilities of use beyond predetermined use cases, letting users see "an entire world of potential actions" and cope with interruptions in ways a function-based approach forecloses (p.36 / pdf 38). A final Krippendorff quote ties functionalist usability to Taylorism and cost-benefit efficiency — measuring lack of efficiency (error rates, steps, learning time) in a northern-European tradition equating optimization with technological rationality; the industrial era's criteria served industry more than users (p.36 / pdf 38). Wendt's irony: user-centered design arose partly as a reaction *against* Taylorism yet often remains mechanistic; treating users as individuals means embracing "the messiness of being human" — our ability to cope, find meaning, and adapt (p.37 / pdf 39).

> **DESIGN-PRINCIPLE (design for coping, breakdown, and meaning, not just error-elimination):** Treat breakdown as revelatory and unavoidable; anticipate its forms and open possibilities for action; reconceive usability around *meaning* and the *confidence to handle disruption* rather than functionalist error-counting (which inherits a Taylorist logic that serves industry over users).

*RO-hook: "meaningfulness of the interface" as what breaks down, and abandonment-when-meaningless, are candidate links to a meaning/desire telos in the rhetorical ontology.*

## 7. Heidegger on technology: technē, poiēsis, physis, standing reserve, enframing (pp. 37–43 / pdf 39–45)

Wendt turns to the later Heidegger and *The Question Concerning Technology*, flagged as the source of critical remarks revisited under post-phenomenology (p.37–38 / pdf 39–40). He distinguishes the ancient Greek terms (p.37 / pdf 39):
- **physis** — nature/form that arises out of itself, "form without intervention";
- **technē** — to make something appear through art, craft, the work of the hands (requires human intervention; "the intentional act of making something appear"; the practical side of poiēsis);
- **poiēsis** — "bringing-forth"; the poetical mode in which meaning *emerges* out of a pre-existing state; the poet/artist does not impose meaning but finds and extracts meaningful points in the world; art as "a hermeneutics of the world."

Heidegger's later view holds that "all that is merely technological never arrives at the essence of technology" (p.37–38 / pdf 39–40). Wendt admits the line long confused him; the gist is that there is "something more to technology" that individual pieces can never contain — stemming from a critique of *instrumentalism* (technology as mere means to an end). As "the essence of 'tree' is not itself a tree," the essence of technology "is by no means anything technological" (p.38 / pdf 40). Wendt notes this seems to contradict early Heidegger's object-focus and looks "pseudo-Platonic" / transcendental — a shift many scholars call **The Turn** (*die Kehre*) (p.38 / pdf 40, continued p.39 / pdf 41). He cites Rutsky that the "essence of technology" is not a static category but "a dynamic, ongoing process or movement," broadening technology into a general concept of making/producing including artistic production; Wendt's own emphasis: technology's value is its **potential to reveal something not visible before** — bringing background to foreground and back, "reading" the world like art (p.39 / pdf 41).

Key themes of the essay (p.39–42 / pdf 41–44):
- **Bringing-forth / *das Entbergen*** — etymologically "bringing-forth what was safely hidden" (*ent-* = forth/out/negating a prior condition; *bergen* = to rescue/keep safe through concealment); echoes the Harman-identified trope of concealment/unconcealment, veiled/unveiled. Wendt asks why there was a need to hide — "What is the threat?" (p.40 / pdf 42).
- **Challenging-forth / *Herausfordern*** — modern technology is *also* a revealing, but does *not* unfold as a bringing-forth in the sense of poiēsis; it is a *challenging* that "puts to nature the unreasonable demand that it supply energy that can be extracted and stored as such" (p.40 / pdf 42). *Herausfordern* connotes provocation/defiance; modern technology defies poiēsis. Wendt notes Heidegger's "nostalgic romanticism" and lament for the loss of poiēsis. The chalice-vs.-Rhine-dam contrast: the Greek craftsman gathers form, matter, finality and brings out the "truth" of his materials, whereas modern technology "de-worlds" its materials and "summons" nature to extrinsic demands, leaving an "objectless heap of functions" (p.41 / pdf 43).
- A further Wendt distinction: **technē/craft exists *for the craftsperson*; design exists *for others*.** The craftsperson creates for him/herself or a small number; the designer works with modern technology, creates for others, and "strips materials of their worldliness" — this is "the threat from which the world hides" (p.41 / pdf 43).
- **Standing reserve / *Bestand*** ("resource well"/"stock") — "very familiar" to experience designers via the discourse of the "invisibility" of technology and "intuitive" interfaces, the promise of technology "here when needed and gone when not" (Mark Weiser's "calm computing," not yet fully realized) (p.41 / pdf 43). The standing reserve is "a sort of pool of technological energy we can tap into at any time"; Wendt quotes that whatever stands by as standing-reserve "no longer stands over against us as object" (p.41–42 / pdf 43–44). The exemplary case is the smartphone — a bundle of affordances waiting in the pocket; Heidegger's fear is that the standing reserve places the user in a **passive** position relative to technology (p.42 / pdf 44). Context-aware/ubiquitous computing (in-car voice switching on motion; Google Now correlating calendar, location, weather, traffic) instantiates this, as do productivity/to-do apps where the focus becomes *feeling* productive rather than *being* productive, offloading the ethical matter of task completion onto technology (p.42 / pdf 44).
- **Enframing / *Gestell*** — the pervasiveness of the standing reserve leads Heidegger to classify our relation to modern technology as an *enframing*: "the gathering of technologically stored energy as it reveals itself to us." The onus is on experience designers to "responsibly design these systems, and preserve the relationship between user and system" (p.43 / pdf 45).

> **DESIGN-PRINCIPLE (responsible design against passive enframing):** Beware the "invisible"/"intuitive"-interface fantasy and the standing-reserve logic that positions users passively; design technology responsibly to preserve an active user–system relationship rather than reducing world and user to extractable resource.

*RO-hook: the active/passive axis Heidegger fears in the standing reserve (user reduced to passive recipient) is a candidate counter-point for an actualization-of-desire account that foregrounds active engagement.*

## 8. Making sense of technology: meaning, thing-ness, and the designer's becoming (pp. 43–48 / pdf 45–50)

Phenomenology at its core concerns *how we make sense of the world*, and the objects we use are how the world reveals itself; spoons, keyboards, doorknobs, books, cars each have unique ways of revealing the world (p.43 / pdf 45). Design, like art and technology, is a *hermeneutic activity*: "we use design to interpret the world." Whereas most of Heidegger's work fixed on the lofty ideal of poiēsis, contemporary philosophy of technology narrows to everyday things, whose significance is veiled by their very ubiquity; contemporary philosophy of technology and design is thus "taking a sort of default phenomenological stance" — a claim Wendt promises to substantiate across the book, beginning with the relationship between things and meaning (p.43–44 / pdf 45–46).

**Making sense.** Knowledge is integral to interaction with things; Dasein is in a constant state of making sense of its surroundings as "an active process of contextual engagement," always reaching out to use objects to make sense of how it relates to the world (p.44 / pdf 46). Wendt quotes Krippendorff that "making sense always entails a bit of a paradox" between making something new/different and the desire to have it make sense (be recognizable/understandable) — innovation vs. the reproduction of historical continuities (p.44 / pdf 46). This raises whether Dasein *extracts* meaning from the world or the *interaction itself creates* meaning: traditional phenomenology might say meaning exists in the world and engagement interprets it, but the ambiguity of "making sense" urges us to consider interpretation as more involved than mere decoding (p.44 / pdf 46).

Individuals have no existence divorced from the network of relations making up context; likewise objects have no meaning outside their context of use. Quoting Krippendorff on product semantics: what something *is* (the totality of what it means) to someone "corresponds to the sum total of its imaginable contexts" — a knife has many uses, cutting merely the most prominent; meaning accounts for *all possible* use contexts, not just the common one (p.44 / pdf 46).

**Care and the equipmental whole.** Dasein is "strangely non-centered" — no tangible anchoring point; it extends itself out to objects: quoting Heidegger (via *Basic Problems*-type material), Dasein "finds itself primarily in things," resting in them by tending and being distressed by them; "each one of us is what he pursues and cares for"; we understand ourselves by way of the activities we pursue and the things we take care of (p.44 / pdf 46). Even if specific devices do not reach the "essence" of technology, Heidegger cannot discount individual objects: Dasein is unstable, fading into the background without a means to manifest and concern itself; **care** is its key attribute — it is concerned with surrounding things and finds its own meaning in them (p.44 / pdf 46).

Wendt distinguishes the things currently of concern from everything else, quoting that "the nearest things that surround us we call equipment," given primarily as the *unity of an equipmental whole* that varies in range (expanding/contracting) and is visible mostly only "in excerpts," experienced rather than analyzed (p.44–45 / pdf 46–47). "Nearness" again refers not to physical proximity but to the extent to which an object is part of a user's current orientation (in use or not): clothing is physically near but "praxically far away"; the book/tablet in hand is proximally close. Being-in-the-world's "in" means being *concerned* with the world, not enclosed in a world-container (p.45 / pdf 47). The equipmental whole presents only as "excerpts" — a foreground and background; we can be concerned with only a finite amount at a time, and these excerpts are what let us cope (otherwise we would be "lost in objects"). By framing our point of view, "we turn objects into things" (p.45 / pdf 47).

**Object vs. thing (the jug).** Wendt deploys Heidegger's jug: a self-sustained vessel, self-supporting/independent, which "differs from an object" — an independent thing *becomes* an object only if we place it before us in perception or representation, but the "thingly character" does not consist in being a represented object; the jug remains a vessel whether represented or not (p.45–46 / pdf 47–48). Thing-ness is determined by our ability to make sense of it; the thing holds an independence, "empowered to assert its own agency"; its connection to the user is too intimate to differentiate, so the difference between user and thing "begins to fade," our energy as active agents "funneled through things" to particular parts of the world (p.46 / pdf 48).

The **mobile device** makes the point: in a store we see many styles/colors/brands, none with particular meaning — "dead material." Quoting Heidegger: "the vessel's thingness does not lie at all in the material… but in the void that it holds." For the device, the material means nothing; what matters is the information stored and the *potential interactions* its use enables. Object-ness (screen, plastic shell) is not meaningless (it communicates through physical design), but active engagement is what carries users *beyond* object-ness into *thing-ness*; the thingly qualities extend beyond physical attributes into "the space of meaning." We inject the dead material with meaning by downloading apps, importing contacts, and using it for goals; "the thing obtains meaning when we go beyond it and bring it closer." Assuming the ready-to-hand relationship (using in-order-to accomplish something else) leads to thingly qualities; picking up and using something "extends Dasein into a future state while still remaining wrapped up in the here and now" (p.46–47 / pdf 48–49).

**Bringing-close / gathering.** This bringing-close *creates* a thing: quoting Heidegger, "the jug stands as a vessel only because it has been brought to stand," by a process of "setting forth," i.e., producing the jug from earth the potter chose and prepared (p.47 / pdf 49). The jug differs radically from the mobile device: the potter's gathering/molding leads "somewhat linearly" to a use case, but the device's thing-ness is given not by a craftsperson or even a "designer" but by **its users** — it "cannot stand on its own," its potential use cases highly variable, the user defining whether it is for connecting, banking, business, warding off boredom, or directions (p.47 / pdf 49). (Note the recurrence of "ward-off boredom" among canonical use cases — p.47 / pdf 49.)

The chapter ends with what Wendt reads as Heidegger's "plea… to designers": quoting that Western thought has long understated the thing, representing it as "an unknown X to which perceptible properties are attached," so that the thing's gathering nature appears as something merely "read into it" (p.47–48 / pdf 49–50). Wendt's closing claim: by neglecting the thing, philosophy has failed to address the *nature of making*; designers and philosophers should come together to examine the nature of the thing **in the process of designing and making** — the "becoming," the designer gathering material and meaning into a thing, will be a central concern of the rest of the book, especially the relationship between the designer's *encoded meaning* and users' *actual behaviors* with the object (p.48 / pdf 50).

> **DESIGN-PRINCIPLE (design for thing-ness / meaning-gathering, design for all imaginable contexts):** Objects become *things* only as users gather meaning into them through active, ready-to-hand use; designers should design for the "becoming" — supporting the user's injection of meaning and the full range of *imaginable* use contexts — and attend to the gap between the designer's encoded meaning and users' actual behaviors, rather than treating an object as an inert bundle of properties.

*RO-hook: the user-completed "thing-ness" via meaning-gathering, and the explicit listing of "ward-off boredom" as a use case, are candidate touch-points for the dissertation's boredom/engagement axis and an actualization-of-desire reading (one major concept).*

## 9. Interlocutors and what Wendt draws from each

- **Martin Heidegger** — *Being and Time* and *The Question Concerning Technology*. The chapter's backbone: Dasein, being-in-the-world as involvement, being-ahead-of-itself, thrownness, the critique of Cartesianism, the tool analysis (ready-to-hand/present-at-hand, withdrawal, conspicuousness), care, the equipmental whole, the object/thing distinction (the jug), and the later technology themes (technē/poiēsis/physis, das Entbergen, Herausfordern, standing reserve/Bestand, enframing/Gestell). Wendt also notes "The Turn" between early and late Heidegger (p.38–39 / pdf 40–41).
- **Edmund Husserl** — named as Heidegger's teacher and foil; "traditional phenomenology" and the maintenance of the subject–object split, which Heidegger reacts against (p.15 / pdf 17). *(Merleau-Ponty, named in the unit-coverage brief, is not in fact developed in this chapter; the lineage Wendt actually foregrounds is Husserl → Heidegger, then Dreyfus/Dourish as contemporary mediators.)*
- **René Descartes** — the primary antagonist; the cogito, mind-over-body dualism, the "I" as wholly distinct from the body (pp.21–22 / pdf 23–24); diagnosed as the root of design's theory/practice and thinking/doing splits.
- **Hubert Dreyfus** — Heideggerian philosopher (AI, MIT); knowing-that vs. knowing-how (the hammer); the critique of rule-based Cartesian AI; and especially *practical coping* as representation-free dealing with the world (pp.24–25, 32–33 / pdf 26–27, 34–35).
- **Paul Dourish** (*Where the Action Is*) — the world-reveals-itself reframing (p.17 / pdf 19) and the lucid mouse example of ready-to-hand withdrawal / present-at-hand interruption (pp.27–28 / pdf 29–30).
- **Klaus Krippendorff** — product semantics and meaning: breakdown as loss of the *meaningfulness of the interface*; user-friendliness as confidence to handle disruption; the Taylorism critique of functionalist usability; meaning as the sum of *imaginable* contexts (pp.35–36, 44 / pdf 37–38, 46).
- **Gui Bonsiepe** — breakdown as a necessary accompaniment to design that "reveals the tissue of the relations"; design target = anticipate breakdowns and open possibilities for action (pp.33–34 / pdf 35–36).
- **Bernard Stiegler** — Dasein as "having-to-be," extending between "already" and "not yet" (Erstreckung) (p.18 / pdf 20).
- **Cameron Tonkinwise** — user testing as a *social learning* process; Apple's generational product education (p.32 / pdf 34).
- **Samuel Beckett** (*Malone Dies*) — extended literary illustration of Dasein-as-being-involved-in-objects; objects as extensions of the body and loci of ambivalent care (pp.19–21, 30–31 / pdf 21–23, 32–33).
- **Graham Harman** — cited for the concealment/unconcealment (veiled/unveiled) trope in Heidegger (p.40 / pdf 42).
- **R. L. Rutsky** — the "essence of technology" as a dynamic process/movement, not a static category, broadening technology toward making/producing incl. artistic production (p.39 / pdf 41).
- **Mark Weiser** — "calm computing" / ubiquitous computing as the (unrealized) promise behind the "invisible interface" and standing-reserve discourse (p.41 / pdf 43).
- **Friedrich Nietzsche** — invoked once via the design-as-thrownness gloss ("a throw of the dice on the table of the gods") (p.18 / pdf 20).

## 10. Consolidated design principles (for the design-lens)

1. **Context-as-involvement** — design for users' *involvement* in a situation, not merely their physical location (p.17 / pdf 19).
2. **Design for future goals** — design toward the end user's future goals; users act to accomplish "something less tangible," not to interact for its own sake (p.18 / pdf 20).
3. **Praxis over premature finality** — use prototypes/unfinished artifacts to test theory-laden assumptions in real use contexts; sketching as externalized thought; "action over making" (pp.25–26 / pdf 27–28).
4. **Acting-through, not acting-on** — center the user's end goal, letting the object/technology withdraw, rather than centering the object itself (p.24 / pdf 26).
5. **Design for the ready-to-hand/present-at-hand continuum** — design for the constant movement between absorbed coping and conscious deliberation; breakage is inevitable, so support coping with it (p.29 / pdf 31).
6. **Design for coping, breakdown, and meaning** — treat breakdown as revelatory; anticipate its forms and open possibilities for action; reconceive usability around meaning and confidence-to-handle-disruption, not functionalist (Taylorist) error-elimination (pp.33–36 / pdf 35–38).
7. **Responsible design against passive enframing** — resist the "invisible"/"intuitive"-interface fantasy and standing-reserve passivity; preserve an active user–system relationship (pp.42–43 / pdf 44–45).
8. **Design for thing-ness and imaginable contexts** — support users' meaning-gathering that turns objects into things; design for the full range of imaginable use contexts; mind the gap between the designer's encoded meaning and users' actual behaviors (pp.44–48 / pdf 46–50).
