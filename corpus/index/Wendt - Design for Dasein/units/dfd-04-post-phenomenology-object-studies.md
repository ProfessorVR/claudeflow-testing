## 0. Argument arc

This is the chapter where Wendt's "phenomenological design thinking" turns from Heidegger's account of equipment into a working theory of how *designed things act back on their users* — the pivot the whole book has been building toward. The arc runs in five moves. (1) He **critiques Heidegger's treatment of technology** and introduces Don Ihde's **post-phenomenology** as both critique and continuation, listing five points of divergence. (2) He installs Ihde's **four interaction styles / mediation relations** (embodiment, hermeneutic, background, alterity) as a finer-grained replacement for the bare present-at-hand / ready-to-hand pair. (3) He generalizes these into a theory of **mediation** — technology does not merely sit between subject and world but *constitutes* the experience — drawing on Peter-Paul Verbeek and Bruno Latour's actor-network theory (ANT). (4) He works the consequences for **designer intentionality**: scripts, delegation, the "designer fallacy," creative misuse, and **multistability** (Ihde's claim that a technology "is what it does," and can do many things across contexts). (5) He closes on **object studies** — Heidegger's "thing that things," Latour's matters-of-fact→matters-of-concern, Bill Brown's thing theory — to argue that experience design must work on the *intangible systems of meaning* objects gather, not on isolated objects.

The chapter's load-bearing thesis for the dissertation's design-lens purpose: **objects are active participants in human–environment systems; the results of design therefore cannot be planned with certainty, and the designer's job is to design for *mediation* and *multistability*, not for a fixed function** (p.90 / pdf 92; p.97 / pdf 99; p.109 / pdf 111).

## 2. Critiquing Heidegger: post-phenomenology as critique-and-continuation

Wendt presents **post-phenomenology** (Don Ihde) as a stance that both calls out classical phenomenology's shortcomings and builds on what Heidegger began, redirected to "newer forms of technology" (p.84 / pdf 86). He enumerates **five differences** from classical phenomenology:

1. **Individual meaning vs. essence** — for Heidegger, technology has an essence beyond any instantiation; post-phenomenology holds that any such essence "can only be known through devices," so individual devices must be treated as unique. Technology's "praxical component" is highlighted (p.85 / pdf 87).
2. **Industrial tech vs. everyday tech** — Heidegger's examples (dams, machinery) are bound to his historical moment; post-phenomenology broadens the scope to "pens, speed bumps, and tables" as technologies (p.85 / pdf 87).
3. **Technophobia → critical acceptance** — Heidegger's standing-reserve fears are justified but must be pushed further; designers are responsible to "reimagine problems with technology" (p.85 / pdf 87).
4. **Mediation** — the ready-to-hand pictures only a user acting *through* an object that fades to the background; it omits "how the object acts back upon the user" (p.85 / pdf 87).
5. **New interaction styles** — Ihde details four modes that capture nuances Heidegger "either ignored or didn't recognize" (p.86 / pdf 88).

Wendt anchors the position with Ihde: industrial "Promethean-style technofantasies" cast technology as humans-over-nature with control residing in the human practitioner; the postwar inversion ("Frankenstein technofantasies") makes control autonomous-through-technology. Ihde rejects both: humans and technologies are **"interrelational and mutually co-constitutive"** — even with a pen, "I 'use' the pen, but the pen … 'uses' me as well" (p.86 / pdf 88, note 117). Wendt illustrates the Promethean case with the Army Corps of Engineers' control of Niagara Falls (p.86–87 / pdf 88–89), then insists post-phenomenology finds technology's power in the **minutiae of mundane, everyday** devices (p.87 / pdf 89).

> RO-hook: the "mutually co-constitutive" human–technology relation is the design-side analogue of the actualization-of-desire chain's refusal to treat the object as a neutral instrument — the object is a co-agent in actualizing (or deflecting) desire. *(anticipatory-application)*

## 3. The four Ihde mediation relations (object type: MEDIATION-RELATION)

Wendt credits Ihde's first book *Technics and Praxis* (1979) for these "four modes of interaction" (p.87 / pdf 89). Each is defined below with its locus and Wendt's example.

### 3.1 Embodiment relations — `(p.87–88 / pdf 89–90)`
Close kin to Heidegger's ready-to-hand: the object is **fully embodied into the experience and fades out of focus**; "something else is experienced *through* the object" (p.88 / pdf 90). Canonical example: **eyeglasses** — the wearer looks *through* the (clean, unbroken) glasses to see the world, so the surroundings, not the glasses, are the focus. Wendt's interaction-design example: the **touch screen**, acted *through* to reach the task — until scratched or dirty (p.88 / pdf 90). Structure: human–(technology–world), the dash marking the technology's withdrawal.

### 3.2 Hermeneutic relations — `(p.88–89 / pdf 90–91)`
Two contrasts with embodiment: the user acts **with** the object rather than through it (the object becomes a **focal point** of the experience, not a transparent means), and a hermeneutic object **must be read and interpreted** to be fully experienced — it "calls for our attention" (p.88 / pdf 90). Verbeek's example: the **thermometer** — read to understand the world, embedded in social/cultural/linguistic norms of measurement. Wendt's example: **Twitter** — a means of conversation that "continually calls attention to itself" (length limits, ads) and lets us read the world from multiple points of view (p.88 / pdf 90). Structure: human–(technology–world), the technology now read like a text. Verbeek: embodiment and hermeneutic are not categorical but **"extreme ends of a continuum,"** with varying mediation along the line (p.88–89 / pdf 90–91).

### 3.3 Background relations — `(p.89 / pdf 91)`
Characterized by its **lack of conscious awareness**. Quoting Verbeek: in background relations "we are related neither explicitly to a technology nor via a technology to the world; instead, technologies shape the content of our experience" without being consciously experienced (p.89 / pdf 91, note 110). Verbeek's example: the **refrigerator** — it stores and preserves food without our attention, yet its non-involvement does not equal unimportance; background technologies "create the context in which embodied and hermeneutic relations can play themselves out" (p.89 / pdf 91). **Wendt's qualification (his own contribution):** Verbeek's view that experience is shaped *only* by conscious awareness is "unnecessarily restrictive" — experience is the end result of **both conscious and non-conscious environmental factors**, and the real difference between embodied and background is one of **proximity / direct involvement**, not awareness (remove the glasses and you notice at once; a broken refrigerator may go unnoticed until next use) (p.89 / pdf 91).

### 3.4 Alterity relations — `(p.89–90 / pdf 91–92)`
Technology becomes **quasi-other**: experienced as anthropomorphized or imbued with human-like attributes (p.89 / pdf 91). Example: Apple's **Siri** — a personified virtual assistant given a personality of "her" own; also "taking care of" a car or "intelligent machines." Wendt reads alterity as "the fantasy of much modern computing" (mobile, context-aware, AI) — to build a technology that operates on a similar yet importantly different level as the user (p.90 / pdf 92). Structure: human → technology-(world), the technology faced as a near-counterpart.

**Synthesis Wendt draws from the four.** Compared with Heidegger's two relations, Ihde recovers complexity Heidegger lost — especially the entire **background of technologies** and **anthropomorphized devices** that didn't exist at scale in Heidegger's time. He cites Verbeek's framing: Heidegger took technology as *the* way worldly meaning was disclosed to Dasein, whereas Ihde concentrated on **interactions with individual devices** as the starting point for Dasein's engagement with the world (p.90 / pdf 92, note 111). For Ihde, **each device interaction produces a unique effect / unique disclosure**, which "opens the possibility to design for particular meanings, instead of a generic revealing of essence" (p.90 / pdf 92). This is the chapter's first explicit DESIGN-PRINCIPLE (see §8).

## 4. Things and the dual identity of objects (transition into object studies)

With Ihde's styles as a starting point, Wendt turns to "human interaction with designed things," noting that design theory has "largely ignored objects as serious focal points" and that **post-phenomenology, object-oriented ontology (OOO), actor-network theory, and "thing theory"** have all worked to recenter objects (p.90–91 / pdf 92–93; cites Lucy Kimbell on objects' importance to design discourse). Even Heidegger, despite the obsession with essence, grounds self-understanding in things: Dasein "finds itself … in things," understanding itself "by way of the activities we pursue and the things we take care of" (p.91 / pdf 93, note 112). Self-understanding is contingent on involvement with things; we "experience the self through the medium of objects."

Post-phenomenological thinking trades in **reciprocation** — every movement has a countermovement (p.91 / pdf 93). So if interacting with objects gives the user self-understanding, **objects in turn obtain identity via human interaction**: a desk is only a desk in relation to what we do with it (put plates on it → dining table; religious artifacts → shrine); "desk-ness" is nothing in the word itself but "only what we do with the desk" (p.91 / pdf 93, note 113). Verbeek: "'The' world does not exist for us"; an artifact without people relating to it is "a piece of junk lying around"; "a technology is always a **technology-to…**," and that "to-…" gives the artifact its identity (p.91–92 / pdf 93–94, note 114). Wendt illustrates with Beckett's *Malone Dies* (sticks, pencils, pots) and grounds the body–thing–world co-dependence in **Merleau-Ponty**: we grasp the unity of our body "only in that of the thing"; the body at rest is "an obscure mass," precise only as it moves toward a thing (p.92 / pdf 94, note 115). The dual relationship between objects and users is "of massive importance to design" yet "often left out" — released objects "create emergent meaning" beyond planned outcomes (p.91–92 / pdf 93–94).

## 5. Mediation as a constitutive force (Verbeek, Latour, context)

The "Mediation" section's goal: show how technology mediates our experience so designers grasp the **active nature of objects** (p.92 / pdf 94). The instrumental view of technology is "only partially true"; objects are "not neutral aspects of the environment — they are active participants" (p.92–93 / pdf 94–95). Wendt's worked example is **Airbnb**: read one way it is a *result* of societal forces (spare space + demand for cheap/authentic lodging); read another way it *creates* new societal factors at scale (commodification of trust, neighbors-as-competitors) (p.93–94 / pdf 95–96). Hence a **reciprocal relationship between social relations and objects**: relations → objects → new relations (he quotes the thesis that societies are held together "by things as well," and technology must be analyzed for "the role it plays in social processes itself," p.94 / pdf 96, note 116). Recognizing this active role is a "crucial, ethical responsibility" of design (p.94 / pdf 96).

He then states **Verbeek's conception of mediation** directly: "Technologies co-shape the human world and … human relations with technology itself"; humans "are not sovereign with respect to technology" but "inextricably interwoven" with it; mediation "shapes the mutual relation in which both subject and object are concretely constituted" — "Someone who wears eyeglasses … is not the same without them" (p.94–95 / pdf 96–97, note 117). The **Internet of Things / Nest thermostat** shows technology acting back: Nest moves fluidly between *hermeneutic* relations (user checks/adjusts) and *background* relations (autonomous energy-saving automation) (p.95 / pdf 97).

**Context.** "Context" = "with text"; following post-structuralism, a text yields a multiplicity of meanings, so context is the **peripheral information used to interpret a situation** (p.95–96 / pdf 97–98). Design implication: qualitative user research must probe the periphery (don't just ask renters their satisfaction — bias floods direct questions; let experiential factors emerge) (p.96 / pdf 98). "Context shapes experience"; the same product differs across contexts (Facebook on the couch vs. airport security vs. desk) — both context and mediation "force designers to think at the level of **systems**" (p.96 / pdf 98). An automobile "can only exist as such in a context" of gasoline, pumps, refineries, highways, mechanics; "in itself" it is only metal (p.96 / pdf 98, note 119). Crucially, **mediating technologies are "mediators," not "intermediaries"** — they do not merely convey the objective world to subjects' minds but "help to constitute what is real for us" (p.96 / pdf 98, note 120).

**The gun (polemical example).** Mediation "constitutes our world" together with the unpredictability of use and the agency of things (p.96 / pdf 98). The NRA slogan "guns don't kill people … people kill people" is contested: holding a gun "literally changes someone" — the gun and user combine into a **"gunman," a new hybrid entity** different from either alone; the gun "mediates the situation to create reality" (p.96–97 / pdf 98–99). Context further changes the situation (officer / robber / soldier / child / hunter / teen), as does mindset (p.97 / pdf 99). Wendt distills the section into **three points**: (1) technology mediates the relationship between self and world; (2) mediation is not a mere intermediary but **helps constitute the entire experience**; (3) movement between interaction styles "can be quite fluid" (p.97 / pdf 99). Designing for mediation is "immensely difficult" and turns on the **specificity of things in context** — function *plus* situated meaning, "here and now," "are the fuel of mediation" (p.97–98 / pdf 99–100, note 121). He closes with **Donald Schön**: designing is "a reflective conversation with the situation"; the designer makes "moves," the situation "talks back," and he responds to its "backtalk" (p.98–99 / pdf 100–101, note 122).

> RO-hook: "mediation is a constitutive act" is the design-theoretic correlate of the chain's claim that the environment/object does not merely *present* an end but participates in *constituting* the desire actualized through it — mediation maps onto the moment where a sensed object becomes a moved-toward end. *(anticipatory-application)*

## 6. Intentionality: actants, scripts, delegation, the designer fallacy

Designers "create the conditions of possibility for desired outcomes," but this never guarantees them; design history is full of **creative misuse** (p.99 / pdf 101). Strong, clearly articulated intent is necessary precisely to "balance the effects of mediation as a constitutive force" — but one must know the limits of intentionality and avoid "the linear trap" of problem→solution (p.99 / pdf 101).

**Latour / ANT.** "What is a tool?" ANT reformulates "agent" into **"actant"** — anything, human or non-human, that "imposes intention within a system"; a tool is "the extension of social skills to nonhumans" (p.99–100 / pdf 101–102, note 123). Wendt reads "extension" as **recognition** that objects can change systems — they "mediate, speak, and manipulate" like human actants (p.100 / pdf 102). Design "helps to determine the aspects of sociality appropriate for an object" and inscribes them; each artifact "has its script, its 'affordance,' its potential to take hold of passersby and force them to play roles in its story" (p.100 / pdf 102, note 124 — Wendt flags Latour's idiosyncratic use of "affordance," to be covered next chapter). **Scripts** are inscribed purposes; designers anticipate use and build prescriptions into the product's materiality via **"delegation"** — to a speed bump "the responsibility was delegated to make sure nobody drives too fast" (p.100–101 / pdf 102–103, note 125).

Worked cases of delegation: **speed bumps** (sign → radar display → curvy road → bump; the last "offload the moral responsibility … onto the object" so the driver *must* slow) (p.100–101 / pdf 102–103); the **hotel-key keychain** (a cumbersome wood weight that "removes the burden of choice" and "instills the object with decision-making power") (p.101 / pdf 103); Latour's **automatic seat belt** (the alarm forces compliance — "Where is the morality? In me … or in the artifact forcing me…?") (p.101–102 / pdf 103–104, note 126). Wendt warns that the UX mantra **"don't make me think"** can become "jarring" when it strips agency, and that startup/VC/profit-and-speed culture erodes "reflection and contemplation in experience design" (p.102 / pdf 104). He cites Heidegger: "language remains the master of man" (p.102–103 / pdf 104–105, note 127).

**Designer intent, limits of.** The slogan "design is the rendering of intent" is "true … but overly simplistic and misleading" — like saying "the sky is blue" (p.103 / pdf 105, note 128). Etymology: "intent" (Latin) = "stretch out / lean toward / strain"; "intend" = "to stretch toward" — orienting oneself toward a future state (p.103 / pdf 105). Wendt invokes **Wimsatt & Beardsley's intentional fallacy** (judging a text by assumed authorial intent is a mistake; the text "stands on its own"), then **Ihde's parallel coinage, the "designer fallacy"** — the "deistic" notion of a "designer-god" whose plastic material yields an artifact that "seems 'intelligent' by design"; instead the design process is a far more complex web of "inter-relations between any designer, the materials … and the uses" (p.104 / pdf 106, notes 129/131). Schön's "conversation" is reread through **Gadamer**: "a genuine conversation is never the one that we wanted to conduct" — end use is an **emergent effect** in which intention is only one factor (p.105 / pdf 107, note 132).

**Husserl & Franssen.** Husserl: consciousness is always **consciousness *of* something** — intentionality makes visible the inextricable human–world connection; humans always think/see/feel *something* (p.105–106 / pdf 107–108, note 134). For built things, **Maarten Franssen**'s "for-ness": technical artifacts are "created with a purpose … that clings to the artifact" after the maker leaves, whereas a stone is "for cracking a nut" only ephemerally; the natural/artificial distinction blurs in use (p.106–107 / pdf 108–109, note 135). Hence: a designed object is *for something* as consciousness is *of something*; artifacts carry **scripts** that "cling to the object," but natural objects' physical properties also "communicate potential use" (foreshadowing affordances) (p.107 / pdf 109).

**Creative misuse.** Users break intended use cases (hammer-as-paperweight, table-edge bottle-opener, tie-on-doorknob, in-flight napkins-and-rubber-bands to prop a broken screen) (p.107–108 / pdf 109–110). Some products are *designed* to mask designer intent (Twitter, **Second Life**) — "the intention built into these products is precisely to undermine … designer intention" (p.107–108 / pdf 109–110). Misuse shows that "agents who typically use artifacts can … be designers" — though Wendt **disagrees** that everyone who uses artifacts is a designer (designer identity is a "particular care and concern," per ch. 3) (p.108, p.115 / pdf 110, 117, note 136). All of this is "contingent on … multistability" (p.108 / pdf 110).

> RO-hook: "scripts / delegation" and the designer fallacy map onto the chain's distinction between the artifact's *inscribed* end and the user's *actualized* end — delegation tries to fix the actualization in advance, while creative misuse is the desire re-actualizing the object otherwise. *(anticipatory-application)*

## 7. Multistability and object studies

**Multistability (Ihde).** Technology "is not pure instrumentality"; its actual use is **multistable** — it accounts for "the difference between what designers want … and what actually occurs," holding that a technology "takes shape not according to what it is but rather what it can do" (p.109 / pdf 111). A technology has "multiple stabilities within its relationship to humans"; scripts are "only a portion of potential roles"; it is *not* "unstable" — designed scripts assert some (context-varying) influence (a phone is an email device, or a prop to avoid eye contact) (p.109–110 / pdf 111–112). Mediating capacity is "no essential property of things themselves" but emerges from "the interplay of things and their context": technologies "are what they are only within the context in which they are used" (p.110 / pdf 112, note 137). Clients resist this "radical variability"; "the possible uses are always ambiguous and multistable," and ambiguity is "a necessary evil within design" (p.110–111 / pdf 112–113, note 138). Ihde again: "an instrument is what it does, and this in a context of assignments"; "No technology is one thing" (p.111 / pdf 113, note 139). Wendt names **"the fundamental question of design metaphysics"**: what a design solution *is*, *should be*, and *can be* — the question of what the object **can be** is "a phenomenological question … of crucial importance for experience design" (p.111–112 / pdf 113–114). He recruits **Baudrillard** (functionality = "the ability to become integrated into an overall scheme"; the system of objects is "schizofunctional," each object split to serve many purposes) (p.111–112 / pdf 113–114, note 140); **Krippendorff** (the PC born by combining typewriter + TV screen as metaphors — multistability as a *source* of new solutions) (p.112–113 / pdf 114–115, note 141); and **Flusser/Ingold** (design's roots in "deceit"; "every object of design sets a trap by presenting a problem in the form of … its solution"; the designer as "trickster" managing imperfection) (p.113–114 / pdf 115–116, notes 142/143). Creative misuse is "a reaction against … design itself," enabled by multistability — including a **metaphysical shift** in the object's "mental model" (screwdriver used not as screwdriver but as "lever"; Wendt's door-stopper bottle; the scalding-kettle story where the object "ceased to be what it was and began to be what it could do") (p.114–116 / pdf 116–118, notes 144/145/147). With designer/maker/user roles "not rigorously distinct" (Ingold: if things are never finished, "can design any longer be distinguished from making?"), **thinking in systems** becomes paramount (p.115–117 / pdf 117–119, note 146).

**Objects, Things, Interactions, and Experiences (object studies proper).** Experience is always **of something** (a focal point), so "designing an experience is dependent on designing the objects of experience" — but this is "too simplistic" given design's **intangibility** (p.117 / pdf 119). Via **Prasad Boradkar**, Wendt sketches a "comically short history" of thinking about things (Thales → Heraclitus → Pythagoras → Parmenides → Plato → Descartes → Leibniz → Kant → Hegel → Heidegger → Latour/Verbeek/Ihde) to show the **progression from phenomenology to post-phenomenology** (p.117–118 / pdf 119–120, note 148). The **hermeneutic circle of experience design**: we can only design experiences via things, "never knowing which comes first" — so experience design is "not concerned with the design of objects as such, but rather … the effects these objects have on intangible systems" (p.118 / pdf 120). **Heidegger's thing**: "the jug is a thing insofar as it things"; "thinging is the nearing of the world"; "thing" means **gathering** (the jug gathers function, cultural/familial meaning, destructive power) (p.118–119 / pdf 120–121, notes 149/150). The **jug's void** — "the emptiness, the void, is what does the vessel's holding" — yields the lesson that "the unseen aspects of the thing … give shape to that which is designed"; experience design must concern itself with "the tangible and the intangible … specifically the interaction between them" (**Folkmann**: material and immaterial effects both involved in evoking meaning) (p.119 / pdf 121, notes 151/152). **Latour**: as "matters of fact" become "matters of concern," "objects are turned into things … rendered into objects of design through and through" (p.120 / pdf 122, note 153). **Bill Brown's thing theory**: "we look *through* objects … but we only catch a glimpse of *things*"; we confront "the thingness of objects when they stop working for us" (the drill breaks, the car stalls) — thingness tied to (failed) functionality (p.120–121 / pdf 122–123, note 154). Closing **Heidegger**: "the jug is not a vessel because it was made; rather, the jug had to be made because it is this holding vessel" — the thing comes into being to serve a purpose (p.121 / pdf 123, note 155). Wendt corrects Heidegger on two points: (1) designers **can design at a systems level**, influencing *how the thing things*; (2) the object does not merely sit apart from the maker but **"things on its own with multistable tendencies"** across contexts — "this is where experience designers tend to play, navigating this variability … to design the intangible" (p.121–122 / pdf 123–124, note 156).

> RO-hook: Heidegger's "thing as gathering" + Brown's "look-through vs. glimpse" supply a design-side reading of how an environment can recede (embodied/background, an unnoticed horizon for desire) or obtrude (hermeneutic/alterity, a focal object that re-solicits desire) — the engagement/boredom oscillation the dissertation tracks. *(anticipatory-application)*

## 8. Explicit DESIGN-PRINCIPLEs (object type: DESIGN-PRINCIPLE)

1. **Design for particular meanings, not generic essence.** Because each device discloses uniquely, designers can target specific meanings instead of a "generic revealing of essence" (p.90 / pdf 92).
2. **Design for the object acting back (mediation).** Treat objects as active participants; account for "how the object acts back upon the user" — recognizing their active role is "a crucial, ethical responsibility" (p.85, p.94 / pdf 87, 96).
3. **Design at the level of systems / context.** Both context and mediation "force designers to think at the level of systems"; research and foresight must probe the periphery and the system of relations, not the isolated object (p.96 / pdf 98).
4. **Hold strong-but-bounded intent.** Articulate clear intent to "balance the effects of mediation," while knowing intent's limits and avoiding the linear problem→solution trap and the "designer fallacy" (p.99, p.104 / pdf 101, 106).
5. **Delegate responsibly.** Inscribing scripts / delegating to artifacts (speed bump, key weight, seat belt) can offload moral responsibility onto objects — use deliberately, aware that "don't make me think" can wrongly strip agency (p.100–102 / pdf 102–104).
6. **Design for multistability and what the solution *can be*.** Embrace ambiguity/schizofunctionality as both a constraint and a generative source (typewriter+TV → PC); the "design metaphysics" question is what the solution *can be* (p.109–113 / pdf 111–115).
7. **Design the intangible systems of meaning, via the thing's gathering.** Experience design works on the effects objects have on intangible systems and on how the thing "things," attending to material *and* immaterial (the jug's void) (p.118–122 / pdf 120–124).
8. **Anticipate creative misuse.** Released solutions take on new meanings; misuse reveals "un-discovered needs, wants, and desires" — design with this in view (p.107–108, p.116 / pdf 109–110, 118).

## 9. Interlocutors and what Wendt draws from each

- **Don Ihde** — *Technics and Praxis* (1979); also *Ironic Technics* / later work. The chapter's spine: post-phenomenology, the **four mediation relations**, the **designer fallacy**, and **multistability** ("an instrument is what it does"; "is what it can do"). Wendt adopts all of these as the engine of his design theory (pp.84, 87, 104, 109, 111 / pdf 86, 89, 106, 111, 113).
- **Peter-Paul Verbeek** — *What Things Do* (and related). Source for the **embodied↔hermeneutic continuum**, the precise **background-relation** definition, the **co-shaping/"not sovereign"** account of mediation, "**technology-to…**," and the Ihde-vs-Heidegger disclosure contrast. Wendt's main conduit for Ihde/Latour, though he **qualifies** Verbeek's "conscious-awareness-only" view of background relations (pp.88–95 / pdf 90–97).
- **Bruno Latour** — actor-network theory. Source for **actant**, **script/affordance**, **delegation**, and the **matters-of-fact→matters-of-concern** thesis; examples of speed bump, hotel key, seat belt; "we have never been modern." Wendt draws the non-human-agency framework but flags Latour's loose "affordance" (pp.99–102, 120 / pdf 101–104, 122).
- **Graham Harman / OOO (object-oriented ontology)** — named among the movements (with ANT and thing theory) that recenter objects as a "primary focus" for design; Wendt aligns the chapter's "object studies" turn with OOO without working its metaphysics in detail (p.90–91 / pdf 92–93).
- **Edmund Husserl** — classical intentionality: **consciousness is always consciousness *of* something**; used to ground the inextricable human–world link that post-phenomenology radicalizes (pp.105–106 / pdf 107–108).
- **Martin Heidegger** — the critiqued-yet-continued source: equipment/ready-to-hand, standing-reserve, and the late **"thing that things"/gathering/jug**; "language remains the master of man." Wendt both builds on and corrects him (systems-level design; the object's own multistable thinging) (pp.84, 91, 102, 118–122 / pdf 86, 93, 104, 120–124).
- **Maarten Franssen** — the **"for-ness"** of technical artifacts vs. the ephemeral purpose of natural objects; blurs natural/artificial in use (pp.106–107 / pdf 108–109).
- **Donald Schön** — design as a **reflective conversation** in which the situation "talks back" (pp.98–99, 105 / pdf 100–101, 107).
- **Hans-Georg Gadamer** — "a genuine conversation is never the one that we wanted to conduct"; deepens Schön into emergent, non-willed outcome (p.105 / pdf 107).
- **Wimsatt & Beardsley** — the **intentional fallacy** in literary criticism, Ihde's template for the designer fallacy (p.104 / pdf 106).
- **Jean Baudrillard** — *The System of Objects*; **functionality as integration into a system**; **schizofunctionality** (pp.111–112 / pdf 113–114).
- **Klaus Krippendorff** — multistability as generative: the PC as typewriter + TV-screen metaphor (pp.112–113 / pdf 114–115).
- **Vilém Flusser** — "design" rooted in deceit/trickery; the machine that "cheats nature" (p.113 / pdf 115).
- **Tim Ingold** — the designer as **trickster**; "every object of design sets a trap"; "can design any longer be distinguished from making?" (pp.113–114, 116–117 / pdf 115–116, 118–119).
- **Prasad Boradkar** — *Designing Things*; the capsule history of philosophies of things, framing the phenomenology→post-phenomenology progression (pp.117–118 / pdf 119–120).
- **Bill Brown** — **thing theory**: "look through objects, glimpse things"; thingness surfaces in breakdown (pp.120–121 / pdf 122–123).
- **Mads Folkmann** — *The Aesthetics of Imagination in Design*; material **and** immaterial effects both at work in evoking meaning (p.119 / pdf 121).
- **Lucy Kimbell** — objects' importance to design discourse; objects often the end result of a design process (p.90 / pdf 92).
- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty** — the **body–thing–world** unity; the body known precisely only in moving toward a thing (p.92 / pdf 94).
- **Samuel Beckett** — *Malone Dies*; literary illustration of objects giving life meaning and gaining identity in use (p.92 / pdf 94).
