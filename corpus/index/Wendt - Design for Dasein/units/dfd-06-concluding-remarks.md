# dfd-06 — Concluding Remarks: "Phenomenological Design Thinking" (Ch. 7)

**Source:** Wendt, *Design for Dasein* (2015), Chapter 7, book pp. 153–164 (pdf 155–166). Depth: **deep**.
**Role in entry:** the synthesis chapter — Wendt draws the whole book's threads (embodied philosophy, post-phenomenology, the problem–solution paradox, technological mediation, embodiment/meaning-formation) into the *beginnings* of a phenomenological design methodology, states what designers should actually do, and flags what he leaves open.
**Discipline:** three voices kept distinct — **Wendt-says** (default), **RO-hook** (≤1 per major concept, explicitly marked, `provenance: anticipatory-application`), **environment-application** (where flagged). No ≥25-word verbatim; anchor-phrases ≤12 words.

---

## 1. Framing: the gap between academia and practice, refused

Wendt opens by recalling the tone he set at the start — pitched "somewhere between academia and practice" — and treats the academia/practice dichotomy itself as the *fundamental flaw* he must answer for (p.153 / pdf 155). He feels the onus is on him to make the "theoretical" parts of the book more applicable to design practice. Crucially, he refuses to deliver a *strict methodology or set of practices*: he has "little interest" in packaging a phenomenology-influenced design practice into an "off-the-shelf" set of practices (p.153 / pdf 155). The chosen move is more modest and, he argues, more productive: **re-examine existing design methods through the lens of phenomenology**, and push the book's key points harder into practice than he has so far (p.153 / pdf 155).

He borrows Mads Folkmann's framing — that phenomenological design thinking must address both the *present and the future* of experience — and the chapter's epigraph: applying phenomenology to design means focusing on how design, "as a medium of meaning formation," both relates to and possibly *changes* the constituents of experience (p.153 / pdf 155, quoting Folkmann). Phenomenology and its "intellectual cousins" are framed as excellent frameworks for the *inductive and abductive* aspects of design; design's distinctive power is to push thinking toward *future scenarios*, for which present experience remains essential (p.153 / pdf 155).

The methodological thesis of the chapter is stated plainly: phenomenological design thinking is "not terribly different from post-phenomenology" — it is *an evolution* of it (p.154 / pdf 156). Post-phenomenology, in its focus on things and interaction, has already begun pushing classical phenomenology into the realm of design; Wendt's claim is that **the next step in post-phenomenology is grounded in design thinking** (p.154 / pdf 156). The remainder of the chapter revisits concepts already introduced, now read explicitly for *design practice*.

## 2. Praxis — dissolving the theory/practice and academia/industry walls

Wendt restates the book's organizing aim: to showcase the importance of **praxis** = theory-informed practice, a *cyclical* relationship between theory and practice in which the practical *process* matters as much as the end result (p.154 / pdf 156). The academia/industry and theory/practice dichotomies are "detrimental," especially in a field (design) that claims to be multidisciplinary; the "walls we put up are completely counterintuitive" (p.154 / pdf 156). Different end-goals (production of knowledge vs. commercial success) are "wrapped up within one another," not mutually exclusive; commercial design can be built on academic knowledge and can in turn feed academic research, keeping research grounded in worldly practice (p.154 / pdf 156).

Phenomenology, he reminds, posits praxis as our *way of being in the world*: it is anti-dualist, rejecting the Cartesian mind/body split and integrating self and world into **Dasein** (p.154 / pdf 156). Dasein's being-*in* — being-*involved*, a conglomeration of self and world that exhibits *care and concern* over itself — carries over to how Dasein makes use of designed objects: humans interact with their world with great care, and **designers must always work around that care** (p.154 / pdf 156). This sets up "designing for care" as a recurring normative thread.

> **RO-hook (provenance: anticipatory-application):** Wendt's praxis-as-being-in-the-world (theory and practice cyclically co-constituting, process valued with the end) is the design-lens analogue of the actualization-of-desire chain's refusal to split deliberation from the embodied movement that completes an action — *energeia* realized in the doing, not antecedent representation driving inert execution.

## 3. The limits of design deliverables: the persona critique

Wendt's first concrete application targets **user personas** as an example of a method "aimed at recognizing praxis and designing for care" that nonetheless "fragments under the weight of commercial clients or academic pressures" (p.154 / pdf 156). Personas usefully summarize research and give teams "a personal identification to users," but their benefits cannot counterbalance an *inherent decontextualization*: "a persona does not experience anything — humans do" (p.155 / pdf 157). Even as a helpful in-process document, a persona *reinforces the Cartesian dualism*; personas represent **detached understanding** — theoretical formulations even when based on rigorous research (p.155 / pdf 157).

The methodological move here is characteristic and explicitly stated: the point is **not to replace old practices with new ones**, but to point out their *limitations* (p.155 / pdf 157). The onus is on designers to *recognize* those limits and avoid using methods "beyond their capabilities." His summary principle: "the ways designers frame their practices make all the difference" (p.155 / pdf 157).

## 4. Primacy of things — experience design has an object

Things are "our means to knowledge and understanding"; interaction with things — not only as "other" objects but as *embodied means of accomplishment* — frames our whole experience of the world and enables hermeneutic interpretation of everyday life (p.155 / pdf 157). Understanding *systems of things* and how humans experience them is "the domain of experience design" (p.155 / pdf 157). The complexity is that experience design deals with *both* the tangible and the intangible: it designs experience, which entails also designing the things that are experienced. Hence **experience design always has an object** — the designer crafts the experience of products/services by ensuring an understanding of things and meaning systems, working "the conditions of possibility for intended results," crafting clear affordances, and playing with "the movement from embodiment to external" (p.155 / pdf 157).

He then revisits Heidegger's **present-at-hand / ready-to-hand** as *not exclusive categories* but a **continuum of interaction** in constant back-and-forth motion (p.155 / pdf 157). Typing on a keyboard is steady ready-to-hand interaction interruptible by a mistyped letter, second-guessing a spelling, or environmental distraction (p.156 / pdf 158). The design-relevant payoff: it is "much too simplistic" to state invisibility, intuition, or readiness-to-hand as a *design goal* — these are modes of interaction judged "good" or "bad" only relative to context of use (p.156 / pdf 158). The same holds for Don Ihde's four relations (**embodied, hermeneutic, alterity, background**): nothing is qualitatively better about a background vs. an embodied relation, and we must not assume an "intuitive" interface is always good. It falls to experience designers to determine the *use contexts and narrative points* at which different interaction styles apply (p.156 / pdf 158).

> **RO-hook (provenance: anticipatory-application):** Wendt's continuum reading of present-at-hand/ready-to-hand — modes sliding in and out under breakdown — maps onto the chain's account of how equipmental absorption is punctuated by disruption that throws the agent back into deliberative/representational comportment, the hinge the RO uses to relate disrupted *aisthēsis* to renewed orexis-formation.

## 5. Multistability — designing with variability, not against it

Wendt establishes the human–technology relationship as **multistable**, and notes this variability/adaptability is hard for designers to absorb, especially under commercial pressure to "portray as much certainty as possible" (p.156 / pdf 158). Clients pay for plans that confirm intended results; the team therefore "meticulously plans, executes, and attempts to measure results" to confirm original intentions (p.156 / pdf 158). Multistability "makes all this pantomime for nothing": designs will almost always be *adapted from* the original intention, and "the powers of creative misuse will always win against the clearest affordances" (p.156 / pdf 158). The reframing of method is sharp: in experience design, **usability testing ceases to be about creating perfection or removing "un-intuitive" features and becomes about observing *coping strategies*** — "the ways that users cope with problems are the core insights for usability and future design" (p.156 / pdf 158).

## 6. The problem–solution paradox — the hermeneutic circle of design

Of all the design paradoxes, the **problem–solution paradox** "probably has the most impact" (p.156 / pdf 158). Wendt names it explicitly: it is **"the hermeneutic circle of design"** — parts inherently connected to the whole and the whole to its parts (p.156 / pdf 158). It creates large problems on commercial projects, where clients want "deep and unflinching knowledge of a 'process'" and designers are "forced to deliver this illusory knowledge knowing that it is insufficient" (p.156 / pdf 158). The paradox calls for **play** — a *fluid movement* from problem definition to solution exploration and back — which in turn causes ambiguity in project plans and billing cycles (p.157 / pdf 159).

His methodological conclusion: experience design "needs better ways to account for the paradox" and to build *plans flexible enough for forward-and-backward movement* between problem and solution (p.157 / pdf 159). He criticizes **waterfall** development for its *hyper-linearity* and rigid phases: waterfall ignores that design is full of error and variability, and assumes a *positivist* model (gather knowledge → plan → execute) that, "much like the Cartesian separation of mind and body," relies on intentional planning to drive execution and leaves "almost no opportunity to adapt" (p.157 / pdf 159). Experience design instead "involves an embodied, situated movement between present and future states" (p.157 / pdf 159).

## 7. Care — specialization as the condition of concern

The chapter's other normative thread concerns **skillful practice** and the erosion of care. As practitioners "add more diverse skills," what counts as an "experience designer" grows ambiguous; the field has "ironically done a terrible job" of applying its own clarity-goals to define itself (the "cobbler's children") (p.157 / pdf 159). Citing Henk Oosterling, Wendt frames **design as an "un-throwing"**: humans design to take an *active part* in their world, to shape an experience rather than remain content with the current situation (p.157 / pdf 159). We call a designer a "designer" because of the *care* exhibited toward materials, users, objects, experiences (p.157 / pdf 159). His worry is that as individuals take on ever more of the process, **care erodes as it "spreads thinly across various concerns"** (p.158 / pdf 160). The actionable consequence: for any complex project, **maintain a degree of specialization** on the team — people who focus and avoid "switching styles of thinking too rapidly"; the division of labor, even if not always financially advantageous, "allows for a deeper sense of concern" for a manageable set of practices (p.158 / pdf 160).

## 8. Practice — phenomenology explains design rather than replacing it; play between past and future

Wendt concedes one might read the book as proposing a phenomenological *design process*, "and in some respects it is," but insists "nothing discussed in this book is revolutionary" — it is *existing theory applied to new contexts* (p.158 / pdf 160). What phenomenology accomplishes for design is to *establish bases of knowledge around experience*: rather than wholly new practices, phenomenological theory **helps explain current practices, add to them, and make them a bit more rigorous**. "Design is inherently phenomenological," so forcing new practices is not particularly beneficial (p.158 / pdf 160).

Design's "most useful peculiarity" is its ability to **push into the future** (p.158 / pdf 160). Where inductive and deductive reasoning establish explanations of what we currently experience (the present "essentially doesn't exist" — is "now" the utterance or its interpretation?), design uses that knowledge to extend into future experiences via the **abductive gesture** (p.158 / pdf 160). Analytical and creative thinking come together "with equal importance": we cannot create the future without knowing the past, nor fully understand the past without envisioning the future (p.158 / pdf 160). This **back-and-forth play between past and future** shapes the design process across understanding → visioning → sketching → prototyping → critique and back (p.158 / pdf 160).

The two most important *movements* within these activities are named explicitly: **familiarization/de-familiarization** and **concealment/unconcealment**, conceived as ends of a spectrum with many points between (pp.158–159 / pdf 160–161). Designers "bring the familiar close, only to make it strange again," moving from macro- to micro-perspectives, concealing and unconcealing what is or is not useful at a given time (p.159 / pdf 161). This **foreground–background movement** runs throughout phenomenology and is integral to design — visible in Heidegger's present-at-hand/ready-to-hand, where objects move in and out of the field of consciousness (p.159 / pdf 161).

He grounds this in concrete methods. *Ethnographic research* de-familiarizes "hyper-familiar bias": his worked example — assuming caregivers for a terminally ill patient struggle to balance patient-time against work/family — is "a valid assumption" whose hyper-familiarity "must be disturbed by actual observation and dialogue," potentially invalidating or enriching it (pp.159–160 / pdf 161–162). *Diagramming* (systems/flow) lets designers zoom between micro and macro: zooming into a single interface screen obscures the rest of the system — "much like Don Ihde's conception of the telescope" that reveals the far while obscuring the near — and zooming to macro flows obscures detail (p.159 / pdf 161). Each perspective has value; **the role of the designer is to decide when to conceal and unconceal**, and to recognize the work of familiarization/de-familiarization (pp.159–160 / pdf 161–162).

## 9. Five summary statements (Wendt's explicit, hedged "design principles")

Wendt is careful that the following are "not necessarily design principles or heuristics" but *general statements* summing up prior discussion (p.160 / pdf 162). They are nonetheless the chapter's nearest thing to enumerated design principles, and I treat them as such (so flagged).

1. **Humans exist in context** (p.160 / pdf 162). Designers say context matters but "our work says something different": the "fetishization of deliverables" yields an *inauthentic* relationship with the work. Documentation is "really just a way to help us think" — a sketch or diagram's primary role is helping the designer *think through* a complex situation; even done well, such documents are "representations," disconnected from use context. The move is not to abandon methods but to "call out what they lack," using personas/use-cases *as thinking aids, not deliverables*: "creating the document is not the real work; understanding results from thinking through the object provides the real value" (p.160 / pdf 162).

2. **Thinking does not always precede action** (p.160 / pdf 162). Against the assumption that representational thought precedes action, Heidegger's insight that behavior is *goal-oriented* (we use objects to accomplish goals) frames interaction design — but it *misses* that **new end-goals emerge only after we begin interacting** with something (users discover goals previously unknown, including *negative* goals to prevent things) (pp.160–161 / pdf 162–163). Embodied cognition is "rethinking the relationship between mind and body from a scientific... perspective"; this anti-rationality leads to **praxis** — "generating theoretical models through practical outputs." The swim lesson (from ch. 2) recurs: teaching a child to swim, one does not study fluid dynamics and kinesiology — "they simply get in the pool"; the embodied experience exceeds discrete facts, and reflection comes *only after* — "true of technological experience as well" (p.161 / pdf 163).

3. **Intention is not primary** (p.161 / pdf 163). The designer's intention is important "but it should not be prioritized over adaptability." Many behaviors are *emergent*. The "designer as deity" (maker-god molding the world to their image — "why we depict designers in pristine, heavenly environments") and the "rendering of intent" definition of design are "too simplistic," failing to encapsulate *embodied use* and *inherent variability* (pp.161–162 / pdf 163–164). Borrowing from post-structuralism: the designer's intent is, in a sense, "irrelevant once the product launches"; intent can *drive* the process but "the ways in which users adopt the product to their own needs is where the most insight comes from." Use is **multistable** — the car as transport, dwelling, weapon, status symbol, or art — and "'launch' is not the end of the design process but simply a step toward understanding"; multistability is "not necessarily a wrench in the design machine" but an area of complexity that can yield *serendipitous* results (p.162 / pdf 164).

4. **Objects are more than just material** (p.162 / pdf 164). Design is "incredibly powerful" and carries *moral responsibility*. Via Verbeek's **theory of mediation**, users adopt technological devices as *interpretive objects*, constantly reinterpreting them: "objects affect the user as much as the user affects the object" (pp.162–163 / pdf 164–165). Weapons, machines, mobile devices mediate the user–world relation "to the point where the user literally changes" — "someone in possession of a gun... is very different from someone without one"; Latour's **hotel-key** example (from ch. 5) shows the keychain "makes the decision" about returning the key, disclosing "the activity of objects" (p.163 / pdf 165). The caution: caught in detail, designers "forget about the systemic effects an object can have" — "the things we design have powerful effects... long after the client's check is cashed." The most nefarious: *persuasive design* and **dark patterns** "attempting to facilitate profitable mistakes" (e.g., the app Secret). Hence: **experience designers are not only advocating for users — they are advocating for *preferable worlds*** (p.163 / pdf 165).

5. **Utility does not trump meaning** (p.163 / pdf 165). Client-driven projects produce "an anxiety of perfection," but "in the real world, nothing works perfectly — we play with things and adapt them." Too many designers approach usability testing privileging "functionality and perfection," producing false expectations. Two correctives: (1) products "do not need to be functionally perfect for people to use them" — imperfection won't necessarily cause "abandonment" (a word that "creates an unnecessary sense of urgency"); humans are "remarkably adaptive when value is high and meaning is strong" — users cope "if they experience enough value"; (2) "functionality is only half of the story" — "the interaction between products and users creates meaning, and that is our ultimate concern" (pp.163–164 / pdf 165–166). Via Klaus **Krippendorff**, the goal of *product semantics* is to "identify and optimize opportunities for creating meaning through interaction" — shifting the conversation from functionality (measurable with a "modicum of objectivity") to **meaning** (purely contextual, varying with each individual) (p.164 / pdf 166). Measuring meaning is "more delicate and complex," "more slippery" to sell to a client, "but we should be up to the challenge... We won't change the multistability of technology, but we can **design with it**" (p.164 / pdf 166).

## 10. In Conclusion — the book's "identity crisis," and what designers should do next

Wendt closes by naming the book's "identity crisis" (flagged in the introduction) (p.164 / pdf 166). On one hand it is written for **practitioners**, to show that "theoretical" or "impractical" thinking is necessary for meaningful design — or at least that practitioners "can and should engage with the intellectual history of their field" beyond "practical" industry conferences and a few recommended books (pp.164–165 / pdf 166–167). On the other, it tries to balance practitioner focus with *depth of analysis*, aiming to **establish a base of knowledge on phenomenological philosophy on which others can build** — rather than presenting "a grand, mostly illusory narrative on how to redefine experience design" (p.164 / pdf 166). The forward-looking charge: the field's "next move as a budding design discipline is to better understand existing theories of experience while pushing forward into exploring the possibilities they afford" — "after all, designers are supposed to be good at this type of thing" (p.165 / pdf 167).

## 11. Open problems Wendt leaves

- **No operational methodology.** By explicit choice, he offers re-framings of existing methods, not a procedure; *how* to institutionalize phenomenological design thinking as repeatable practice is left open (p.153 / pdf 155).
- **Measuring meaning.** He concedes meaning is "delicate," "contextual," and "slippery to sell," giving no method for measuring/communicating it to clients — only that designers "should be up to the challenge" (p.164 / pdf 166).
- **Billing/planning under the problem–solution paradox.** He calls for "plans flexible enough for forward and backward movement" but supplies no concrete planning or billing model to replace waterfall (p.157 / pdf 159).
- **Defining the discipline / locating care.** What constitutes "an experience designer" remains "more ambiguous"; specialization is prescribed to preserve care but no boundary for the discipline is drawn (pp.157–158 / pdf 159–160).
- **Operationalizing multistability and emergent goals.** Designing *with* multistability and capturing *emergent* end-goals are endorsed but not given method (p.162 / pdf 164).
- **Ethics of mediation in practice.** "Advocating for preferable worlds" and resisting dark patterns is asserted as responsibility without a decision procedure for the moral implications of designed objects (p.163 / pdf 165).

## 12. Synthesis arc (how the chapters are tied together)

The chapter functions as a *retrospective braid*: **praxis** (the book's anti-dualist spine, §2) underwrites every subsequent move; the **primacy of things** and the **present-at-hand/ready-to-hand continuum** (§4, from the Heidegger/embodiment chapters) supply the ontology of interaction; **multistability** and the **problem–solution paradox** (§§5–6, from the paradox and post-phenomenology chapters) reframe core methods (usability testing → observing coping; waterfall → flexible, abductive play); **care** (§7) supplies the normative thread; and the **five statements** (§9) condense embodiment/meaning-formation and mediation (from the embodiment and post-phenomenology chapters) into hedged principles. The consistent *methodological signature* — stated repeatedly — is **diagnostic, not prescriptive**: name a method's limits, reframe its purpose phenomenologically, and shift the designer's stance from *controlling intention* to *observing situated, embodied, multistable use*. The destination is not a method but a *disposition*: design as praxis that plays between past and future, conceals and unconceals, and advocates for preferable worlds.
