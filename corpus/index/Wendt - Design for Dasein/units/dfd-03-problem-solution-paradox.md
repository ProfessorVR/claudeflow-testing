## 1. Argument arc (the chapter in one motion)

The chapter is the hinge Wendt uses to pivot from phenomenology to post-phenomenology, and the pivot is staged on a single paradox he names the **problem-solution paradox** (p.72 / pdf 74). The arc runs: (1) two reigning design methodologies — design thinking and lean startup — are shown to share an unresolved tension (pp.72–73 / pdf 74–75); (2) that tension is formalized as a two-sided paradox: we cannot think solutions until we understand the problem, yet we cannot understand the problem until we think solutions (p.73 / pdf 75); (3) each side, taken alone, fails — the linear side reifies a "final answer," the praxis side discards theoretical insight (pp.73–74 / pdf 75–76); (4) the paradox is relocated within a lineage of *non-dualist* philosophies (Heidegger's hermeneutic circle, Saussure's chain of signification, Schön's reflective conversation) (pp.74–75 / pdf 76–77); (5) the "problem-solution dichotomy" is then dissolved — it is *not* a dichotomy except under strict dualism (pp.75–77 / pdf 77–79); (6) prototyping and sketching are reread, via Cross, as externalized thinking that lets one engage a problem *through* its solutions (pp.77–78 / pdf 79–80); (7) Dorst's account of **co-evolution** and the **emergent bridge** supplies the positive model (pp.78–80 / pdf 80–82); (8) the paradox is reframed as an *antinomy* — something not to be solved but redefined — with Morozov's "solutionism," Basalla's evolutionary theory of technology, and Nelson & Stolterman's twelve paradoxes closing the chapter on the irreducible complexity of design (pp.80–83 / pdf 82–85). The terminal claim: exploring this complexity is "the key goal of phenomenological design thinking" (p.83 / pdf 85).

The structural payoff for Wendt's larger book: the paradox is what *forces* the move beyond a phenomenology of the designer's intentional consciousness toward a post-phenomenology in which problems, solutions, and the systems they inhabit co-constitute one another.

## 2. Section structure (as the chapter itself partitions)

Wendt gives the chapter three internal headings; the analysis below follows them.

1. **"The Paradox of Design"** (pp.72–75 / pdf 74–77) — sets up design thinking + lean startup, states the paradox, diagnoses the failure of each half, and grounds the paradox in non-dualist philosophy (hermeneutic circle, Saussure, Schön).
2. **"Questioning Design Dualisms"** (pp.75–82 / pdf 77–84) — the longest section. Argues that design is not always problem-solving; that the "dichotomy" is not a dichotomy; deploys Cross/MacCormac on the design brief, the Cartesian "waterfall," praxis-based design and rapid prototyping, the analytic/abductive (present/future) split, Dorst's co-evolution and emergent bridge, the paradox-as-antinomy reframe, Morozov on solutionism, and Basalla on technological evolution.
3. **Chapter close** (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85) — Nelson & Stolterman's twelve design paradoxes (antinomies), the warning against collapsing any pole, and Latour on design's failure to "draw together" the full complexity of a thing.

## 3. Core concept: the problem-solution paradox

Wendt's own formulation: the paradox "states that we cannot think about solutions until we understand the problem, and we cannot understand a problem until we think about solutions" (p.73 / pdf 75). He treats this as the *meta example of a wicked problem* (p.72 / pdf 74) — a tension "inherent in all design disciplines" that "defies traditional conceptions of solutions" (p.72 / pdf 74).

He anatomizes the two halves:

- **First half (the familiar, linear half).** Good process defines a problem space before designing solutions, moving "linearly from understanding to framing to ideation to validation" (p.73 / pdf 75). Two flaws: it assumes a *final answer* waits at the end of the understanding phase (whereas design is abductive — designers seek "the many truths" appropriate to specific contexts, not Truth) (p.73 / pdf 75); and it assumes a problem space can be grasped *divorced from* potential solutions, leaving no room to explore solutions' effects on the system — making it a merely *theoretical* stance (p.73 / pdf 75).
- **Second half (the praxis half).** It "solves these issues" only by *reversing* them: it explores possibilities via prototypes but discards the valuable insight that theoretical work yields; using solutions to understand a problem is "a purely practical approach" that can be misread as an excuse to skip the complex observation of behavioral systems (pp.73–74 / pdf 75–76).

Crucially, neither design thinking ("the process is not as linear as it might seem") nor lean ("prototypes account for a praxis-based process, eliminating upfront research") escapes the paradox; each rebuttal "only account[s] for half of the paradox" (p.74 / pdf 76). Wendt picks these two not because they are *en vogue* but because they *tacitly attempt* to address the paradox and their assumptions "extend far beyond their processes" (p.74 / pdf 76).

> RO-hook: the paradox' structure — problem and solution mutually constituting, neither prior — rhymes with the actualization-of-desire chain's refusal of a fixed "first" term: the object of desire and the act that pursues it co-specify, much as problem and solution do here. (anticipatory-application; not Wendt's claim.)

## 4. Core concept: design as non-dualist (the dissolution of the dichotomy)

Wendt's positive thesis is that the "problem-solution dichotomy is *not* a dichotomy" (p.75 / pdf 77). Problems and solutions are "antithetical and relationally linear" *only* under "the most strict dualist frameworks," and only in those models do designers "identify a problem and apply a solution to it" (p.75 / pdf 77). His non-dualist alternative "sees design as more complex than a problem-solution equation," holding that "problems and solutions are wrapped up in one another, and that we can only understand one through the lens of the other" (p.75 / pdf 77).

Two qualifications he insists we keep (p.75 / pdf 77): (1) problems are *not always user-centric* — design teams also work business problems (low sales, competition), and the user/business overlap is not guaranteed; (2) the dichotomy is "convenient" but not a real dichotomy. He concedes design is *not always* about solving problems: much of the designed world is profit-driven, meeting "a previously unmet need in the market" or manufacturing felt need "through the magic of marketing," the paradigm case being the 2007 iPhone and the iTunes/App-Store ecosystem — Apple "was not solving a problem" but meeting a "previously-unarticulated need" (pp.75–76 / pdf 77–78).

The diagnostic context: modern experience design shows *heightened* dualism because commercial clients are "hyper-concentrated on a solution (or even have a solution in mind before design begins)," so agencies reinforce what the client wants rather than the best solution; Wendt's aim is to "recontextualize the problem-solution relationship within experience design to decrease this urge" (p.76 / pdf 78).

## 5. The critique of linearity: Cartesian "waterfall" vs. thinking-and-making

Through architect Richard MacCormac (quoted via Nigel Cross), Wendt fixes the central reversal: the design brief "is not a specification for a solution, but the starting point for an exploration" (p.76 / pdf 78). Formulating a problem space is therefore "not a step in a process but rather an emergent, constantly evolving, reflective activity"; one does not move from problem to solution — designers *discover*, or at least *evolve*, the problem space "in the process of creating solutions." This is "the combination of thinking and making" (pp.76–77 / pdf 78–79).

The foil is the **Cartesian / "waterfall" method** (pp.77 / pdf 79): the designer withdraws from the situation to analyze it "objectively," planning and mapping intentions later executed in a separate phase. Wendt's charge is that waterfall "creates the illusion of scientific certainty," holds a theoretical perspective "for much too long," and lets teams finalize theories and promise deliverables to clients "long before that vision is ever enacted." The buried premise — that "the performance of theory is not necessary for the validation of that theory" — is "consistent with Cartesian philosophy, but completely at odds with phenomenological design thinking" (p.77 / pdf 79).

## 6. Prototyping and sketching as externalized thought

Wendt rereads **praxis-based design** (of which lean "might be one"), which emphasizes the interaction of "internal" and "external" as the basis for design; **rapid prototyping** is its primary instance (p.77 / pdf 79). The prototype is "a disposable, fickle object used... specifically to learn something" — "a pseudo-object designers use to think," "the externalization of thought into the material world in order to think beyond the head" (p.77 / pdf 79). As speech both connects individuals and "creates its own space of truth," prototypes connect designers and users while "creating an emergent space independent of designer and user" (p.77 / pdf 79).

Cross supplies the supporting passage: sketching/drawing/modeling puts the designer "into the design situation" and supports the "dialogue... between problem and solution," because there is "a cognitive limit to the amount of complexity that can be handled internally" (pp.77–78 / pdf 79–80). Wendt reads the externalized artifact as a "materialized speech object" serving at least two purposes — assisting the act of thinking, and facilitating conversation (designer↔situation, designer↔user, designer↔designer) — and, "beyond these immediate functions," allowing "the exploration of potentiality" (p.78 / pdf 80).

That **potentiality** is "crucial to abductive reasoning, and therefore to design thinking": designers must hold both present (analytical reasoning) and future (abduction), because "technological shaping of the lifeworld happens in terms of *possible* technical mediations, not just actual" ones (p.78 / pdf 80). Science fiction, R&D, speculative design, design fictions, even tech advertising perpetuate "an orientation toward potential technological scenarios" of which designers must be "hyper-aware" (p.78 / pdf 80). Wendt aligns this with Roger Martin's **reliability vs. validity** distinction: quantitative data yields "the illusion of certainty" and only confirms "what one already knows," not the future; Martin locates design thinking at "the intersection of valid and reliable thinking" (p.78 / pdf 80).

> RO-hook: prototype-as-thinking ("think beyond the head," an emergent space independent of maker and user) parallels the ontology's externalized, world-involving cognition — the artifact functions like an *equipmental* mediator through which the designer's directedness becomes determinate. (anticipatory-application; not Wendt's claim.)

## 7. Co-evolution and the emergent bridge (Dorst)

The positive model arrives through **Kees Dorst**. Dorst names "a design paradox," exploring the prototype "as a means of discovering possibilities," and stressing that "the course of the problem-solving process and the very structure of the ill-structured problem are determined by the possibilities for action that the problem solver considers" — the "neat... design process model loses most of its value" once "the adoption of a prototype" intervenes (pp.78–79 / pdf 80–81). Wendt distills the heart of the paradox: "the complete understanding of a design problem is impossible before solution exploration"; "only solutions allow us to formulate and articulate the problem space" (p.79 / pdf 81).

Dorst's key passage (quoted by Wendt) defines **co-evolution**: creative design develops "together both the formulation of a problem and ideas for a solution," iterating analysis/synthesis/evaluation across two notional "spaces" — *problem space* and *solution space* — to "generate a matching problem-solution pair, through a co-evolution"; the spaces are "evolving, and are unstable until (temporarily) fixed by an emergent bridge, which identifies a problem-solution pairing" (p.79 / pdf 81). The "uneasy conclusion": in describing design "we cannot presuppose that there is something like a set 'design problem' at any point in the design process" (p.79 / pdf 81).

Wendt then draws the practical edge: because there is "no hard separation between the design space and solution space," and because business stakeholders "couldn't care less about abductive thinking" but "definitely care about risk mitigation," the merger of problem/solution spaces, praxis-based design, and an anti-dualist mindset are reframed as "means of managing risk" (pp.79–80 / pdf 81–82). Yet he concedes the move is "inherently risky" and resistant to project plans and budgets, and that experience designers "have thus far failed to fully explore this co-existence and co-construction of problems and solutions, especially with commercial clients" (p.80 / pdf 82).

## 8. The paradox as antinomy; solutionism; technological evolution

Given its nature, the paradox "is not something to be solved as a common problem might be" — it is "a necessary one — an antinomy of sorts," and is *only* a paradox "if we begin from the assumption that the movement from problem to solution is linear" (p.80 / pdf 82). Reframed in light of phenomenology, "the two sides... are not necessarily at odds"; the "solution to the problem of the paradox is more like a rethinking of the problem" (p.80 / pdf 82). Dorst's gloss (quoted) treats a paradox as a complex statement of "two or more conflicting statements" all of which are "true or valid, but... cannot be combined," requiring "a redefinition of the problematic situation in order to create a solution" — illustrated by a product that must be present to function at one moment and invisible/space-free at another (pp.80–81 / pdf 82–83).

Wendt's reframing task: "to formulate a non-dualist theory of design... thus rendering the paradox moot," whose "first step... might be to rethink the nature of a solution" (p.81 / pdf 83). Here he enlists **Evgeny Morozov's "solutionism"** — the tendency to treat "the world as a problem to be solved" (p.81 / pdf 83). Heeding Morozov, designers could concentrate on "only the most important problems — the so-called wicked problems" that demand multi-organizational time and money; but capitalism keeps this "mostly an ideal," since tech-industry experience designers are "more concerned with commercial returns than with creating actual change" (p.81 / pdf 83). Orienting design toward a "less dualist framework is in-itself a wicked problem," requiring "a radical shift in the economics of who finances design" (p.81 / pdf 83). Wendt is explicit about epistemic humility: he does "not claim to have the answer" but believes phenomenology can yield "a non-dualist — or at least 'less dualist' — theory of design" to counter "solutionism and pure profit-driven design practices" (pp.81–82 / pdf 83–84). Profit per se is not the problem; its becoming "the overwhelming norm" shifts the "design zeitgeist" so that "solutionism runs rampant" and we get "isolated products designed to solve a problem that no one really has" — or worse, "both the creation and solution of a problem for profit" (p.82 / pdf 84).

**George Basalla** is cited for the "fallacy in thinking about technological objects as isolated solutions to discrete problems," and for a Darwinian-evolutionary theory in which technologies become "physical manifestations of how humans choose to organize and extract meaning from their world" (p.82 / pdf 84). Wendt qualifies Basalla as "a bit extreme" — he would not say solutions *never* map to discrete problems — but affirms that normalized solution-based thinking "creates problems for design as a means of shaping the world," and that phenomenology's "focus on holistic experience" lets designers attend to "how individuals experience multiple political, social, and economic systems" (p.82 / pdf 84).

## 9. Twelve design antinomies (Nelson & Stolterman) and Latour

The chapter closes with **Nelson & Stolterman's** list of twelve paradoxes, which Wendt prefers to read as **antinomies** — "contradictions that *must* be," not to be solved (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85): design is *non-attachment and total engagement; flux and permanence; knowing and naivete; experience and fresh eyes; collaboration and solitude; process and structure; cyclic and episodic; control and uncontrollable; unique and universal; infinite and finite; timeless and temporal; splendor and evil* (p.82 / pdf 84). Collapsing any pole is disastrous — pure "design is control" yields "controlled experiments... and a constant repetition of sameness"; pure "design is naivete" means designers "would never get anything done"; "these truths need their antitheses to function" (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85). And the poles "never remain static": there is no "optimal spot... between their poles," because that point "is always shifting depending on the context" (p.83 / pdf 85).

These antinomies "point out the fundamental complexity of design," which designers "often attempt to push away in lieu of focusing on individual practices" (p.83 / pdf 85). **Bruno Latour** seals the point: design has perfected the "practical skills for drawing objects" (architectural drawing, blueprints, scale models, prototyping), yet those drawings have always lacked "an impression of the controversies and the many contradicting stakeholders"; objects "are always assemblies, 'gatherings' in Heidegger's meaning of the word... *things* and *Dinge*," and we remain "utterly unable to draw together... what a thing in all of its complexity, is" (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85). Wendt's last line: "Exploring this complexity is the key goal of phenomenological design thinking" (p.83 / pdf 85).

> RO-hook: Latour's "gatherings"/*Dinge* and Nelson & Stolterman's antinomies model the *thing* as an irreducible assembly — congenial to the ontology's treatment of the environment/object as a relational field rather than a discrete target, which is precisely how a VLE should be modeled (system-effects, not point-solutions). (anticipatory-application; not Wendt's claim.)

## 10. Interlocutors and what Wendt draws from each

- **Kees Dorst** — *design paradox; problem space / solution space; co-evolution; emergent bridge.* Wendt's principal positive source: co-evolution and the emergent bridge give him the model of mutually-constituting problem/solution, and the "no set design problem" conclusion (pp.78–80 / pdf 80–82).
- **Nigel Cross** (quoting **Richard MacCormac**) — *the brief as a "starting point for an exploration," not a specification; sketching as external store supporting the problem-solution "dialogue."* Source for thinking-and-making and the externalized-artifact account (pp.76–78 / pdf 78–80).
- **Martin Heidegger** — *the hermeneutic circle* (parts depend on whole, whole on parts) and *the artwork as non-"thingly," dependent on the whole system of art*; later, via Latour, *objects as "gatherings"/Dinge.* Anchors the non-dualist lineage and the closing notion of the thing (pp.74–75, 83 / pdf 76–77, 85).
- **Ferdinand de Saussure** — *signs have no meaning in themselves; the "chain of signification."* A second non-dualist precedent: meaning is differential/relational, mirroring problem-solution interdependence (p.74 / pdf 76).
- **Donald Schön** — *design as a "conversation with the situation."* The reflective-practice basis Wendt extends with Cross's externalized artifact (pp.74, 77–78 / pdf 76, 79–80).
- **Horst Rittel & Melvin Webber** — *wicked problems; the antinomies of linear problem→solution movement.* First "allusion" to the paradox; supplies the "wicked problem" frame (pp.74, 78–79 / pdf 76, 80–81).
- **Roger Martin** — *reliability vs. validity; design thinking at their intersection.* Used to defend orientation toward potentiality/future against data-driven "illusion of certainty" (p.78 / pdf 80).
- **Evgeny Morozov** — *"solutionism."* Source for "rethink the nature of a solution" and the critique of treating every encounter as a problem to be solved (p.81 / pdf 83).
- **George Basalla** — *technological evolution; the fallacy of objects as isolated solutions to discrete problems.* Supports anti-isolation of solutions, though Wendt judges him "a bit extreme" (p.82 / pdf 84).
- **Harold Nelson & Erik Stolterman** — *twelve design paradoxes/antinomies.* The closing taxonomy of design's irreducible complexity (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85).
- **Bruno Latour** — *objects as assemblies/"gatherings"; design's inability to "draw together" the controversies and stakeholders of a thing.* The capstone on complexity and the limits of representation (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85).
- **(Background) Design thinking & lean startup** — *not authors but the two methodological frameworks* whose shared, unresolved tension launches the chapter (pp.72–74 / pdf 74–76).

## 11. Explicit design principles (DESIGN-PRINCIPLE)

These are stated or directly entailed by Wendt's prose in this chapter; each is a normative instruction for designing experiences (and, by extension under the project's lens, virtual learning environments).

1. **Resist the urge to seek final answers.** Design is abductive — pursue the many context-appropriate truths, not Truth (p.73 / pdf 75).
2. **Treat the brief as a starting point for exploration, not a specification for a solution** (p.76 / pdf 78).
3. **Do not separate problem-understanding from solution-making; discover/evolve the problem space *through* creating solutions** ("thinking and making") (pp.76–77 / pdf 78–79).
4. **Reject the waterfall/Cartesian stance** that withdraws to plan and validates theory without enactment; enact early because theory's "performance" *is* necessary to its validation in phenomenological design (p.77 / pdf 79).
5. **Use prototypes/sketches as externalized thinking** — disposable pseudo-objects "to think beyond the head" and to open an emergent space independent of designer and user (pp.77–78 / pdf 79–80).
6. **Design for potentiality, not only the actual** — orient toward *possible* technical mediations (speculative design, design fictions) and hold present-analysis and future-abduction together (p.78 / pdf 80).
7. **Seek the intersection of valid and reliable thinking** (Martin) — do not let quantitative "illusion of certainty" foreclose the future (p.78 / pdf 80).
8. **Aim to generate a matching problem-solution pair via co-evolution, fixed only temporarily by an emergent bridge** — do not presuppose a fixed "design problem" (p.79 / pdf 81).
9. **Recast anti-dualist, praxis-based design as risk management** to make the stance legible to commercial stakeholders, and **build the language to articulate co-construction** to clients (pp.79–80 / pdf 81–82).
10. **Treat the paradox as an antinomy to be redefined, not solved** — "rethink the problem" rather than force a linear resolution (pp.80–81 / pdf 82–83).
11. **Rethink the nature of a solution; resist "solutionism"** — reserve effort for genuine (wicked) problems rather than manufacturing problems for profit (pp.81–82 / pdf 83–84).
12. **Do not treat technological objects as isolated solutions to discrete problems**; design for systems and holistic experience across social/political/economic contexts (p.82 / pdf 84).
13. **Hold both poles of each design antinomy; never optimize to a fixed point**, since the right balance shifts with context (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85).
14. **Aim to "draw together" the controversies, stakeholders, and assemblies of a thing** (Latour) — make complexity, not just the object, designable (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85).

## 12. Key terms / concepts (with loci)

- **Problem-solution paradox** — neither problem nor solution is intelligible without the other; the "meta example of a wicked problem" (pp.72–73 / pdf 74–75).
- **Wicked problem** — a problem that "defies traditional conceptions of solutions"; the paradox is its meta-instance (p.72 / pdf 74).
- **Non-dualism** — the philosophical stance (Heidegger, Saussure, Schön) that wholes and parts, problem and solution, co-depend; the chapter's governing commitment (pp.74–75 / pdf 76–77).
- **Hermeneutic circle** — understanding of the whole depends on the parts and vice versa; Wendt's phenomenological warrant for co-evolution (p.74 / pdf 76).
- **Chain of signification** (Saussure) — signs mean only differentially/relationally; a linguistic analogue of problem-solution interdependence (p.74 / pdf 76).
- **Design as a conversation with the situation** (Schön) — designing as ongoing exchange with the environment (pp.74, 77–78 / pdf 76, 79–80).
- **Thinking and making** — problem space is evolved in the act of producing solutions, not analyzed in advance (pp.76–77 / pdf 78–79).
- **Waterfall / Cartesian method** — linear, theory-first, enactment-deferred design; the chapter's foil (p.77 / pdf 79).
- **Praxis-based design** — design grounded in internal/external interaction; rapid prototyping its exemplar (p.77 / pdf 79).
- **Prototype as pseudo-object / externalized thought** — disposable object "to think beyond the head," opening an emergent space (pp.77–78 / pdf 79–80).
- **Potentiality / abduction** — designing for *possible* (not only actual) technical mediations; abduction as the future-facing mode (p.78 / pdf 80).
- **Reliability vs. validity** (Martin) — repeatable data vs. valid future-insight; design thinking at their intersection (p.78 / pdf 80).
- **Problem space / solution space** (Dorst) — the two co-evolving "notional spaces" of creative design (p.79 / pdf 81).
- **Co-evolution** (Dorst) — joint, iterative development of problem and solution toward a matching pair (p.79 / pdf 81).
- **Emergent bridge** (Dorst) — the temporary fix that identifies a problem-solution pairing amid unstable spaces (p.79 / pdf 81).
- **Antinomy** — a necessary, unsolvable contradiction requiring redefinition rather than resolution (pp.80, 82–83 / pdf 82, 84–85).
- **Solutionism** (Morozov) — the disposition to treat the world as a problem to be solved (p.81 / pdf 83).
- **Technological evolution** (Basalla) — technologies as evolved manifestations of human meaning-making, not isolated solutions (p.82 / pdf 84).
- **Design antinomies** (Nelson & Stolterman) — twelve paradoxical pole-pairs naming design's irreducible complexity (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85).
- **Gatherings / *Dinge*** (Latour, via Heidegger) — objects as assemblies of controversies and stakeholders that design cannot yet "draw together" (pp.82–83 / pdf 84–85).

## 13. Connective summary (why this unit matters to the lens)

This unit is the chapter where Wendt earns the move from *designer-centered* phenomenology to a *relational* post-phenomenology: once problem and solution co-evolve and the "thing" is a gathering of stakeholders and controversies, design can no longer be modeled as an intentional subject applying solutions to objects. For the project's VLE work, the operative transfer is twofold — (a) a VLE is not a point-solution to a discrete learning "problem" but a system whose introduction "changes the conditions in which the problem exists" (p.74 / pdf 76, paraphrased), so evaluation should track *system-effects on experience*, not problem-closure; and (b) prototyping/co-evolution licenses an iterative, enactment-first VLE design method in which the "learning problem" is itself discovered through built, disposable environments. Both transfers are anticipatory-application of the lens, not claims Wendt makes about virtual learning environments.
