# Von Uexküll — Phase 0: Global Overview & Unitization Confirmation

## Book Overview

**Full title**: Jakob von Uexküll, *A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans* (1934) with *A Theory of Meaning* (1940), trans. Joseph D. O'Neil. University of Minnesota Press, 2010.
**Original**: *Streifzüge durch die Umwelten von Tieren und Menschen* (1934); *Bedeutungslehre* (1940).
**PDF**: 281 pages. **Offset**: book page + 4 = PDF page (uniform).

The volume contains two distinct but related works by the Baltic German biologist Jakob von Uexküll (1864–1944):

1. **A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans** (1934): A popular-scientific "travelogue" through the perceptual worlds (*Umwelten*) of various animals — from ticks and paramecia to dogs and jackdaws. Establishes the core vocabulary: Umwelt, Merkwelt/Wirkwelt, Funktionskreis, Merkmal/Wirkmal. Written with Georg Kriszat (illustrations). 16 chapters, ~97 book pages (pp. 41–137).

2. **A Theory of Meaning** (*Bedeutungslehre*, 1940): A theoretical extension of Umwelt theory into biosemiotics and natural philosophy. Introduces meaning (*Bedeutung*), carriers of meaning (*Bedeutungsträger*), dwelling-shell (*Wohnhülle*), body-plan (*Bauplan*), and the composition theory of nature (*Kompositionslehre*). Pervasive musical metaphor: organisms as instruments, nature as composer, environments as symphonies. 12 chapters, ~70 book pages (pp. 139–208).

The volume also includes substantial paratexts: Dorion Sagan's Introduction (34 pp., biosemiotic and Heideggerian framing), O'Neil's Translator's Introduction (6 pp., key translation decisions), and Geoffrey Winthrop-Young's Afterword (35 pp., media-theory reception history).

## Core Claims (10 theses)

1. **Every organism is a subject, not an object.** Biology must treat animals as subjects with perceptual worlds, not as machines (Foreword, pp. 41–43).

2. **Each subject has its own Umwelt.** The Umwelt is a closed, self-contained perceptual world composed of Merkwelt (perception world) and Wirkwelt (effect world), connected by the Funktionskreis (functional cycle) (Introduction, pp. 44–52).

3. **Without a living subject, there can be neither space nor time.** Space and time are subject-relative; each organism has its own spatial and temporal structure, including a species-specific "moment" (Introduction, p. 48; Perception Time, pp. 70–72).

4. **All animal subjects are inserted into their environments to the same degree of perfection.** The simplest organism's Umwelt is as complete as the most complex one's. Poverty of features enables certainty of action (Introduction, p. 46).

5. **The same object takes on different meanings in different Umwelten.** The oak tree appears as entirely different "objects" to the forester, the fox, the ant, the bark beetle, etc. Properties of things are perception marks imprinted by subjects (Same Subject, pp. 126–132; Summary, p. 201).

6. **Carriers of meaning are objective yet Umwelt-relative.** Things carry meaning (*Bedeutung*), but that meaning is constituted only within a specific functional cycle. Meaning is neither purely subjective nor purely objective (Carriers of Meaning, pp. 139–145).

7. **The dwelling-shell is the extension of the organism into its environment.** The Wohnhülle is the totality of meaning-relations that surround and constitute the subject's lived space (Environment and Dwelling-shell, pp. 146–149).

8. **The Bauplan (body-plan) is shaped by meaning-motifs from other organisms.** The spider's web is fly-like because the fly's primal score (*Urpartitur*) shapes the spider's Bauplan. Form and meaning are intertwined across species (Spider's Web, pp. 157–160; Composition Theory, pp. 171–181).

9. **Nature is a composer, not a mechanism.** The Kompositionslehre replaces mechanistic and Darwinian explanations: organisms are instruments; their environments are melodies; Nature composes the whole (Composition Theory, pp. 171–181; Summary, pp. 200–208).

10. **All environments are composed into a "world-score" beyond time and space.** The *Foray*'s Kantian perspectivism becomes, in *Theory of Meaning*, a quasi-transcendental vision of Nature as a unified compositional totality (Summary, p. 208).

## Candidate Tensions (9)

1. **Umwelt vs. Umgebung** — The Umwelt is primary and self-contained, yet the Umgebung is needed as the shared substrate from which different Umwelten are "cut." Uexküll never resolves how a non-perspectival Umgebung is accessible.

2. **Subject vs. Machine** — The Foreword's central polemic: animals are subjects, not objects/machines. Yet Uexküll describes the functional cycle in quasi-mechanical terms (receptors, effectors, reflex arcs reinterpreted).

3. **Mechanism vs. Planmäßigkeit** — Anti-Darwinian, anti-mechanistic, but not straightforwardly vitalist. Purposiveness (*Planmäßigkeit*) is structural, not supernatural — yet the musical metaphor pushes toward a cosmic composer.

4. **Darwinism vs. Design** — Uexküll explicitly rejects natural selection's power to explain functional harmony (Conclusion; Winthrop-Young Afterword). But his alternative (Nature as composer) risks teleological overcommitment.

5. **Subject as Perceiver vs. Subject as Object** — The same entity is constituted differently in different Umwelten. The oak is five different "objects" for five species. Ontological perspectivism without relativism — each perspective is complete and real.

6. **Meaning as Imposed vs. Meaning as Encountered** — Carriers of meaning are "objectively" present yet only mean within a specific Umwelt. The meaning-germ (*Bedeutungskeim*) is prior to the organism, yet only realized through organismic form.

7. **Simplicity vs. Completeness** — The tick's three-stimulus Umwelt is as "perfect" as the human's. Poverty enables certainty. This challenges any hierarchy of organisms.

8. **Musical Metaphor vs. Biological Mechanism** — The pervasive musical language (tone, score, counterpoint, composition, melody, carillon, clavier) coexists with the technical vocabulary of receptors, effectors, and functional cycles. The metaphor does real theoretical work but blurs the line between analogy and explanation.

9. **Kantian Perspectivism vs. Transcendental Nature** — *Foray* is neo-Kantian (each subject constitutes its own world). *Theory of Meaning* shifts toward a transcendental Nature that composes all worlds into a "world-score beyond time and space." The Kantian critique of metaphysics sits uneasily with this cosmic-compositional vision.

## Translator's Introduction — Key Translation Decisions

From O'Neil's Translator's Introduction (pp. 35–40):

| German | O'Neil's choice | Rationale |
|--------|----------------|-----------|
| Umwelt | "environment" | Literal; echoes Luhmann's system/environment distinction |
| Umgebung | "surroundings" | Distinguished from Umwelt |
| Merkmal | "perception mark" | Not "feature" (standard German meaning); emphasizes subject's active perception |
| Merkzeichen | "perception sign" | Distinct from Merkmal: Zeichen has semiotic referent, Mal does not |
| Wirkmal | "effect mark" | Parallel to perception mark |
| Merken | perception / noticing | Active attention, not passive cognition (Wahrnehmung) |
| Wirken | producing effects | Active production, not mere causation |
| Natur (capitalized) | "Nature" | Quasi-subject, the "composer"; distinct from lowercase "nature" (particular nature of a thing) |
| Bildung / Gestaltung | development / formation | Close synonyms; Bild=image, Gestalt=figure/form |
| Klaviatur | "clavier" | Keyboard metaphor; cells as keys, Nature plays |
| Glockenspiel | "carillon" | Cell-bells contributing to melodies |

**O'Neil's key insight**: Uexküll's Merk-/Wirk- prefix system creates a systematic vocabulary that resists smooth English translation. O'Neil prioritizes consistency and systemacity over readability, preserving the architecture of the German.
