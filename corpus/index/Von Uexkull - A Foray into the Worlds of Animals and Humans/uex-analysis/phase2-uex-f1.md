## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### Environment Spaces (pp. 53–62)
1. **Perception marks as subject-relative** (pp. 53–54)
   - Gourmet/tick analogy: each subject selects biologically significant perception marks from surroundings
   - "Every subject spins out, like the spider's threads, its relations to certain qualities of things" (p. 53)
   - Perception signs are always spatially and temporally bound (p. 54)
2. **Critique of universal space** (p. 54)
   - Illusion of one shared space for all beings
   - Every human lives in three interpenetrating spaces
3. **Effect space (Wirkraum)** (pp. 54–58)
   - Built from directional steps (~2 cm minimum); six directions in three pairs (p. 54)
   - Coordinate system fixed to the head: three planes (right/left, up/down, front/back) (pp. 55–56)
   - Cyon's discovery: semicircular canals as organ of three-dimensionality (p. 56)
   - "House-door compass": semicircular canals track cumulative movement for homing (pp. 57–58)
   - Bee hive experiment: bees navigate by effect space, not vision (pp. 57–58)
   - Limpet homing: returns to bed via effect-space compass despite primitive eyes (p. 58)
4. **Tactile space (Tastraum)** (pp. 60–61)
   - Built from places (Orte), not directional steps
   - Weber's compass experiment: local signs (Lokalzeichen) vary by skin region (p. 60)
   - Subject confers mosaic of place upon objects (pp. 60–61)
   - Nocturnal and cave-dwelling animals live predominantly in tactile space (p. 61)
5. **Visual space (Sehraum)** (pp. 61–63)
   - Built from visual elements (retinal units), each assigned a local sign → a place (p. 61)
   - Objects shrink with distance until vanishing into a single place (p. 61)
   - Visual vs. tactile space competition: cup appears to grow visually but not tactilely (p. 62)
   - Mosaic fineness depends on number of visual elements; fly's eye → coarsened world (pp. 62–63)
   - Spider's web invisible to its prey because of the fly's coarse visual mosaic (p. 63)

### The Farthest Plane (pp. 63–69)
1. **Definition of the farthest plane** (p. 63)
   - Visual space is walled off by an impenetrable boundary: the horizon / farthest plane (fernste Ebene)
   - Sun, moon, stars on same plane with no depth differentiation
2. **Subjectivity of the farthest plane** (pp. 63–67)
   - Uexküll's typhus anecdote: farthest plane at 20 meters, objects not farther but only smaller/larger (p. 66)
   - Lens accommodation: within 10 m, lens muscles provide near/far directional signs; beyond 10 m, originally only size changes (p. 66)
   - Helmholtz childhood anecdote: boy sees workers as dolls because church is in his farthest plane (pp. 66–67)
   - Distance signs gradually push farthest plane to 6–8 km in adults (p. 67)
3. **Farthest plane in animals** (pp. 67–69)
   - Difficult to determine experimentally when object is "nearer" vs. merely "larger" (p. 67)
   - Housefly farthest plane ~0.5 m (hand-catching experiments) (p. 68)
   - Fly circling chandelier: Exner's hypothesis that rhabdom depth functions as accessory lens (pp. 68–69)
4. **Soap-bubble conclusion** (p. 69)
   - Every animal enclosed in a soap bubble that closes off its visual space
   - Each bubble shelters different places and contains the directional planes of effect space

## 4. Main Positions / Claims

| Claim | Evidence type | Strength | Page ref |
|-------|-------------|----------|----------|
| There is no single universal space; every human lives in three interpenetrating spaces (effect, tactile, visual) | Philosophical argument + phenomenological demonstration | Strong — argued from structural analysis of perception | p. 54 |
| Effect space is structured by a coordinate system grounded in the semicircular canals | Empirical (Cyon's experiments on semicircular canals) | Strong — supported by comparative anatomy across species | pp. 55–56 |
| Animals navigate primarily through effect space ("house-door compass"), not vision | Empirical (bee hive displacement, limpet homing, bee antennae removal) | Strong — multiple species, experimental evidence | pp. 57–58 |
| The subject confers spatial properties (mosaic of place) upon objects; space is not in surroundings but given by the subject | Empirical (Weber's compass experiment) + theoretical | Strong — demonstrated for tactile and visual space | pp. 60–61 |
| Visual space is bounded by a farthest plane whose distance is subject-relative and developmentally variable | Empirical (Helmholtz anecdote, Uexküll typhus anecdote, fly catching experiment) | Strong — multiple lines of evidence across species | pp. 63–69 |
| The fineness of the visual mosaic determines how much detail an animal's world contains | Empirical (fly vs. human eye comparison, grid photographs) | Strong — demonstrated with photographic simulations | pp. 62–63 |

## 6. Cross-Pipeline References

### 6a. Heidegger B&T Connections
- **Three interpenetrating spaces** → Heidegger's analysis of spatiality in B&T §§22–24 rejects homogeneous Cartesian space in favor of existentially constituted space (Zuhandenheit structures nearness and region). Uexküll's argument that space is subject-constituted and plural (effect/tactile/visual) provides a biological analogue. [INTERP-high]
- **Soap-bubble metaphor** → Echoes the closed structure of Dasein's world (In-der-Welt-sein); each organism is enclosed in its own spatial totality, paralleling Heidegger's claim that world is always already Dasein's world. [INTERP-high]

| Visual vs. tactile space | Sehraum | Tastraum | contrasts_with | Objects grow smaller in visual space with distance but not in tactile space; "tactile space predominates" when both are engaged (p. 62), creating a hierarchy among supposedly interpenetrating spaces |
| Effect-space compass vs. visual orientation | Haustürenkompass | visual space | contrasts_with | Bees with antennae rely on effect space; without antennae they fall back on vision. The two systems are shown as competing, yet both belong to the same organism's Umwelt |
