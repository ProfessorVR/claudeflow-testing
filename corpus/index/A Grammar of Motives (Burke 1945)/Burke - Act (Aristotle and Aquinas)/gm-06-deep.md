# GM-06 — Part 2.III: Act (Aristotle and Aquinas)

*Book pages 227–274 (PDF 248–295). Pentad term featured: Act. Philosophic school featured: scholastic realism (Aristotle + Aquinas). Deep-analysis narrative; paraphrastic throughout.*

## (i) Chapter overview and role in the volume

The third chapter of Part 2 is the strongest exegetical anchor in *A Grammar of Motives* to the existing corpus indices for Aristotle, the Aristotelian *phantasia* secondary literature, the Bowin work on motion and time, and (anticipatorily) the Heideggerian readings of Aristotelian *pathē* in the *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* (BCAP). Burke here assigns the pentad term *Act* to scholastic realism, and he does so on a grammatical rather than a doctrinal basis. The argument is that any vocabulary which derives motivation from the actualization of a potency in accordance with the form proper to a kind features Act as its locus of motive; realism is the family of vocabularies that proceeds in this way. Burke develops the claim in six interlocking subsections: a primary exegetical pass through Aristotle and Aquinas, a methodological apparatus around the so-called pathetic fallacy, a long subsection on incipient and delayed action that draws on Richards, Mead, and Korzybski, a subsection contrasting realist family-vocabularies with nominalist aggregate-vocabularies, a further pass on act and potency that culminates in Burke's formula that every pentad ratio is reversible and has potential-to-actual form, and a closing "Psychology of Action" subsection that re-maps the entire chapter as a typology of four motivational pairs and pivots the pentad-tour from Act to Agency by way of an Indo-European philological argument concerning the middle voice. This is the chapter in which Burke's general operationalization of Act is constructed; consequently it is also the chapter through which every Heideggerian and Aristotelian cross-pipeline edge in the dissertation runs.

## (ii) Aristotle and Aquinas: the chapter's exegetical strategy

Burke opens with a citation chain drawn from Baldwin's *Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology*, accumulating attestations to the proposition that in Aristotelian usage things count as real to the degree that they are in *energeia* — actuality — rather than mere potency. The chain proceeds through Aquinas's gloss that a form, of itself, makes a thing be in act; through the Thomistic formula that treats existence as the act of an essence; through the hylomorphic definition of soul as the act of an organic body capable of life; and through Gilson's reading of Thomistic existence as an act rather than a thing (with Burke's characteristic sociological footnote locating this act-vocabulary's appeal in feudal status-maintenance). The opening's load-bearing exegetical formula is the agent-act ratio that Burke draws from the *Summa Contra Gentiles*: every thing acts according as it is in act. Agent and act are mutually constituting; this is realism's primary ratio.

Burke then performs the chapter's most explicit cross-walk. Reading Aquinas reading Aristotle in the Everyman's Library commentary on the four causes, he aligns Aristotelian aetiology with the dramatist pentad: material cause = Scene, efficient cause = Agent, final cause = Purpose, formal cause = Act, with agency incidental in Aristotle (gathered under the Organon-as-instrument heading and under Aquinas's Ibn-Sina expansion of the efficient cause into an auxiliary or secondary efficient cause that maps onto the agent–agency overlap). The mapping is confirmed by negative way through the medieval scholastic hexameter — *quis, quid, ubi, quibus auxiliis, cur, quo modo, quando* — which Burke distributes across the pentad such that *quid* (whatness, *quidditas*) is left precisely for Act. The exegetical move is decisive for the volume because it converts an Aristotelian apparatus into a pentadic one without remainder.

The exegesis then turns to the *Poetics*. Burke reads the designation of plot as the foremost element of tragedy, and the formula that tragedy is an imitation not of persons but of action and life, as evidence that Aristotelian poetics is structurally act-featuring. He aligns Aristotle's six tragic elements with the five pentad terms: plot = Act, character = Agent, thought = Purpose, melody and diction = Agency, spectacle = Scene-as-scenic-agency. The conclusion of the opening subsection is that Aristotle neutralized Platonist realism by denying the Ideas and God any generative function; Aristotle's teleology has each kind of thing striving to be perfectly the kind of thing it is — the entelechy seed — and this *in rebus* placement of the universal allowed inductive study of genera and species. Aquinas could then adopt the Aristotelian vocabulary, in Burke's characterization, as a rationalizer of the Christian drama.

The Aquinas exegesis carries on through the chapter and centers on three texts: the *Summa Contra Gentiles*, the commentaries on Aristotle's *Metaphysics* and four causes, and (mediated through Gilson) the *Summa Theologica*'s existence-essence distinction. Burke uses Aquinas's two-directions formula — God present in things both after the manner of an efficient cause and as the object of operation in the operator — to resolve, or at least to articulate, the Aristotelian-mystical tension between God-as-source and God-as-end. The dictionary's compressed report that existence is to essence as act is to potency furnishes the chapter, by way of Bréhier, with a scholastic proportion that Burke deploys later to interrogate the matter-potentiality equation.

## (iii) The pathetic fallacy: motion-action ambiguity as watershed

Burke pivots from primary exegesis to his first methodological apparatus by way of an Aristotelian admission, in *Metaphysics* IX, that *energeia* is thought to be identical with movement though it has in the main been extended from movements to other things. Burke notes the Greek-root semantic terrain (*ergon* as work or deed; *pragma* as thing-to-be-accomplished; *chrēma* as thing-to-be-used) and observes that pragmatism, viewed pentadically, might more accurately be chrematism.

The methodological apparatus proper takes its name from Ruskin and is developed against the background of Josephine Miles's monograph on nineteenth-century usage. Miles's restricted definition tracks the attribution of human feelings to non-human objects — trees as gay, mountains as mourning — and Burke notes that, on his pentadic analysis, this restricted definition tracks the scene-agent ratio, the lyric ratio in which properties of agents are projected onto scenic objects. Burke then extends the term to cover a broader phenomenon: the action-motion ambiguity. Wheat that is tossed in the wind exhibits motion; wheat that tosses its head in the wind has been personified into an action. Coleridge's empathy-theory of imagination — what does a shooting star do, move or act — is read as a probe of the same ambiguity. Eliot's mid-century imagery (smoke that slides along streets, windings of violins, light that creeps between shutters) acquires its force, on this reading, by drawing on the action-motion ambiguity as Aristotelian actualization.

The critical move is that the action-motion ambiguity marks a watershed: it divides dramatist from operationalist vocabularies. The realm of motion is the realm of instruments, and an instrument can record nothing in the realm of action — that is, in the realm of ideas — except insofar as the matter is first reduced to motion. Cartesian dualism reappears here as a partition that follows the watershed. Burke notes that naturalist philosophers draw doctrinal sustenance from unrecognized consequences of the pathetic fallacy extended in this manner, and he flags the active or policy-making function integral to any vocabulary of motivation — realistic when it arises from tribal experience (proverbs), tending toward idealism in proportion as it derives from managerial agents.

## (iv) Incipient and delayed action

The chapter's longest subsection (about twelve pages) develops what Burke names the incipient act — and it is the chapter's load-bearing concept-cluster for any cross-pipeline bridge to cognitive science, to a phenomenology of intention, or to the dissertation's affective layer.

Burke opens with I. A. Richards's *Principles of Literary Criticism*. Richards's claim is that every perception probably includes a response in the form of incipient action: we are continually making preliminary adjustments, getting ready to act in one direction or another. On the literary side, Richards prizes the case in which symbolic representation arouses imaginal activities and tendencies-to-action while making allowance for the fictional frame — the impulse stays incipient, no overt action is called for, and the maintenance of incipiency is for Richards a mark of intellectual refinement. Burke immediately names what he takes to be the central ambiguity that Richards's formulation makes available. The incipient act is double: an attitude can be a substitute for an act, or an attitude can be the first step toward an act, and the same attitude can be both at once. The rhetoric of advertising and propaganda exploits this duality, inducing action through attitude-formation while maintaining the alibi of mere attitude.

The second pillar is George Herbert Mead, in *Mind, Self, and Society* and *The Philosophy of the Act*. Mead's signature formulation, that attitudes are beginnings of acts, gives Burke a second authoritative warrant for placing the incipient act at the center of any social-psychological vocabulary. Burke notes that *The Philosophy of the Act* could nearly be re-titled *The Philosophy of the Attitude* if one bears in mind Mead's concern with the incipient as the introductory rather than the substitutive. Mead distinguishes action from motion but locates his action internally, in the organism encountering resistance — what Burke (drawing on Santayana) calls action-minus or attitude-minus, halfway between motion and action. The Meadian social self emerges as the individual learns to take the attitude of the other and to anticipate object-resistance: the individual preparing to grasp the distant object takes the attitude of resisting his own attitude in grasping. Burke notes the Hegelian-dialectic pattern (Spirit alienated as Nature) lying just under the surface of Mead's formulation. Vocal gesture is, for Mead, the device by which we arouse in ourselves the attitudes language serves to arouse in others.

The third pillar is Korzybski's *Science and Sanity*. Where Richards seeks to keep certain responses incipient and Mead reads attitudes as the beginnings of social action, Korzybski offers a wholesale technique for delaying every response on the grounds that ordinary linguistic explanations are askew. The famous chair example carries the formula: Korzybski sits down, with the usual attitude of delayed action, on a chair that turns out to be frail; the chair collapses but Korzybski, having maintained the delayed-action posture, does not collapse with it. Burke's gloss is Greek-grammatical: by participation in the Korzybski *mathēma*, through the medium of a delayed *poiēma*, we avoid a disconcerting *pathēma*. The triplet *poiēma / pathēma / mathēma* — making/suffering/learning — will recur in the closing subsection as the chapter's master schema for organizing the action-passion pair under temporal sequence.

Burke's critical engagement with Korzybski develops three lines. First, Korzybski works better as a Rhetoric than as a Grammar; the sanity is personal-social-political but the science is physics-physiology, and the criterion by which sanity is judged is not itself grounded in the physical-physiological frame from which Korzybski derives the analytic. Second, Korzybski's signature concept — consciousness of abstracting — is, on Burke's diagnosis, a haphazardly rediscovered aspect of Neo-Platonism, and what is needed is its expansion into a consciousness of dialectic in general. Third, Burke exposes a paradox internal to Korzybski's semantics: from the standpoint of action, the consciousness-of-abstracting is a delay; from the standpoint of motion, however, the body that hosts this delay must be unusually prompt in instantiating the particular nerve, muscle, and endocrine configuration that corresponds to the arrested posture. There is at least as much neural motion in the hesitating body as in the unhesitating one; a state of delayed action cannot be a corresponding state of delayed motion; the mental attitude of arrest must have its own bodily posture. This three-moment paradox — physiological, mental, and a tertium-quid neuro-semantic — exposes a more general truth: action-motion vocabularies cannot be merged without the merger inheriting the ambiguity rather than dissolving it.

Burke then pivots back to Aristotle. The attitude-as-incipient-act is, he argues, a special application of the Aristotelian concept of potentiality, which in *Metaphysics* IX is the reciprocal of actuality and is qualified by the principle that every potency is at one and the same time a potency of the opposite. The hot is potentially the cold; matter, as the realm of potentiality, is in being potentially good potentially bad. Latin inceptive and inchoative verb-forms (*calesco*, *irascor*) confirm the ambiguous-incipient territory in another register. The realm of the incipient is therefore the realm of symbolic action par excellence, because symbolic action exhibits the same ambiguous potentialities as overt practical action: actual conflicts can be transcended in the symbolic realm with results sometimes fatal and sometimes felicitous.

The subsection closes with the image-as-attitude paragraph. Images can have the force of attitudes; lyric structure (which lacks an act in the full dramatic sense) can be analyzed by tracking the progression and development of imagery. Burke's worked example is Shelley's *Ode to the West Wind*, whose five-stanza arc he reads with attention to the abyssal middle stanza's submerged-vegetation imagery; the Wordsworth Westminster Bridge sonnet is then contrasted as a pure lyric of arrest-as-summation. The distinction generated by these readings is that, from the dramatic point of view, the moment of arrest is a pre-act (a preparation for action); from the lyric point of view, it is a summation (transcendence of overt action by symbolically encompassing its end). Hamlet figures here as the limit case, in which Shakespeare transforms preparations-for-action into devices-for-postponing-action and thus nearly dissolves drama into a pre-action that amounts to no action — the opposite pole from O'Neill's *Mourning Becomes Electra*, which dissolves the agent into stimulus-response. Burke generalizes the Hamletic strategy to legislative practice: fact-finding committees serve as devices for postponing the vote, and Hamlet on this reading is the first great liberal.

## (v) Realist family and nominalist aggregate

The next subsection returns to Korzybski's consciousness-of-abstracting but now from a grammatical angle. The Korzybskian ladder — *chair*, *furniture*, *conveniences*, *commodities*, *things*, *objects*, *being* — moves from an aggregate of particulars up through increasingly inclusive abstractions, and Burke classes the method as characteristically scientist and characteristically nominalist (citing Baldwin: nominalism holds that universals have no objective existence or validity but are creations of language for purposes of convenient communication). Realist Grammar works the other way: it begins with a tribal concept and treats individuals as participants in a common substance or element.

The chapter's signature formula follows: realism treats individuals as members of a group, whereas nominalism treats groups as aggregates of individuals. The realistic pattern fits the clan-origin of Greek democracy and the familial pattern of Western feudalism. Plato treats the perfect Idea of a chair as the reality and individuals as imperfectly participating in it; Aristotle, by his theory of the entelechy, achieves the same act-genus-generation tie-up within an uncreated world — each kind of thing strives to be perfectly the kind of thing it is. The scientist explains abstraction through nouns; the dramatist places the stress on Act, which suggests an origin in verbs (run, go, do are abstract and general; nouns and proper names individuate). Realism's key word — being, essence — is itself a verbal noun, which Burke offers as the most abstract and general form of the most abstract and general act.

Burke's gloss on essence-as-a-way — Shakespeare's literary style as his essence-as-poet, *animalitas* and *rationalitas* as Santayana's life of reason — reinforces the verbal-noun-as-most-abstract-act test. Duns Scotus is then introduced as the surface-nominalist who nevertheless retains the tribal-familial pattern: the formula that the humanity in Socrates is not the humanity in Plato (and therefore that Socrates's essence resides in a special *socratitas*) treats the individual as if the individual were itself a genus. The subsection closes by characterizing contemporary scientific realism as variously euphemistic for materialism, pragmatism-like, or jurisprudential, and by warning that nominalism, if carried through consistently, obliterates consubstantiality and leaves a world of individuals united only by monetary symbols and the deceptions of an idealistic rhetoric.

## (vi) Further remarks on act and potency

The next subsection opens with a methodological caveat that Burke's appreciation of Aristotle is itself un-Aristotelian and that his remarks about Aquinas would not satisfy a Thomist. He is reviewing dialectical resources from the dramatist standpoint, not arguing for a traditional application of scholastic realism. Modern science asks how acts are motivated; Aristotelian science tells how motions are activated. Platonism, on this characterization, left motivation in the tribal stage with universals *ante rem* (transcendent; the pure form of a family in heaven, individuals partaking imperfectly on earth). The Aristotelian entelechy resides in sensory things (*in rebus*), and on this basis Burke restates Aristotle's principle that actuality is prior both to potency and to every principle of change.

Aristotle's metaphysics is then characterized as more dialectical than Plato's in the specific sense that, where Plato used dialectic as a means to the end of truth, Aristotle isolated formal logic as a method and also attributed dialectical structure to the very nature of the universe. The form-matter hierarchy is read as a ladder: forms are arranged such that the form of beings at each level constitutes the matter of beings at the next higher level, ascending until one arrives at pure form, the motionless prime mover who moves all else by being loved — the object of desire. Burke's action-motion translation maps the ladder onto a sequence in which each order's motion constitutes the conditions of action for the next higher order (human action depends on animal motion; animal action depends on vegetative motion; vegetative action depends on physical motion).

The reversibility of pure act follows. Motions toward the prime mover may be read as motions derived from it — the final cause folds into the efficient cause — and this reversal is what theologians took over when they restored a generative principle to the Aristotelian God. Mystics distinguish *egressus* from God and *regressus* to God, and Burke reads the oxymoron of these two joined moments as the same reversibility in religious-rhetorical register. Aquinas's two-directions formula — God present in things both as equivocal efficient cause (after the manner of the sun's likeness in the things generated by its power) and as object of operation in the operator — supplies the doctrinal placement.

Burke then takes up matter-as-substrate. The Greek *hypokeimenon* — the placed-beneath, the subject — possesses potentialities that may variously be actualized; Windelband's phrase for matter, an impulse to be formed, characterizes the substratum as potency. The paradox-of-substance shows up here in a grammatical key: God-as-pure-act takes the grammatical form of the passive (object of desire, *erōmenon*), while matter as Windelband's impulse-to-be-formed receives a name that grammatically sounds active. Burke's gloss is that no passivity ever possessed a more active a name than potency. The implicit-to-explicit family of proportions follows: *Deus implicitus / Deus explicitus*, Leibnizian monads as pre-formed possibilities, the modern psychological rephrase of the unconscious as virtually (potentially) the conscious. The Lombroso "criminaloid" and the jurisprudential category of potentialities-for-crime appear as Burke's negative exhibits: examples of bad-faith potentiality-actuality machinery.

The tendency-paragraph develops the social-scientific corollary. Social science cannot do without the notion of tendency (and its companions *caeteris paribus* and *mutatis mutandis*, which Burke treats as Janus-terms), but a tendency is fundamentally ambiguous because a tendency-to-do is also a tendency-not-to-do. The Frank Knight perfect-market example illustrates: the ideal market is a direction toward which Knight would have the actual market tend, and the analyst cannot be embarrassed by the failure of the tendency to actualize because the tendency remains intact in its unactualization. Burke contrasts ambiguous tendency with inevitability, in which the substantiality of an underlying principle merges permanent and changing such that what will be is said in effect already to be. Marxist accounts of proletarian development share this substantiality. The parallel between dramatic suspense (whose ending is implicit in the beginning) and historical inevitability is offered as evidence that formal-substantiality cuts across the literary and historiographic domains.

The Heisenberg digression sharpens the diagnosis. Burke notes that instruments are profoundly Grammatical and cannot escape the paradox-of-substance; the principle that a margin of indeterminacy is inevitable in measurement refines Zeno's paradox but does not entail that indeterminacy is intrinsic to nature itself. Bréhier's scholastic proportion — existence is to essence as act is to potency — is reintroduced. Aristotle's actuality-prior-to-potency is then unfolded in two senses: temporally (one actuality always precedes another in time, ultimately back to the eternal prime mover) and in the form-and-substantiality sense (the man is prior to the boy because the man has attained complete form). *Entelechy* — having its end within itself — is treated as a synonym for actuality, and *teleios* — perfect, complete, having attributes of an end — connects the completed man with the infinite God. The *poiēma* family supplies a productive ambiguity (deed/doing/action; made/work). The subsection closes with Burke's signature formula: every ratio is a formula indicating a transition from one term to another, and the mediated necessarily differs from the immediate as translation differs from original. Members of each pentad ratio are related as potential to actual; since ratios are reversible, the standard ten dyads expand to twenty.

## (vii) Psychology of action

The closing subsection re-maps the entire chapter as a typology of motivational pairs: action-passion, *actus*-status, action-motion, and the potential. Burke describes Spinoza's affections as one classical exhibit of the active/passive sortal apparatus and notes that the active and the passive function as bins into which various kinds of particulars are sorted, with each philosopher's distribution of the bins differing from each other's. A dramatist may select any two ethical motives and enact them as the interrelation and transformation of active and passive principles. Burke remarks at the meta-level that characters rarely speak the dramatist Grammar specifically, but Eliot's St Thomas in *Murder in the Cathedral* is an exception (Burke attributes the explicitness to Eliot's Dantean theological interests), and the turning-wheel image in that play equates action and passion. The Dry Salvages passage on a different kind of motion — that which is only moved and has in it no source of movement — illustrates the action-motion contrast from the lyric side. Lear's complaint of being more sinned against than sinning is read as a complex bit of action-passion grammar in which the ingredients of passion are folded into a sin-grammar with active form.

The *poiēma–pathēma–mathēma* temporal series re-emerges as the master schema. Action organizes resistant factors which call forth passion; a moment of transcendence arises when the sufferer is enabled, through suffering, to see in more comprehensive terms. Christian martyrdom is the paradigm case in which the act of self-sacrifice is identical with the sufferance: the Saint suffers temptation (the *pathēma*), resists (the *poiēma*), and the trial's understanding (the *mathēma*) equips him for martyrdom on a new action-passion level. Burke generalizes gods-as-tribal-motives: where tribal identity is uppermost, supernatural vocabulary feels adequate (guilt as tribal judgment, pursuit by Furies as an external existence), and individualism reverses the development by treating tribal motives as super-ego (with Jung's collective unconscious as a reaffirmation of the tribal stratum). The ruling-passion theory inherited from Tacitus and Theophrastus is described as a productive oxymoron — dominant subjection, sovereign bondage. Ben Jonson's humour-vocabulary is exegeted as a sequence of translations from scenic liquid into bodily liquid into state of mind into dramatic form.

Hegel's Absolute Spirit using blind passions of men as its medium furnishes another exhibit of the action-passion pair on the historical-philosophical scale. Burke then turns to freedom as a dialectical term: Aristotle's freedom (as a liberal art, the science free in the sense that the free man exists for his own sake) is not in opposition to authority but a participation in an authoritative structure (intellect controlling enslaving passions); modern freedom is by contrast the polemic-propagandistic doctrine of partial slaves in partial revolt. The Stoic *hegemonikon* (the rule of reason as the rule of self as the rule of state) is the link-vocabulary, and Rousseau's transposition of the law of necessity-in-things over the caprices-of-other-men marks a partial shattering of the Stoic equation.

The *actus*-status transformation receives its sharpest formulation here: a ruling class can become transformed from a class that does to a class that does not — the substantiality of properties inherits independently of any generating act. Christian doctrine universalizes servitude (all men slaves of God, hence none substantially slaves) and provides the paradoxical vocabulary of *passionals* in which suffering counts as act and willing-one's-subjection makes submission an act because sufferance is an act.

In a single brief paragraph Burke addresses the way modern depth psychology can be re-read in dramatist terms. Frustration, fixation, and the symptom-cluster generally are placed as passion; adjustment and normality as action; sublimation as transcendence; the suppression-vocabularies as the new dialectic of reason as *hegemonikon*. Burke is careful to flag that he is not asking modern psychology to abandon its terms; he is asking that the dramatist exegesis be performed on the existing vocabulary. McDougall's sentiments are placed, on the Burke-and-Mead account, as incipient action. *Burke surveys the psychiatric and depth-psychological literature on action-pathologies at the meta-level only, to show that its terms can be re-mapped onto the four motivational pairs without doctrinal endorsement of any particular clinical theory.*

The chapter's closing pages mount the Indo-European philological argument that pivots the pentad-tour from Act to Agency. Aristotle in *Physics* I.6–7 asks whether the basic principles of nature are two or three or some greater number, and Burke reads the question grammatically — whether the active-passive opposition needs a mediating substratum (the bronze-to-statue, wood-to-bed analogy as potentiality). The philological observation is that in the Indo-European family the passive voice is a late development: originally only active and middle (reflexive) forms existed, and the passive evolved from the middle (Greek middle and passive conjugations are alike in many tenses). Prior to the development of the passive, passive ideas were expressed actively-but-reflexively (action directed by the self upon the self). Passive forms, in Burke's gloss, probably indicate a high degree of development from *actus* to status with a corresponding increase in the sense of mental states. Caldwell and Hemingway are noted, in a brief stylistic observation, as exhibits of post-passive behavioristic narrative. The chapter closes by flagging that the reflexive moves in the direction of means and will be considered under Agency: we have now considered the big three — scene, agent, and act — and shall now consider the remaining two, Agency and Purpose, that draw together in the means-ends relationship.

## (viii) Act-term operationalization for the dissertation

The operationalization the chapter offers is paraphrastically as follows. Act is the pentad term that answers the grammatical question *what was done?*; on the realist construal Burke develops here, an act is an actualization — the bringing of a potentiality (*dynamis*) into a full realization (*energeia*) in accordance with a form proper to its kind. An act has internal directionality (the entelechy formula of having its end within itself); it is reciprocally related to potency (every potency is potency of the opposite); it is to be distinguished from mere motion (which lies in the realm of instruments); and it admits a spectrum of intermediate states — attitude, incipient act, delayed act. The decision rules generated from these formulas distinguish realism (motive derived from actualization in accordance with form-of-kind) from idealism (motive in heavenly Idea, transcendent universal) and locate the action-motion watershed as the diagnostic for whether a vocabulary remains dramatist or has crossed into operationalist scenic-reduction.

For the dissertation, the chapter supplies the strongest single contribution to the affective layer's apparatus: Burke's incipient act is the apparatus that lets game-user-motive analysis treat attitude-formation, plan-formulation pre-execution, and rule-system-induced affective potentialization as a single phenomenon rather than as a sequence of discrete states. Calleja's micro-phase (the three moments of plan-formulation, readiness-to-act, and execution-of-act) is structurally homologous to Burke's incipient/actualized/transcended act; Calleja's microtactic, considered at the limit of moment-of-action, is Burke's incipient act in its temporally compressed form. Korzybski's delayed-response gives the temporal-distance qualification. The mapping to Calleja's Ludic, Narrative, and Kinesthetic dimensions runs through Act = the actualization of plans into executed moves (Ludic), Act = plot as the foremost element (Narrative), and Act = the actually executed move (Kinesthetic). The Uexküllian crosswalk identifies Burke's act with the *Wirkmal* — the effector-side mark that closes the functional circle by altering the environment — and reads Burke's hylomorphic touchstone (the soul as the act of an organic body capable of life) as the deepest anchor for an embodied-affordance reading of the pentad.

## (ix) Exegetical cross-pipeline bridges

This is the only chapter in the *Grammar of Motives* pipeline whose "awaits-ontological-grounding-in" edges to Aristotelian and Thomist targets carry the provenance flag *exegetical-direct* rather than *anticipatory-projection*. Every Heideggerian BCAP edge that runs through Burke's *pathē*-pathway, every Aristotle Complete Works edge from *energeia/dynamis/entelechy*, and every Phantasia Cluster edge from imagination-as-incipient-act runs through this chapter's primary engagement with Aristotle and Aquinas rather than through a dissertation-side projection.

The exegetical-direct anchors are: Burke reads Aristotle on the four causes, on the *Metaphysics* IX potentiality-actuality reciprocity, on the *Poetics*' designation of plot as the foremost element of tragedy, on *De Anima*'s hylomorphic definition of soul (mediated through Aquinas), on *Physics* I.6–7's active/passive/substratum question, on the *Organon* as instrument, and on the entelechy. The Aquinas exegesis runs through the *Summa Contra Gentiles* (the agent-act ratio), the commentaries on the *Metaphysics* and on the four causes, and (mediated through Gilson) the existence-essence distinction.

The anticipatory-projection bridges run, secondarily, to Heidegger BCAP (act-passion grammar / Heidegger on *pathē* as ground-determinations of Dasein; incipient-act ambiguity / Heideggerian *Befindlichkeit*; entelechy / *Worumwillen*); to Calleja's *In-Game* (incipient act / micro-phase; Korzybski delayed-action / temporal-distance microtactic qualification; act-genus-generation tie-up / Ludic-Narrative-Kinesthetic dimensional weaving); to Gross's *Uncomfortable Situations* (incipient-act ambiguity / not-quite-emotion not-quite-physiology framing); to Rickert's *Ambient Rhetoric* (motion-action watershed / environment-as-rhetorical-actor); to Uexküll's *Foray* (act as *Wirkmal*; hylomorphic soul as biosemiotic closure; Mead's organism-confronting-resistance as *Funktionskreis*); and to Burke's own *Rhetoric of Motives* (1950), where incipient-act and symbolic action anticipate consubstantiation and identification.

The permission-structure for the dissertation's use of this apparatus is supplied by Burke's own caveat that his appreciation of Aristotle is un-Aristotelian and his remarks about Aquinas would not satisfy a Thomist. The chapter reviews dialectical resources from the dramatist standpoint, not as a manifesto for scholastic realism: the Act-operationalization can be deployed without inheriting either Aristotelian hylomorphism or Thomist hierarchy.
