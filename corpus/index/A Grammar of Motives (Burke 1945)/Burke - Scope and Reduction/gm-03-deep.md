# GM-03 — Part 1.III: Scope and Reduction (Burke, *A Grammar of Motives*, 1945)

*Deep analysis (Phase 2). Pages: book 59–126 / PDF 80–147. Sixty-eight pages — the longest chapter in Part 1 and the methodologically richest in the entire Grammar.*

## (i) Overview

"Scope and Reduction" is the chapter in which Burke names and develops the two analytical instruments that are most likely to survive transposition out of dramatism into other inquiries: the *representative anecdote* and the *circumference*. Around them, and using a long footnote-warned excursus through the theology of creation and the grammar of pantheism, Burke also discharges a third task that the chapter's title only partly advertises — he secures Act as a non-reducible locus of motives alongside Scene, Agent, Agency, and Purpose. The chapter then turns to an extended applied case-study of *monetary reduction*, which occupies almost half its length and develops Burke's most-cited single claim about modernity: that money has become "a technical substitute for God." A closing typology — Love, Knowledge, Authority — generalizes the methodology beyond economy by reading historical motivational vocabularies as expressions of three perennial orders of power.

The chapter opens with a compressed thesis statement: a vocabulary of motives must be *faithful to* (reflective of) the reality it covers, *selective* (a reduction of that reality), and is at hazard of becoming a *deflection* whenever the reduction outruns the scope. Three terms — reflection, selection, deflection — supply the chapter's regulative ideal: scope and reduction are cooperatively constitutive, not opposed, and pathology arises only when their balance is broken.

## (ii) Representative anecdote as methodology

Burke's first move is to name the form-giving instrument: every vocabulary of motives is constructed *in conformity with* an originating case, the "representative anecdote," which "contains in nuce the terminological structure that is evolved in conformity with it" (book p.60). This is not the methodological cliché of "starting from examples." It is a much stronger claim: that the terminology one ends with is internally entailed by the case one began with, and that the only way to test a motive-vocabulary is to ask whether its anecdote is in fact representative of the class of phenomena the vocabulary is supposed to illuminate.

Burke's negative example carries most of the argumentative load. The behaviorist's conditioned-reflex experiment is "notably informative" but "not representative" — animals lack linguistic rationalization, and so a human-motive vocabulary derived from an animal-experiment anecdote will be a vocabulary whose seed-form has already excluded the property Burke takes to be definitive of human motivation. The proof is internal: the very behaviorist who would say humans are "but chemicals" will, in practice, "induce responses in people by talking to them" — a break in conformity that signals the vocabulary cannot mean what it claims. Burke's diagnostic is sharp: watch for the moment when an analyst is forced, in his own practice, to abandon the implications of his anecdote.

Burke's positive choice is drama. Drama is representative because it is the kind of case in which personality and action — which Burke briefly suggests can be merged in the single term "role" — are co-present with the linguistic and symbolic medium that he takes to be constitutive of human motives. Drama also has the second property Burke needs: it admits of a *paradigm* or *prototype* case, a "pure" or "consummate" act that can be used to test the formal limits of the concept of Act. That prototype, famously, is the theologians' Act of Creation — useful "in principle" even when not literally believed, because it lets the analyst discern dimly in lesser acts the qualities the pure case possesses clearly.

From these moves Burke generates a working **rubric** of features any good representative anecdote must satisfy. (a) *Representativeness* — it must contain in compressed form the constitutive properties of the class of phenomena it stands for. (b) *Scope* — it must be supple and complex enough to be representative of the subject-matter the vocabulary is designed to calculate. (c) *Reduction* — it must possess the simplicity that makes the case tractable as a generative seed. (d) *Containment in nuce* — the system to be developed from it must follow from it; it cannot be retrofitted. (e) *Paradigm/prototype availability* — the analyst should be able to derive a pure or consummate instance by following the anecdote's implications to their limit. (f) *Conformity test* — when the analyst's own practice breaks the conformity that the vocabulary claims with the anecdote, the vocabulary has revealed its inadequacy. The rubric is dissertation-portable: applied to a vocabulary of game-user motives, for instance, it asks both what compressed case is being used as seed and whether the analyst's own moves remain consistent with that case.

A candid passage acknowledges what could otherwise look like circular justification: "the representative anecdote [is] itself so dramatistic a conception that we might call it the dramatistic approach to dramatism: an introduction to dramatism that is deduced from dramatism, and hence gains plausibility in proportion as dramatism itself is more fully developed" (p.59). Burke does not resolve the circularity but discloses it — a methodological honesty worth carrying forward.

## (iii) Act as locus of motives

The Way of Creation section transcribes a long letter from William James to his father (via Ralph Perry's biography, vol. 2 p.711) on the difference between creation and pantheism. Burke confesses in a footnote that the following pages may strike the reader as quibbling and that the main line resumes on p.74; the reader is given permission to skip. But the quibble does the chapter's load-bearing analytical work.

From James's claim that creation must contain "an arbitrary and magical element . . . undetermined by anything external to the agent," Burke extracts a generalizable principle. If the scene of an act is already given, the agent's nature given, the agency given, and the purpose given, then any novelty in the act must have a motivational source in the act itself. Otherwise the act would be merely an unfolding of conditions already present in the other four loci. Without an irreducible "modicum of novelty" — a self-caused element, what Burke calls a *causa sui* in Spinoza's idiom — Act would be epiphenomenal; the pentad would collapse into a tetrad.

The argument has three confirming registers. *Theological*: Augustine's claim that God's act of Creation must be without motives, lest his will be constrained — this is the act-of-magic in pure form. *Scholastic*: Aquinas's definition of God as *actus purus*, and the formula Burke quotes via Bréhier (p.331) — "Dieu meut tous les êtres selon le mode de chacun" — which preserves real Act-locus even within a doctrine of universal divine motivation. *Vernacular*: proverbs (*l'appétit vient en mangeant*; *Übung macht den Meister*), and the experience of writing a long book in which "the act of the writing brings up problems and discoveries intrinsic to the act, leading to developments that derive not from the scene, or agent, or agency, or extrinsic purposes, but purely from the foregoing aspects of the act itself." Burke's earlier *poiemata / pathemata / mathemata* alignment (from the chapter on antinomies) is invoked again: an act produces sufferances which produce new insights, so the conditions of action are altered by the act itself. Spinoza's universal Substance as cause of itself, and Coleridge's view of poetry as a "dim analogue of Creation," are the philosophical and aesthetic precedents.

## (iv) Pantheism and ontology

The pantheism/ontology subsection extends the meditation into a structural diagnosis. To treat the relation between a first and a second "in terms of being" is to make one step collapse into the other; the two become coeval, the Creator/creature distinction merges in the higher unity of Being. Spinoza's *Deus sive Natura* and the *sub specie aeternitatis* perspective are Burke's leading examples. The grammatical exhibit is the reflexive form: *causa sui*, *natura naturans / natura naturata*, *res cogitans / res extensa*. In Aristotle's *Physics*, the reduction of natural principles to active, passive, and middle voice is, for Burke, "a paradigm of grammar." Each of these is a way of putting scene and agent into one, freedom and necessity into one.

This is also the chapter's structural account of secularization. The pantheistic moment is "a bridge leading from theology to naturalism": once the personal principle has been merged with the impersonal scene, it can be dropped from the scene with no further loss of inferential force, since (by Occam's Razor) one term has become superfluous. The historical motion from a teleological Creation circumference to an efficient-cause / *vis-a-tergo* circumference is, in this grammatical reading, not a discovery but a translation — a narrowing of motivational scope that the pantheistic reflexive made available.

## (v) Circumference — the scope-of-scene apparatus

Borrowing James's own word, Burke names the second master construct: *circumference*. "When defining by location, one may place the object of one's definition in contexts of varying scope" (p.77). The choice of circumference is the choice of the size of the scene within which an act is located, and — by the logic of the scene-act and scene-agent ratios — that choice constrains in advance the kinds of motives that can be attributed to the act.

Burke's apparatus admits of a working **rubric**. (a) *Circumference selection is itself an act*, indeed "an act of faith" (p.84); it is not a methodological preliminary but a constitutive analytic move. (b) *The scene-act ratio governs it*: a narrower scene yields more mechanistic acts, a wider scene more teleological or personal acts. (c) *Identifiable ranges* (cosmic / natural / cultural-historical / epochal / biographical / episodic) generate distinct motive-families. (d) *Narrowing-of-circumference* is a directional historical motion that accompanies secularization. (e) *Wider circumferences may be poorer in content* (Windelband's law that intension varies inversely with extension); world-views can be the narrowest of all. (f) *Same-scene-different-circumference* is an empirical test that isolates circumference as an independent motivational variable. (g) *Monographic placement* is the workaday default, and its diagnostic failure is the silent conversion of "a part of" into "apart from."

Burke gives four worked illustrations. **Behaviorism**: the laboratory imposes an "animal circumference" on human motives, but is ironically a "human" scene (no other species builds laboratories), and so reveals its own circumference-choice as constitutive rather than empirical. **Theology-to-naturalism**: the historical shift from teleological to mechanistic philosophies is the sharpest single instance of the same operation, with the rational/personal/purposive aspects of Creation (formerly *scenic*, applying to the outer circumference of the ground) replaced by sheer concatenations of motion. **Stark Young on Clifford Odets** (*Night Music*): Young's "no theatre can surpass its epoch in solidity, depth or philosophic summation" is a scene-act ratio applied with a circumference *narrowed to its own epoch* — which Burke punctures with Shakespeare, who "has surpassed whole centuries, whole populations, whole cultures." **Mitya and Alyosha Karamazov**: the chapter's leading literary case. Mitya and his elder brother inhabit identically the same scene; Mitya's circumference is local (escape, change of place, the scene-act ratio yielding a new act yielding a new man by act-agent ratio), while Alyosha's circumference is wider — a "transcendence" or "higher synthesis." Same scene; different circumference; different motives. The case isolates circumference as a variable independent of the scene-content itself.

The Monographic Terms of Placement section, often skimmed, is one of Burke's most precise diagnostic instruments. He works through James Marshall's *Swords and Symbols, the Technique of Sovereignty* — a competent monograph on political power whose vocabulary (force, power, threat, police, appetite, fraud) "clusters logically and necessarily about the theme." Within its declared circumference the book is wholly proper; Burke even credits its method. But "the very limitations inherent in the terms proper to the subject serve to give the thesis an appearance of too great scope": readers receive it as a statement about the ultimate origin of the ethical when in fact it is a statement about ethics-as-considered-in-terms-of-material-needs. The misreading is the silent conversion of "a part of" into "apart from." Burke's rule: a narrow circumference can pass for a wide one whenever the reader does not recognize the genitive shift.

## (vi) Monetary reduction as long case study (200-word summary)

The second half of the chapter applies the apparatus to a single sustained case. The historical narrowing of motivational circumference proceeds in stages: the rationality of the Good in Christian theology is naturalized to "natural law," made more circumstantial as "environment," narrowed to "economic factors," and further restricted to the monetary and financial. At a critical point money "transubstantiates" from agency (medium of exchange) into ground/purpose (the motive that organizes production and distribution); men work in response to money as motive. Capitalist "freedom" is grammatically deducible from a scene whose circumference has shrunk to the market. The narrowed circumference can credibly claim to do the work formerly done by religion: money is integrative, mediates among private motives, allows heterogeneity-without-disintegration, and supplies a universal symbol — a "lowest common denominator" that substitutes "technically" or "scientifically" for the godhead as public principle. Protestantism's relocation of God to a private principle is read as the structural counterpart of money's having taken the public mediating role. Capitalism, Fascism, and Communism may all be variants of one "monetary psychosis" because all three are grounded in the technological occupational diversity that requires a universal monetary medium.

## (vii) Kinds of reduction

The Kinds of Reduction section disambiguates three uses of the same word. **(1)** Reduction of the non-verbal to the verbal — a cosmology, Burke writes, is "the world in terms of words." This is the irreducible, definitional reduction in which every vocabulary engages by being a vocabulary. **(2)** Reduction of one terminology to another by translation — *x* in terms of *y*. Most philosophical disputes are reductions of this kind; the relation can be honorific (an idealist reduces matter to ideas; a Bonaventura subordinates art to theology) or pejorative. **(3)** Reduction in the everyday pejorative sense of lowering or narrowing — debunking (Bentham, La Rochefoucauld), atomistic "building-block" philosophies, materialist reduction. Burke's diagnostic warning is that the third type often hides an "inverted perfectionism": "tough-minded" vocabularies that reduce all motives to one pejorative (greed, fear, lust for power) thereby commit the same purity-of-motive fallacy they accuse idealism of committing. The complement-thesis follows: every motivational simplicity, on close analysis, "contains a diversity." Titular reductions are chapter-headings whose contents must be filled out; the pentad terms themselves are titular in just this sense.

Burke's hypothetical Berkeleyan/Thomist universe of consubstantial ideas works through the principle in slow motion: even with a "correct" model of motivation, multiple schools can stress different aspects (consubstantiality, division, the epistemological miracle, the dialectic of one-and-many, developmental stages) and so the simple motive at the root admits a diversity of analytical voices. The Aquinas formula (Bréhier p.331) — God moves all things, each according to its mode — gives the doctrine its sharpest statement.

## (viii) Money as substitute for God

The fullest statement of the monetary thesis appears in this subsection. Money is the public mediating term: where religion was once "a sort of Rome towards which all roads lead," money has the same unifying attribute. It is the abstraction into which all communicative acts can in principle be translated. The communion-service dialectic of the one and the many (men made one by partaking in common of a substance) is mirrored in the philosophy of the market (private acquisition with public reference). Money is the "lowest common denominator" that mediates among an infinity of private motives, with "less waste motion" than ritual works. Burke argues this is why Protestantism — arising as monetary reduction matured — *relocated* the godhead to a private principle: with the public mediating role already taken by money, the religious symbol could survive only by becoming an inward, direct communication with God.

The historical grammar shifts. "God's personality, therefore human personality" (a calculus of *therefore*, consistent humanism) becomes "nature's impersonality, nevertheless human personality" (a calculus of *nevertheless*, compensatory humanism). The capitalist-psychosis vignette — the stock-market column equating a hurricane's destruction of physical wealth with a market's destruction of symbolic wealth — exposes how thoroughly the monetary circumference becomes "second nature." Even the analyst's reluctance to "talk about money matters" is, Burke notes, formally analogous to the religious taboo on naming or beholding the deity: in both cases, the silence around the motive-center indicates that the term is functioning as god-term.

## (ix) The representative-anecdote and circumference apparatuses as dissertation-deployable methodology

Both constructs translate directly into the dissertation's analytical work. The representative-anecdote rubric supplies a *seed-form test* for any vocabulary that proposes to analyse user-motives in games: what compressed case is being used as the form-in-conformity-with-which the vocabulary is constructed? The behaviorist failure (a vocabulary whose anecdote excludes the very property it claims to study) has obvious analogues in attempts to derive a vocabulary of player motivation from input-event logs, click-stream telemetry, or controlled experimental stimuli — each is "notably informative" but, on Burke's test, not yet representative if the symbolic-linguistic-narrative dimension of play has been excluded at the seed. Burke's positive choice (drama; capable of generating both a paradigm/prototype and a flatland of vernacular examples) suggests a methodological parallel: a worked-through dramatistic case (e.g., a single game-session read with the pentad) can function for the dissertation's vocabulary the way Creation functions for Burke's — as the prototype whose properties illuminate, by extension, the lesser cases. The six rubric features (representativeness, scope, reduction, containment-in-nuce, paradigm-availability, conformity-test) are deployable as a checklist for any motive-vocabulary proposed in the dissertation's downstream chapters.

The circumference rubric is, if anything, even more transferable. Game-studies and player-experience analysis routinely operate with implicit, undeclared circumferences — sometimes the scene is "the game-session," sometimes "the platform-ecosystem," sometimes "the player's life-history," sometimes "the cultural moment." Burke's apparatus makes the choice visible: every motive-attribution in player analysis can be re-described as the joint product of an act and a circumference, and the analyst's burden is to justify the circumference selected. The Mitya/Alyosha test (same scene, different circumference, different motives) maps onto a familiar empirical phenomenon — two players in identically the same game-state read it through circumferences of radically different scope (one biographical-episodic, one ethical-cosmic). The monographic-placement diagnostic gives the dissertation a clean tool for assessing the field's existing literature: when a study confines itself to a narrow circumference (e.g., flow-state, MDA, operant conditioning), the verdict is not that it is wrong but that its readers must not mistake it for a universal statement about player motivation.

## (x) Cross-pipeline bridges

Several edges from this chapter connect to other dissertation-relevant indices, all carrying the provenance discipline required by the plan §9 risk table.

Toward the **Calleja In-Game / PIM** pipeline: the circumference apparatus is the dissertation's natural complement to the PIM's six dimensions. Where the PIM gives the structural-elements axis (what is incorporated, at what phase-scale), the Burke circumference gives the motive-attribution axis (at what scope of scene does the analyst locate the player's act). The 1:1 mapping between Burke's circumference-narrowing (cosmic → natural → cultural → episodic) and a PIM-phase scale (macro/micro phase, ambient/focal incorporation) is anticipatory-projection only; it is not Burke's claim.

Toward the **Aristotle / Phantasia** corpus: Burke's quotation of *Sophist* 247e (Being as "that which has the power to act or be acted upon," p.119) and his Aristotle-Physics analysis of active/passive/middle voice are direct exegetical anchors. These are *exegetical-direct* edges, since Burke himself is grounding the dramatistic vocabulary in Aristotelian terms.

Toward the **Heidegger BCAP / Pathos** thread: Burke's reading of pre-Socratic *hylozoism* not as animism but as "perception of things... as essentially active" (p.119) sits adjacent to the Heideggerian *pathos*-as-disclosure thesis. The bridge is exegetical-suggestive rather than direct.

Toward **Uexküll**: the circumference-of-scene concept has obvious affinity to the *Umwelt* — both are constructed environments whose scope constrains what motives/significances are visible to the agent. The mapping is anticipatory-projection, since Burke does not cite Uexküll.

Toward the **Rhetoric of Motives** (1950) forward-reference: the Love/Knowledge/Authority triad is the chapter's anticipation of the *Rhetoric*'s identification/consubstantiation analysis. Love as *ubertas* (biological-familial substance) prefigures the consubstantiation grammar; Authority as *auctoritas* (group cohesion via originator/progenitor/leader) prefigures identification's persuasive substrate.

The chapter's closing typology — Love, Knowledge, Authority, three orders of power that have shaped motivational vocabularies — is itself a candidate dissertation-deployable instrument, complementary to the pentad but not subsidiary to it. Read alongside the pentad: where the pentad asks *which term locates the motive*, the Love/Knowledge/Authority triad asks *which historical order of power is supplying the vocabulary*. The two questions are independent. A pentad analysis foregrounding Scene can be conducted with a *ubertas*-derived vocabulary (climate, season, fertility) or a *veritas*-derived vocabulary (laboratory, mechanism, correlation) or an *auctoritas*-derived vocabulary (jurisdiction, sovereignty, command) — and each yields a different motivational reading of the same Scene-featured act. This is the precise methodological payoff that justifies treating "Scope and Reduction" as Burke's single richest methodological chapter and as one of the dissertation's two most important methodology-source units in the entire Burke corpus.
