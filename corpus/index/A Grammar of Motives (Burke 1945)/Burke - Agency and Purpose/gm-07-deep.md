## (i) Agency — the Philosophy of Means

Burke's chapter opens by taking the Baldwin dictionary's gloss on "tender Pragmatism" — the species of Kantian hypothetical imperative prescribing the means necessary to happiness — as warrant for what is in fact a far stronger thesis: Pragmatist philosophies, taken as a coherent school, are *generated* by the featuring of one pentad term, Agency, as motive-locus. The diagnostic that has organized the whole of Part 2 of the *Grammar* — materialism features Scene, idealism features Agent, realism features Act — now arrives at its fourth assignment. The argument is not that pragmatism contains an Agency-term (every coherent motive-statement contains all five), but that pragmatism *features* Agency as the originating ground of motive. Action is to be explained by the means by which it is brought about.

The opening pages assemble the school's canonical inventory under this featuring. Dewey's "Instrumentalism" advertises the diagnosis in its very name. William James, having declared pragmatism "a method only" and having adapted Peirce's notion that beliefs are rules for action, reaches the formulation that "theories thus become instruments" — and Burke notes that this formulation runs Aristotle's contrast between theory and practice in the opposite direction. James self-classifies pragmatism in the company of nominalism (appeal to particulars), utilitarianism (emphasis on practice), and positivism (disdain for verbal solutions). Behind these moves, Burke locates a Grammatical fact: modern science is "*par excellence* an accumulation of new agencies," and this locus of new power has called forth philosophies of science that would raise agency to first place among the five terms. The Papini hotel-corridor image, which James himself reproduces, is exactly the figure of agency-as-mediator: each room (each philosophic occupation) is private, but all guests pass through the same corridor; pragmatism is the principle of mediation common to all rooms.

The chapter's second move (book p. 276) is exegetical and historical. Aristotle's *organon* is "incipiently" pragmatist — logic conceived as the instrument of human reason — but Burke insists that Aristotle's slave-culture position led him to subordinate agency. Within the four causes (material, efficient, formal, final), means are considered in terms of ends. Modern science's non-Aristotelian element is exactly the inversion of this priority: once final cause is played down, the distinction between agent and agency sharpens, and ends become treated in terms of means. The everyday case is vocational training: "because there are cars, some men learn to become automobile mechanics, their conception of a life purpose deriving from the nature of the instrument which they would service." Agency has become *ancestral* — the term from which the others descend. Money, Burke notes, supplies a derivative "absolute purpose" over and above the per-instrument purposes; the chapter will return to money as the pivot of the Agency/Purpose ambiguity.

## (ii) Pragmatism: James, Dewey, Mead — and the Limit-Case of Operationalism

Burke walks the school in roughly genetic order. Emerson's *Nature* (Commodity, Beauty, Language, Discipline) is read as transitional: agency has not yet become ancestral but is still seen in terms of universal purpose. "In God, every end is converted into a new means" marks the seam. Only when one drops final cause and narrows the circumference does true pragmatist agency-as-ancestor emerge. Burke replaces Emerson's transcendentalism and James's lingering personalism with Dewey-Mead biologism (*Experience and Nature*, *The Philosophy of the Act*): bodily organs are means-toward-ends with no theological remainder; agency can be considered without theological or personalistic final cause; Santayana's "animal faith" is admitted on this level.

The Phase 0 disambiguation flagged "James" and "Mead" as bare-surname risks; both are now resolved. All "James" references in the chapter belong to *William* James the pragmatist, with one calculated exception at book p. 298 where *Henry* James appears as a literary-craft contrast within the Symbolic-of-Agency discussion. All "Mead" references resolve to *G. H.* Mead, never to Margaret Mead. The deep-analysis JSON records both resolutions and the textual locus that produces the contrast (Henry-as-novelist appears in the same paragraph as William-as-pragmatist precisely because Burke is building a Symbolic argument about the brothers' divergent vocational mediations).

The chapter's limit-case is P. W. Bridgman's "operationalism" (book pp. 279–281). In *The Logic of Modern Physics*, Bridgman equates meaning with the corresponding set of operations; two procedures for measuring the same temperature count "in principle" as two concepts. Burke admits Bridgman as the purest instance — "pragmatism pure and simple would not be reached until we come to P. W. Bridgman's operationalism." But the chapter then turns the operationalist program against itself. Bridgman's own conceptual vocabulary — "principle," "in principle," "purpose" in arguing for the elimination of purpose — is possible only to substantialist thought. When Bridgman writes that "the world of laboratory experimentation is not understandable without some examination of the purpose of physics," he is "writing about his purpose in eliminating the concept of purpose, a state of linguistic affairs which calls for a kind of analysis not possible to his method." Burke's diagnostic gem on this page is structurally important for the dissertation: instruments "perform satisfactorily without purpose only because they have purpose imbedded in their structure and design. An instrument like a thermometer has its purpose so thoroughly built into its very nature, that it can do its work without purpose, merely by continuing to be itself." This is the agency-side seam where the dissertation's *technē*-grounding finds its anticipatory Burke-anchor.

The chapter's Conditions / Facts subsections then trace a genealogy of "conditions" through Aquinas (formal) → Kant (transcendental) → Fichte (Ego/Non-Ego) → Hegel (the *Vermittlung* of *Ausserung*) → Marx (economic conditions). Hegelian mediation is the explicit Burke-named anticipator of pragmatism through idealism. Marx is held *outside* the pragmatist circle on the grounds that "a pronounced sense of form" pervades his history — pragmatism is form-dissolving. Pragmatist history proper enters with the "documentary" ideal: documentary-facts are the medium with which the historian works, so writing "pure document" places major stress on the medium itself — a featuring of agency. Essentialist demands return surreptitiously: the documentary historian "must give us essences while disclaiming any such purpose."

## (iii) Symbolic of Agency and the Purposive Agencies of Applied Science

The Two Principles of Truth in James (book pp. 283–284) is the most compressed Burke-on-James page in the volume. James's pragmatism, Burke argues, sits midway between dramatist and positivist ideals of truth. The grammatical fact at its base is that human acts are not "verifiable" the way purely scenic statements are: "the proof of a human act is in the doing." Agency contains within itself two verifications: (1) verification of an act by an act (believing as the test of belief), and (2) verification of an act-less scenic statement by an act framed in accordance with the statement (a map tested by following). Agency's essence as mediator — between dramatic action and behavioristically-observed scene — is exactly what enables it to straddle these two verifications. The dissertation can take this passage as the strongest Burke-side anchor for an "operational" account of player-action: the player verifies a game-state-statement by acting on it, and verifies a game-act by enacting it.

The Symbolic of Agency (book pp. 283–286) is the chapter's most exotic detour. Burke argues that, because instruments are "essentially" human (the products of human design), a personal ingredient stems from the formative experience of personal utility in infancy — the mother as first agency. This produces the figure of "Mother Nature" Burke had noted in Hellenistic and Emersonian incipient-pragmatism. The Agency–Purpose grammar in sexual terms reads: maternal woman = utility (agency); erotic woman = purpose (the desired). Courtship's task is the transcending of maternal utility into erotic purpose, and Bentham's "crabbed bachelor" Utilitarianism is then diagnosed as a deflected erotic-purpose translated back into maternal agency — including Bentham's "Symbolic castration" in his neutral scientific vocabulary. Baudelaire's *La Géante* is the literary witness for the merging of maternal and erotic principles. Burke explicitly brackets this excursus: "the systematic consideration of such possibilities belongs rather in the Symbolic of Motives." For the dissertation this matters because Burke himself flags the proto-Hawhee thematic of body-thinking-as-rhetorical-agency at the very point where he refuses to develop it.

The Purposive Agencies of Applied Science (book pp. 286–287) delivers the chapter's structural punchline for its first half. Agency's prestige derives first from the Grammatical fact that it covers applied science, the area of new power. The reduction of action to motion can be re-described as reduction to Agency; tools are "more purposive" than impersonal backgrounds, so the action-motion ambiguity is conveniently straddled by Pragmatism. Money's "cash-value" contributes; the stock-market personification ("it very frankly acts as if it wanted to have an old-fashioned reaction but is afraid to carry it out") reveals corporate-entity-as-agent as a near-automatic precipitate of agency-language. Once Agency is foregrounded, the other terms accommodate themselves: scenic materials become means; the organism is a confluence of means; reason becomes adjustment; empiricism's senses play a mediatory role. Burke closes this half with an entropy figure: "we seem to be confronting a principle of entropy, as with the second law of thermodynamics, with the distinctions of the various philosophic schools levelling off towards their 'heat-death' in Pragmatism." Papini's corridor returns as thermodynamic image: the pragmatist mediator is what all schools devolve into when their differences are dissolved.

## (iv) Purpose — Modifications and Ends

The Ends subsection (book p. 287) pivots the chapter. Baldwin's *Mysticism* entry — "forms of speculative and religious thought which profess to attain an immediate apprehension of the divine essence or the ultimate ground of existence" — already names "the divine essence," "the creative source," "the Being of beings." Burke then makes the diagnostic move that mirrors the Agency-half: Mysticism features Purpose as motive-locus. The corrective is exact: "not mere unity, but unity of the individual with some cosmic or universal purpose is the mark of mysticism." The standard "mysticism = unity" gloss misses the *purposive* tenor that distinguishes unity-with-divinity from unity-with-anything. The negative-by-contrast confirms: anti-vitalist / anti-teleological philosophies regularly herald their attainments as the *elimination* of "mysticism" and "teleology" — teleology being the metaphysician's word for Purpose / final cause.

The paradox-of-pure-purpose breaks open immediately. A purpose so "pure" — the mystic's union with the All-Purpose — becomes much the same as no purpose at all. The mystic oxymorons (black radiance, bitter sweetness, *docta ignorantia*) record this collapse; absolute Purpose passes into compulsion. "Such considerations explain why the psychology of mysticism is close to the psychology of neurosis." Sociologically, mystic philosophies flourish in periods of confusion about human purpose — James cites Tolstoy's pre-rebirth question ("Is there in life any purpose which the inevitable death awaiting me does not undo and destroy?"). Crucially for the chapter's whole argument: "of the five terms, Purpose has become the one most susceptible of dissolution." Modern materialist-behaviorist reductions eliminate purpose formally; pragmatism retains it implicitly in agency. Burke calls the survival of teleological "experience" beneath non-teleological style "*Grammatical repression*" — the child's "What are the hills for?" corrected to "How do the hills come to be?" while the teleological intent survives vestigially.

## (v) Purpose in Aristotle — the EXEGETICAL Forward Bridge

The "Purpose in Aristotle" subsection (book pp. 292–293) is the strongest exegetical hinge in the chapter and one of the two strongest exegetical hinges in the whole volume (the other being Burke's *energeia/dynamis* reading in GM-06). Burke writes that "the most realistic synonym for purpose is the Aristotelian 'happiness' (*eudaimonia*)." It stands at a level of generalization next beneath Purpose itself. Burke then walks Aristotle's *Rhetoric* I.5 explicitly: men individually and in common have some aim, in the attainment of which they choose or avoid certain things — the aim is happiness and its component parts; the deliberative orator is concerned with the expedient; men deliberate not about the end but about the means to the end; the expedient is the good; the good is "whatever is desirable for its own sake, or for the sake of which we choose something else"; "an end is a good"; virtue defined as "a faculty of providing and preserving good things." The seven causes of human actions (chance, nature, compulsion, habit, reason, anger, desire) furnish oratorical commonplaces. Burke then leaps to Aristotelian *teleology* of artefacts: "Instruments are considered by Aristotle in teleological terms, as their form is said to be derived from the end desired by their users." And then to living things: the vegetative–sensitive–rational hierarchy of immanent purposes, with rational/moral life atop. *Politics* I.1 ("every community is formed for the sake of some good") and *Nicomachean Ethics* I.1 ("every art and every inquiry... is thought to aim at some good; hence the good has rightly been called that at which all things aim") are explicit citations. The boundary-claim is sharp: "the analysis is anything but mystical. Indeed, it is wholly realistic, involving the usual Aristotelian concern with action... only when purpose becomes total does it fit our prescription for mysticism."

For the dissertation's ontological grounding of Purpose, this subsection means that the Burke→Aristotle bridge is *EXEGETICAL* — Burke himself reads Aristotelian *telos* and eudaimonia as the realistic anchor for the pentad's Purpose term. By contrast, the Burke→Heideggerian *Worumwillen* bridge is *anticipatory*-projection (Burke 1945 has 0 Heidegger hits, and the Macquarrie-Robinson *Being and Time* is seventeen years out): the convergence between *Worumwillen* (Dasein's ultimate for-the-sake-of-which, *Sein und Zeit* §18 and §69) and Burke's "unity of the individual with cosmic or universal purpose" is parallel-discovery, not influence. The dissertation's anticipatory grounding of Agency in Aristotelian *technē* is similarly anticipatory: Burke names the *organon* as "incipiently pragmatist" but does not develop *technē* (craft / productive knowledge with its own internal *aitia*) as the proper home of agency.

## (vi) Platonist and Neo-Platonist Purpose

Plato's equation Good = One brings purpose nearer to mysticism than Aristotelian eudaimonia. The Neo-Platonists complete the move. Plotinus (*Enneads* I.3): "Our journey is to the Good, to the Primal-Principle." Burke reads the dialectic of the Upward Way as "what Korzybski would call a development to higher and higher levels of generalization, or abstraction, until one comes to the principle of Unity (or the 'First')." Then the course reverses: unity is resolved into particulars, but the particulars on descent are infused with the spirit of the First. The First is beyond all merely human comprehension except in the stage of mystic transcendence. From the Grammar's standpoint, the First is "the absolutizing of the concept of purpose."

## (vii) Physiology of Mysticism and the Mystic Moment

The Physiology of Mysticism subsection (book pp. 294–295) is the chapter's biological-substrate node and is anticipatorily-important for the cross-pipeline bridges to Uexküll, BCAP-pathos, and the dissertation's kinesthetic dimension. Burke re-cites his earlier *Permanence and Change*: the mystic trance as a possible "assertion *in vacuo*" — all conflicting nervous impulses called into play at once. Because directed muscular movement requires the repression of competing impulses, if the nerves could be stimulated without muscular movement, even conflicting impulses could "glow" simultaneously without repression. This would explain both "pure action" and "total passivity" as descriptors of the same state, and the sense of attainment as both complete and non-combative — a oneness with the universal texture "as thorough as that which the organism must have experienced during its period of 'larval feeding' in the womb." Burke now adds (this is the 1945 increment): this would amount to "a kind of 'total purpose,' in a transcendent state of 'pre-motion.'" The phrase *pre-motion* is the strongest single Burke-side anticipatory anchor for Uexküll's receptor/effector decoupling of *Merk-Organ* and *Wirk-Organ* in the *Funktionskreis*, and for BCAP's reading of Aristotelian *pathos* as incipient affective comportment.

The Purposiveness in the Negative subsection then performs an extraordinary grammatical move. Burke adds "the negative" to his synonyms for Purpose, via the mystical *via negativa* of negative theology. The First Principle's negative attributes ("infinite," "incomprehensible," "unending," "incorporeal") flow from its status as highest level of generalization — particulars carry sense; broader generalizations lose sensory nature. Spinoza inverts: Universal Substance is positive, determinate Modes are negation ("all determination is negation"). Bergson's *Creative Evolution* makes the negative a function of desire/expectation/interest: "the ground is not damp" implies a positive condition (e.g., dry). Burke dramatistically revises: the origin of the negative is not informational but moral — "thou shalt not" of the Decalogue. Greek/Latin verbs of fear take negative forms for positive states (*vereor ne veniat*). Negative theology begins in fear and apprehension prior to the "semantic" negative; the equating of the Good with Purpose is already a step away from this more fearsome religion.

The Mystic "Moment" subsection (book pp. 305–308) supplies the chapter's temporal-grammatical signature of mystic conversion. Unity is not the same as First. The Upward Way proceeds by stages until a generalization-level not originally envisaged is reached, whereupon particulars look different in light of this "higher vision" — the Grammar of *rebirth*. Burke's homely figure: passing the tenth floor in the elevator on the way up is a different experience from passing it on the way down — *nine-plus-one* and *eleven-minus-one* are not the same number from a different "first." Proust's *Past Recovered* (many moments all one moment) is the literary witness. Dostoevsky's *Crime and Punishment* receives the chapter's most extended treatment: Raskolnikov's murder is "mystical" because it is a kind of "absolute" act, "conceived independently of its conditions" — a representative-of-pure-purpose, seeking the essence of power. Criminality produces "oneness with the universe" in leading to a sense of universal persecution; the teleological view "lost by evolutionism" returns under the form of criminal guilt. Every next person may be the one who knows of the offence.

The reflexive structure of mystic unity (book pp. 299–301) accumulates witnesses: Melville's *Pierre* (incest/self-abuse imagery); James's Xenos Clark almost catching up with himself, kissing his own lips under anaesthetic; Nietzsche's eternal recurrence as the cosmic-replica of the moment of ecstatic communion; Eliot's *Murder in the Cathedral* wheel-with-still-centre and *Little Gidding*'s "Midwinter spring" as "essence of transition"; Shelley's *Alastor* (poet-on-foot → boat-trance → stream "imagest my life"). Mysticism and Idealism reinforce each other through the abstract Self: Symonds-via-James reports "obliteration of space, time, sensation... pure, absolute, abstract Self"; Kant's "synthetic unity of apperception" is the same figure in idealist key.

The Images-and-the-Demonic-Trinity / Silence-and-the-Hunt subsections push the biological substrate further. The "demonic trinity" = erotic + urinary + excremental, united in the cloacal; rain may serve vicariously for urination and erotic-purpose; the "excremental" nature of invective allows translation of erotic-purpose from "love" to "war." At the centre of mobility is the purpose of the hunt — hunger for the desired, the *Twelfth Night* hart/heart pun, Rousseau's *Emile* hunt-as-stage-prior-to-sexual-quest. Mystic silence has its roots in the purposive: in the quest one is naturally silent — animal stalking quarry, thinker meditating an idea, Socratic question that "begins in the silence of the quest," Hamlet's "the rest is silence." Three aspects of the ineffable round out the discussion: Korzybski's "unspeakable" things-themselves (rites of desanctification as first "mystical" appreciation of powers — word-magic as deteriorated form of original distrust of words); ineffability of visceral processes (no sensation describable to one who has not experienced it); ineffability of linguistic relations themselves (any explication becomes a new level of implication).

## (viii) Mysticism of Means

The Mysticism of Means subsection (book pp. 309–311) is where Agency and Purpose are knotted back together at the chapter's culmination. The "moment" is to what follows as implicit is to explicit, as *deus implicitus* is to *deus explicitus* — the pantheist vision of a god whose unfoldment is the world. Burke diagnoses Aldous Huxley's pacifism as a "pragmatist kind of mysticism": the doctrine that impure acts must follow from impure means treats means as ancestor of end, so that "only by peaceful means can we get peace." Burke's critique is structurally important. The logical conclusion of Huxley's doctrine would be that peace as an end is either impossible or unnecessary (if peaceful means already yield peace, we have peace; if non-peaceful means cannot yield peace, no point attempting). More fundamentally: all means are necessarily impure — they bear properties for other possible uses, including hostile uses — and we act by "a progressive purification" of them.

From the dramatistic standpoint, "it would seem wrong to speak of ends as resulting from means." A statement confined to terms of means and end "eliminates *act* as a special locus of motives by treating the act simply as means to an end." Action, in dramatistic perspective, strategically overlaps with being: "action is not merely a means of doing but a way of being. And a way of being is substantival, not instrumental." Burke gives the example of America's pragmatist objection to socialism ("wouldn't work"); when Russian resistance to the Hitlerite armies demonstrated workability, the rhetoric shifted to idealist/realist ("not the American way") and the "American way" itself became "pure mysticism" — business-pragmatism transformed into mystical nationalism, mediated by money as pure-purpose. The Thomas à Kempis *Imitation of Christ* — "In all things behold the end" — is the Christian-monastic figure of the Upward Way as discipline: contemning of the world as means toward inner imitation of Christ.

## (ix) Means and Ends of This Grammar — Burke's Self-Reflexive Close

The Rationalism-and-the-Verbal-Medium subsection (book pp. 311–317) reads as Burke's most concentrated self-positioning in the volume. Three Baldwin senses of rationalism — religion to be rationally explained or rejected; reason as independent source higher than sense; elementary concepts from which all is to be deductively derived — are exemplified by Descartes (*Discourse on Method*), Spinoza (*Ethics more geometrico*), Leibniz (universal logic-and-language), and pushed farthest by Hegel ("the world is Reason"). Rationalism is the forerunner to pragmatism in its method-stress, yet the two become opposed because pragmatism aligns with empiricism. Burke resolves the apparent contradiction by distinguishing rationalist dialectic (end implicit in beginning) from empiricist dialectic. He then declares his own *Grammar* "rationalistic in this dialectical sense" — making the implications of language explicit — and "empiricist" in recognizing every linguistic structure as a "new thing." "We have been spinning five terms into a book, by making their implications explicit." Money's monetary-rationality and capitalist symbolism are then folded back: investment as the "incipient act" of consumption, the "representative moment" of capitalist delay; St Ambrose's debt-ransom Christology as resonance of the religious-monetary atonement that has split, in modernity, into the rationale of accountancy on one hand and psychoanalysis on the other. Psychoanalytic repression is "the reciprocal of purpose": if purpose is the cameo, repression is the intaglio — "a kind of negative purpose." Freudian system organized about the purposive as fully as Aristotle, but adding the dialectical laws by which the purposive becomes moralistically transformed into its negation.

The Means-and-Ends-of-This-Grammar subsection (book pp. 317–320) is Burke's closing program. The five pentadic terms are "transcendental rather than formal (and are to this extent Kantian) in being categories which human thought necessarily exemplifies. Instead of calling them the necessary 'forms of experience,' however, we should call them the necessary 'forms of *talk* about experience.'" The project is "the analysis of language rather than... the analysis of 'reality.'" The needed orientation is "neo-Stoic cosmopolitanism, with ideals of tolerance and resignation to the bureaucratic requirements implicit in the structure of modern industry and commerce" — against the twin failures of fanaticism (imposing one motive-doctrine on a world of many motivational situations) and dissipation (isolationist surrender to whatever gratifications happen to present themselves). The needed attitude is mildly "hypochondriasis" / "cultural valetudinarianism" — a "gymnastic of ideas" alongside a "clinic of ideas." The book's motto: *Ad Bellum Purificandum* — "Towards the Purification of War." War cannot be eliminated from an organism that has the motives of combat in its essence, but it can be refined "to the point where it would be much more peaceful than the conditions we would now call peace." The closing sentences set up Part 3: questions of motivation come to a head in questions of substantiation and transubstantiation; Constitutions (GM-08) for substantiation, transubstantiation as the "representative moment" of dialectic in general (GM-09).

## (x) Agency and Purpose as Dissertation Operationalizations

The two pentad-term operationalizations the plan §5 calls for are now grounded. **Agency** is operationalized in the deep JSON as: the means/instruments/methods/mediating media through which an act is brought about; diagnostic question "How? By what means?"; featured by pragmatism (James, Dewey, Mead, Bridgman); Calleja PIM mapping = *Kinesthetic* (controls / motor-mediation) plus *Ludic* (rules-as-means / game-system operations); Uexküll mapping = *Merk-/Wirk-organ* pairing (receptor/effector poles of the *Funktionskreis*); ontological grounding = Aristotelian *technē* — *ANTICIPATORY* (Burke flags *organon* as "incipiently pragmatist" but does not develop *technē* itself). The Bridgman-passage "instrument has purpose imbedded in its structure and design" is the strongest Burke-side anchor for the dissertation's claim that game-mechanics carry designer-purpose which the player exercises without articulating that purpose; the Two-Principles-of-Truth-in-James passage is the strongest anchor for an act-by-act and act-by-scenic-statement double-verification structure of player-action.

**Purpose** is operationalized as: the end / *telos* / motivating-toward-which; diagnostic question "Why?"; featured by mysticism (Plotinus, Plato, Bonaventura, Thomas à Kempis), with Aristotelian eudaimonia as the realistic-not-mystical synonym; Calleja PIM mapping = *Ludic* (goal-pursuit) plus *Narrative* (story-arc closure); Uexküll mapping = closure of the functional loop on biologically-valenced object (the *Bedeutungsträger* keyed to need); ontological grounding = Aristotelian *telos* (EXEGETICAL — Burke's "Purpose in Aristotle" subsection is the explicit bridge) plus Heideggerian *Worumwillen* (ANTICIPATORY — Burke has 0 Heidegger hits volume-wide and is 17 years before Macquarrie-Robinson). The dissertation can deploy Burke's diagnosis that Purpose is "the one most susceptible of dissolution" and re-emerges where formally eliminated to argue that ludic-theories of games that purport to be purposeless (pure-Agency theories) covertly retain Purpose in their design-intent.

The philosophic-school mapping the chapter completes — pragmatism × Agency with James, Dewey, Mead; mysticism × Purpose with Plotinus and Bonaventura — sits alongside the GM-04 (materialism × Scene), GM-05 (idealism × Agent), GM-06 (realism × Act) mappings as the volume's four prior school-term assignments. The non-bijection rule applies here as elsewhere: a school *features* one pentad term but typically deploys all five; a term is featured by one canonical school but appears in all five chapters. For the dissertation's game-studies application this means that any game-theory can be characterized by which term it features (procedural rhetoric and ergodic literature feature Agency; narratology and transcendent-flow theories feature Purpose; embodied-cognition game studies features Act-on-Agency border), without thereby exhausting the term's appearance in rival theories.
