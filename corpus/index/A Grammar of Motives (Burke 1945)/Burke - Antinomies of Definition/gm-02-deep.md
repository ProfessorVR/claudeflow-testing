## (i) Overview

The second chapter of *A Grammar of Motives*' first part builds, alongside the pentad established in GM-01, the volume's *second* analytical apparatus: a vocabulary of *substance* that catalogues the distinct ways in which philosophical, theological, and rhetorical discourse stabilizes the question *what is this thing*. Whereas the pentad lays down five interrogative-grammatical slots (act, scene, agent, agency, purpose) into which any motive-statement must distribute determinate values, the substance vocabulary tracks the *modes* by which any single value gets fixed when discourse refers to "what a thing intrinsically is." Burke's central thesis is that every such mode is structurally paradoxical, because the word that designates the intrinsic etymologically refers to the extrinsic — and that this paradox, far from being a defect to be reformed away (as Locke, Korzybski, and twentieth-century positivism in their different ways attempt), is rather the productive engine of human motive-talk. The chapter is therefore both an etymology-driven topography of substance-modes and a meta-argument that the topography itself is unavoidable.

The thirteen subsections move methodically. Burke opens with the Indo-Germanic etymology of the Stance family and the Lockean impairment of "substance" (book pp. 21–23). He then develops two foundational definition-strategies: contextual definition (Spinoza as paradigm, book pp. 24–26) and familial definition (the Christian and Platonic models, book pp. 26–29). A taxonomic Survey of Terms (book pp. 29–33) introduces the geometric, the nutritive, the directional, and prepares the over-all category of dialectic substance (book pp. 33–35). The paradox of purity (book pp. 35–38) inflects all of these — pure substance flips into its negation. Burke then develops the dialectic of tragedy (book pp. 38–41) via the triad poiēma/pathēma/mathēma, which he reduces to the load-bearing dyad of actus and status (book pp. 41–43) — the chapter's tightest bridge to the pentad's Act-Agent ratio. Universal motives as substance (book pp. 43–46) extends the analysis to monotheism, the monetary motive, and the Marxist factory situation; intrinsic and extrinsic (book pp. 46–51) frames the meta-clarification using Hegel, Korzybski, Dewey, and Spinoza's *causa sui*. The closing three subsections — the rhetoric of substance (book pp. 51–53), two kinds of departure (book pp. 53–55), and the centrality of substance (book pp. 55–58) — re-deploy the apparatus toward observable rhetorical tactics and arrive at the thesis that *substantiation is citable reality, not illusion*.

This deep analysis treats the substance vocabulary as a parallel apparatus to the pentad — *not* a competitor with it but a cross-cutting set of "ways of placement" (Burke's running-head for Part 1) that supplies the rhetorical and dialectical mechanics whereby motive-statements stabilize ambiguous referents. The chapter is the canonical source for Phase 3's substance-vocabulary synthesis deliverable; its eighteen substance types catalogued in this analysis directly feed that deliverable.

## (ii) The paradox of substance and the Stance-family etymology

Burke opens with a deliberately literary etymological exercise. There is, he writes, a "Stance family" of English words — *consist, constancy, constitution, contrast, destiny, ecstasy, existence, hypostatize, obstacle, stage, state, status, statute, stead, subsist, substance, system* — all of which derive from the Indo-Germanic root *sthā-* (Sanskrit *sthā*, to stand). In German, *stellen* belongs to the same family, surfacing in *Vorstellung* — a philosophical-psychological term for representation, conception, idea, image. One could, Burke claims, "build a whole philosophic universe by tracking down the ramifications of this one root." It would be a regional universe, well-implemented with stables, staves, staffs, stalls, stamens, stamina, stanchions, stanzas, steeds, stools, and studs, in which the Southern Agrarians might take their stand.

This is not a joke. The lexical density of the *sthā-* family demonstrates Burke's first thesis: a single morphological root structures an enormous semantic territory in which *being-placed*, *being-stably-positioned*, and *being-the-ground-of-something-else* are simultaneously available meanings. The most philosophically prominent member of the family is "substance." But — and here Burke locates the chapter's master irony — *sub-stance* literally means *standing-under*: what stands beneath or supports a thing. Etymologically, therefore, "substance" designates what is *outside* the thing, what *supports* the thing from below, even though it is conventionally used to designate what the thing *intrinsically is*. The Greek *hypostasis* (literally "a standing under") carries the parallel structure: it ranges from "anything set under" (stand, base, prop) through "starting point" (subject-matter, argument, beginning) to the metaphysical senses of subsistence, reality, real being, nature, essence, and the ecclesiastical *Persona* of the Trinity. Burke adds, with characteristic glee, that the medical *hypostasis* names "a suppression, as of humours that ought to come to the surface; also matter deposited in the urine; and of liquids generally, the sediment, lees, dregs, grounds" — and that this set of meanings can admonish us, in pursuit of fundamentals, to be on the lookout for what Freud might call "cloacal" motives, "furtively interwoven with speculations that may on the surface seem wholly abstract."

The paradox of substance, then, is not a clever discovery; it is structural. Any word that names what a thing *is* must, by the grammar of definition, refer at least implicitly to something the thing *is not* — its supports, its surroundings, its ancestors, its boundaries. This is why Locke's celebrated impairment of substance, however philosophically devastating, does not eliminate the function. When pressed for the support of qualities, Locke's user can only answer "something — he knows not what," like the Indian who said the world rests on a great elephant which rests on a great tortoise which rests on something he knew not what. But banishing the term, Burke argues, "far from banishing its functions one merely conceals them." The dramatistic counter-strategy is to dwell on the embarrassments and "potentialities of transformation" of substance-talk, so as to detect its covert influence even where it is overtly absent. The implicit polemic is against Korzybski's general-semantics project and its many heirs, who treat substance-talk as a residue of bad Aristotelian elementarism — but whose own substitute vocabularies, Burke will show in section (ix), reimport substance covertly.

## (iii) Contextual and familial definition

If the master irony of substance is the intrinsic/extrinsic instability, the first two definition-strategies that exploit it are *contextual* and *familial*.

**Contextual definition** locates a thing by placement within its context, surroundings, location, or scene. Burke derives this from *de-finire*, to mark boundaries: to define a thing is to "use terms that possess, implicitly at least, contextual reference." Spinoza is the paradigm. Starting from the Aristotelian notion that substance is to be considered *kath' auto* (in itself; *id quod per se concipitur*), Spinoza observed that nothing less than the totality of all that exists can meet this requirement, so only the universal scene — *nature*, equated pantheistically with *God* — can be considered "in itself"; everything else is "defined" (determined, limited, bounded) by the surrounding others. Hence Spinoza's slogan, *omnis determinatio est negatio*: all determination is negation; to define a thing in terms of its context is to define it in terms of what it is not. Less heroically scaled instances are everywhere. The Southern Agrarians titled their manifesto *I'll Take My Stand* — and Burke notes the productive ambiguity: a stand *in* Dixie is a conditioning, scenic kind of cause; a stand *for* Dixie would be a teleological, purposive kind. Historicists who treat art "in terms of background" are accused of contextual dissolution; their formalist opponents, who treat the work "in itself," are accused of failing to see scene-act, scene-agent, and agent-act ratios at all.

**Familial definition** — Burke's term for the second strategy — defines a substance by ancestral cause. The Christian derivation of mankind and the world from God, the Nazi "blood" philosophy, Plato's theory of forms (each thing imitating its eponym in heaven), and Aristotle's *genus* are all ancestral substance-strategies. *Natura* (from *nasci*, to be born) and Greek *physis* (to grow) carry the biological-derivational sense at the root of "nature." Burke gives the mediaeval *universalia* debate a Burkean dramatist gloss by asking the three questions in tribal form: *does the tribe give birth to its members?* (yes = extreme mediaeval realist, *universale ante rem*); *does the tribe exist in its members?* (yes = Aristotelian, *in re* — anchored in *entelechy*, the individual's potentialities for becoming a fully representative member of its class); *is the tribe merely the name for the sum of its members?* (yes = thorough nominalist, *flatus vocis*, post rem, moving toward the disintegration of tribal thinking). The four-word cluster *general / generic / genetic / genitive*, all from the same root, marks the philological gradient from biological-familial to logical-classificatory: *genitive* unambiguously ancestral, *generic* fading the tribal note, *general* almost extinguishing it. A family of right-angled triangles "in general" no longer evokes generation; yet Burke notes one can still, with a little effort, look on them as a family in the sense that a common set of principles is required for generating them.

The interweaving of contextual and familial strategies is constant. Theologies that treat God as both ancestor of mankind and ground/context of mankind use both at once. And as Burke notes at book p. 28, if one extends the Stance family by including different roots similar in meaning, one moves into a set of live and dead metaphors — *abstractions* — where the five pentad terms, the ten ratios, and the substance-strategies "could be seen emerging in all sorts of places." The Greek *hypokeimenon* (underlying), with its Latin companion *sub-jectum* (thrown under), and the verb *hypotithēmi* (to place under, assume, take for granted) generate the lexical family from which we get thesis, antithesis, synthesis. Latin *pono* (to place) gives proposition, opposition, composition, position, positive, posit — the whole now-you-see-me-and-now-you-don't apparatus by which metaphysicians "posit" principles without being too clear what kind of base those principles are being placed upon.

## (iv) The Survey of Terms and dialectic substance

The Survey of Terms (book pp. 29–33) is a *taxonomic* moment. Burke enumerates "characteristic forms which the grammar of substance may embody in particular calculi" — geometric, familial, directional, dialectic, with nutritive cross-cutting them.

**Geometric substance** is a specialization of the contextual: an object placed in a setting whose relations are logically (and "necessarily") derivable, with Spinoza's Euclidean method as exemplar and Theodore Dreiser's view of motive as "chemism" as a vulgar materialist heir. **Familial substance** in pure form stresses literal biological descent, but in practice "spiritualizes" the family to include all social groups sharing covenant, founder, or originating act — and once doctrines of creation extend familial descent to cover the relationship between craftsman and product, we reach the agent-act ratio in its purest form ("the potter and the pot"). Plotinus's God as *to proton* and Bonaventura's *rationes seminales* (the world's development from seminal reasons) combine logical and biological descent. **Nutritive substance** — the transubstantiation of external elements into internal — names the place where contextual and familial substance merge: the Eucharist; totemic rites; the "internal environment" (Bernard's blood); the biosphere as a single physical situation in which organism and environment merge (Angyal's *Foundations for a Science of Personality*). **Directional substance** — biologically derived from the experience of free locomotion — stresses motion-from-within, the way, the "where are you going?" Its sociological extension yields the language of *tendency* and *trend*, in which the paradox of substance is "not far in the offing": a tendency to do X is by the same token a tendency not to do X. The directional cluster lurks in the word *motivation* itself: *motion* as motive in materialist treatments; *movement* as motive when individual acts are referred to a larger curve; *cause* (Latin *causa*: that by, on account of, or through which anything takes place or is done); *emotion* (I. A. Richards's pun reducing emotion to motion); *moment* in the mystical sense ("eternal present").

**Dialectic substance** is the over-all category Burke now introduces. *Dialectically considered*, men are not only *in* nature: the cultural accretions made possible by the language motive become a "second nature" with them. Symbolic communication is not merely an external instrument but intrinsic to men as agents. Its motivational properties characterize both "the human situation" and what men are "in themselves." Dialectic substance derives its character from the systematic contemplation of the antinomies — from the recognition that we necessarily define a thing in terms of something else. And the something else is not merely *some* other but *the* other: the antagonist. The *agon* — protagonist motivated by the nature of the antagonist — is its sharpest instance. Burke gives the period-political examples (socialist motives unified by resistance to capitalism; the Allied unification by opposition to Hitler), and a deep dialectical pun from Coleridge: *rivales* are the opposite banks of the *same* stream. George Russell's "We become the image of the thing we hate" is the same structure. The devil must be made an angel for the Christian *agonia* to be thoroughly imagined.

The most thoroughgoing dialectical opposition is Being / Not-Being. The contextual approach, by inducing one to postulate a ground for everything-that-is, leads thinkers "by dialectical necessity" to affirm that the only ground of Being is Not-Being. Plotinus carried this to its limit in negative theology — the divine substance designated only by the absence of any attributes such as material existence affords us — and "in his belief that material existence is estrangement from God, he is said to have been unwilling to name either his parents or his birthplace (the abstract concept of dialectical substance here leading him to proclaim his identity by a *negative* reference to the familial and the geometric)."

## (v) The paradox of purity and divisive substance

The paradox of purity (book pp. 35–38) is the chapter's most generative formal device. *When a substance is purified by abstraction to its absolute, it tends toward its negation.* God as "pure" or "absolute" Person is impersonal — and the impersonal is synonymous with the negation of personality; hence Pure Personality = No Personality. Pure Being is Not-Being (Hegel made much of this). Aristotle's God is definable either as "Pure Act" or as complete repose, "a rest that is eternal, unchangeable, immovable." Leibniz's "virtual innateness of ideas" — which Burke glosses as "unaware awarenesses" — works the same paradox: the most innate would be unaware. Kandinsky's claim that to the uninitiate the "inner beauty" of music must seem ugliness; Parker Tyler on Julien Gracq's *Château d'Argol* (where the road is "pure direction" because behind, the way has ceased to be); Harold Rosenberg on democratic art trending toward purity precisely as escape, with the corollary that pure escape is perpetual imprisonment in escape. With regard to symbolic literature, Burke names the paradox in slogans: *witheriness of withinness*, *atop-the-atop*. He cites Melville (the "blackness of darkness" sermon in *Moby Dick*), Andrew Marvell ("annihilating all that's made / to a green thought in a green shade"), and Parker Tyler again reviewing a judo manual that treats combat as a "pure" art done for love of the figures.

The paradox of purity yields, as one of its proper subspecies, *divisive substance*. Any term for a collective motivation — class, nation, "the general will" — becomes "pure" when matched against an individual locus of motivation. The collective motive is thus the *negation* of the individual motive; yet it may also be treated as the source from which the individual motive is familially derived. A soldier may be *nationally* motivated to kill his country's enemies even though *individually* he is motivated by a horror of killing his own; conversely, the patriot may sacrifice himself for his country and yet, as an individual, wish to profit. Or a man's business code may differ from his private code so greatly that he is a man of "two substances" — a "split personality" or "divided substance." These histories embody a *grammatical form* which guarantees that we should not expect dualism of motives to be automatically dissolved by science (as some optimistic apologists of science believe ethics will become unnecessary in a scientific world).

## (vi) Dialectic of tragedy: *poiēma / pathēma / mathēma*

Burke now develops the dialectic of tragedy (book pp. 38–41) as a tightly structured grammar of dialectic substance. Galileo, characterizing experimental testing as an "ordeal," gives Burke his point of entry: a dialectical-agonistic approach to knowledge proceeds through the *act* of assertion, whereby one "suffers" the kind of knowledge that is the reciprocal of one's act. This is the tragic process: the agent's action involves a corresponding passion, and from the suffering of the passion there arises an understanding of the act, an understanding that transcends the act. The act, in being an assertion, calls forth a counter-assertion from the elements that compose its context; and when the agent is enabled to see in terms of this counter-assertion, the agent has transcended the state characterizing him at the start. In the final state of tragic vision, intrinsic and extrinsic motivations are merged.

Burke supports the reading philologically. The Greek proverb *ta pathēmata mathēmata* — "the suffered is the learned" — is, in his interpretation, properly a triadic structure. *Mathēma* means both "things learned" generally and the "mathematical" sciences (arithmetic, geometry, astronomy) — and a relation between dialectic and mathematics can be glimpsed in Plato's stress upon mathematics. *Pathēma* (same root as the English *passive*) is the opposite of a *poiēma* (a deed, doing, action, act — anything done; a poem); a *pathēma* can refer variously to suffering, misfortune, passive condition, situation, state of mind. The initial requirement for tragedy, however, is an *action*. Hence the proverb, complete at the risk of redundancy, would carry three terms: *poiēmata, pathēmata, mathēmata* — the act organizes the opposition (bringing to the fore whatever factors resist or modify the act), the agent "suffers" this opposition, and as he learns to take the oppositional motives into account, widening his terminology accordingly, he arrives at a higher order of understanding. The three distinctions can collapse into a single "moment," allowing movement from one to the others in any order.

A similar grammar (with a similar attenuation of the tragic) is discernible beneath the scholastic *intelligere est pati* — "to understand is to be affected by" — which can be translated very broadly. *Pati*'s deponent verb carries the range: to bear, support, suffer, endure, be afflicted with, pass a life of suffering, permit, experience, undergo, be in a certain state of mind, "have a passive nature." Understanding would be "passive" in the sense that its way of sizing things up is moulded by the actual state of affairs. The dramatistic character of this realistic formula is best appreciated when contrasted with Berkeley's subsequent idealist formula *esse est percipi* — "to be is to be perceived" — which reverses the direction by treating *actuality* in terms of *knowledge*.

Burke ends the subsection with a sharply Burkean pun. Strictly speaking, the act of an agent would be the movement not of one *moved* but of a *mover* (a mover of the self or of something else by the self). For an act is by definition active, whereas to be moved (or motivated) is by definition passive. To consider an *act* in terms of its *grounds* is to consider it in terms of what it is not, namely, in terms of motives that, in acting upon the active, would make it a passive. The concept of activation thus implies a kind of *passive-behind-the-passive*; for an agent who is "motivated by his passions" would be "moved by his being-movedness," "acted upon by his state of being acted upon." Lear's complaint that he is "more sinned against than sinning" exhibits the two integral aspects of tragedy, action and passion, becoming dissociated.

## (vii) Actus and status

The actus/status subsection (book pp. 41–43) is the chapter's tightest articulation with the pentad. Burke reduces the *poiēma / pathēma / mathēma* triad to a dyad by collapsing *pathēma* and *mathēma* — since *pathēma* means not only suffering but state-of-mind, condition, and knowledge is a state. Reduced to a dichotomy, the relation can be formalized as *act* and *state*, *actus* and *status*. The pair has many possibilities. Often it coincides with the action-passion alignment; at other points it diverges. A "passion" and a "state of mind" are much the same, but we strongly distinguish a political *state* and a political *passion*. The basic contrast between motion and rest is a variant. The contrast between drama and lyric is another (drama centers in an action; the lyric arrests some one mood or moment). The traditional faith / knowledge pair — *pistis* / *gnōsis*, *fides* / *intellectus* — is an instance of the same grammatical form: faith as *act* ("an act of faith"), knowledge as the *state* derived from the act, just as the tragic hero's action attains its rest and summation in his understanding. (Faith is also an act in the nature of God as man's ground, so the faith / knowledge pair has a scenic reference as well.) From here it is but a brief step to the act-scene pair: the knowledge derived from the act is a knowledge of the act's context.

Philologically, the actus/status pair characterizes a major historical development. The Greek word for "virtue" — *aretē* — and the Latin *virtus* are from the same root as *Ares* (god of war) and the Latin words for art and military arms; "prowess" would be a good translation in their origins. Gradually the concept of virtue came to place less stress upon action per se and more upon the *potentialities of action*. A parallel transformation befell "hero": a hero is first of all a man who does heroic things; next, a hero is one with the potentialities for heroic action (soldiers on the way to the wars are heroes in this sense; their heroism resides in their *status as soldiers*); finally, a man may be considered a hero because he *had done* heroic things — though in his present *state* as a hero he may be too old or weak to do such acts at all. "Virtues" may become in the end purely *states of mind*, or proper attitudes; or *not killing, not stealing, not coveting* — sheer negations.

Sociologically, the actus-to-status movement involves **class substance**. Different occupational acts each have their corresponding *properties*, and out of these differences in properties develop, in time, corresponding differences in *status*. Notions of familial substance remain prominent in class-thinking. Because occupational action requires properties, and property is not an act but a state, ruling classes drift from a stage where actus and status are co-balanced to a stage where the sense of inherited position is stressed. (Sometimes a new kind of act, not germane to the originating state, takes hold — a nobility whose privileges grew out of horsemanship-in-war turning to pageantry and sport, employing inherited privileges to hire or command others to fight in its place.) Kingship is originally an act, like heroism; but gradually, as inherited, it becomes a sheer state, the nature of the king's extrinsic properties enabling him to be a king by reason of their substantiality alone. He can *be* a king while commissioning ministers to perform the kingly acts in his stead. (In the mediaeval frame, the primary act, the act of God, is *to be*.) Louis XIV's "L'état c'est moi" makes natural the merger of two meanings of state: the state as governmental property and the state as the property of kingship — the administrative and tax-gathering machinery and the royal domains could be looked upon as an extension of the king's person, a property of his character like facial traits.

Burke closes by noting that the actus-status alignment also maps onto potentiality/actuality. Status considered as potentiality and actus as its actualization is the logic he will develop more fully later. The pair is also the principal site at which the substance vocabulary articulates with the Act-Agent ratio of the pentad: occupational acts harden into agentive properties, agentive properties harden into class-defining status, and the resulting alignments stabilize whole motivational economies.

## (viii) Universal motives, intrinsic and extrinsic

The next two subsections (Universal Motives as Substance, book pp. 43–46; Intrinsic and Extrinsic, book pp. 46–51) deploy the apparatus on the largest scenic scales.

**Universal motives**. All gods are substances — names for motives or combinations of motives. Polytheistic divinities, besides their personalistic aspects, often represent decidedly geometric or scenic motivations: local gods are theological prototypes of environmentalist or geographic motives. A river-god is different from a mountain-god because a river calls for a different set of human actions than a mountain. Universal religions, proclaiming a single principle of divinity, supply believers with an over-all substance that travels with them across local scenes; the believer's *hypostasis* — sense of personal identity, of a real motivational substratum underlying him — survives the diversity of local textures. The British official's habit of dressing for dinner in the Empire's remotest spots is in effect the transporting of an idol, the vessel of a motive that has its sanctuary in the homeland. Once the monotheistic motive becomes embodied in a structure of world empire it has usually been transformed into its secular analogue, *the monetary motive*: the incentive of monetary profit, like the One God, can be felt to prevail as a global source of action over and above any motivations peculiar to the locale; it serves the needs of empire precisely because it "transcends" religious motives, making for tolerant commerce among men whose religious vocabularies differ widely. Yet the more diffused such a motive, the more it must adopt "adjectival" modifications peculiar to specific local needs; "saints" and local nationalisms are the polytheistic re-introductions internal to a monotheism (incipiently polytheistic).

The Marxist calculus is the chapter's worked example. As industrialization spreads under capitalism, workers share the same social motives because they share the "factory situation" — the scene that shapes their acts and their natures as agents. Stated in terms of money, the motive common to the workers is *wage slavery* — universal wherever wages and taxes are paid in symbols rather than in kind. The over-all capitalist motive divides into two broad economic classes (possessors and dispossessed), each status analyzable as a different substance, a contrasting bundle of motives. Translated dramatistically: the sheer *work* in a factory would not be an *act* but mere *motion*. It becomes *actus* only when the workers' status is understood in terms of socialist organization — an act of revolutionary import, since the sheer ownership of the factories is a *state*, and the property relation correspondingly passive, while the proletarian relation becomes increasingly active. But reversals in keeping with the antinomies complicate the pattern: the owners' state, in its governmental aspects, is anything but passive — the property structure contains an act of expropriation, since workers receive less than they produce, and the structure of the state is designed to keep that act in force. The socialist revolution will reverse the state and then "wither" it — but Burke's grammar leads him to doubt whether a state can really wither in a complex industrial society. No continuity of social act is possible without a corresponding social status; the antinomy persists.

**Intrinsic and Extrinsic**. The treatment of material properties as a *state* brings actus/status into line with intrinsic/extrinsic. Burke quotes Hegel's *Philosophy of History*: as the essence of Matter is Gravity, so the essence of Spirit is Freedom. Matter has its essence out of itself; Spirit is *self-contained existence* (*Bei-sich-selbst-sein*). "If I am dependent, my being is referred to something else which I am not." Matter is here equated with the extrinsic, Spirit with the intrinsic. But before Hegel has proceeded far, his remarks on the relation between potential and actual lead into the peculiarly Hegelian theory of the State as the vessel of freedom: the Spirit is free, we are told, and the State is "the perfect embodiment of Spirit." The intrinsic and the extrinsic begin to change places. The ambiguity is detectable by experimentally shifting the accent: "embodiment of Spirit" versus "*embodiment* of Spirit"; "materialization of Spirit" versus "*materialization* of Spirit." The expression is got by merging antithetical terms; whichever is stressed, the formula can be made to mean its near-opposite. (This is precisely the operation the Marxist reversal of Hegel exploits.) As a reliable rule of thumb: whenever a distinction between the internal and the external, the intrinsic and the extrinsic, the within and the without (Korzybski's *inside-the-skin* and *outside-the-skin*) is invoked, the paradoxes of substance are nearby.

The gerontologist's "intrinsic resistance to disease" — proposed as a target for chemical stimulation — is Burke's anti-paradigm. "Intrinsic resistance" places motivational sourcing within the body as agent. But the use of chemical means to stimulate this motive transforms it from intrinsic to extrinsic, since the agent now becomes the channel through which the chemical materials (administered "from without") affect the chemistry of the body. Indeed, since the body is chemistry, and all outside the body is chemistry, the very mode of thought that forms a concept of the "intrinsic" in these terms must by the same terms dissolve it. The intrinsic must also not be confused with the *unique*: the most common fallacy is the equating of intrinsic value with what distinguishes a thing from all members of its class. A poet's lines are not made distinct by their differences from every other poet's lines alone; their intrinsic value resides also in attributes the work shares with many other poets. *The differentiated also has significant attributes as members of its class.*

Spinoza's *causa sui* — substance as "the cause of itself" — marks the limit. Burke contrasts supernaturalist and humanist strategies. Supernaturalist strategies derive the attributes of human substance and motive from God as their ancestral source, assigning a *derived* dignity to man. Humanist strategies situate the motivational principles within human agents themselves, assigning an *inherent* or *intrinsic* dignity. Any motive humanistically postulated in the agent would be a *causa sui* insofar as it is not deduced from any cause outside itself. Since agents require placement in scenes, humanism gets its scenic counterpart in naturalism — the values of life founded in human nature itself, in human-with-human social relationships, not deduced from any cause "outside the constitution of man and nature" (Dewey, quoted at book p. 50). "Foundation" and "constitution" are themselves stance-words, capable of merging intrinsic and extrinsic reference.

## (ix) The rhetoric of substance and the centrality thesis

The Rhetoric of Substance (book pp. 51–53), Two Kinds of Departure (book pp. 53–55), and The Centrality of Substance (book pp. 55–58) deploy the apparatus on the working tactics of ordinary discourse and arrive at the chapter's closing thesis.

The ambiguity of substance, Burke writes, affords "a major resource of rhetoric." The simplest tactic is the adverbial: "*substantially* true," "*essentially* free," "*in principle* valid," "*in the long run* warranted." Each of these adverbs lets a speaker assert the identity of a state of affairs at a level of generality that cannot be falsified by the particulars. A slave could be defined in Christian doctrine as "substantially free" by reason of qualities inherited "substantially" from his creator, even where the case does not justify the usage grammatically. A judge ruled a citizens' petition with mixed genuine and false signatures "substantially fraudulent"; the higher court reversed by ruling that the genuine signatures should be considered "in themselves, not contextually." Positivists who would discard the category of substance assert that the only meaningful propositions are those capable of scientific proof — and then blandly concede that the proof is not always possible *actually*, but must be possible *in principle*. "Principle" is etymologically a "first," a beginning, a commencement, an origin — so the positivists who reimport substance under "in-principle" are reaffirming exactly the ancestral substance-logic they professed to outlaw. Or *universally valid*: to say a proposition is "valid" is to say that "in principle" it can secure the universal agreement of all who abide by scientific method — a stance-word disguised as a method-word.

Two Kinds of Departure formalizes a related point. Because the five pentad terms can be considered as "principles" with overlapping margins, a thinker can use one term as point of departure in two opposite senses. *Consistent*: derive consequences from the term's nature (naturalism beginning as man-in-nature derives the dignity of man from nature). *Compensatory*: depart from the term toward its near-antithesis (naturalism, transformed by technology and the monetary, becomes the name for an applied-science philosophy whose ideals are almost the antithesis of nature). Religious doctrines of "personalism" may be formulated not because the individual is in a position of paramount importance but precisely because he is *not* — to take up the slack. The first reasoning proceeds by "therefore," the second by "however." Descartes (per his editor Veitch) gave "new proofs of the existence of God" that subtly changed the term's content; those who understood by "God" only the character possessing the attributes of the *old* proofs were justified in calling Descartes an atheist. By the time the departure has become obvious, the new order has become stable enough that the old god-term can be discarded for another. *Operationalism*, modeled after technological procedures, would, if carried out rigorously, lead to the *stabilization* of technological operations rather than to the development of new ones; "naturalism" leads via technology away from nature; perhaps "operationalism" leads, in the name of operations, away from operations.

The Centrality of Substance closes the chapter. Modern science, in proposing to abandon the categories of substance and causality, has done speculation a good turn — it has clarified the difference between philosophic and scientific terminologies of motivation. Philosophy, like common sense, must think human motivation dramatistically, in terms of action and its ends; but a science is freed of philosophic taints only insofar as it confines itself to terms of motion and arrested motion (figure, structure). This convention, almost Puritanical in its severity ("surely we should not be far wrong in calling it a secularized variant of Puritanism"), has brought about magnifications of human powers so vast that any "objection" has about as much force as an attempt to "refute" Niagara Falls. But spectacular results do not justify abiding by the same terminological conventions when *treating* of human motives — for one could confine the study of action within the terms of motion only by resigning oneself to gross misrepresentations of life as we normally experience it. Burke makes a modest concession: one might grant that the treatment of motives in terms of "action" and "substance" is wholly fallacious, yet defend it as central to the placement of statements *about* motives. The pentad need not be the philosopher's stone, but it is "a philosopher's stone for the synopsis of writings that have sought the philosopher's stone." Even the most modern scientific tracts can be adequately placed as a development in this long line. From this point of view, terminologies of motion and "conditioning" are to be treated as *dialectical* enterprises designed to *transcend* terminologies of action and substance.

Burke closes with the *citable-realities* thesis. Each time you scrutinize a concept of substance, it dissolves into thin air; but the moment you relax your gaze, it re-forms. Things *do* have intrinsic natures, whatever may be the quandaries that crowd in as soon as we attempt to decide definitively what those intrinsic natures are. Korzybski's aversion against Aristotelian *elementarism* is so evasive a treatment of the subject that "in a very long book he contrives to convey little more than a negative attitude towards it." The transformations Burke has been studying as a grammar "are not 'illusions,' but citable realities." The structural relations involved are observable — nothing is more imperiously there for observation and study than the tactics people employ when they would injure or gratify one another, and one can readily demonstrate the role of substantiation in such tactics. *To call a man a friend or brother is to proclaim him consubstantial with oneself, one's values or purposes. To call a man a bastard is to attack his whole line, his "authorship," his "principle" or "motive" (as expressed in terms of the familial). An epithet assigns substance doubly, for in stating the character of the object it at the same time contains an implicit program of action with regard to the object, thus serving as motive.* This is the chapter's productive forward-anchor for *A Rhetoric of Motives* (1950): the consubstantiation apparatus that the next volume will develop into its principal rhetorical category is here named, demonstrated, and grounded in the substance vocabulary that this chapter has elaborated.

## (x) Substance-as-apparatus alongside the pentad

The eighteen substance types catalogued in the JSON companion file form, with the five pentad terms and ten dyadic ratios established in GM-00 and GM-01, the third major component of Burke's analytical apparatus. The relationship is not parallel-but-equal; it is parallel-but-functionally-distinct. The pentad asks *which determinate value does this motive-statement fix in each of five interrogative slots?* The substance vocabulary asks *by what mode does this motive-statement stabilize the values it fixes?* A motive-statement that features Scene as its central locus could deploy contextual substance (Spinoza's universal scene), geometric substance (the Euclidean derivation of consequents), nutritive substance (the agent constituted by environmental ingestion), or directional substance (the agent moved by larger-curve trends). A motive-statement that features Agent could deploy familial substance (class derived from ancestry), divisive substance (the agent split between collective and individual loci), tragic-dialectic substance (the agent constituted through suffering), or consubstantial substance (the agent identified by epithet). A motive-statement that features Act enacts the actus/status pair as its central grammar: act-as-motion vs. act-as-state; act-as-potential vs. act-as-actual; faith-as-act vs. knowledge-as-state. A motive-statement that features Agency frequently exploits rhetorical substance — the adverbial qualifiers "substantially," "in principle," "in the long run" that mark how the linguistic means deploys the substance category. A motive-statement that features Purpose typically deploys universal-monetary substance (the One God / Gold Standard supplying an over-all motive across local scenes) and/or the paradox of purity (the god-term flipping into its negation when intensified).

This is not a tidy bijection — the substance types criss-cross the pentad. But that criss-crossing is precisely the apparatus's analytical strength. The Phase 3 substance-vocabulary synthesis deliverable can therefore be built as a two-axis matrix: pentad term × substance-mode, with paradigmatic examples in each cell, illuminating exactly how Burke's apparatus diagnoses any given motive-statement at two analytical depths simultaneously.

The chapter's meta-thesis — that substantiation is a citable reality, not an illusion — is what makes this two-axis apparatus *dramatistic* rather than merely *taxonomic*. Burke is not cataloguing strategies a thinker may freely choose; he is naming the structural mechanisms by which all motive-talk is unavoidably organized. Every motive-statement *is* substantiated in one or more of these modes whether the speaker recognizes the substantiation or not — and recovering the recognition is exactly what a Grammar of Motives is for.

## (xi) Cross-pipeline bridges

**Aristotle (exegetical)** — This chapter offers the clearest exegetical bridge to the existing Aristotle Complete Works index. Burke cites Aristotle directly five times in the chapter (book pp. 25, 27, 35, 41, 50 region) on: (i) *kath' auto* as the criterion of being-in-itself, which Spinoza inherits and radicalizes (book p. 25); (ii) *entelechy* as the individual's potentialities for being a fully representative member of its class — the formal anchor of familial substance (book p. 27); (iii) God as "Pure Act" or eternal repose — the limit case of the paradox of purity (book p. 35); (iv) the active/passive grammar of pati that frames the dialectic of tragedy and the actus/status pair (book p. 41); and (v) implicitly through Spinoza's *causa sui* (book p. 50), which derives from the Aristotelian unmoved-mover problematic. The actus/status pair maps directly onto Aristotle's *energeia*/*dynamis* and onto the act/potency analysis of *Metaphysics* Θ and *Categories*. This is the strongest cross-pipeline edge in GM-02.

**Heidegger SZ §§9–13 (anticipatory-projection)** — Burke 1945 is pre-Heidegger-reception in English (Phase 0 cite-check returned zero Heidegger hits in the volume). Yet the paradox-of-substance section and the intrinsic/extrinsic instability anticipate, with remarkable precision, *Sein und Zeit*'s critique of *Vorhandenheit* substance-metaphysics. The very *Vorstellung* family that Burke names as a member of the Stance family in his opening paragraph is the German vocabulary whose metaphysical commitments Heidegger destructs in §§19–21 (the analysis of Descartes's *res extensa*). Burke's *consist, constancy, constitution, contrast, destiny, existence, hypostatize, obstacle, stage, state, status, statute, stead, subsist, substance, system* — and German *stellen / Vorstellung* — is essentially Heidegger's destructive target vocabulary captured from the opposite philological direction. The cross-pipeline anchor for the dissertation will be: Burke's substance grammar provides a *grammatical-rhetorical* topography of the same vocabulary whose *ontological* deconstruction Heidegger undertakes — anticipatory parallel rather than influence.

**Heidegger BCAP (anticipatory-projection)** — Heidegger's *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* lecture course converges with Burke's chapter at two specific points. First, BCAP's reading of *energeia*/*dynamis* and *kinēsis* parallels Burke's actus/status pair and his treatment of motion/rest as a substance-mode. Second, BCAP's analysis of *pathos* as load-bearing for the ontology of action (with Heidegger reading *pathos* not as a "passion" but as a determinative ontological structure) parallels Burke's *intelligere est pati* and his treatment of the dialectic-of-tragedy triad. The active-passive grammar that Burke locates in *pati* / *paschein* (book pp. 40–41) is exactly the grammar Heidegger's BCAP makes structurally fundamental.

**Burke's *Rhetoric of Motives* (1950, pending forward pipeline)** — The closing thesis on consubstantial epithets (book p. 57) is the load-bearing forward-anchor. The 1950 volume develops what is here named and demonstrated into the apparatus of *consubstantiation* / *identification* that organizes the rhetorical project. The Phase 3 cross-pipeline synthesis should flag this edge as the strongest forward bridge in *A Grammar of Motives*: when the *Rhetoric* pipeline is built, Phase 4 should retroactively annotate GM-02 §xi as the canonical Grammar-side anchor for *Rhetoric*'s identification chapters.

**In-Game (Calleja 2011, anticipatory)** — The substance vocabulary anticipates a useful operationalization for game-user motive analysis. Calleja's six PIM dimensions (kinesthetic, spatial, shared, narrative, affective, ludic) can each be read as a substance-mode by which the player-as-agent is placed within the game-as-scene. Kinesthetic involvement is directional substance with embodied actuation. Spatial involvement is contextual substance in Burke's strict sense. Shared involvement is consubstantial substance — players identifying with co-players or with represented agents by epithets and shared frames. Narrative involvement combines familial substance (character lineage and motive-ancestry) with tragic-dialectic substance (the act-passion-learning arc that defines plot). Affective involvement is intrinsic/extrinsic in the gerontologist's sense — emotion as motivation simultaneously inside-the-skin and provoked-by-scene. Ludic involvement is actus/status applied to play — the ongoing act of play vs. the achieved state of skill, score, level. The Phase 3 cross-pipeline graph can deploy this mapping as a two-axis structure: PIM dimension × substance mode.

**Uexküll (anticipatory Layer C)** — Burke's nutritive-substance argument (book pp. 30–31) — in which Angyal's *Foundations for a Science of Personality* is cited for the morphological-distinction-between-organism-and-environment-is-impossible thesis — directly anticipates the *Umwelt* analytic. The two arrive at structurally similar pictures of motive-locus as bounded subjectively-constituted environment, from non-overlapping traditions; the convergence is parallel-discovery, not influence, and the Phase 3 graph must flag it as such.

The chapter is, finally, what Phase 0 named it: the volume's most subsection-dense Part 1 chapter — thirteen subsections in thirty-eight pages — and the canonical source for the substance-vocabulary synthesis deliverable. Its grammar of substance is parallel to but not part of the pentad; the eighteen substance types here catalogued, together with their internal cross-relations and their articulation with the Act-Agent ratio via actus/status, supply the second analytical apparatus by which the *Grammar* diagnoses the structure of motive-statements across every philosophic idiom in human discourse.
