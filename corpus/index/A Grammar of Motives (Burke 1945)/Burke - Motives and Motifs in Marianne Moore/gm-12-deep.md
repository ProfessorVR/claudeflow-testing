## i. Overview — Moore's oeuvre as poetic strategy

Burke's third appendix opens with an unusually direct programmatic sentence: he will "characterize the substance of Miss Moore's work as a specific poetic strategy." Two interpretive commitments are bundled here that govern the entire essay. First, the analytic *object* is not any single Moore poem but the substance of her oeuvre — what she repeatedly does across two collections (*Selected Poems*, 1935, and *What Are Years*, 1941, with *Observations*, 1924, as backstop). Second, the analytic *idiom* is substance-as-strategy: substance is not a metaphysical residue inside the works but the recurrent configuration of motivational choices visible only when the works are read together. This double commitment makes GM-12 the volume's most explicit demonstration of *oeuvre-level dramatism*. Where GM-10 (the Keats appendix) had worked the entire Ode on a Grecian Urn as a single dramatistic exhibit, GM-12 widens the unit of analysis to a body of work and shows the apparatus scaling.

The substantive thesis Burke develops is that Moore's poetry is organized by a relation between "external and internal, or visible and invisible, or background and personality" (book p. 485). Her poems characteristically place a finely observed object — an animal, a clock, a glacier, a butterfly — in such a way that the observation discloses something that is *not* the observed object, while leaving the observed object's own integrity intact. Moore herself names this axis in the epigraph Burke seizes upon: "The power of the visible / is the invisible." Burke reads her earlier title *Observations* as a "deceptively technical synonym for 'visions'" — Moore observes visibles to be visionary about the corresponding invisibles.

This puts pressure on the taxonomic labels Moore had inherited from her milieu. Burke distinguishes Symbolism (subject dominates object), Imagism (subject and object balanced), and Objectivism (object, once subjectively selected, is studied in its own right) as "recipes all having the same ingredients but in different proportions" (book p. 486). The proportion-theory of literary idioms is one of the chapter's load-bearing methodological transferables: a label like "objectivist" is rejected as exhaustive ("only by taking away the epithet in part") and replaced by a substance-diagnosis that registers which ingredients dominate. Moore's signature, on Burke's reading, is objectivist surface backed by selectivity so disciplined that the surface alone betrays a stable motivational substance.

The chapter's three internal sections (the roman-numeral breaks fall near book pp. 491 and 495) progressively deepen the substance-claim: Section I establishes the visible/invisible axis and the act/scene/agent vocabulary Burke openly attributes to "the philosophy of drama embodied in Henry James's prefaces"; Section II develops the synecdochic reversibility of motifs ("a chasm in the sea, then, becomes rather the sea in a chasm") and the captive-affirmation pattern; Section III names the *substantiality* condition ("all the branches spread from a single trunk") and reads contractility, jeopardy, and the menagerie-as-thesaurus-of-protectivenesses as the unifying motivational trunk.

## ii. Motives and motifs

The chapter's title gives the key methodological term-pair. *Motives* are the underlying motivational structures (the substance, the strategy, the why-behind-the-why); *motifs* are the recurrent surface elements (animals, water, clocks, armor, the visible/invisible dyad) whose convergence is the evidence-base from which motives are inferred. Burke's working procedure is to track motifs across poems, observe their family-resemblance, and then propose the motivational substance whose unity they jointly express.

The motifs Burke catalogs are a recognizable Moore inventory: armored animals (pangolin, jerboa, elephant, paper nautilus, fish, snail); birds (the captive bird "grown taller as he sings"; the guillemot, the hen of the heath, the linnet "spinet-sweet"; the fledgling mocking-birds and the stalking cat); water — in particular water rising under captivity ("water in motion is far from level… when obstacles happened to bar / the path, rise automatically"); precision-instruments and time-pieces (the four quartz clocks, the Waterford glass weight); creatures-in-fitting-scenes (the basilisk in its basilica, the jerboa "honoring the sand by assuming its colour"); and the recurrent vocabulary of fastidiousness, contractility, compression, jeopardy.

Burke insists, against the would-be plank-and-platform style of literary cataloging, that these motifs are not additive. They form a substance: "all the branches spread from a single trunk" (book p. 496). This is the chapter's signature axiom and the dissertation's load-bearing transferable. A genuine motivational substance is recognizable by branch-convergence: the motifs of a substantively unified oeuvre converge on a single underlying configuration even though they appear in different poems, different surfaces, and different ostensible subject-matters.

The unifying configuration Burke proposes for Moore is a complex of *appreciation*, *contractility*, *jeopardy*, and *withinness-of-withinness*. Appreciation is the redemptive operation that converts complaint into precision: where one might be querulous about a thing's imperfections, one chooses instead to be precise about its properties. Burke explicitly invokes Henry James's *Spoils of Poynton* preface, where James calls appreciation "the closest secular equivalent to the religious motive of glorification." Contractility — the term Burke borrows from Moore's own "To a Snail" — names the compression of style that is simultaneously a moral virtue: the snail's occipital horn is the principle of style. Jeopardy names the threat-under-which contractility operates; Moore's armored animals are not merely armored but defensively contracted against a stalking world. Withinness-of-withinness, the "motives-behind-motives" theme, names the doubling Burke locates in Moore's clocks-for-clocks: not merely the interior but the interior of the interior.

## iii. The pentad in Moore

GM-12's pentad-application is unusual in two respects. First, Burke is openly transferential about the source: he uses "act, scene, and agent (I use the three terms central to the philosophy of drama embodied in Henry James's prefaces)" — naming Henry James, not Aristotle or his Part 2 philosophic-school sequence, as the immediate provenance for the working three-term sub-pentad. This is a striking authorial gesture: Burke acknowledges that the dramatistic vocabulary he has built across the *Grammar* has, for the worked Moore-oeuvre case, a Jamesian rather than a philosophic-school source. The dissertation can mine this gesture: the pentad is portable across idiomatic homes, and an oeuvre-level application can adopt its term-source from inside the corpus being analyzed (literary criticism in Moore's case) rather than from outside.

Second, only three of the five pentad terms are actively featured. Burke works act, scene, and agent throughout; agency and purpose remain recessive. The dominant ratios are *act–agent* (Moore's signature attentive act is diagnostic of Moore-as-agent: the act expresses the agent's character) and *scene–act* (descriptive scene is read as logical compositional ground of an assertive act). A subsidiary *scene–agent* ratio operates in poems like "People's Surroundings" where each agent is paired with the scene appropriate to its role — Moore's catalogue of correspondence "between various kinds of agents and the scenes related to their roles" (book p. 488). The basilisk lives in a basilica; the jerboa honors the sand by becoming sand-colored; the elephant speaks for itself out of its tree-trunk image.

The reading of "Spenser's Ireland" exhibits the scene–act ratio doing compositional work. The poem ends with the assertion "I am troubled, I'm dissatisfied, I'm Irish." Burke treats this terminal assertion as the *act* of the poem and the preceding descriptive material — family, marriage, independence, captive belief, birds — as the *scene* that contains the same quality as the act. The descriptive surface is the logical ground of the final assertion: "Surrounded with details appropriate to my present mood … I, an Irish girl … am dissatisfied." The objectivist surface dissolves into a covert lyric whose scene–act ratio is its compositional engine.

Burke pushes further into Moore's grammar by proposing that her objectivist idiom systematically rewrites *because* as *and*. Where the romantic lyrist says "I am sad because the birds are singing thus," Moore says "There are such and such birds — and birds sing thus and so — and I am sad" (book p. 491). The conjunction-for-causation substitution preserves objectivist surface — no causal pretension is mounted — while encoding the same scene–act ratio at depth. This is one of the chapter's strongest microclaims and a dissertation-portable diagnostic: a corpus that systematically conjoins where it could subordinate may be expressing a scene–act ratio in objectivist clothing.

The strongest pentad-moment in the chapter is the trunk-as-act exhibit. Reading "Smooth Gnarled Crape Myrtle" — where the elephant's trunk appears in passing as "an at will heavy thing" — Burke argues that the trunk-image is illegible as mere thing-perception because the earlier "Black Earth" poem has already charged the image with the elephant's volitional self-assertion ("my back is full of the history of power"; "that tree-trunk without / roots, accustomed to shout / its own thoughts to itself"). The condensed later usage is legible as an *act* only when read against the earlier overt usage. Burke calls this "a glimpse of realism in its purity": the object reveals itself as a volition, a thing whose substance is its motivational charge. This is the chapter's single most-dense pentad-statement and the methodological pivot at which oeuvre-level analysis becomes load-bearing — the analyst *needs* the cross-poem sediment to read the surface poem rightly.

## iv. Burke's symbolic-strategy method

The methodological apparatus Burke deploys can be reconstructed as a sequence of analytic moves. First, *establish the corpus boundary*: Burke names exactly two volumes plus a backstop. The corpus is finite, known, and named. Second, *catalog recurrent motifs* across the corpus without pre-committing to which are central; let the inventory accumulate. Third, *identify cross-motif convergence*: if armored animals, water rising under captivity, contractility, and the visible/invisible axis all converge on a single motivational configuration, the substance is candidate-confirmed. Fourth, *diagnose the dominant pentad term(s) and ratio(s)*: which of act/scene/agent/agency/purpose is featured? Fifth, *use one paradigmatic poem as exegetical key*: "Black Earth," "To a Snail," "Spenser's Ireland," and the butterfly poem function as Moore-internal exhibits that decode the rest. Sixth, *test the substance against refutation*: if the motifs do not converge, the proposed substance is wrong. Seventh, *read each artifact as both finished and provisional*: "a usage in any given poem is a finished thing … but becomes in effect 'work in progress' when we align it with kindred usages" (book p. 497).

Three secondary procedures are worth naming. The *proportion theory* of idioms: rather than classifying a body of work under one label, diagnose the proportions of its constitutive ingredients (in Moore: dominantly objectivist, with symbolist motivation conditioning selection and imagist balance partially preserved). The *substantiality test*: motifs of genuine poetry are branches of a single trunk; an additive list of themes is a sign that the analyst has missed the substance. The *because → and diagnostic*: a corpus whose grammar systematically substitutes conjunction for causation may be encoding scene–act ratio in non-causal clothing.

The Henry James borrowing is also methodologically substantive. Burke openly attributes the act/scene/agent triad in this chapter to James's prefaces rather than to his own Part 2 architecture. This signals that oeuvre-level dramatism is portable across idiomatic homes — the pentad's vocabulary can be sourced from inside the corpus's own critical tradition. For a dissertation operating on game-corpora, this licenses sourcing the working vocabulary from inside game studies (Calleja's PIM dimensions, for example) rather than mechanically importing Burke's five terms whole.

A final feature is Burke's permission to *read against the surface*. Moore looks descriptive, looks objectivist, looks methodologically Imagist. Burke argues that the cross-poem motivational pattern shows the surface to be misleading. "Behind her 'objectivist' study and editorializing, what are essentially the lineaments of a lyric." Oeuvre-level analysis licenses reading against the apparent idiom of any individual work — because the substance is recoverable only across the corpus, the substance can correct any single work's surface classification.

## v. Chapter as template for oeuvre/corpus-level dramatistic analysis

GM-12 is the dissertation's methodological template for applying dramatism at *corpus scale*. The transfer is not by analogy but by structural identity: wherever the unit of analysis can be a body of work rather than a single artifact, the procedure of GM-12 applies. Three dissertation-relevant targets follow directly.

**Player-corpus dramatism.** A player's recorded sessions across N games, or a community's accumulated forum/wiki archive, or a streamer's video catalog, is structurally a Moore-oeuvre. The analyst catalogs recurrent player-motifs (route preferences, build types, self-imposed constraints, social postures, recurrent rhetorical moves in community discourse) and tests whether they converge on a single motivational substance. If a player's body of play across roguelikes, FromSoftware titles, and immersive sims all displays the same configuration of mastery-display-through-self-imposed-constraint, that is the player's motivational substance — readable only across the corpus, not in any single session. The pentad diagnosis follows: which terms are featured? Calleja's six PIM dimensions provide a target-vocabulary native to game studies that maps onto the Burkean pentad (kinesthetic ↔ Agency; spatial ↔ Scene; shared ↔ Agent; narrative ↔ Scene+Purpose; affective ↔ Act; ludic ↔ Act+Agency). A community-corpus featuring kinesthetic-spatial dimensions features the agency-scene ratio; a narrative-shared corpus features scene-agent. The analyst diagnoses the proportions.

**Studio-corpus dramatism.** A game studio's released titles form a Moore-oeuvre. Recurrent design-motifs (environmental storytelling, agency-restoration arcs, mechanical signature systems, recurrent character archetypes) are tested for cross-title convergence. The substance is the studio's strategy. The *because → and* diagnostic applies: does the studio systematically conjoin where it could subordinate? FromSoftware's juxtaposition of impossible-to-cohere lore-fragments may be read as Moore-style objectivist conjunction where Bioware-style causal narrative subordinates.

**Designer-corpus dramatism.** A designer's career across studios and titles is the oeuvre. The Crape-Myrtle-through-Black-Earth move applies natively: a designer's later condensed system is legible only when read against earlier overt expression of the same volitional charge. The trunk-as-act exhibit is the methodological pivot.

The template's refutation conditions are explicit. Cross-motif convergence is the test of substantiality; failure to converge means the substance was misidentified, not that the corpus has no substance. The proportion theory forbids monolithic labeling: a corpus is rarely *purely* anything; the proportions of its ingredients are the diagnosis. And the oeuvre-versus-poem dual scale is built into the method: each artifact is both finished (its surface is full) and provisional (its surface is provisional against cross-corpus sediment).

What this template explicitly *avoids* is the reduction of player/designer/community analysis to a single "representative" artifact (the single-poem mistake Burke is in this very chapter refusing to make about Moore), the additive cataloging of features without substance-identification (the planks-not-branches mistake), and the conflation of label with strategy (the symbolist/imagist/objectivist mistake, or its game-studies equivalent: ludologist/narratologist/proceduralist).
