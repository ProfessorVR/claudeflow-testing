## (i) Overview and the necessity for a representative case

The Dialectic of Constitutions is, by Burke's own arrangement, the only chapter in *A Grammar of Motives* whose work is to demonstrate that the apparatus built across Parts 1 and 2 — the pentad, the ten dyadic ratios, the substance vocabulary of GM-02, the philosophic-school distributions of GM-04 through GM-07 — can be operationalized at document-corpus scale. The chapter is not a *theory* of constitutions in the political-science sense. It is a worked example in which Burke uses constitutional texts (the U.S. Constitution; Herbert Read's *Poetry and Anarchism*; H. B. Parkes's *Marxism: An Autopsy*; Coleridge's Pantisocracy correspondence and *The Ancient Mariner*; Marshall's *Marbury v. Madison* opinion; FDR's political rhetoric; Denis de Rougemont on Swiss federalism) as the analytic feedstock on which the apparatus is exercised.

Burke opens by reasserting the doctrine of the representative anecdote (carried forward from GM-03, *Scope and Reduction*). The opening pages of the chapter argue that any analysis of human relations whose terminology has been shaped on a non-representative anecdote will produce non-representative terms. He recites his own criterion from the "discarded pages": the anecdote must be sufficiently demarcated to permit analysis and sufficiently complex to defy simplist description, summational in character, contemplable as the statuesque. He then rejects two candidate anecdotes — first the mind-body parallelism anecdote of behaviorism (a metonymic reduction of the mental to the physical), and second the railway terminal (the Grand Central anecdote, which makes mobility require fixity but quickly carries the analyst into a vocabulary that lies outside its orbit). Both are metonymic; what is required is a synecdochic anecdote, part-for-whole rather than reduction-to-physical-substrate. The constitution is the synecdoche.

The methodological apparatus laid out in the first third of the chapter — Two Circles, Terminal as Anecdote, Total War as Representative Case, Constitutive vs. Admonitory, Peace Constitutive or Directive, Religious vs. Secular Futurism, Imagistic and Conceptual Summaries, Five Basic Terms as Beginnings, Meanings of "Constitution" — is the precondition for the case-study work in the remaining two thirds. The case studies do not illustrate the apparatus as a separate display; they *are* the apparatus exercised on selected documents.

## (ii) Total war as anecdote

Burke spends six pages (book pp. 328–335) entertaining total war as a candidate representative anecdote for modern human relations. The case for it is strong: in the modern state, with its dispersion of technological and commercial enterprise, the act that comes closest to the synecdochic convergence-role once played by tribal totemic festivals — and, in Christian Europe before industrialism, by the communion service / agape — is the act of war. War, like revolution, is the dramatic moment of explosion after preparatory charges; being a crisis it helps criticism; it forces the dialectical participation of "the enemy" into every motive-calculus.

But there are two scruples. First, if we make war our constitutive anecdote, we proclaim war as the essence of human relations — a choice "too drastic to be taken unless absolutely necessary." Second, modern total war is itself too unwieldy: it is more of a confusion than a form. So Burke uses war only as a partial heuristic, not as the master synecdoche. The chapter's resolution: select "Constitution" as anecdote, but treat its representative anecdote as one that "may not be an anecdote of peace — but it may be an anecdote giving us the purification of war." The constitutive vs. admonitory distinction is introduced here to license the partial use: war can figure admonitorily (as what mankind is in danger of becoming) without serving as the constitutive ground.

A subordinate but crucial move happens in the next subsection, Peace: Constitutive or Directive? Most pacifism, Burke argues, is *directive* — it tacks "Let's have peace" onto whatever statement about human substance is otherwise made. Constitutive peace — Christ's "the Kingdom of Heaven is within you" — makes peace identical with Being. Only the latter is ontological; the former is futuristic. Burke uses this distinction to launch the religious-vs-secular-futurism analysis (book pp. 333–336). Religious futurism reduces future to present (find what now is within you); secular futurism reduces present to future (find what will bring promises, and you have found what is worth doing now). The Puritan-utilitarian-investor mode of secular futurism is paradigmatic: the present is treated as a means whose only worth is its promissory yield. Burke's critique is that secular futurism produces "philosophies of the merely About-to-Become."

## (iii) Constitutive and admonitory; substance and motive

The Constitutive and the Admonitory subsection (book pp. 330–332) is methodologically load-bearing for the entire chapter. A constitutive anecdote says: this is what mankind substantially is. An admonitory anecdote says: this is what mankind is in danger of becoming. Burke's worry is that admonitory idioms create no image but the enemy, and "if men are to make themselves over in the image of the imagery, what other call but that of the enemy is there for them to answer?" His diagnostic of "progress" doctrines is that, although future-aimed, they substantiate only the warlike past — and so end up ushering in the gloom they meant to usher out. The constitutive-vs-admonitory axis governs every later constitutional analysis: Read's "anarcho-syndicalism" mixes the two, Parkes's "positive laissez-faire" disguises the directive as the constitutive, FDR's "All-Out Aid to the Democracies" is an admonitory slogan dressed as constitutive purpose.

The reason this matters dramatistically is that "a thing's substance is that whereof it is constituted; hence a concern with substance is a concern with the problems of constitutionality" (book p. 338). Substance and motivation are *Wechselbegriffe* — convertible terms. The chapter's analytic strategy is to read every constitutional text as a substance-statement (a claim about what is) that doubles as a motive-statement (a claim about what therefore moves men) and as an opponent-implication (a claim about what is not, against which the substance is defined).

## (iv) The five basic terms applied to constitutions

Pages 340–341 settle the long suspension by announcing that the five terms of the pentad need nothing prior to them; they can be stated as a "Let there be — and there was" beginning. Maitland's reference to the first British Parliament as "less a body than an act" is offered as confirmation that the constitutional anecdote is fundamentally pentadic. Burke's six-meaning dictionary citation for "Constitution" (book p. 341) demonstrates that the word covers all five terms at once: it is an act (or process), a state-of-being (scene/structure), an aggregate of inherited physical qualities (agent-as-body), an aggregate of mental qualities (agent-as-temperament), the fundamental law of a polity (agency/scene), and an authoritative ordinance (act-as-command, especially Roman-imperial or ecclesiastical).

In the case studies that follow, Burke does not say "this constitution features X" in a one-word verdict. He instead diagnoses the pentadic featuring at each rhetorical move. Read features Agent (the unmediated individual, the brotherhood, the anti-father). Parkes features Scene (the market as ground of motive), with the corollary that the agent is already monetarily socialized — so what looks like a free agent is the scenic-quality internalized. The U.S. Constitution features Agent (popular sovereignty) on the surface and Scene (business as Constitution-behind-the-Constitution) below the surface, with the scene-act ratio creating the tension that produces judicial review.

The scene-act ratio is the chapter's master ratio. Burke states it cleanly at book p. 336: "The quality of the situation in which we act qualifies our act — and so, behind a judgment, there lies, explicitly or implicitly, the concept of a constitution that substantiates the judgment." Every later analysis depends on this: when the scene shifts (industrial revolution, monopolistic concentration, world war, New Deal emergency), the explicit Constitution's clauses must mean differently because the scene that gave them meaning has changed. The Constitution-behind-the-Constitution concept names this scenic ground.

## (v) Anarchism and anarcho-syndicalism

Pages 344–349 work the Herbert Read case. Read claims immunity from practicability tests because he is presenting a "vision." Burke concedes the immunity: ideal anarchy is by definition the realm where the lion and the lamb lie down together, where contradictions reconcile (the "unwritten clause" of every utopian project), where each ideal is a sovereign state proclaiming and maintaining its identity independently of other sovereign states. Burke proposes one test that does apply to visions: moral grandeur and stylistic felicity, comparing Read's text to the Decalogue, the Sermon on the Mount, the British constitutional proclamations, the Declaration of Independence, and the Marx-Engels "pre-Constitution" for Communism.

But Read does not stay in the vision-realm. As soon as he proposes the organizational form by which anarchism is to be embodied (syndicalism), he sacrifices the immunity. The hyphenated term *anarcho-syndicalism* yokes a heavenly term to a worldly one. By Burke's reading the pair adds to "disorganized organization" — an oxymoron. The first member belongs to the Sermon-on-the-Mount realm, the second to the Pauline-epistolary realm of organizational problems. In the realm of the body the lion either eats the lamb or starves.

The diagnostic move here is the chapter's structural template: every ideal Constitutional term, when embodied in an organizational practice, translates ideal-mergers into practical-contradictions. The embodiment is necessarily a translation that either copies or betrays the original ("traduttore traditore"). Burke catches Read in a specific inconsistency: Read treats poetry-vs-business as in conflict but imagination-vs-reason as in equilibrium. The "rule of reason" smuggles authority back into an anti-authoritarian project — the same move by which Read's anarchism quietly reinstalls a kind of "anarchist rule."

## (vi) Laissez-faire and the spectrum of freedom-to-capitalism

Pages 349–360 work the H. B. Parkes case. Parkes does not present his book as a vision, so Burke can analyze it for internal contradictions directly. The diagnostic problem: Parkes distinguishes "negative" laissez-faire (the eighteenth-century hands-off doctrine) from "positive" laissez-faire (a modified form that licenses government control at strategic points to "preserve the principles" while changing the practice). Burke renames this "hands-off à la hands-on."

The spectrum Burke extracts from Parkes (book p. 350) is one of the chapter's signature analytic devices:

| heavenly pole | freedom |
| | humanism |
| | laissez-faire |
| | free market |
| | price system (money) |
| | industrialism |
| worldly pole | capitalism |

Each term restricts the circumference of "freedom" further while retaining ambiguous trace of the divine original. "Free market" is "just ever so faintly an oxymoron" because at the very base of the concept is the labor market, and the freedom-to-sell-one's-services is also the necessity-to-sell-one's-services. Burke takes Spinoza's "necessity from within = freedom" and points out that a labor-market necessity comes from without, not within, unless one has already internalized money as motive (which is Parkes's strategy).

Burke's "Strategic Choice of Circumference for 'Freedom'" subsection (book pp. 354–355) is the chapter's most generalizable methodological insight. Parkes does not derive his value of freedom from a theological or naturalist circumference; he takes it from the socially-given humanist tradition. If he had derived it from heaven or from nature, he could not identify freedom with the free market (no market exists in either heaven or nature). The diagnostic generalization: where the theorist locates a key term's motivational ancestry determines what is permitted to count as its faithful extension.

The "Money as 'God-term'" subsection (book pp. 355–356) names the analytic concept that the dissertation will reuse most heavily. A God-term designates the ultimate motivation, or substance, of a Constitutional frame. For Parkes, money is the God-term: once monetary motivation has become "second nature," the qualities of the scene are internal to the agent; the migrating-for-wages worker is free relative to the dictator's transferred-population worker. "Where one can volunteer, there one is free." The Marxist God-term against which Parkes positions himself is "inevitability" (Spinozist necessity inherited via Hegel into the Marxian formula "freedom is the knowledge of necessity"). The two constitutions oppose each other as rival Gods.

## (vii) Money, strategic circumference, and the agonistic structure of constitutions

The diagnostic generalization Burke extracts from the Read and Parkes case-studies is the "Principles and Reform" subsection (book pp. 356–357). The word "reform" carries two senses: (i) a sinner reforms = abandons sin and returns to existing principles; (ii) "reform" = "transform" = change the principles themselves. Parkes's program of governmental control of the money and labor markets is presented as (i) but is actually (ii). The ambiguity is the rhetorical device that holds his Constitution together.

This becomes "Constitutions and the Opponent" (book pp. 357–360): constitutions are agonistic instruments. They imply an enemy. The accession-oath, the royal charter, the Bill of Rights — all "substantiate an ought" by reference to what should not be. Parkes uses Marxism as dialectical foil, and the foil shapes the substance of his own Constitution: where he finds Communist practice wrong, the error derives from the principle; where he finds capitalist practice wrong, the error derives from departure from the principle. The chosen opponent licenses the asymmetric reading.

Burke generalizes via Coleridge's *Friend* essay on Edmund Burke: a statesman of principles meets theorist-opponents with appeals to expediency and meets calculator-opponents with appeals to feeling. The constitution-text adapts its rhetorical posture to its opponent. This is the principle that connects to "Political Rhetoric as Secular Prayer" later in the chapter.

## (viii) Principles, reform, and the Constitution-behind-the-Constitution

The Constitution-Behind-the-Constitution subsection (book pp. 362–364) is the chapter's master concept. Any explicit constitution is an enactment within a wider scene (social, natural, supernatural) whose qualities qualify the explicit enactment via the scene-act ratio. "Positive" law tries to confine the criterion of judicial interpretation to the explicit document — abandoning natural law and divine law as criteria — but it is impossible to so confine the circumference. In the U.S. case, "the values, traditions, and trends of business" have functioned as the Constitution-behind-the-Constitution. Burke cites Beard's reading of the Constitution as a capitalist Constitution framed in a period of post-Revolutionary reaction; quotes the *Encyclopaedia of the Social Sciences* (IV, 254) on the asymmetry that U.S. constitutional guarantees protect against governmental abuse but not against capitalist exploitation; and notes that even the Thirteenth Amendment's bar on peonage was a dialectical reflex against the feudal Southern plantation system, not against capitalist exploitation as such.

The Shifts-in-the-Locus-of-the-Representative subsection (book pp. 363–365) follows from this. In feudal thought the nobility is the representative class; after a "fall" the representative part becomes the divisive part. Rights asserted against the Crown become rights asserted against the people's own government once the Crown is removed (the Court repeatedly nullified laws passed in strict accordance with popular sovereignty). The new opponent shapes the new substance. Burke's fencer-statue metaphor (book p. 365) captures the dialectical problem: the parry remains memorialized while the thrust it was answering has shifted from sword to pistol.

The Generalizing-of-Wishes subsection (book pp. 365–367) names how constitutional principles attain durability: by being raised to a sufficiently high level of generalization. The golden rule (= lex talionis translated from threat to cajolery) operates as a "chapter-head" each tribe fills out differently. The U.S. Constitution's "unchanging provisions adapted to the infinite variety of the changing conditions of our national life" works the same way. The price of universality is generality.

The Limits-and-Powers-of-a-Constitution subsection (book pp. 367–368) follows: a constitution is in a sense supererogatory (rights it "guarantees" you can already exercise need no guarantee; rights you cannot exercise no constitution can confer). Its real work is to single out certain motivational coordinates for attention. By centering attention on one calculus of motives, the U.S. Constitution labels Marxist, Fascist, naturalistic, theological, and psychological motivations "un-Constitutional" — even though such motivations operate in the Constitution-behind-the-Constitution.

The Coleridge Pantisocracy and Ancient Mariner subsection (book pp. 368–371) is the chapter's most literary moment. Coleridge's Susquehannah commune was designed to make virtue inevitable by "aspheterism" (socializing of property, removing partisan interest as motive). The Ancient Mariner's blessing of the water-snakes "unaware" is the parallel poetic move: blessing them in their essence so command and obedience are one, motive and impulse merged. Babbitt's objection that this lacks "inner check" is met by Coleridge's distinction (in *Aids to Reflection*) between Stoic virtue (acting virtuously in spite of feelings) and Christian virtue (moralizing the feelings). Burke uses Coleridge's motive/impulse distinction throughout: if a man is pushed off a cliff and clutches at something, the clutching is an act (motive); the descent is a natural event. The "total act" — motive and impulse merged — is an ideal incapable of realization in an imperfect world, hence the Pantisocracy project's failure.

Principles-of-the-Conflict-Among-Principles (book pp. 373–376) distinguishes two senses of "principle": (i) principles-as-wishes (volitional, ideal, mutually independent in their pure ideality); (ii) principles-as-interrelationships (structural-mandatory facts about how wishes conflict when embodied in cases). The Constitution provides (i); judicial review's work is to handle (ii). Constitution-Makes-Extra-Constitutionality-Mandatory (book pp. 376–378) follows: when wishes conflict, what is mandatory is not obedience-to-the-text but a new act of arbitration drawing motivation from the Constitution-behind-the-Constitution. Even an oration praising the Constitution must go outside the Constitution for reasons. Positive law's claimed purity is self-undermining.

## (ix) Essentializing vs. proportional; the dialectics of federation

The Essentializing-and-Proportional Strategies subsection (book pp. 380–385) is the chapter's most reusable hermeneutic generalization. Essentializing = select one clause and judge a measure against it (the litigant's strategy). Proportional = test against the recipe of all wishes, naming an explicit hierarchy among them (the judicious method). Burke's analytical preference is proportional, but the actual practice of judicial review and political-platform construction is overwhelmingly essentializing — because (a) nineteenth-century thinking is "one grand gallery of rival essentializations" governed by the law of parsimony, (b) judges are litigant-trained, and (c) generalized principles produce "where do you draw the line" problems that any partisan can extrapollate to absurdity.

Marshall's *Marbury v. Madison* argument (book pp. 385–388) is Burke's textbook example of advocate-essentializing. Marshall selects as his test case a clear conflict (an export-duty law vs. the explicit prohibition on export duties from any state) and presents the Court's decision as mandatory upon the Court. But the same Justice also established the "broad interpretation" doctrine and the Tenth Amendment's "or" ambiguity ("to the States respectively, or to the people") — both of which open the proportional discretion that his Marbury argument formally disallows. The essentializing case is paradigmatic for advocacy; the proportional case is paradigmatic for judiciousness; the same office must do both.

Constitutional-Unity-and-Political-Diversity (book pp. 388–391) names that a political constitution is itself an act and therefore partial — every day since 1789 has been a day of "emergency" in the sense that motivational ingredients not in the explicit document have been required. Party platforms are the most disparate constitutions of all: motivations-for-the-nonce slung together to please each voting group; political-party structure developed outside the provisions of the Constitution (Washington's Farewell Address warns against the party system we have since been taught to glorify).

The Role-of-the-President subsection (book pp. 391–393), with FDR as paradigm, names a structural feature of executive politics: the President must find a unifying slogan/substance from which all his major policies can descend ("The New Deal," "All-Out Aid to the Democracies"). To remain a "free agent" he needs strong opposing groups to balance; if labor unions go too weak, the President at the centre of the seesaw becomes "prisoner" to the heavier weight. Burke speculates that FDR's polio gave him a "distinction between action and motion" since he had to act by proxy through enlisting the wills and movements of others — a motivational background for his political tactics.

Political-Rhetoric-as-Secular-Prayer (book pp. 393–394) is one of the chapter's most-quoted moves. The President softens the sharply pointed (a drastic measure presented as moderate, "a measure of partial control done for their own good") and sharpens the pointless (a modest measure thundered about with great noise). Burke's example: Roosevelt's "death sentence" to holding companies signaled (to Burke) that the holding companies would in fact fare quite favorably, because if real dissolution were intended the President would soften the blow to avoid additional opposition. Political language functions like prayer because both attempt to motivate action by *style* in addition to content.

War-and-Collectivistic-Sacrifice (book pp. 394–398) reads the World War II political economy: a capitalist war economy resolves capitalism's wage-earner/wage-spender contradiction (the worker is both feared as wage-earner and flattered as wage-spender) by shrinking consumption and expanding destruction goods. But sacrifice as motive cannot be individualist; war demands a collectivistic compensatory concept. Fascism offered booty; Russia offered communist faith; the United States in 1941–42 offered the promise of return to an already-unworkable order — leaving the slogan "All-Out Aid to the Democracies" vague where the Fascist slogan was specific. Burke's reading: New Deal collectivism was deliberately not refurbished for World War II because its potentiality as "incantatory imagery" might bring about the end of "business as usual."

The Dialectics-of-Federation subsection (book pp. 398–401), reading Denis de Rougemont's Swiss-tradition essay, names federation as union-in-diversity: not a unitary "system" (which is anti-federalist in essence) but the harmonization of essentially-different complementary parts, treated as marriage rather than Gleichschaltung. Burke supplements: economic interests at odds are antagonistic not complementary, so de Rougemont's spiritual emphasis is partial. Burke then maps federation's union-in-diversity onto Coleridge's fancy/imagination distinction: "unity of plurality" vs. "unity of plurality" (e pluribus unum). The Supreme Court oscillates between Jeffersonian states'-rights and Hamiltonian national-federation depending on which pole it elects to feature, "giving either imaginative decisions or fanciful ones."

## (x) The chapter as paradigm of applied dramatistic analysis

GM-08 is the volume's master demonstration that the pentad is not a theoretical schema but an analytical instrument. Burke does not say "constitution X features pentad term Y" as a one-word verdict; he diagnoses pentadic featuring at each rhetorical move. The walkthrough proceeds along eleven coordinated moves: (1) constitution as substance, substance as motive (Scene featured); (2) constitutions addressed by Agents to Agents (Agent featured); (3) constitutional enactment as Act (Act featured); (4) procedure / judicial review as Agency (Agency featured); (5) ends, ideals, the "unwritten clause" of reconciliation (Purpose featured); (6) scene-act ratio as master ratio; (7) agent-act ratio in the relation between constitution-maker and addressee; (8) agency-purpose ratio in "reform vs. transform"; (9) deployment of GM-02's substance vocabulary; (10) deployment of GM-13's four tropes (synecdoche for representative anecdote, metonymy for reduction, irony for liberal substance, metaphor for heuristic display); (11) diagnostic-not-classificatory featuring (a constitution features one term as locus of motive but deploys all five).

The dissertation's game-design operationalization chapter will inherit exactly this method. A worked corpus of game-design theories can be analyzed (a) for which pentad term each features as locus of motive, (b) for which dyadic ratios each most relies upon, (c) for which God-term each implicitly invokes (its terminological ultimate substance), (d) for its strategic choice of circumference (the implicit Constitution-behind-the-Theory — biology? cognitive science? semiotics? phenomenology of perception?), (e) for whether its claims are constitutive or admonitory/directive, and (f) for the essentializing-vs-proportional structure of its hermeneutic. Ludology, narratology, embodied-cognition game studies, and procedural-rhetoric become recognizable as distinct constitutional projects with their own opponents, God-terms, and shapes of unity-in-diversity.

## (xi) Cross-pipeline bridges

Several bridges emerge for Phase 3 synthesis and the dissertation Manual Section E:

— **Aristotle (exegetical)**: book p. 340 explicitly cites Aristotle's "final cause as prime mover" as the prior resolution of the beginnings/endings problem; the substance-as-motive lineage Burke uses in GM-08 inherits directly from *Metaphysics* and *Politics* via the GM-06 Act-and-Realism chapter.

— **Calleja PIM (anticipatory-projection, dissertation)**: Burke's strategic-choice-of-circumference + God-term-of-frame methodology becomes the operationalization template for diagnosing which PIM dimension (kinesthetic, spatial, shared, narrative, affective, ludic) a given game theory features as its God-term. The Constitution-behind-the-Constitution concept maps directly onto the implicit ground of any particular game-experience theory.

— **Burke 1950 *Rhetoric of Motives* (forward-reference)**: the chapter's use of "consubstantiality" (book p. 372 — the Declaration of Independence as making consubstantiality "defined dialectically by reference to a contrary term") prefigures the identification/consubstantiation apparatus of the forward-pipeline volume.

— **Burke 2018 *War of Words* (forward-reference)**: the constitutive-vs-admonitory war-anecdote work, the war-economy substance-shift, and political-rhetoric-as-secular-prayer all anticipate the late-Burke conflict-analytic material.

— **Heidegger BCAP / *Being and Time* (anticipatory-projection)**: Constitution-behind-the-Constitution as implicit ground-of-acts parallels Heideggerian *Worumwillen* / *In-der-Welt-sein* structurally; since Burke 1945 contains zero Heidegger hits, the bridge is parallel-discovery rather than influence.

— **Rickert *Ambient Rhetoric* (anticipatory-projection)**: Constitution-behind-the-Constitution as ambient scenic ground; political-rhetoric-as-secular-prayer parallels Rickert's ambient-rhetoric framework.

— **Gross *Uncomfortable Situations* (anticipatory-projection)**: constitutive vs. admonitory anecdote parallels his account of incipient-acts in situated emotion; the scene-act ratio in constitutional theory is the structural cousin of his framework.

The chapter is, in summary, the volume's load-bearing demonstration that dramatistic analysis is exegetical-and-operationalizable rather than purely classificatory. Every concept in the constitutional vocabulary (God-term, Constitution-behind-the-Constitution, strategic choice of circumference, constitutive vs. admonitory, essentializing vs. proportional, principles vs. reform, generalizing of wishes, political rhetoric as secular prayer, union in diversity) is reusable as a diagnostic instrument on any motive-statement, in any domain. That reusability is the precise import the dissertation's empirical-adaptation chapter will exploit.
