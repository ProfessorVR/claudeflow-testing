## §1 Overview: the chapter's argumentative spine and structural function

The first chapter of Burke's *Grammar of Motives* performs three interlocking jobs in eighteen book-pages. First, it announces and develops the master spatial metaphor of dramatism: scene contains act and contains agent. The chapter's title — *Container and Thing Contained* — is not ornament but doctrine: the central image of motive-analysis is figured as containment, and the principle of dramatic consistency (act and agent must be of a quality fitting their scene) follows directly. Second, it enumerates the full inventory of dyadic ratios among the five pentad terms — ten in all — and names them as such for the first time in the volume. Third, it argues that the ratios are not a catalogue but the *analytical engine* of dramatism: any coherent ascription of motive is implicitly a claim about which ratio is operative, and the rhetorical-strategic selection among competing ratios is itself a load-bearing move in the Rhetoric of Motives.

The chapter is structured into five published subsections — *The Scene-Act Ratio* (pp. 3-7), *The Scene-Agent Ratio* (pp. 7-9), *Further Instances of These Ratios* (pp. 9-11), *Ubiquity of the Ratios* (pp. 11-15), and *Range of All the Ratios* (pp. 15-20) — but the operational architecture is better described as: (a) two paradigm-case literary workings of scene-act (Ibsen) and scene-act-reversed (O'Neill), pp. 3-6; (b) a counter-development of scene-agent through Carlyle, Wordsworth, and Swift, pp. 7-9; (c) demonstration of the ratios' reversibility and of the action-vs-motion distinction that protects dramatism from behaviorist absorption, pp. 9-11; (d) a long catalogue of the ratios' ubiquity across philosophical, military, political, and theological discourse, pp. 11-15; and finally (e) the canonical enumeration of all ten ratios with extended treatment of act-agent, the principle of selectivity, the Edenic paradigm of total ratification, and forward-flags to Attitude and the actus/status dialectic, pp. 15-20. The closing remark — that only scene-act and scene-agent fit comfortably under container-and-thing-contained, while act-agent "tugs at its edges" and other ratios "move us still farther afield" — is the chapter's honest admission that its master metaphor strains under the actual diversity of motive-relations it is asked to organize.

For the dissertation, this chapter is doubly load-bearing: it is the canonical statement of the ten-ratio engine that will serve as the analytical apparatus for game-motive-ascription, and it is the load-bearing source for the Phase 3 ratio-catalog deliverable. The Calleja-PIM crosswalk (§viii below) is built on the assumption that the ten ratios provide an exhaustive (though, per Burke, not necessarily exclusive) diagnostic for which pentad term any given game-studies theorist features.

## §2 The scene-act ratio — Ibsen's *Enemy of the People* worked example

Burke selects *An Enemy of the People* (book pp. 3-5) as the chapter's first sustained worked example precisely because its scene-act correlations are unusually transparent. The five-act structure is read as a sequence of scenes each calling forth, by their quality, an act of corresponding quality. Acts I and II are set in Dr. Stockmann's sitting room, a bourgeois interior fitting the bourgeois social drama of public-health corruption and family rivalry. Within these acts Burke draws attention to a scene *prior* to the play's opening: Stockmann's earlier withdrawal alone to the far North, during which he conceived the plan for the public Baths. Burke reads this reported scene as the dramatist's device for materializing a spiritual process — purification, pollution — through scenic counterpart. The Baths themselves figure as scene-objects whose polluted condition is the moral pollution of the conservative business interests.

Act III takes place in the editorial office of the *People's Messenger*. Burke notes the act takes on a more forensic reference in keeping with the nature of the place. This is the play's peripety: Stockmann learns the article will be suppressed, and the scene-act ratio is materialized in the stage-business of Peter Stockmann's hat and stick — properties symbolizing mayoral authority — passed from one brother to the other. Act IV's semi-public, semi-intimate gathering room in a friend's house mirrors the semi-public character of Stockmann's appeal to his fellow-townsmen, who reject him. Act V, in Stockmann's study, presents the scene as a quasi-symbolic disorder: torn clothes, broken windows, general disorder of the study. Burke explicitly flags this as bidirectionally readable — these scenic details can be read either as properties of the scene OR as a reflection of the agent's post-conflict condition. The play closes with Stockmann's plan to enroll twelve disciples for a school of social education, and his exalted pronouncement on standing alone. Burke immediately points out that this pronouncement is modified by its situation: Stockmann is in fact surrounded by his loyal family, so his "standing alone" must be read as solitary in direction rather than absolute. The whole plot is summarized as "an internality directed outwards" — a progression from a reported scene of solitary conception to an enacted scene of public re-engagement.

The Ibsen reading exhibits the scene-act ratio in its forward-deterministic mode: each scene calls forth, by its quality, an act of consistent quality. But Burke is careful to preserve ambiguity. The reading admits that scenic features can be re-read as agent-properties (the disordered study as Stockmann's state of mind), which already presages the ambiguity-rules to come: when scene is read as relations among dramatis personae, characters become scenic to one another, and any act becomes part of the context modifying subsequent acts.

## §3 Scene-act reversed — O'Neill's *Mourning Becomes Electra*

The chapter's second paradigm case (book pp. 5-6) is the closing moment of O'Neill's *Mourning Becomes Electra* trilogy. Burke quotes the stage directions: Lavinia commands Seth to close the shutters and discard the flowers; Seth obeys; the shutters close with a decisive bang; Lavinia, "as if this were a word of command," pivots sharply and marches woodenly into the house. Burke's gloss is that we end on the motif of the shut-in personality, *quite literally objectified*. The crucial analytical move: once Lavinia's command has produced the scenic modification (closed shutters, discarded flowers), the modified scene *itself becomes the motivation* of her next act. She walks like an automaton in response to the closing of the shutter.

This is the chapter's key demonstration of ratio-reversibility. The scene-act ratio is not unidirectional; the act constructs a scene that then commands further action. But it is also a demonstration of a limit-case: Lavinia's automaton-march has degenerated from action into sheer motion (the Dramatistic distinction Burke will elaborate at p. 14). She does not act, she is automatically moved; her last moments must be relegated to stage directions alone. Burke draws the structural moral: "the trilogy did not end a moment too soon; for its close represented not only the end of Lavinia, but the end of the motivating principle of drama itself." The playwright had come to the end of a line and would have to "turn back" — or abandon drama for some more "scientific" form (Burke's parenthetical suggestion: a behaviorist sociology of motorist-types and their responses to traffic signals).

The O'Neill example does double work: it shows scene-act reversibility, and it shows the limit beyond which scene-act reversal collapses into behaviorist motion-reduction. The chapter's critical alignment with the action/motion distinction is thus made through a literary case rather than a philosophical argument. Burke also cites Horatio's warning to Hamlet about the sea-cliff that beetles over its base — a citation he uses to demonstrate that the idea of natural scene as sufficient motivation for desperate action persists across centuries (Hardy, Virgil's *genius loci*, Darwinian environmentalism, contemporary geopolitics).

## §4 The scene-agent ratio — Carlyle, Wordsworth, Swift, and Burke's nineteenth-century thesis

Burke defines the scene-agent ratio (book p. 7) as a synecdochic relation between person and place. The Carlyle citation from *Heroes and Hero-Worship* presents the Arabs and their country in secular synecdoche: the savage rock-mountains, the great grim deserts, the wide silent sand-sea fit a swift-handed deep-hearted race. The Wordsworth sonnet ("It is a beauteous evening") is offered as a theological treatment: the octave is all scene, infused with supernatural overtones (the holy time, Abraham's bosom, the mighty Being); the sestet is all agent (the child made divine by being at one with the divine scene). Burke's structural reading is precise: the contents of a divine container synecdochically share in its divinity.

Swift's Laputans on the floating island (book p. 9) are used as a counter-deployment of the same ratio: their habitation depicts their character. To suggest that Laputans are "up in the air," Swift portrays them as living on an island that floats in space. Burke returns to Swift later (book p. 10) for the asymmetric eye-direction (heads inclined to right or left, one eye turned inward and the other up to the zenith) as scene-agent character-signature.

The Seurat passage (book p. 8) is a boundary case: pointillism extends scene-agent fusion to the point where figures dissolve into background. But Burke notes this passes "beyond strictly scene-agent matters into the area better covered by our term, agency" — because the extreme consistency between scene and agent is now conveyed by *the distinctive terms of the method* (the medium) common to both. The Seurat example is therefore a near-instance of agent-agency or scene-agency, and Burke uses it to demonstrate the strategic interpenetration of ratios.

Burke's most interesting historical observation in this subsection (book p. 9) is the thesis that variants of the scene-agent ratio "abound in typical nineteenth-century thought, so strongly given to the study of motives by the dialectic pairing of people and things (man and nature, agent and scene)." He names the idealist's signature formula — *Einklang zwischen Innen- und Aussenwelt* (the concord between inner-world and outer-world) — as the German-philosophical home of the ratio. The remark functions as a brief intellectual-historical diagnosis: an entire epoch of Romantic and post-Romantic thought can be characterized as scene-agent thought.

The subsection closes with Burke's important critical observation about the naturalist novelist's embarrassment. A humanitarian novelist may indict bad conditions (capitalism, the slum) by showing their brutalizing effect upon the indigenous people. But the scene-agent ratio, strictly observed, would require that the brutalizing scene contain *brutalized* characters as its dialectical counterpart. The novelist therefore portrays characters that, being as brutal as their scene, "are not worth saving." Restricting scene to motive-force restricts personality. This is Burke at his most diagnostic: the ratio is not a neutral analytic tool but a structural commitment that forces certain narrative and political consequences.

## §5 Further instances of these ratios — reversibility, behaviorism, and the committee-meeting anecdote

The third subsection (book pp. 9-11) explicitly articulates the reversibility principle. The scene-act ratio either calls for acts in keeping with scenes or scenes in keeping with acts — and similarly for scene-agent. Burke returns to Lavinia (her command modifies the scene; the modified scene then "commands" her) and contrasts this directly with behaviorist metaphysics. Burke's phrasing — "behaviorists would call it No Metaphysics" — is acid: by reducing action to sheer motion, behaviorism radically truncates the possibilities of drama and erases the action-vs-motion distinction.

Burke also returns to the Ibsen disorder-of-the-study, showing that the same details can be read either scene-first (scenic properties as motive) or agent-first (scene as expression of the role, the body itself as scenic, theologians' treatment of body as the dwelling-place of soul). Swift's Laputans recur briefly.

The subsection's culminating example is a real-life committee-meeting anecdote (book pp. 10-11), reported by Burke as illustrative. A committee member, seated within a railing with the rest of the group, quietly stands, opens the gate in the railing, steps outside, closes the gate, picks up her coat, lays it across her arm, and stands waiting. Only then, when the chair recognizes her, does she announce her resignation. Burke's gloss: the resigning member had set the scene for the pattern of severance — chairman and members within the pale, sitting, without coats; she outside the pale, standing, with coat over arm preparatory to departure. The scene she had strategically arranged "implicitly (ambiguously) contained the quality of her act." The anecdote is the chapter's clearest non-literary demonstration that ratio-reversal works in lived practice as well as in dramatic art: the agent strategically modifies the scene to make a forthcoming act intelligible.

## §6 Ubiquity of the ratios — Burke's strong universality claim

The fourth subsection (book pp. 11-15) is the chapter's most encyclopaedic. Burke's strong claim: "If we but look about us, we find examples of the two ratios everywhere; for they are at the very centre of motivational assumptions." The catalogue that follows is meant to defamiliarize ordinary motive-talk by revealing its underlying ratio-structure.

Machiavelli's introduction to the *Discourses* — complaining that people read history without applying its lessons, as though the order of motions and power had changed — is glossed by Burke as a remark about scene-constancy across changes of historical scene. The chapter then lists, with extraordinary economy, the surface synonyms of scene: "society," "environment," "12:20 P.M." (a scenic statement par excellence), Milton's *L'Allegro* and *Il Penseroso* (scenic contrast yielding contrasting actions), period-terms ("Elizabethan period," "romanticism," "capitalism"), Locke's "scene of ideas," Carl Becker's "climates of opinion" in *The Heavenly City of the Eighteenth-Century Philosophers*. Burke notes that the latter two phrases combine scene with the implicit agent-substrate of ideas (ideas as properties of agents) — a strategic overlap.

The philosophical term "ground" is examined for its double sense (background AND reason). Burke shows Hegelian idealism exploiting both senses by positing Reason as the *Grundprinzip* of all history — at once the mover of history and the substance of which history is made. Burke famously declines to adjudicate what it means to say Reason is both mover and substance: "Let us not worry, at this point, what it may 'mean' to say that 'Reason' is at once the mover of history and the substance of which history is made. It is sufficient here to note that such terministic resources were utilized, and to detect the logic of the pentad behind them."

The military maxim "terrain determines tactics" is glossed as a strict localization of the scene-act ratio. Political commentators' use of "situation" is glossed similarly. The Marxist phrase "objective situation" is explicitly motivational, and Burke notes the Marxist theorist's discussion of "policies" as political acts enacted in conformity with scenes. Burke distinguishes two surface modes — deterministic ("the policy had to be adopted in this situation") and hortatory ("the policy should be adopted in conformity with the situation"). He cites a wartime traveller characterizing politicians as "prisoners of the situation" (deterministic scene-agent) and a speaker advocating "unusual powers" for President Roosevelt in an "unusual international situation" (hortatory scene-act). Justice Hugo L. Black's Supreme Court ruling on Japanese exclusion is analyzed as scene-act reasoning: the war scene authorizes the executive act of exclusion.

Marx's claim, transmitted by Lenin's *State and Revolution*, that justice can never rise superior to the economic conditions of society is presented as one of the most succinct scene-act formulations in dialectical materialism. Burke contrasts this with the agent-located reading of justice (justice as a property of personality). Trotsky's irony about Stalinist policies as the inevitable result of attempting socialism under given conditions, and Coleridge's libertarian Pantisocracy project (designed to "make virtue inevitable" by arranging the social situation), extend the pattern.

The subsection closes with Burke's long discussion of "act" itself. Any verb of consciousness or purpose qualifies. A stumble is motion; a willed stumble is act. The basic Dramatistic unit is the human body in conscious or purposive motion. Burke notes the ambiguity of "adjustment" and "adaptation" — terms ambiguously suited to cover both action and motion, in good favor with thinkers wishing to scientize the human realm while keeping vestiges of "animism." Words like profession, vocation, policy, strategy, tactics are act-words; "position," "occupation," and "office" carry scenic overtones; capitalist "jobs" often reduce action to a maximum of sheer motion (conveyor-belt timing, clerical operations). The closing pages of the subsection examine way-words — *hodos* (Christ's "I am the way"), *tao*, *yoga*, *dike* — as originally act-words readily overlapping with agency-words when "way of life" becomes "means of doing" (scientific *met-hodos*).

## §7 Range of all the ratios — the canonical enumeration, act-agent in extenso, and the principle of selectivity

The closing subsection (book pp. 15-20) is the most analytically dense. Burke names all ten ratios in a single sentence: "scene-act, scene-agent, scene-agency, scene-purpose, act-purpose, act-agent, act-agency, agent-purpose, agent-agency, and agency-purpose." He declares the ratios "principles of determination" and announces that two of them (scene-purpose and agency-purpose) will be examined elsewhere in the *Grammar* (in fact GM-07), while "the rest will figure in passing." This is the chapter's canonical statement that the ten-ratio inventory is exhaustive of dyadic combinations among the five pentad terms.

Burke then offers extended treatment of *act-agent*, which he describes as not strictly positional but temporal or sequential: agent authors acts (good progeny if good, wise progeny if wise, etc.) and acts in turn make or remake the agent. The two political examples are load-bearing. The first: a former cabinet member's claim that "you can safely lodge responsibility with the President of the United States" because of the "tremendously sobering influence of the Presidency on any man, especially in foreign affairs." The act of occupying the office produces important modifications in character — paradigm act-agent. Similarly, donning priestly vestments or judicial robes is credited with transforming the wearer. The second example is more politically pointed: Russian wartime resistance can be read scenically (Soviet political-economic structure as the major scenic factor — including socialism) or via act-agent (Russian patriotism, "attachment to Mother Russia," "incoherent but cohesive force of Russian patriotism," "the depth of his devotion to the Russian soil"). Burke is explicit: the act-agent reading "allows for a much more felicitous explanation from the standpoint of capitalist apologetics" by deflecting attention from socialism as scenic motive-source. This is one of the chapter's strongest demonstrations that ratio-selection is rhetorically loaded.

The democracy example continues the contrast. Many Britons and Americans think of these nations as "vessels of democracy" — democracy as residing in *us*, intrinsically, because we are "a democratic people." Democratic acts are derived from democratic agents (act-agent reading): the democratic people will perform democratic acts even when conditions require the temporary curtailment of basic democratic rights. Burke contrasts this with a scene-act reading: there are "democratic situations" and "situations favorable to dictatorship, or requiring dictatorship." The technological scene requiring the planning of a world order, Burke suggests, might favor "a large measure of 'dictatorship' in our political ways." The choice between act-agent and scene-act democracy is itself a political position.

Burke flags "picturesque effects" obtained by conflicting ratios — fools in wise situations (their acts wiser than they know), children as "wise" in this sense, Chaplin's incongruity, Empson's pastoral. The Francis Biddle citation (book p. 18) is analyzed as a four-term cascade: agencies of communication (railroad, telegraph, telephone, etc.) have, by accumulation, become scenic; these scenic-agencies motivate political acts ("patches and arrangements"); unsatisfied "needs" return motivational locus to agent (since needs are a property of agents). Burke distinguishes "primal needs" (proper to agent) from "new needs" (which might best be treated as "a function of the situation" — i.e., scenic), citing the educator Eduard C. Lindeman's complaint about morale as situation-function.

Burke's softening move is the principle of selectivity (book p. 18): "The ratios may often be interpreted as principles of selectivity rather than as thoroughly causal relationships." A given political situation does not change people in their essential character; it "votes for" certain agent-types (cautious or daring, traditionalist or innovator) as its appropriate voice. The inappropriate temperaments "simply do not 'count for' so much as they would in situations for which they are a better fit." Burke's haunting phrase: "mute inglorious Miltons" — the seeds of unfit situations, never planted.

The chapter's culminating speculative passage is the Edenic paradigm (book p. 19). If an agent acts in keeping with his nature as agent (act-agent), he may change the nature of the scene (scene-act), and thereby establish a state of unity between himself and his world (scene-agent). Or the scene may call for an act that produces a corresponding agent. In reality, Burke notes, we are capable of only partial acts. But theological notions of creation and re-creation approach the concept of *total acts*. The Lutheran theologian Striegel held that Christ's work on the Cross changed God's attitude towards mankind — "a total action, a total passion" — amounting to a radical change in the structure of the Universe (since God's attitude is the ultimate ground of human action). Burke's culminating Greek example is the close of Aeschylus's *Oresteia*, where Orestes' sufferings terminate in the changed identity of the Furies — Erinyes becoming Eumenides under the new gods. Their nature as motives takes on a totally different accent: from avenging evil to rewarding good. This is the chapter's premier Greek-tragic example, and one of only two scattered Greek references (the other being the etymological discussion of *dike*).

The chapter closes with forward-flags. Attitude is briefly introduced as the preparation for an act, a kind of symbolic or incipient act — but also classifiable under agent as a state of mind. This is the seed of Burke's later sixth-pentad-term addition in the *Rhetoric of Motives* (1950). The "total action, total passion" formulation invites a dialectic pairing of active and passive, and a reference to "state of mind" invites a pairing of *actus* and *status* — both forward-flagged for development in GM-02 *Antinomies of Definition*. The closing paragraph reminds the reader that "agent" embraces not only words for person, actor, character, but also words for patient, motivational properties, drives, instincts, states of mind, and collective forms (nation, group, Freud's super-ego, Rousseau's *volonté générale*, the Fichtean "generalized I").

## §9 Anticipated dissertation deployment and Calleja-PIM crosswalk

The ten-ratio engine is the dissertation's primary analytical apparatus for game-motive-ascription. The dissertation's planned analytic move parallels Burke's own move in Part 2 of the *Grammar*: identify which pentad term a game-studies theorist features as motive-locus, and the theorist's idiom becomes legible (ludology features Act; narratology features Scene plus Purpose; embodied-cognition game studies features Agency). The ratios then permit finer diagnosis: a theorist who reads game-experience in scene-act terms (the gameworld affords particular gameplay) differs in commitment from one who reads in act-agent terms (the player's choices construct who the player becomes through alterbiography), even where both feature Act.

The crosswalk to Calleja's Player Involvement Model (whose six dimensions cluster naturally with particular pentad terms) is direct. Scene-act maps primarily to SPATIAL ↔ LUDIC: the gameworld affords particular gameplay; conversely, gameplay constructs the operative gameworld. Scene-agent maps primarily to SPATIAL ↔ KINESTHETIC: the player becomes embodied-in-the-place through the synecdochic figure of spatial internalization (Calleja's Ratchet-to-Feathermoon route-planning case is precisely this — the player coming to inhabit the place). Act-agent maps primarily to LUDIC ↔ SHARED with KINESTHETIC modulation: Burke's principle that "acts can make him or remake him" is the alterbiography mechanism Calleja develops in Ch. 7. Scene-agency, scene-purpose, act-agency, act-purpose, agent-agency, agent-purpose, and agency-purpose all map onto pairs of PIM dimensions (see the JSON `anticipated_dissertation_deployment.calleja_pim_crosswalk` for full mapping).

Two further dissertation-relevant features of GM-01 deserve emphasis. First, the rhetorical-deflection function of ratio-selection (Burke's Russian-resistance example) is critical for the dissertation's analysis of game-studies discourse: when a theorist explains player behavior in agent-terms (e.g., player personality types) versus scene-terms (e.g., game-design affordances), the theoretical choice is rarely neutral. Second, the principle of selectivity rescues the dissertation's deployment from rigid scenic determinism: a game-design feature does not produce a player-type ex nihilo but selects among the players already present. Burke's "mute inglorious Miltons" formulation is, mutatis mutandis, the dissertation's defence against the charge that dramatistic game-analysis collapses player-agency into game-design.
