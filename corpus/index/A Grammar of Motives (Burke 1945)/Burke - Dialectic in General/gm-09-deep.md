## i. Overview — dialectic vs. dramatistic

Burke opens the chapter with a definitional move that retroactively reframes the entire *Grammar*. "Dialectic" in the most general sense, he says, means the employment of the possibilities of linguistic transformation, or the study of such possibilities (book p. 402). Crucially, dialectic in this general sense is **broader** than dramatistic: although Burke has often used the two terms as synonymous, dialectic in the general sense includes idioms that are non-dramatistic — lyric, scientist, mystical, mathematical. The Part 3.II chapter is therefore the closing theoretical chapter of a book whose central method is dramatistic, but whose final task is to expose the dialectical apparatuses that operate even outside the dramatistic frame.

The chapter's organizational principle is announced immediately: dialectic in its most general sense will be considered under three heads — (1) merger and division, (2) the three major pairs (action-passion, mind-body, being-nothing), and (3) transcendence. To these are added the cross-cutting apparatuses of transformation-of-terms (the master apparatus organizing the chapter), the dialectic of the scapegoat, per genus et differentiam, the temporizing of essence, and the dissolution of drama. What we have, in effect, is a toolkit: a set of first-class dialectical apparatuses that can be deployed transferably outside the pentad's dramatistic confines.

## ii. The Transformation of Terms

The chapter's master apparatus is presented on book pp. 402–403. To analyze the possibilities of linguistic transformation in particular instances, one looks for *key terms*, decides which are ancestral and which derivative, and expects to find terms whose ambiguities allow them to bridge the gulf between other terms or otherwise serve developmental functions. Burke names two distinct operations the analyst tracks: the *dis*position of terms (their alignment) and the *trans*position of terms (their swap or migration). The pentad is itself an instance of this transformation-vocabulary in miniature — the pentad's generative power resembles, Burke notes, Kantian transcendentalism, in that the pentadic ratios name forms *necessarily exemplified* in any imputation of human motives (book p. 402).

The chapter accumulates a striking string of alternate glosses for dialectic on book p. 403: reasoning from opinion; the discovery of truth by give-and-take of converse and redefinition; the art of disputation; the processes of interaction between verbal and non-verbal; the competition of cooperation or the cooperation of competition; the spinning of terms out of terms; the placement of one thought or thing in terms of its opposite; the progressive development and reconciliation of opposites; or simply *spiraling*. Burke treats all these as variants or special applications of the three master headings that organize the chapter.

## iii. Merger and Division

The two foundational operations of dialectic are sourced from Plato's *Phaedrus*: merger as "the comprehension of scattered particulars in one idea," and division as carving "an idea at the joints, not breaking any part as a bad carver might" (Burke quoting *Phaedrus* via the standard idiom on book p. 403). Burke then traces the merger-division pair through a sequence of historical instances: Spencer's evolution from homogeneity to heterogeneity; Kant's three-fold homogeneity / specification / continuity (in the Transcendental Dialectic of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, book p. 404); Eric T. Bell's mathematical reading of the continuity-vs-discreteness pair (book p. 405); and the Occamite injunction *entia praeter necessitatem non esse multiplicanda* counter-balanced by the corresponding *entium varietates non temere esse minuendas* (book p. 404).

The chapter's first structural claim emerges here: any merger-division pair generates pressure toward a *third / pontificating term* — what Burke calls the *tertium quid* (book p. 405). Kant's third principle of continuity is one instance; Bell's mathematical "third basic pigment" is another; Coleridge's polarity-synthesis-indifference-predominance vocabulary (book p. 412) is a third. Whatever logical problems such a third term may give rise to, Burke writes, we are being logical in feeling the need for it. Two-valued dialectics are unstable; the discovery of the third is structural, not accidental.

The chapter then recapitulates from GM-02 the paradox of substance, but recast as a dialectical staple (book p. 405). The offspring is "substantially one" with the parent (merger), but its history is a development from merger to division (division at parturition — the first biological revolution). Merger and division are operating *jointly* on the same term, and the metaphysician's task becomes deciding which propositions to connect with "therefore," which with "however," and which with sheer "and" (book p. 406).

## iv. Dialectic of the Scapegoat (KEY)

The Dialectic of the Scapegoat (book pp. 406–408) is, by my read, the single most portable apparatus in the chapter. Burke's analytic decomposition of the scapegoat-mechanism into a three-step ritual is exact: (1) an original state of merger, in which iniquities are shared between the iniquitous and their chosen vessel; (2) a principle of division, in which the elements shared in common are ritualistically alienated onto the vessel; (3) a new principle of merger, in which the unification of the purified is defined in dialectical opposition to the sacrificial offering.

The scapegoat is "charismatic," Burke writes — a *vicar*. Its profound consubstantiality with persecutors is what makes ritual cleansing possible at all: those who look upon it as a chosen vessel cleanse themselves by loading the burden of their own iniquities onto it. The "curative" power of the scapegoat resides entirely in this prior consubstantiality (book p. 406). This is not Frazerian projection; it is Burkean substance-operation. The scapegoat *represents* (synecdochically) the iniquities of those who attack it.

Burke's negative example is exact and chilling: Hitlerite Anti-Semitism is the cleanest scapegoat-paradigm "of evil" — the scapegoat as the *essence* of evil, the *principle* of discord. The alienation of iniquities from the self to the scapegoat amounts to a *rebirth* of the self, promising a conversion to a new principle of motivation. When this is conceived in familial or substantial terms, Burke notes, it amounts to a change of parentage (book p. 407). Christian atonement is a more complex variant — the vicarious sacrifice took the world's burdens upon Himself, but in retaining ambiguities of power only insofar as He suffered calumny (book p. 408). And when scapegoating is *frustrated* — as when the Allied victories began making the Hitlerian self-projection onto Jewry impossible — the discords must again be faced *within*, and motives of self-destruction (called "honor") come to the fore (book p. 408).

The chapter's other scapegoat-vignettes are equally portable: Ahab pursued by the white whale he pursues; the debunker closer to the debunked than others are; Aristotle's note that the physician should be a bit sickly himself; Auden's "every brilliant doctor hides a murderer" (book p. 407).

## v. Per Genus et Differentiam

The classical scholastic definitional formula — definition by genus plus specific difference — is read on book pp. 408–410 as a dialectical operation: merger via genus + division via differentia. Burke flags the third stage: the Scotist *haecceitas*, the "thisness" or particular way of the individual thing, considered as the merger of all three layers (generic + specific + individual).

The subsection's diagnostic punch is its handling of *reduction*. Reducing motivation to generic essence is the materialist temptation ("man as a bundle of chemicals"); reducing to specific essence is the perfectionistic or scientist temptation. Burke's key claim is that the technological and financial vocabularies of modernity smuggle in *specific* essences from non-human domains — money-as-essence, tool-use-as-essence — and thereby commit the principle-of-specification violation (book p. 410). He also gives a wonderfully concrete reading of the dramatic-unities cult: the original Aristotelian definition of drama *per genus et differentiam* lost its epic-section in transmission, and the surviving specifications were extracted without their generic context, producing French neoclassical over-strictness (book p. 409).

## vi. The Antithetical Pairs (Mind-Body, Being-Nothing, Action-Passion)

The Three Major Pairs (book pp. 418–420) generalize the first major steps usually taken toward the localizing of identity. Their analytic structure is the same in each case. The members of a pair can be (a) in apposition, (b) in opposition, (c) one primary and the other derivative, or (d) aspects of an underlying reality that grounds both. Rhetorically, by shifting from one member to the other, one has the opportunity to "idealize" one's own cause while "materializing" the cause of one's opponents (book p. 419).

- **Mind-body pair** (book pp. 418–419). Either term can represent the other; physicalist or idealist reductions are readily available. The pair underwrites the scene-agent ratio (body-as-scene; mind-as-agent).
- **Being-nothing pair** (book p. 419). Its most prevalent form is essence vs. existence. Plato chooses being as the reality (essences as substantial, things in flux as existing only by sharing with non-existents); Santayana's variant of Platonism inverts this; historicist frames take the form of *becoming* vs. *having-become*. This pair, crucially, is the *engine* of the temporizing-of-essence apparatus that comes in section ix.
- **Action-passion pair** (book pp. 419–420). Localizable as peace-war, love-hate, love-death (Wagnerian), cooperation-competition. From the action-passion pair Burke recovers the *actus* and *status* distinction: status = the properties accompanying the agent's act. This in turn recovers the Stance-family and Power-family of substance-vocabulary developed in GM-02. And, with a direct internal bridge to GM-08, Burke writes that "enactments survive as constitutions" (book p. 420).

The transition out of the three pairs is itself the chapter's structural fulcrum: theories of the development from implicit (unconscious) to explicit (conscious), Burke notes, can be treated as variants of the action-passion terminology. "But here obviously we are moving close to the third aspect of our subject: *alloiosis*, transformation, transcendence" (book p. 420).

## vii. The Socratic Transcendence

The Socratic Transcendence subsection (book pp. 420–430) is the chapter's argumentative heart and its longest stretch. The key claim is structural: when a distinction *becomes a contrast* — when a synecdochic part formerly representative of the whole becomes divisive with respect to the whole of which it was a part — a critical point has been passed; a new quality of motivation has been introduced. Transcendence is the moment of crisis where a new motive is discovered en route (book p. 420–421). The example: the nobility, which had represented a culmination of feudal society, became antithetical to the society of trade — except insofar as they adapted themselves to the new conditions by themselves becoming *embourgeoisés*.

Burke's central correction of Jowett occupies book pp. 421–423. Jowett described Platonic transcendence "from without" — as the appearance, to the reader, of jolts and inconsistencies. Burke insists it must be read "from within": a Platonic dialogue is itself an *act* of transcendence, not merely a *plea* for one. The position at the end transcends the position at the start, so that the start can only retrospectively be reinterpreted in light of the new motivation. Burke then performs a worked five-level reading of the *Phaedrus* (book p. 423): Lysias' speech (false rhetoric of the conceit that lovers should defer to non-lovers) → Socrates' first speech (deeper moral significance but the same conceit) → Socrates' second speech (impassioned celebration of love, dialectically matured by subdivision into kinds of love and the matching of Eros with Anteros) → formal appreciation of the dialectical principles → the spoken word as superior to the written word.

The structural climax of this stretch is the dialectical reading of the *Phaedrus*' opening "feast of discourse" (book p. 424): the figure is not a mere metaphor but a juncture of two levels (bodily appetite + sociality + verbal-discursive appetite). The deeper unity of love, rhetoric, and dialectic in the dialogue is articulated through this juncture; Yeats' "Mother of God" with its Annunciation "through the hollow of an ear" is read as Christian-symbolic recurrence of the same Platonic pattern of verbal insemination (book p. 425).

Then Burke gives the chapter's most striking interlock with the scapegoat-subsection: **Socrates as scapegoat of transcendence** (book pp. 426–427). The Athenians' charge of *corruptor juventutis* must be read transcendentally: pedagogical insemination via dialectical intercourse was the transcendence-of-tribalism that the citizens were *themselves* incipiently enacting. They chose Socrates as their representative victim *because* he most fully embodied the cultural movement they were enacting. "Socrates died that Plato and Aristotle might live" (book p. 427) — and the *Phaedrus* / *Republic* / *Laws* form a dialectical-historical series: dissolution of tribal homogeneity, then idealistic reclamation on a higher level, then Benthamite legislation (book pp. 427–428).

Section vii closes with mathematics as a dialectical discipline (book pp. 429–430). Mathematical formula-progress works precisely the Platonist movement: imagery is transcended in generalization until what Burke calls a "Title of Titles" is reached. Faraday's search for a unified energy-formula is the modern instance; the limit-objection is Galileo's — that explaining everything explains nothing.

## viii. The Temporizing of Essence (KEY)

If the scapegoat-dialectic is the chapter's most portable apparatus, the temporizing of essence (book pp. 430–440) is its most epistemologically consequential. The apparatus is "the pun on sequence": the logically prior can be expressed as the temporally prior, and vice versa. The ways of transcendence, when aiming at the discovery of *essential* motives, may take the historicist form of "going back" — a search for origins narrating an essence. Conversely, an essayistic search for essence can be read as a retrospect, as Proust conceptualizes the project of *Remembrance of Things Past* (book p. 430).

The translation-rule is symmetric. Burke uses Neurath's "growing-out-of phraseology" (book p. 431) — the proposal to replace "cause-effect" with "derived-from" — to show how a single phrasing can satisfy temporalist and essentialist alike. Freud's primal horde (book pp. 431–432) is read as essence-smuggled-into-existence-claim: Darwinism forced Freud into a historicist vocabulary, but the underlying claim (essential rivalry-and-allegiance in the patriarchal family) is essential, and survives anthropological discrediting of its historical existence. Plato's *Meno*-doctrine of recollection is the paradigm in the other direction: a "storial" way of stating that there is fixed *essential* knowledge (book p. 432). Original sin can be translated from historical-legendary terms ("our lineal descent from a long line of murderers") to essential terms ("man as essentially transgressor") (book pp. 432–433).

The worked dramatic case is Ibsen's *Peer Gynt* (book pp. 433–439). Peer's search for his "true self" — that is, his essence — is dramatized as a "going back" to the maternal/familial origin. Solveig, who was to be his wife, becomes his spiritual mother; he is translated into the role of the Christ-child, his conception read as an Annunciation. Peer's essence resides in merger with the spiritualized maternal grounding, which replaces his real mother and (by extension) the drunken father with an ideal parentage. Burke also reads Proust as the essayistic-modernist culmination of the same time-essence pun: moments separated in time are linked outside time by an idealized synthesizing quality; their fulfilment as terms for the designation of essence occurs when some *later* moment is felt to partake of their same quality (book p. 439).

The rhetorical-level pay-off (book pp. 439–440): a person who opposes a policy absolutely may simply assert he objects to it *now*; this temporizing lets categorical convictions function as day-by-day alliances under ever-changing conditions. Few are absolute pacifists, but nearly all men are against war at any particular time. The dialectical search for the one constant motive underlying a faction's shifting policies is thus the discernment of an *essential* motive beneath the particular appearances of many *temporized* motives.

## ix. Dissolution of Drama

The closing theoretical move (book pp. 440–441) is the recognition that the dramatistic perspective contains a point at which it abolishes itself in its own enunciation. The permanent structure of interrelations within the pentad — the ten ratios, the five terms — are "principles of development" that cannot themselves develop. To define dramatism by its contextual opposite is to find the perspective abolishing itself in the very act of its enunciation.

Burke enumerates four canonical paths by which drama dissolves: (1) drama dissolved by the turn from dramatic *act* to lyric *state* (status is the reciprocal of actus — already an internal dissolution); (2) drama dissolved by terminologies that reduce action to motion (scientism); (3) drama dissolved by philosophies *of* dramatism — Burke's own move — coordinates derived from the contemplation of drama, yet used non-dramatically; (4) drama dissolved by philosophies of super-drama — a monotheistic god treated as super-person becomes *impersonal*, an impersonality that scholastic theology paradoxically prepares for the secular terminologies of science (book p. 441).

## x. A Neo-Liberal Ideal

The Grammar's closing programmatic statement (book pp. 441–443) names Burke's overarching purpose as expressing toward language an *attitude* embodied in a *method*. The attitude is linguistic skepticism — which Burke synonymizes with linguistic appreciation: a methodical quizzicality designed to perceive the full scope of language's resourcefulness. The Grammar is "directed towards the purification of war" — encouraging tolerance through speculation, since it operates at a level of motivation that even wholly rival doctrines of motives must share in common (book p. 442). The position is tempered between two contrary failures: *dissipation* (the local opportunist who takes whatever opportunities are nearest at hand) and *fanaticism* (the imposer of a single terminology upon the whole world, regardless of the dialectical interchange still to be completed). The closing attitude is "neo-Stoic resignation" / "neo-liberal speculation" (book p. 443): an attitude of appreciation in which what Burke calls *hypochondriasis* on the negative side becomes, on the positive side, the appreciation of the many ingenuities of speech.

## xi. The chapter as toolkit of dialectical apparatuses

The Phase 0 manifest pre-flagged GM-09 as the chapter whose central deliverable is a **transformation vocabulary** — and that pre-flag is borne out. The chapter is, in effect, the *Grammar*'s methodological self-supplement: a set of first-class apparatuses transferable beyond the pentad. The eleven apparatuses (logged in the `gm-09-deep.json` `transformation_vocabulary` array) are:

1. **Transformation of Terms** — the master apparatus.
2. **Merger and Division** — the two foundational operations.
3. **Dialectic of the Scapegoat** — the three-step ritual; perhaps the single most portable apparatus.
4. **Per Genus et Differentiam** — the scholastic definitional formula as dialectical operation.
5. **Mind-Body Pair** — antithetical merger-division resource underwriting scene-agent.
6. **Being-Nothing Pair** — essence-existence convertibility; engine of temporizing.
7. **Action-Passion Pair** — recovers actus-status and the Stance/Power families.
8. **Socratic Transcendence** — upward move where new motive is discovered en route.
9. **Temporizing of Essence** — the KEY epistemological apparatus; convertibility of historicist and essentialist vocabularies.
10. **Distinction Becomes Contrast** — synecdochic-part-becomes-divisive engine of transcendence.
11. **Dissolution of Drama** — the four ways the dramatistic perspective abolishes itself.

To these the 1962 Addendum (book pp. 443–444) adds the proposal of **Attitude** as a possible sixth pentad term, distinct from agency (manner *quo modo* as distinct from means *quibus auxiliis*), enabling new ratios such as scene-attitude and agent-attitude. The Addendum also clarifies that ratio-order matters (scene-act ≠ act-scene) and that all ratios are essentially *analogies*: the nature of one term is implicit / analogously present in the nature of the other.

- **Forward-anchor to Burke's *Rhetoric of Motives* (1950)** — the 1962 Addendum's proposal of Attitude as a 6th term, plus the *identification* / *consubstantiation* operations implicit in the scapegoat subsection, are the chapter's explicit forward-pointers to the *Rhetoric*. Internal cross-links (GM-02 substance vocabulary, GM-08 enactments-as-constitutions, GM-13 four tropes operating dialectically) are also tagged in the JSON `phase3_handles` block. The temporizing-of-essence apparatus is, on the trope-level, *synecdoche* operating dialectically (part-for-whole = origin-for-essence); distinction-becomes-contrast is the metonymy-synecdoche threshold; the scapegoat dialectic involves all four tropes operating jointly (metonymy of iniquity-transferred-by-contiguity onto vessel; synecdoche of vessel-as-essence-of-evil; irony enabling the alienation; metaphor of the charismatic vicar).

The chapter is, in sum, a methodological self-supplement to the *Grammar* whose transferable apparatuses (especially the scapegoat dialectic and the temporizing of essence) are precisely the ones the dissertation pipeline can operationalize for game-studies analysis without losing dramatistic fidelity.
