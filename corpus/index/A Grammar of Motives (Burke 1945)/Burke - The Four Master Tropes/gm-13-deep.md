## (i) Overview — tropes as truth-apparatuses

Burke opens Appendix D with a declaration that flatly reverses the inheritance of two and a half millennia of rhetorical theory: his primary concern with metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, and irony is "not with their purely figurative usage, but with their role in the discovery and description of 'the truth.'" The four classical figures, which since Aristotle's *Poetics* and Quintilian's *Institutio* have been catalogued among the ornaments of style, are here promoted to the rank of master operations — the four ways human discourse constructs, transforms, and tests what counts as real. Burke is not interested in figures as decoration; he is interested in figures as instruments of inquiry.

The opening table is the load-bearing scaffold of the entire essay. For metaphor, substitute *perspective*. For metonymy, substitute *reduction*. For synecdoche, substitute *representation*. For irony, substitute *dialectic*. The literal-realistic substitutions are the active terms; the figurative names mark only their inherited residue. Within the first page Burke also tells the reader that the four tropes "shade into one another." Give a thorough analyst any one of them and exploit its possibilities, and the other three emerge. The proof is structural: a dialectic gives representation by the interaction of perspectives, and the resulting perspective-of-perspectives is necessarily a reduction (a chart drawn to scale is a reduction of the area charted). The four pairs overlap, and the essay's five-section architecture works the transitions in order.

The methodological consequence is that the four-trope apparatus is not a typology of figures the analyst chooses among; it is a single integrated system of operations human discourse necessarily deploys. The scientist who reduces motive to behavior is doing metonymy; the metaphysician who identifies microcosm with macrocosm is doing synecdoche; the historian who recognizes that what arose in time must in time perish is doing irony.

A first-page footnote makes a crucial admission: "dialectic" is used here in the restricted sense — partner of irony alone — while in the broader sense all four transformations are dialectical. The footnote establishes that the apparatus is itself dialectical: irony is both one trope-pair AND the meta-operation governing the other three. Irony is the trope of tropes — the perspective of perspectives applied to the trope-apparatus itself — which is why the essay culminates in section IV.

## (ii) Metaphor as perspective

Section I (book pp. 503–505) develops the first substitution. Metaphor is "a device for seeing something in terms of something else"; it brings out the thisness of a that, or the thatness of a this. Burke introduces here one of his most useful terminological tools: the placeholder *character*, used as a general term for whatever can be thought of as distinct — any thing, pattern, situation, structure, nature, person, object, act, role, process, or event. To consider character A from the point of view of character B is to use B as a perspective upon A. Metaphor-as-perspective is the formal name of this operation.

The most provocative move of section I is the inversion of the relativist commonplace. It is customary, Burke notes, to say that objective reality is dissolved by the shifting of perspectives. He argues precisely the contrary: it is by the approach through a variety of perspectives that we establish a character's reality. The example is everyday: if we are in doubt as to what an object is, we deliberately try to consider it in as many different terms as its nature permits — lifting, smelling, tasting, tapping, holding in different lights, subjecting to different pressures, dividing, matching, contrasting. Multi-perspectivism is not the dissolution of objectivity; it is the procedure by which objectivity is constituted.

From this Burke derives the "poetic-realist" axiom: characters possess degrees of being in proportion to the variety of perspectives from which they can with justice be perceived. Plants admit more perspectives than minerals; animals more than plants; humans more than animals. Each higher order admits and requires a new dimension of terms not literally relevant to the lower orders. This is the axiom that will return in section II as the basis for the prohibition against reducing higher orders to lower-order vocabularies.

Section I closes with a sample list of motive-perspectives — human motivation considered in terms of conditioned reflexes, chemicals, class struggles, the love of God, neurosis, pilgrimage, power, planetary motion, geography, or sun spots. Burke's list is deliberately ecumenical: each item names a discourse-regime once deployed as the master perspective on motivation. The catalogue offers the dissertation chapter on game user-motive a model framing-set, with explicit acknowledgement that each is a perspective, not the perspective.

## (iii) Metonymy as reduction (the incorporeal-to-corporeal move)

Section II (book pp. 505–508) gives the second substitution: metonymy = reduction. The basic strategy of metonymy is "to convey some incorporeal or intangible state in terms of the corporeal or tangible." "Heart" for "emotions" is the textbook case. Trail any vocabulary of spiritual or mental states back far enough, Burke argues, and you find a metonymic origin: "emotion" itself encodes "motion," and the central strategy of Western materialism has been the reduction of consciousness to motion. I. A. Richards's playful substitution of "commotion" for "emotion" in *Principles of Literary Criticism* is named as a quietly metonymic move that re-archaicizes the term.

The two-direction history of language Burke sketches is one of the essay's most important contributions. Language develops by metaphorical extension — borrowing words from the corporeal, visible, tangible and applying them by analogy to the incorporeal, invisible, intangible. In the course of time the original corporeal reference is forgotten; only the incorporeal extension survives. Poets then regain the original relation in reverse, by a second carrying-over from the spiritual back into the tangible. This archaicizing return is what Burke names metonymy proper. Metonymy is therefore not merely a figure of speech: it is a load-bearing operation in the historical genesis and the artistic re-vivification of language.

The hinge of the section is the diagnosis of science. Science, qua science, is concerned with correlation, not substance, and is therefore not properly concerned with motivation — which is a substantial question. Any attempt to deal with human relations after the analogy of naturalistic correlations becomes the reduction of some higher or more complex realm of being to the terms of a lower or less complex realm. This is the operation Burke names *reduction* in the restricted sense: the one-direction metonymic move from quality to quantity, from higher to lower, from spiritual to material, that is the trope-signature of scientific realism. Reduction is metonymy disowned of its figurative status — a metonymy that presents itself as a "real" reduction rather than as a symbolization.

Burke's most consequential distinction in this section is the contrast between poetic and scientific behaviorism. Both translate the spiritual into bodily equivalents. The poet who writes that the shamed person averts the eye, colors at the cheek, or stiffens the muscles is doing metonymy: bodily equivalents stand as figures, symbolizations of the state. The scientific behaviorist who reduces the state itself to its bodily correlates is doing reduction: bodily equivalents are offered as the substantial reality of the state. The poet treats the body as figure; the scientist treats the body as substance. Crucially, Burke adds, the scientific behaviorist does this "not qua scientist, but only by reason of the materialist metaphysics, with its assumptions about substance and motive, that is implicit in his system." Science as such is innocent of the move; materialism-disguised-as-science is the culprit.

Section II closes with the pivot to synecdoche: a reduction is a representation. The relief-map of the United States is, within its limits, a representation. Metonymy overlaps synecdoche as it overlaps metaphor.

## (iv) Synecdoche as representation (part-for-whole; macrocosm-microcosm)

Section III (book pp. 508–511) gives the third substitution. Synecdoche is taken in the full dictionary range: part for whole, whole for part, container for contained, sign for thing signified, material for thing made, cause for effect, effect for cause, genus for species, species for genus. All such conversions imply an integral relationship — a relationship of *convertibility* — between the two terms. This convertibility is the essential feature distinguishing synecdoche from metonymy in Burke's working sense: representation runs along the road in both directions; reduction runs along it in one direction only (quality-to-quantity).

The "noblest synecdoche" — the perfect paradigm — is found in the metaphysical doctrines that proclaim the identity of microcosm and macrocosm. The individual is treated as a replica of the universe and vice versa; either whole can represent the part or part the whole. Burke offers Leibniz's monadology as the grand-scale paradigm, noting that *representation* is Leibniz's own word for this synecdochic relationship. The monad-as-mirror-of-the-universe is the metaphysical archetype, and the political theory of the *volonté générale* in Rousseau is its political analogue: some part of the social body (traditionally established, elected, or revolutionary) stands for the society as a whole. In a complex civilization any act of representation automatically implies a synecdochic relationship, insofar as the act is truly representative.

Burke extends the synecdochic concept by deliberate coaching to cover reversible pairs whose convertibility is not always recognized: before for after, implicit for explicit, temporal sequence for logical sequence, name for narrative, disease for cure, hero for villain, active for passive. The reversible-pairs extension is what makes synecdoche the bridge to irony — once you grant disease-for-cure convertibility, the dialectical structure of irony becomes available. The two literary examples Burke offers at this hinge are critical. In Coleridge's *Ancient Mariner*, the Albatross is "a gerundive": its nature when introduced is that-which-is-to-be-murdered. Its implicit future is contained in its explicit present. In *Moby-Dick*, Ahab as pursuer is pursued; his action is a passion. The synecdochic reversal of active and passive is structurally identical to the irony of peripety that section IV will name.

The methodological hinge of section III is the introduction of the *representative anecdote* concept (which the chapter on Scope and Reduction, GM-03, prepares in extenso). A terminology of conceptual analysis, if it is not to lead to misrepresentation, must be constructed in conformity with a representative anecdote — whereas anecdotes scientifically selected for reductive purposes are not representative. The scientist who selects a laboratory rat-experiment as the informative anecdote for human motivation, then transfers the terminology to adult acts, has not produced a representative interpretation but a "debunking." Two contemporary instances are named: Alfred Korzybski's "Structural Differential" (a contraption of string, plugs, and tin) as a reductive way-in to meaning, and Thurman Arnold's reduction of social relations to terms of the psychiatric metaphor. Otto Neurath's isotypes — visual statistics — are flagged as reductive representations, contrasted with realistic portrait painting as qualitative representation. The widespread belief that the mathematico-quantitative ideal of the physical sciences should serve as the ideal of the social sciences, leading to "the almost fabulous amassing of statistical surveys in the name of sociology," is named as the consequential tactical error.

## (v) Irony as dialectic (perspectives on perspectives)

Section IV (book pp. 511–516) is the longest of the five and the climactic. It opens with the equation of dialectic with the dramatic: where ideas are in action, we have drama; where agents are in ideation, we have dialectic. One might therefore speak of Socratic irony as dramatic, and of dramatic irony as Socratic. The equation is the meta-claim of dramatism itself: dialectic and drama are the same structure under two phenomenologies.

The operational definition of irony then follows: "Irony arises when one tries, by the interaction of terms upon one another, to produce a development which uses all the terms." From the standpoint of this total form — the "perspective of perspectives" — none of the participating sub-perspectives can be treated as either precisely right or precisely wrong. They are all voices, personalities, or positions, integrally affecting one another. When the dialectic is properly formed, they are exactly the number of characters needed to produce the total development.

Three temptations of irony are catalogued. The first is the collapse into *relativism*: relativism is got by the fragmentation of either drama or dialectic — isolating one agent's position and seeing the whole in its terms. In relativism, Burke says, there is no irony. Cleanth Brooks's observation that the very absence of irony in relativism makes it maximally susceptible to irony is acknowledged: the monologic absolutism that flattens all perspectives into one is itself the maximal case for ironic reversal. The greater the absolutism of the statements, the greater the subjectivity and relativity of the position of the agent making them.

The second temptation is the *Pharisaic* one, and it is the load-bearing distinction of the essay. Burke devotes the middle pages of section IV to the contrast between *romantic* irony and *classic* (or *true*, or *humble*) irony. The polemical occasion is a passage from Allen Tate on T. S. Eliot's *The Waste Land* seduction scene, which Tate had read as exemplifying the poet's humility. Tate's definition of irony — "that arrangement of experience, either premeditated by art or accidentally appearing in the affairs of men, which permits to the spectator an insight superior to that of the actor" — is the formulation Burke explicitly opposes. The Eliot of the seduction scene, on Burke's reading, is not humble; he is congratulating himself for not being like the petty clerk. "There but for the grace of God go I" is what Tate says the poet means; what the poet actually means, Burke argues, is "thank God I am not like other men." If this was humility, the Pharisee is Humble Citizen Number One.

The genuine model of irony-and-humility is offered as a counter-figure: Newton observing the criminal led to execution and remarking that the man was going to prison for him. Newton did not mean that the man was a criminal and he, Newton, was not; he meant that he too was a criminal, but that the other man was going to prison in his place. Newton was simultaneously both outside the criminal and within him. This is Burke's structural definition of consubstantial irony: the analyst is both inside and outside the position he analyses. Flaubert's "Madame Bovary, c'est moi" is the literary paradigm; Thomas Mann's "Judas psychology" is its applied psychology; Anatole France's gentle surrender is its softened form. In a long footnote, Falstaff is named as the gloriously ironic figure — convivially identifying with his victim, converting "thine" through "ours" into "mine" by sympathetic distortion of religious values — and contrasted with Tartuffe, who exploits cooperative values for competitive ends but does so as a pure hypocrite (Tartuffe simulates cooperation; Falstaff inhabits it). Eliot's religious problem is recast as the attempt to convert romantic irony into classic irony, with *Murder in the Cathedral* as the ritual of motive-purification.

Dialectic irony in this consubstantial sense Burke calls "a kind of technical equivalent for the doctrine of original sin." Folly and villainy are integral motives, necessary to wisdom or virtue. The Marxist example follows: in libertarian rhetoric slavery is bad, but from the standpoint of the development as a whole slavery must be treated ironically, as in Engels's "Without the slavery of antiquity, no modern socialism." Utilization of the vanquished by enslavement was a great cultural advance over the wasteful practice of slaying the vanquished.

The third temptation of irony is "the simplification of literalness." Although all the characters in a dramatic or dialectic development are necessary qualifiers, there is usually some one character that enjoys the role of *primus inter pares* — the summarizing vessel, the synecdochic representative of the development as a whole. Socrates plays this role in the Platonic dialogue; the proletariat plays it as "the Socrates of the Marxist Symposium of History." This character has a dual function: *adjectival* (one qualification among others) and *substantial* (the conclusion of the development as a whole). Irony is sacrificed to literalness when this duality is neglected — when the primus-inter-pares character is read either as merely one voice among others, or as the only voice that matters. The first error makes the dialectic relativistic; the second collapses it into doctrine.

Section V (book pp. 516–518) is the brief synthesis. Irony approached through either drama or dialectic moves into the area of "law" and "justice" — the necessity or inevitability of the *lex talionis*. Form-as-anticipation-and-fulfilment in art parallels prophecy and prediction in history. The over-all ironic formula is offered: "what goes forth as A returns as non-A." This is the basic pattern that places the essence of drama and dialectic in the irony of the *peripety*, the strategic moment of reversal. Burke pairs the peripety with "internal fatality": true irony involves a principle operating from within, though its logic may also be grounded in the nature of the extrinsic scene whose properties contribute to the same development. The point of casuistry is the fitting of these general laws of inevitability to the unique cases of history — exactly which new characters, born of a given prior character, will be the inevitable vessels of that prior character's deposition.

## (vi) Mutual transformability of the tropes

Burke's opening claim — that any one trope fully developed generates the other three — is the structural rule the essay exhibits. Metaphor shades into metonymy because to consider A in terms of B is a carrying-over that may be elaborated as reduction. Metonymy shades into synecdoche because a reduction is a representation (the relief-map argument). Synecdoche shades into irony because the reversible-pairs extension (disease-for-cure, hero-for-villain) gives the dialectical structure irony operates on. Irony shades back into metaphor because the perspective of perspectives is itself a perspective — the cycle closes.

The deeper rule is that irony, as the meta-trope, governs the others. Every metaphor is potentially ironic (the perspective taken may turn out to require its opposite); every metonymy is potentially ironic (the reduction may peripeteically restore the higher terms); every synecdoche is potentially ironic (the part that stands for the whole may stand for what undoes the whole). This is why the essay's structural climax is the analysis of irony in section IV.

## (vii) Trope–pentad interaction

Every trope-deployment is also a pentad-deployment. The matrix is not stated by Burke as such, but it is implicit in every page of the essay; constructing it explicitly is Phase 2's load-bearing extension.

Metaphor most-implicates *Agency* (a perspective is a means-of-considering) and *Scene* (the chosen perspective re-scenes the act). Each philosophic school in Part 2 features its pentad term via a metaphor-perspective on motivation: scene-as-frame for materialism, agent-as-frame for idealism, act-as-frame for realism, agency-as-frame for pragmatism, purpose-as-frame for mysticism.

Metonymy most-implicates *Act* (act reduced to behavior — the poetic-vs-scientific-behaviorism distinction) and *Agency* (reduction as methodological apparatus). The decisive cross-pentad collapse is the materialist motive-account that simultaneously reduces Agent to Scene and Purpose to Agency.

Synecdoche most-implicates *Agent* (part-whole convertibility between agent and class/society/cosmos — political and sensory representation, microcosm-macrocosm) and *Purpose* (the macrocosmic whole instantiated by the microcosmic part). Every representative-anecdote selection is a synecdochic election.

Irony most-implicates *Purpose* (the peripety A → non-A: what was aimed at returns as its reversal) and *Act* (peripety as reversal-of-act). Structurally, irony implicates ALL FIVE pentad terms — irony requires that all the terms participate, none precisely right or wrong; it operates on the ratios as a whole.

## (viii) Parallel apparatus to the pentad

GM-13 is appended as Appendix D, but its content is a fully self-standing analytical apparatus operating in parallel to the pentad rather than subsidiary to it. The pentad gives the five terms any statement of motive must deploy; the trope-apparatus gives the four operations those terms undergo. Together they constitute a five-by-four matrix the dissertation will work — every user-motive analysis names both its featured pentad term and its featured trope-operation. The parallel-apparatus claim justifies the unit's full-unit classification (target 35 edges, generating its own Phase 3 synthesis deliverable, the `gm-four-master-tropes-manual`); this Phase 2 deliverable produced 76 edges because cross-pentad-term and cross-pipeline bridges multiply rapidly once the apparatus is taken as parallel.

GM-13 directly anticipates Hayden White's *Metahistory* (1973), which deploys the same four tropes as the master typology of nineteenth-century historians (metaphor/Michelet, metonymy/Ranke, synecdoche/Tocqueville, irony/Burckhardt). Burke's 1945 apparatus is the proximate ancestor of the tropological historiography that White, Frye, and the Yale narratologists developed in the 1970s.

## (ix) Dissertation deployment for game user-motive analysis

The four-trope apparatus is the dissertation's primary lens for distinguishing how game-discourse constructs the user-motive. Each trope underwrites a recognizable discourse-regime.

*Metaphor / perspective*: cross-domain framing of the player. Gamification literature ports work/school/fitness into "play," framing the player as consumer, laborer, co-author, inhabitant, athlete, citizen, or believer. Each framing is a perspective on the user-motive that selects different pentad ratios for visibility. Game-studies sub-schools can be mapped onto featured perspectives: Aarseth's ergodic theory features labor; Bogost's procedural-rhetoric features rhetor; Calleja's incorporation features inhabitant; Sicart's play features citizen. None is the perspective; each is a metaphor whose representativeness can be examined.

*Metonymy / reduction*: telemetric reduction of the player. KDA, win rate, MMR, engagement minutes, retention curve, conversion funnel — the quantitative correlates by which platforms track the user — are metonymic reductions of player-motive to scoreboard-numbers. Burke's distinction between metonymy-as-figure and metonymy-as-substantial-reduction is the load-bearing methodological move. Telemetry-as-figure (numbers as symbolization of a quality whose substance lies elsewhere) is benign; telemetry-as-substantial-reduction (motive IS the number) is the source of consequential design pathologies. Gamification rhetoric often deploys the second while claiming the warrants of the first.

*Synecdoche / representation*: avatar-stands-for-player is the canonical game-studies synecdoche. Sub-deployments include party-for-player (RPG), civilization-for-player (4X), username-for-player (multiplayer), screenshot/clip-for-session (streaming), leaderboard-rank-for-skill (esports). Each is a synecdoche to analyse for representativeness — does it genuinely represent the player (two-way convertibility) or reductively misrepresent (collapsing into metonymy)? The well-formed session is internally synecdochic: tutorial contains endgame; endgame sums tutorial.

*Irony / dialectic*: meta-gaming, speedrunning, glitch-exploitation, RMT, botting, grief-play, and role-playing-against-roleplay are moments where the peripety A → non-A operates on the designed objective. A difficulty-curve designed for mastery returns as toxic gatekeeping; a cooperation-system returns as harassment-vector; a retention-economy returns as compulsion-loop critique. Burke's distinction between romantic / Pharisaic irony (the developer who treats the toxic player as "not me") and classic / humble irony (the developer who recognizes the toxic optimizer as the rational extension of the designed incentive) is the dissertation's load-bearing normative criterion.

The five-by-four matrix yields twenty cells the chapter argument will work. Two especially load-bearing cells are (synecdoche × Agent) the avatar question, and (irony × Purpose) the consubstantial-developer question.
