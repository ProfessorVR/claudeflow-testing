## (i) Agent in general / Idealization / Unification (book pp. 171–177)

Burke opens the chapter with the Baldwin dictionary's definition of idealism — a theory holding the universe to be throughout the work of reason and mind — and immediately recodes the philosophical position as the pentad-grammatical school that features Agent as motive-locus. The diagnostic move is precise: idealist philosophies are recognizable not by which terms they use (because every philosophy uses all five pentad terms) but by which term they treat as the originating ground from which the other four are derived. Idealism derives scene, act, agency, and purpose from properties of the agent — the constituting subject whose ranged vocabulary Burke catalogs early: ego, self, super-ego, consciousness, will, the generalized I, the subjective, mind, spirit, the oversoul. To this inventory of individual agents he immediately adds the collective and transcendent agents he calls "super-persons": church, race, nation, and the practice of treating historical periods or cultural movements as personalities. Such super-persons are typical indicators of idealism wherever found.

The aesthetic-idealist cluster is named at once as a bridge between metaphysical and aesthetic idealism: sensibility, expression, self-expression, consciousness, the unconscious. Burke immediately exemplifies with Henry James's preface to *The Portrait of a Lady* — James's account of the artist's prime sensibility as the soil out of which his subject springs and which grows the work of art. Here, Burke observes, the book is treated as an act grounded in the author's mind as its motivating scene. The same idealist pattern carries into James's compositional method, where some sensibility serves as the appreciative centre of the story and the reader follows the narrative through this single consciousness. The note is structural: idealism's close connection with epistemology — with the problem of knowledge — is due to the same bias, since to approach the universe by asking how knowledge is possible is to ground speculation psychologistically, in the nature of the knower.

The first internal subsection, "Idealization," supplies the sociological invitation to idealism. Wherever economic relations have been *idealized* or *spiritualized*, idealism is invited as a philosophy. Burke's case study is the Greek *Moira*. In modern English the word names the ancient deity who assigns to every man his lot — Destiny, the Three Fates. The Greek dictionary recovers a much more realistic original sense: a part (as opposed to the whole), one's due, a share or portion as of a meal. The realistic *moira* referred to the amount an individual tribe-member received when goods were divided up; class differentiation gradually shifted the meaning so that one's *moira* came to be decided when one was born into one social class rather than another, a peasant's lot being traditionally different from the king's. In proportion as the word lost its realistic-distributive reference, it became idealized or spiritualized. Words of this sort, Burke notes, are particularly serviceable when, unity having given way to disunity, there is a call for unification. Plato's *Republic* is the paradigm: it aims at a unified State founded upon a vision of absolute Good as a reaction to the individualistic and relativistic teachings of the Sophists, who had defined justice realistically as *dike* (a way of life), noting that different ways of life produce different justices. Plato seeks a higher concept of justice transcending all the different concrete justices; dialectically, any conflict between two concepts can be removed by adopting a remoter term broad enough to encompass both, as a distinction between farmhouse and palace can be resolved by classing both as dwellings.

Burke amplifies the idealization analysis through the written contract and the corporation-as-person legal fiction. Before the spread of literacy a promise could be broken by simply forgetting it; after literacy, broken promises require lawyers to argue that words no longer mean what they were meant to mean. Corporation lawyers, eventually become judges, write into the law of the land the idealistic fiction that financial corporations are persons (deserving the freedom granted to human beings by divine, natural, or constitutional law). Idealism's creative aspect is also marked: an ideal serves as standard, guide, incentive — the visionary futurism of Washington or Lenin, of Shelley, of the promoter and his investors — and so an idea of justice may make possible some measure of its embodiment in material situations. Coleridge's view of poetry as a "dim analogue of Creation" exemplifies the strong idealist bias in aesthetic theories. Positive law theories likewise fall under idealism: Rousseau's *volonté générale*, the U.S. fiction that the will of the people today is consubstantial with the will of the Founding Fathers, the document considered as a structure of motivations grounded in human attitudes.

The "Unification" subsection then states a near-aphoristic cultural law: realism plus money equals idealism. The tribal pattern of thinking, broken by the new ways money promotes, calls for the unification we find in nationalism. Money introduces a new term that gives barter a new dimension; the greater the development of the financial rationale, the greater the "spirituality" in man's relations to material goods — which he sees less in their actual nature as goods and more in the ideal terms of future and of monetary symbolic profit. Technology compounds the same idealizing pressure on two counts: like money, it produces diversity and unequal rates of change that require unifying ideas as social corrective; and as applied science it invites major stress on knowledge, which is the epistemological problem, which falls under agent. Emerson's *Nature* passage — about how no man owns the landscape but the poet whose eye can integrate all the parts — is offered as paradigm idealistic-unification image.

## (ii) Berkeley (book pp. 177–181)

Berkeley is offered as the representative idealist not because he is the originator (Descartes and Leibniz precede him, the German Big Four follow) but because in his *Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge* the idealist terminology is put forward with as much clarity and directness as the Hobbesian materialist terminology in *Leviathan*. The inquiry begins psychologistically — and therefore in terms of agent — by questioning Locke's "abstract ideas." Berkeley denies that one can conceive a triangle in the abstract: one must have a picture, more or less accurate, of some visible or tangible triangle. By reducing abstract ideas to mere words, Berkeley is surprisingly able to reduce all sensory experience to ideas. The famous Berkeleian equation follows: to be is to be perceived — *esse is percipi*. Note that he makes ideas and sensations synonymous: the world we navigate is a world of ideas. Reversing the usual application of Occam's parsimony, Berkeley argues that no hypothesis of matter is necessary to account for experience; matter could be missed from the world entirely and everything would as well be conceived.

For Burke's diagnostic purposes the steps need only show clearly a functioning of agent. The active principle is named more directly: the perceiving active being is what Berkeley calls mind, spirit, soul, or myself. Ideas themselves are inactive (the things the agent thinks); things in the everyday sense are collections of ideas; an apple is a certain color-taste-smell-figure-consistency observed to go together. The position is summed in paragraph 26: the cause of ideas cannot itself be an idea, must therefore be a substance, but there is no corporeal or material substance, hence the cause of ideas is an incorporeal active substance or Spirit. Spirit is one simple undivided active being — as it perceives ideas it is called the understanding, as it produces or operates about them it is called the will. Spirit can be perceived empirically only through its effects, which allows for empirical evidence of the existence of God, who happily for Burke's purposes Berkeley calls the "Almighty Agent." Burke's gloss is decisive: God is Berkeley's equivalent for the ultimate scene — scene as translated into terms of agent. The Berkeleian *esse is percipi* may be translated, for Burke's purposes, as "to be is to be grounded in the term super-agent."

The strategic operation is the most concentrated example in the chapter of how idealism features Agent: every scenic remainder gets translated, by the Grammatical logic of the philosophical idiom, into terms of super-agent. This is what it *means* for idealism to feature Agent — not that scene drops out, but that scene is recoded into the agent vocabulary.

## (iii) Hume (book pp. 181–184)

Hume is the transitional figure who narrows Berkeleian idealism's circumference. By equating ideas with sensations — and then, by parsimony, dropping the rationalist/intelligible side of the equation — empiricism focuses on the side that lends itself the better to laboratory investigation. Hume's *Inquiry Concerning Human Understanding* divides perceptions into impressions (more lively) and ideas (less forcible); creative mental power amounts to compounding, transposing, augmenting, or diminishing the materials of sense; where a sense is defective, no idea is possible; the test for the meaningfulness of any term is to inquire from what impression the idea is derived.

Applied to causality, the test is devastating. Cause-and-effect is not attained by reasoning *a priori* but arises from experience; we learn from experience that an unsupported stone will fall, but there is no logical way of anticipating such an effect. If we came into the world with fully developed powers of reason but without experience of fact, we should not know what to anticipate. The principle directing our expectations is psychological: custom or habit. Habit gives rise to a scene-agent ratio on a purely experiential basis, in contrast with the Stoic doctrine of a universal rationality pervading both natural scene and human agent.

Burke marks the symbolic-level resonance: in Hume's skepticism, the great dramatist cluster of terms — reason, substance, cause, necessity, action, idea, God, Nature, generation, power — is beginning to fall apart. The "placid bachelor" symbolism is a Burke note in passing: a metaphysical questioning of potency and progeny by a thinker who has himself opted out of those generative cycles. The dethronement of causality is the rejection of an essentially ancestral or parental term; on the Symbolic level there is a pattern of "race suicide" implicit in the turn from causality to the cult of sheer correlation. Yet Hume is correct, Burke grants, that there is no purely empirical evidence for concepts like causality, power, necessary connection. Factual sequences can be interpreted as causality but with equal justice as Divine Purpose, so far as sense-evidence is concerned. Causality or power themselves are not empirically observable any more than God is. The impression of such ideas arises merely from reflecting on the operations of our own minds — which is to say, in Burke's diagnostic, from agent.

## (iv) Leibniz (book pp. 184–187)

Leibniz is treated briefly as the rationalism of idealist cast that Kant inherits alongside Hume's skepticism. Leibniz stressed substance and necessary logical connection, but in a psychologistic-perceptual key: substance was endowed with the power to represent. The Leibnizian universe is a world of individuals — monads — each with its own particular point of view, each realizing itself to the extent the limits of its nature permit. Burke's diagnostic comment: Leibniz's monads are atom-agents, each developing its own inner potentialities, its own particular range of growth (widest in the case of man) from the implicit to the explicit. We see in Leibniz, Burke notes, the beginnings of our modern psychologies of the unconscious. The pre-established harmony — God as Monas Monadum adjusting all monads to one another so that their development has the same effect as if they were mutually constraining — is the *deus ex machina* device that solves the windowless-monad coordination problem (the monads represent one another by representing their own particular points of view, without inter-monadic perception). Leibniz stressed plurality as strongly as Spinoza had stressed unity, but God brought plurality to a unified head.

The Leibnizian idealizing of substance — allowing a strong individualist emphasis along with an over-all principle of unity — gave exactly the pattern of agent and super-agent that we find, Burke observes, in enterprisers and nation. The unification-pressure that Leibnizian philosophy supplied to Kant is the German-cultural-political pressure of small states separated by cultural traditions and tariff barriers, feeling the individualism of rising capitalism, moving toward national union. The Idea, allowing for futuristic developmental optimism, is needed precisely because unification implies a gerundive — a *to-be-unified*.

## (v) Kant + Moral Transcendence in Kant (book pp. 187–198)

Burke approaches Kant by a deliberate constructive subterfuge: imagine ourselves trying to work up a Kantian vocabulary in accordance with the linguistic resources, temptations, and embarrassments studied so far. This is the chapter's dramatistic-pedagogical centerpiece. Three requirements: (a) recognize Newtonian celestial mechanics as the regnant scientific paradigm — terminology must accommodate strict laws of motion; (b) inherit Leibnizian principle of action, an ethical principle wider in scope than sheer motion, but substitute for Leibniz's finalism something more "scientific"; (c) confront the Humean jolt, which has dethroned causality on empirical grounds.

The Humean passage Burke quotes makes the request explicit: there is between the propositions "I have found such an object always attended with such an effect" and "I foresee similar objects will be attended with similar effects" a step taken, a process of thought, an inference, that wants to be explained. There is required a *medium* enabling the mind to draw such an inference. That request for a medium is, Burke says, our cue. One usually thinks of a medium as something in which an agent acts (scene) or which an agent uses (agency); but what if we equate it with the very nature of the agent itself? People interpret natural sequences in terms of cause and effect not because of something in the natural scene requiring this interpretation, but because they are the sort of agents that see things in terms of necessary relations. Burke's operationalization is precise: Kant solves Hume by equating medium with agent. To attain objective necessity rather than mere private opinion, we universalize agent: not your understanding or mine but "the understanding" in general.

The Kantian apparatus unfolds in three layers. First, the action-passion pair splits the universal agent: the sensibility (passive) receives intuitions; the understanding (active) performs unification by concepts. Objects are given to us by sensibility (which yields intuitions); they are thought through the understanding (from which arise concepts). Second, the concern is raised to objects-in-general; the conditions of possibility of knowledge are located transcendentally in the agent rather than in the object. Space and time are forms of sensibility; the categories are concepts of understanding; together they comprise the "constitutive" — a scene with circumference narrowed to the scope of motion, derived from a function of agent. Burke's grammatical translation: it is not materialism (because scene is said to derive from a function of agent) and not yet supernaturalism (because the agent from which the scenic nature derives is a universalized human, not a divine super-agent).

Burke notes a problem already at this stage: the transcendental synthesis is called spontaneous and original (active) but is also called inevitable — the mind cannot see otherwise than in terms of the categories. So the active begins to look as compulsory as the avowedly passive; we have a description of conditions as they necessarily are for human experience, but no room yet for *wills*, *oughts*, *shoulds*, or *thou-shalt-nots*. This sets up the Critical move of the third Critique stage.

The "Moral Transcendence in Kant" subsection is the chapter's most concentrated piece of pentad-grammatical analysis. Burke borrows from Coleridge the strategic operation of *desynonymizing*: Coleridge in the *Biographia Literaria* made a distinction-in-kind between *imagination* and *fancy* (Latin *imaginatio* and Greek *phantasia* had previously been taken as synonymous). The great departures in human thought, Burke generalizes, can be reduced to a moment where the thinker treats as opposite, key terms formerly considered synonymous, or vice versa. In Kant the strategic desynonymizing is between *transcendental* and *transcendent*. The empirical realm is the realm of appearances; the transcendental realm is the realm that gives things the nature they seem to have in the empirical realm; the transcendent realm is the realm of things-in-themselves, of things-without-context, of the unconditioned. Grammatically — and this is Burke's pentad-grammatical re-mapping — relations are determinations (termini); a thing considered apart from context is undetermined; the undetermined is the unconditioned; and the unconditioned is the realm of freedom. Hence the transcendent is the scene that allows for the possibility of action.

The remaining moves complete the structure. By definition (since we have defined knowledge by empirical tests), the transcendent realm of unconditioned things-in-themselves cannot be *known*, but it can be *thought*. Kant's preface to the second edition is glossed: I must abolish knowledge to make room for belief. The intelligible is redefined as that-which-cannot-be-sensed (an ingenious reversal of empiricism's original equating of intelligible with sensible). Phenomena and noumena pair off, the noumena being a present-passive participle from the same root as Greek *nous*. The Kantian dialectical move from conditioned to unconditioned, from things-in-relation to things-in-themselves, from determinate to indeterminate (from necessity to freedom) Kant frankly labels dialectical — which, Burke observes, conceals from us and himself the equally dialectical ingredients in the first two sections. All three involve linguistic operations that, by the very nature of language, transcend the terminology of the senses.

The als-ob principle bridges: we cannot know there are God, freedom, and immortality, but we ought to act as if there were. Moral action is rooted in these ideas, which refer back to the transcendent realm. Burke supplies a non-Kantian-but-not-anti-Kantian Grammatical analogue: think of an enactment, as with the enacting of a constitution. The enacting is the forming of the constitution; the constitution is the permanent form left by the enactment. Men come together of their own free will to enact the constitution; the resulting document is not free — it must be just what it is, the clauses necessarily related to one another. So freedom (in the noumenal realm) and necessity (in the phenomenal realm) are bridged through enactment and constitution as a pentad-grammatical schema. Plato's *Republic* Book V passage on the city's courage under all conditions is offered as a paradigm of how ideals of action can be said to transcend empirical conditions.

A long footnote that I treat substantively here flags the role of the *transcendental imagination* (Kant's mediating "schema" / "schematism" between sensibility and understanding). Burke is careful to note that some commentators consider imagination in Kant the principle of unification *par excellence* — and this anticipates the chapter-closing Imagination subsection, where imagination becomes the cluster term inherited from Kant via Coleridge into Stevens.

## (vi) Idealism after Kant (book pp. 198–200)

The post-Kantian sequence (Fichte / Schelling / Hegel / Schopenhauer) is treated compactly. Burke marks one Grammatical embarrassment Kant slid over: if the phenomenal is the realm of relationships and the noumenal is the realm of things-in-themselves (without relationships), how could there be a bond between the two realms? Kant compromised with the weasel-word *influences* — the noumenal "influences" the phenomenal. Fichte responded by abolishing the thing-in-itself: it is not necessary; the Ego and a Non-Ego derived from it suffice. The pattern is obviously closer to Berkeley than to Kant — idealism returns to the agent-monolithic terminus.

Burke notes a Symbolic-level resonance: the Fichtean Not-I (*Nicht-Ich*) produces in German a word pronounced like *nichtig*, whose dictionary range is unreal, vain, frivolous, empty, hollow, futile, flimsy, transitory, ineffectual — derived from the word for nothing, like English *naughty*. The Ego claims too much in Fichte's scheme, so that too little is left for the Not-Ego. From Fichte onward, the pattern of idealism pure and simple is set. Schelling, Hegel, and Schopenhauer trace the genealogy of the objective world from the subjective, treating nature as externalization or expression of spirit — edifying in Fichte, aesthetic in Schelling, optimistic in Hegel, pessimistic in Schopenhauer (where the externalizations of universal will are treated rather like the involuntary expressions that post-Schopenhauer psychologies will call "compulsion neuroses"). The post-Fichte pattern is one of *externalization*: implicit-to-explicit, unconscious-to-conscious, magma-to-lava, visceral-to-excretory. This is Burke's compressed diagnostic on the entire post-Kantian idealist movement as a Symbolic-level inner-to-outer figure.

## (vii) Marxism / Hegel / Communist Manifesto / Dramatist Grammar for Marxism (book pp. 200–215)

The chapter's second center of gravity follows. Burke's reading of Marxism is the chapter's most distinctive interpretive move and the principal reason GM-05 (Agent in General) is the locus for Marxism rather than GM-04 (Scene-as-materialism). The reading rests on three connected diagnostic moves.

First, Marxian dialectical materialism grows out of idealism *by antithesis*. Hegel had stressed a developmental feature in the expression, externalization, embodiment, or utterance (*Äusserung*) of the spirit through the medium of nature and history. The process of mediation (*Vermittlung*) yielded a much more concrete view of conditions than the high abstractions of Kant. In effect we got a super-agent (Spirit) manifesting itself in progressively changing historical conditions — scenes of narrowed circumference. Marx materialistically reversed the genealogy: matter as *prius*, with consciousness/reason/sensation derivative, connected only with the higher (organic) forms of matter. Lenin (in *Materialism and Empirio-Criticism*) detects the idealistic bias in Machian post-Kantian empiricism, which took its start from the data of sensation (a property of agent), and reverses it: matter is the prius.

Second, however, Marxism is *dialectical* not vulgar or mechanical materialism — and Burke immediately translates this as "idealistic materialist." If one is to trace key terms from a heraldic source in scene, and is to do so with good Grammar, there must be some quality of agenthood permeating the scene itself. Lenin supplies the move: a property similar to sensation in the foundation stones of the structure of matter itself. The doctrine of thought-and-consciousness as products of the human brain (and man himself as product of Nature) yields a correspondence-doctrine in which thought is of the same substance as nature and can therefore be a reflection of nature — and this allows for the gradual accumulation and perfection of natural knowledge.

Third — and this is the chapter's most consequential structural finding — Marxism's constant call upon human agents and above all its futuristic stress on kinds of social unification are intensely idealistic. The class concept is the agent-locus. Burke is explicit that to characterize Marxism's strategy *from without*, in over-all dramatistic terms, he must present the position in somewhat non-Marxist terms. The Marxist project, in his Grammar, is not a fifth materialism alongside Hobbes-Spinoza-Darwin-Lucretius; it is a hybrid that extends and inverts Hegelian idealism while retaining the Hegelian super-agent-structure (now reconfigured as class-as-collective-agent).

The "Communist Manifesto" subsection then treats the document as a *constitution*. The Manifesto is not merely regulative but soundly constitutive: it grounds its political principles in statements about the nature of the universal scene (a scene narrowed to naturalistic limits, defined with relation to the laws and directions of human history). The class-struggle dialectic — freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman — yields the freedom-promise that the dialectic of class antagonism must be followed through to the termination at which all production has been concentrated in the hands of a vast association of the whole nation, the public power will lose its political character, and the proletariat will sweep away the conditions for the existence of class antagonisms. This last step marks the withering-away of the State that Lenin discusses in *State and Revolution*.

The Manifesto deploys the scene-agent ratio materialistically when asserting that every change in conditions of man's material existence is accompanied by change in man's ideas, views, and conceptions — consciousness as reflection of conditions. The Manifesto contains about fifteen references to the role played by conditions in the motivating of social change. Yet, Burke notes the structural tension: the Manifesto AS act of propaganda presupposes that ideas are social forces and that the course of human action will be in some degree altered by the diffusion of these ideas. The closing challenge of the Manifesto — the communists disdain to conceal their views and aims — performs Agent-feature even while the rhetoric reflects-conditions.

The Lenin gloss on *What Is To Be Done?* completes the analysis: against those who relied on spontaneity, Lenin insisted that the task of preparing for the revolution required a triple struggle (theoretical, political, economic) under the leadership of a centralized revolutionary party. Burke marks the critical moment in dialectical-materialist motivation-theory: it is here that the dialectic must transform into characterization of motivational structure as a whole.

"A Dramatist Grammar for Marxism" is then Burke's tentative reconstruction of the Marxist doctrine in act-featuring terms. Each social class, insofar as it has a way of life distinct from that of other classes, is distinct in *actus*, hence in *status*, hence in *properties*. Burke imports the GM-02 substance-vocabulary (the *actus*/*status* pair) into the Marxist reconstruction. The motivational table he supplies — translated into general dramatistic terms — runs through twenty-odd propositions: properties of a class may become unsuited to society's productive forces; an idea is "active" insofar as "adequate" (it accurately names the malign and benign properties of society); society suffers social passions insofar as ideas are inadequate; the ruling class's inadequate ideas are reinforced by State resources; a class suffering deprivation has incentive to question the State's structure; the agencies of the State prevent transformation of passion into action by suppressing adequate ideas; revolutionary act = transformation of class-passion into class-action under guidance of party-as-class-within-class with adequate ideas; revolutionary act is itself irrational (passion) insofar as it produces dislocations; revolutionary body transcends passions insofar as ideas are adequate and lead to revolutionary success; new status follows; new common properties → new common *actus* → new common status; inadequate naming of new properties yields conditions for new conflicts. Burke himself characterizes this as a Spinozist-translated reconstruction "class-angled" — i.e., a Spinozist apparatus (idea-as-active-when-adequate; ordo idearum / ordo rerum parallelism) class-angled by the recognition of class-consciousness as partly-active and partly-unconscious. Class-consciousness in the un-aware sense is more aptly termed *class unconsciousness*; class-consciousness in the deliberately-cultivated sense involves the rationale of Marxist propaganda. Capitalist propaganda gives the concept of consciousness another twist — frigidaire-conscious, two-car-conscious — where the aim is to impose on the critical faculty an automatic (uncritical) response.

The Marxism cluster closes with a careful misreading-correction: anti-Marxists who complain against Marxist materialism miss the step in which the philosophy is most characteristically idealistic — Marx's criticism of false idealism derived from the concealed protection of materialistic interests. The whole purpose of materialist criticism, Burke notes, is to bring about such material conditions as are thought capable of releasing men from their false bondage to materials. The chapter's "Fetishism of Commodities" reference catches the move precisely: the human personality itself comes to be conceived in the abstract terms of impersonal commodities, and the materialist critique aims to release agents from this abstraction. Marxism is, in Burke's Grammar, the chapter's hybrid case: idealistic-materialist, with class-as-collective-agent as motive-locus and a futuristic-unification stress that places it structurally in the Agent chapter, not the Scene chapter.

### Santayana (book pp. 215–223)

The transition to Santayana is given as ironic: another materialism that also grew antithetically out of German transcendentalism, more explicit than Marxism in stressing the material basis of man's spiritual fulfilment, structurally parallel to Marx as an idealistic-materialist. Santayana's skepticism — extending doubt to its most exacting lengths — yields one unquestionable knowledge: that we see what we see. There is no illusion in appearances; appearances are exactly what they are. Burke marks the move: from there Santayana turns materialist via *animal faith*, the natural-organism-faith that "posits" the existence of the external material world. *Setzen* is the German Stance-word; we might gloss "posits" with "supposes." Material world taken on faith; faith is traditionally an act word; matter as field of action.

Four realms of being: matter, essence, spirit, truth. Spirit is grounded materialistically yet its actuality is located in its freedom from material conditions — "moral fruition of physical life." Spirit can transcend the mechanical flux of nature yet is located in an animal psyche dependent on material conditions. Marx avoided pragmatism via the class concept; Santayana, at Harvard during the period when German idealism was being transformed into American pragmatism, shows pragmatist influence (equating realm of matter with utility) but submerges the pragmatist strain beneath strongly dramatistic patterns (equating spirit and essence with meanings directly opposite to utility).

The doctrine of essence is the idealist core inside the materialist frame. Essences are appearances considered in themselves — an infinity of eternal possibilities, only a small fraction ever embodied in natural existence. Existence proceeds by embodying an endless succession of essences, advancing at each moment from character to character. Santayana likens existence to an eye following a thin stream of script as "re-agent" thus selecting one line of possibilities from among the infinite number inscribed in the totality of the eternal essences (the "indelible background"). Burke immediately marks the pattern as a variant of the scribe-script-scroll metaphysical-poetic pattern as exemplified in Avicebron's *Fons Vitae*. With Avicebron the scene is matter (the scroll), the act is form (the script), the agent (the scribe) is will. In Santayana's variant the scroll corresponds to the entire immaterial realm of essence; script-and-scribe combined equal the narrow bit of text selected by existence as re-agent.

Santayana's essences differ from Plato's Forms: Platonic essences were above all else familial — principles of generation, pure sources from which all members of their kind are descended. Santayana's essences are uncompromisingly individual, even individualistic, without progeny; explicitly not seeds. The philosophical discipline of arriving at them is likened to chastity prolonged until late in youth. The dialectical role of essence is that of rest — and since the orbit of mechanical motion is ambiguously called the field of action, the rest here indicated is ambiguously both the cessation of motion and the end of action. Santayana's pentad-mapping alignment, in Burke's reconstruction, is: (1) motion ambiguously "biological action"; (2) essence as corresponding stasis; (3) spirit as dramatic actuality-and-passion; (4) essence as end of spirit. Spiritual action transcends biological utility; its ultimate delight is in the contemplation of essence, which in the last analysis is a benign contemplation of death — *thanatopsis*. Santayana's culmination is a termination. His philosophy is, Burke notes, the exact opposite of a patriarchal one: spirit is powerless, all power is in nature, nature is to spirit as mother is to child. He thinks as one who is the end of a line; his concern is with culminations.

The Santayana subsection is structurally important to the chapter because it instantiates the same structural parallel as Marxism — a materialism grown antithetically out of German transcendentalism that retains an Agent-featuring core. The dissertation-load-bearing finding: idealism's afterlives in both Marxist and Santayanan materialism preserve the structural Agent-feature even where surface vocabulary inverts the genealogy.

## (viii) Agent as pentad-term operationalization for the dissertation

Drawing the chapter's terminological work together, the Agent operationalization for dissertation deployment can be stated compactly. *Agent* is the pentad term that names the doer of any act — the constituting subject from which, in idealist construal, motive originates. Idealism features Agent by deriving scene, act, agency, and purpose from properties of the agent; non-idealist construals deploy Agent without featuring it. The question Agent answers is *Who?*, with the derivative questions: in whose consciousness? by which mind? by which subject? by which collective subject (class, party, race, nation)? when does the agent-position shift across the player/avatar/character chain in a worked dramatistic case?
