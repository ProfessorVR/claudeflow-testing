## i. Scene in general

Burke opens Part 2 with a programmatic statement that recasts the entire second half of the *Grammar* as a typology of philosophic idioms generated by the pentad. The pentad terms overlap, he reminds us at book p. 127, and any thinker can begin from any one of them and make his way to the others — either by reducing all five to a single ancestor, or by deducing them from one. This generative flexibility means that the same five terms, when redeployed with one taken as `Ausgangspunkt`, produce qualitatively distinct philosophic vocabularies. Burke states the correspondences as bare propositions at book p. 128: featuring of scene yields materialism; featuring of agent yields idealism; featuring of agency yields pragmatism; featuring of purpose yields mysticism; featuring of act yields realism. Nominalism and rationalism are added as two cross-cutting movements (atomistic versus collectivistic emphasis; method-as-substance) that bring the typology to seven primary idioms.

The diagnostic move is sharpened on the following pages. Each philosophic school can be recognized by which one of the five terms it lets generate the others; the philosophy's `problems`, derived terms, and characteristic confusions all follow from the chosen ancestor-term. Burke compares the situation to a perpetual motion machine whose original design becomes fantastically complex as each new contrivance corrects a defect of the previous one. The pentad's role, by contrast, is to extricate the analyst from the resulting intricacies by displaying what kind of assertion each school exemplifies in a "hypothetical state of purity."

The "Scene in General" subsection that closes the preamble (book pp. 131–132) supplies materialism's defining sources. Burke quotes Baldwin's *Dictionary of Philosophy and Psychology*: materialism is the metaphysical theory which regards all the facts of the universe as sufficiently explained by the assumption of body or matter, conceived as extended, impenetrable, eternally existent, and susceptible of movement or change of relative position. He cites Hobbes's epigrammatic line ("all that exists is body, all that occurs motion"), Paulsen's gloss on the reduction of psychical processes to the physical, and the Britannica's similar formulation. He immediately adds the load-bearing qualification: with materialism the circumference of scene is `so narrowed` that action is reduced to motion. The materialist need not deny the existence of a personal God; he need only omit the personal principle from the scope of his motivational vocabulary. Santayana's pamphlet *Aspects of Scientific Rationalism in the Nineteenth Century* supplies the epigrammatic close — the atom, to do scientific work, must be quite dead, its substance devoid of all spontaneity.

This narrowing-of-circumference move is the chapter's master analytic. Every subsequent philosopher — Hobbes, Spinoza, Darwin, Lucretius, Aurelius, Epictetus — is read as drawing the scenic circumference at a particular radius and then deriving the other four pentad terms from the ground so circumscribed. The variations within materialism are variations in how the circumference is drawn, how strictly it is enforced, and what symptoms appear when the suppressed pentad terms press back against the constraint.

## ii. Hobbes

Hobbes is presented (book pp. 132–137) as the most thorough and picturesque exemplar of a vocabulary systematically built on extension — Democritus surviving only in fragments, Hobbes is the available standard-bearer. Burke quotes the *Leviathan* on nature as the art whereby God hath made and governs the world; the body as automaton with springs (heart), strings (nerves), and wheels (joints); and the State as a gigantic man, an artificial man of greater stature and strength than the natural, made for protection and defence. Burke's diagnostic is that the metaphor of the machine functions as Hobbes's `Ausgangspunkt`: the figure of speech that organizes the entire vocabulary, despite Hobbes's own warnings against metaphor. The parallel to Coriolanus's belly-and-members parable is invoked at book p. 132 to mark the difference between Shakespeare's figurative use of body-politic analogy and Hobbes's literal mechanistic redeployment of it.

The chapter's mid-section walks through Hobbes's progressive reductions. Sense, in the first chapter "Of Sense," is caused by the external body or object pressing the organ; the sensory qualities the object seems to possess are really motions of matter pressing the organs diversely. Imagination is "decaying sense" — a weaker motion. Memory is the same motion further faded. Thoughts succeed each other as motions; reason in Chapter V is mere addition and subtraction of names ("a man `reasoneth`... does nothing else but conceive a sum total, from addition of parcels"). The full mechanistic reduction means reason is to be modelled by something like a comptometer; the logical conclusion is that the mind has "no other motion" besides the train of thoughts that constitute it.

The reduction extends to the passions. Hobbes treats vital and animal motion, locates the "first internal beginning" of voluntary motion in imagination, and names the small invisible motions before walking, speaking, or striking `endeavor`. Endeavor subdivides into `appetite` and `aversion`, words Hobbes derives from the Latin and notes are equivalent to the Greek `hormē` and `aphormē`. Will, in Hobbes's notorious formula, is "the last appetite in deliberating." Burke reads this as the strategic core of the materialist reduction: the apparent act of free will is in reality the mechanical triumph of a stronger motion over a weaker. Imagine, Burke says, that one places appetite on one side of a balance and aversion on the other; the result follows from the disproportion of weights.

But — and this is the diagnostic finding — at precisely this point Burke notices the symmetry break. Hobbes does not speak of "motion" here but of "action" (will as "the act, not the faculty, of willing"). Burke's reading is that Hobbes is treating a traditionally theological/voluntarist subject and uses the inherited action-vocabulary as a convenience, even though the whole point of his philosophy is the explanation of such actions in terms of motion. Burke generalizes: when a philosopher would feature one of the terms, recreating the others in its image, the original claims of those other terms are nonetheless still in the offing. A billiard ball is neither moral nor immoral, for it cannot act — only move or be moved. Hobbes, in carrying his mechanism into the moral realm, is necessarily treading on domains more directly governed by `act` and `agent`. Burke promises return to this problem with Kant in GM-05, and observes — anticipating his concluding move — that the realm of political and ethical relations "calls naturally" for treatment in terms of action because that is what the study of ethics simply is.

## iii. Spinoza and the alignment of terms

Spinoza receives the chapter's heaviest treatment (book pp. 137–152) — two consecutive subsections, an Active/Passive ledger reproduced verbatim from Burke's reading, and the watershed-of-Western-thought designation. The opening move at book p. 137 makes the analytic key explicit. Spinoza's basic synonymy "God equals Nature" (*Deus sive Natura*) can be translated, with justice, as "action equals motion." The translation works because Spinoza occupies the watershed moment when Western thought was narrowing the scope of its terminologies by the Occamite law of parsimony, narrowing the scenic circumference from one comprising creator-and-creation to one comprising creation alone. The world's `constitution` becomes the object of inquiry; the question whether creation was originally enacted by a divine superagent dissolves into the question of how the constituents now stand related.

Burke's central interpretive thesis is that Spinoza's terminology *is* scenic — but with the action-side of the action-motion equation stressed, in contrast to Hobbes who stresses the motion-side. The fact that Spinoza's naturalism is `primarily ethical` rather than physical (in contrast, say, to Galilean physics) invites him to use the full vocabulary of action and passion formally and systematically — not, like Hobbes, glancingly, when the strict mechanistic reduction grows unwieldy. Burke reads the *Ethics* as a noble philosophic accountancy whereby, through the cultivation of `adequate ideas`, one transforms the passives of human bondage into the actives of human freedom.

The watershed structure is elaborated through several devices. The doctrine of `causa sui` ("cause of itself") is double-signified: both an active sense (`causa` sui — God as adequate cause) and a passive sense (causa `sui` — the self that is caused). The *natura naturans* / *natura naturata* distinction performs the same active/passive partition of a single scenic whole; Burke applies his diagnostic question for any two-merged-into-three pair ("Which of the two equal terms was foremost?") and concludes that, for Spinoza, the active (`naturing`) term is foremost. The conatus — the endeavor of each being to persevere in its being — performs the function regularly covered by `agent`, but it is scenically derived: a limited application of the definitions Spinoza gives for the total context (God, Nature, Substance, the Self-Caused). The geometrical method, Burke insists, is more than borrowed Euclidean stylistics; it is dramatistically scenic — finite things are accounted for as `negations` within the total positive context, in Spinoza's own formula "all determination is negation."

The "Alignment of Terms in Spinoza" subsection (book pp. 146–152) supplies the full ledger. Where ordinary readings start from the Cartesian thought/extension pair, Burke insists the dramatistic alignment is built around action/passion: self-caused versus externally caused; infinite (positive) versus finite (negative); God/Substance versus modes; *natura naturans* versus *natura naturata*; freedom (existing solely by the necessity of its own nature) versus bondage (constrained by external determination); existence-in-itself versus existence-in-something-else; reason and intuition versus intellect-as-mostly-passive, will, opinion, imagination; perfection versus imperfection; virtue (=power, good, useful, determined by reason, adequate ideas) versus infirmity (bad, hurtful, determined by emotion, inadequate ideas). The conatus performs the bridging function between bondage and freedom; the three kinds of knowledge (imagination/opinion, Reason, intuition) order the ascent.

Burke's key reading of intuition is the load-bearing scenic move. Where Bergsonian intuition transcends reason (and is hostile to it), Spinozistic intuition is `derived from Reason` — the third kind of knowledge acquired through perfection in the second kind. Since Reason in Spinoza is thoroughly scenic, intuition too remains scenic. Burke quotes Spinoza's tradesman illustration of the three kinds of knowledge (1:2::3:?) and notes that the intuitive grasp is `uno intuitu` "from the sheer nature of the relation itself" — i.e., from the total context. Even at the end of the *Ethics*, where the language of the eternity of mind and the intellectual love of God moves toward an idealistic transcendence, Burke maintains that the upper-level vocabulary is still scenic: particulars are understood `sub specie aeternitatis` in their total context, which is the apex of scenic comprehension rather than its abandonment.

Burke does concede the limits of his case. He admits that the perfect symmetry of the scenic reading is impaired at the moment Hobbes speaks of "action" rather than "motion" — and impaired in a different direction in Spinoza when the *Ethics* closes with the mind-not-destroyed-with-the-body proposition. The lessons Burke draws are the same in both directions: each pentad term continually presses for consideration; no featuring is exhaustive; the diagnosis is of `Ausgangspunkt` and generative direction, not of inventory.

## iv. Darwin

Darwin (book pp. 152–159) is presented as the cleanest available test case for materialism in the modern scientific idiom — and as a case in which the pressure of the suppressed pentad terms is most visibly readable. Burke opens by quoting the end of Chapter VI of *The Origin of Species*, where Darwin proposes that all organic beings are formed on two great laws (Unity of Type and Conditions of Existence) and concludes that the latter is the higher law because it includes the former. Burke's verdict is that the last sentence is as nearly perfect an instance of materialism, or reduction to scene, as one could hope for — the scenic ground (Conditions of Existence) generates the organic structures it selects for.

But Burke then catalogues the ways Darwin's actual vocabulary leaks agency. Many of his keys — "adaptation," "competition," "struggle for life," "natural selection," "survival of the fittest" — can all be read as action words; they import the pathetic fallacy by appearing to personalize impersonal events. A thermometer's adjustment to a change in temperature is automatic; a person's adjustment to a situation can be deliberate. Darwin slides between these readings. More tellingly, Darwin himself slips into talk of `purpose` in explaining his "Natural System," writing that the colors of flowers are designed to be readily seen, visited, and fertilized by insects, the seeds disseminated by birds. Burke reads this as Darwin's conscious materialist intention failing to suppress the pressure of the agency-purpose pair.

The Mivart exchange is Burke's diagnostic centerpiece. Mivart proposed that species change through "an internal force or tendency"; Darwin replies with the minimum possible concession — variability is allowed, but only as a `tendency to ordinary variability`, with the heavy lifting done by external selection. Burke reads even this concession as the moment "we see the pressure of agent." He then improvises a Grammatical term — the biologist's organism is `agent-minus`, a partial agent classifiable under agent only because its behavior has to be accounted for in part by some internal principle of self-movement, however slight.

The "variability" analysis is the chapter's most refined Janus-term discussion. The term, Burke shows, allows two distinct meanings: a cause from without (the scenic reading: variability as response to changing environmental conditions) and an internal principle of motion (the agentic reading: variability as the organism's own propensity to vary). Darwin can use the term for both kinds of explanation. The principle of Continuity of Type, when applied with variability, becomes a Janus apparatus, accounting for varieties whose functions are better suited to conditions elsewhere — explanation that requires both the scenic and the agentic side. Burke's diagnosis here is not that Darwin is confused but that variability is productively ambiguous, exploiting a real overlap between scene and agent in the biological subject matter.

The Mendelian contrast (book p. 156) generalizes the diagnosis. Mendel's experiments — laws governing the inheritance of dominant and recessive characters — Burke calls `idealoid`, because they apply to agents (or agent-minuses) practically to the total exclusion of scenic concerns; the cloistered priest noticed selective competition not at all and instead derived new species from individual variations. Darwin and Mendel both wrote midway in the Century of Idealism par excellence; their differences are differences in scenic versus agentic featuring within biology itself. Burke closes the section with an important last move: even within materialism, the scenic strategy can be applied within an organism by treating a changed habit as an environmental factor affecting the function of some particular organ. The whole organism, scientifically speaking, can be treated as "environment" for any of its parts.

## v. The two great Hellenistic materialisms

The "Two Great Hellenistic Materialisms" subsection (book pp. 159–161) is shorter than its philosophic weight, because Burke treats it as a stylized example of the structural device — `pre-agentification of scene` — that the chapter has been preparing.

Both Epicureanism and Stoicism feature scene; both consider mind as a finer kind of body; both hold the soul-particles disintegrate at death. But each endows the scene with properties of agent in its own way. Burke's Epicurean device is the atomic swerve. Lucretius (*De Rerum Natura*) supplies the seminal passage: atoms of differing weights would, in empty space, fall at the same rate and never collide if not for a slight tendency to swerve (Latin *clinamen*; Greek `parenklisis`). The swerve has often been disparaged as a break in the symmetry of an otherwise purely motion-based atomism. Burke's dramatistic reading is that the swerve is the principle by which a scenic vocabulary lodges an internal seed of action within the atomic ground — a pre-agentification of scene. He notes that the Epicurean physics is in fact the basis for an `ethics`, so the swerve is properly read not as anomaly but as the device by which the ethical (action-bearing) implications of the cosmology are made available.

Lucretius himself, Burke notes, is explicit on the point. The argument runs: if all motions involved one another, each leading inevitably to the next, the power to advance according to the dictates of the will would be impossible; the swerve `breaks the bonds of destiny`. Burke summarizes: in their role as `swerving atoms`, the atoms give a scenic derivation for human freedom and so maintain the symmetry of the scene-act and scene-agent ratios. In their role as `swerving atoms`, however, they account for the world purely evolutionarily, without derivation from a divine agent. The terminology is suited to allaying the terrors of superstition by interpreting acts of god in terms of sheer motion.

Stoicism's pre-agentification works in the opposite direction. The Stoic dialectic equates Nature with Providence (*pronoia*), and so with Reason, the Will of God, and strict logical Necessity. The principle of moral action is introduced into a universe of necessary motions by an `ambiguously naturalistic` device — each thing is required to live according to its nature, and man is by nature rational. Rationality is conceived in political/social terms; all men are intended to serve one another. Reading Marcus Aurelius from the standpoint of the *Grammar*, Burke isolates the kind of `moral utilitarianism` arising from the great stress upon the purposive factor: the agency-purpose ratio (means/ends) provides a bridge "from Providence to pragmatism." To say that all things are brought about by God for a purpose is to say that all things have a `use`. The Stoic teleology, Burke insists, is `still a long way from` modern pragmatism — Marcus Aurelius's role as administrator of an empire prior to modern technology calls for a moral rather than intellectualistic pragmatism — but the structural bridge is in place.

## vi. Rhetorical and Symbolic Levels

The chapter's closing subsection (book pp. 161–170) is Burke's demonstration that the same scene-featuring philosophy yields different content at the Grammatical (definitional/structural) level, the Rhetorical (persuasion-effects on audiences) level, and the Symbolic (latent imagery, guilt, identification) level. The six numbered observations function as diagnostic windows rather than exhaustive analyses.

(1) Lucretius's *De Rerum Natura* is read for cross-purposes. The poem opens with a magnificent invocation to Venus as the all-mother, develops a theme of divine fertility (Venus, Mother Earth), but ends on a scene of plague, dying populations, and riot at funeral rites. Something has gone wrong with the direction of the poem, at least as regards philosophic solace; calamities are not given a medical treatment but a poetic one, as vivid and picturesque as possible. (2) The Symbolic plane suggests Lucretius is `goaded` — like Bentham's childhood fear of ghosts later transformed into adult debunking of fictitious entities, Lucretius's monumental attempt to assure men the wrath of the gods will not pursue them in this life or the next reads as the transformation of ghosts into corpses. (3) The Poeni/Poenae pun (book pp. 164–165) lets Burke find in a Book III passage about Carthaginian wars an echo of "goddesses of Vengeance coming to the attack from all sides" — a linguistic-symbolic resonance Empson's studies-of-ambiguity warrant.

(4) Stoicism's outbursts against the flesh (Aurelius's diary) Burke calls "transcendence of offal" — when moral exaltation is high, the doctrine effects a `scrupulous` transcendence of the body's offal; when exaltation collapses, the result is mere transcendence-from-disgust. (5) Stoicism covers a wide range; Epictetus (manumitted slave) and Aurelius (emperor) deploy `prokopē` (progress) in opposite scenic situations. Epictetus's progress is a ladder from spiritual slavery to spiritual freedom, the freedman's hard-won ways and means of emancipation; Aurelius's progress is a moralized self-subjection within the deeply moral burden of administering the commonwealth. (6) Aurelius's heavily disciplined sexual life, Burke ventures, can be read in Reichian terms as a sex-repression that protects political stability by dispiriting the lower ranks. The conscientious emperor's diary is not addressed simply to himself; among its many ideal sub-personality auditors is a "philosophy-minded subject" inclined to grumble at being placed in a lowly position, whom the emperor persuades through his secret self-discipline to live accordingly. The Grammatical area, Burke concludes, impinges upon the areas of Rhetoric and Symbolic in ways the present chapter only illustrates.

The chapter's closing sentences (book pp. 169–170) flag the two further materialisms postponed to later chapters: Marx (powerfully public, modern, aggressive) and Santayana (serenely private, modern, retiring). Both, Burke says, are strongly affected by German transcendentalism; their consideration must wait until after the functions of `agent` have been examined.

## vii. Scene as pentad-term operationalization for dissertation game-user-motive analysis

Bringing the chapter's full apparatus into the dissertation's operationalizing register, Scene is the pentad term answering the question "where, and in what situation, was the act done?" — the background, setting, situation, environment, or containing-principle that frames an act and within which an agent moves. Operational definition (≤80 words, distilled from book pp. 127–132 + book pp. 137–152): Scene is the containing principle for action, fixing the circumference within which motive may be located; it carries the implicit claim that the act and agent it surrounds can be in part derived from it. Paradigm examples from Burke are the Hobbesian external body causing sense, the Spinozistic God-Nature as scenic substance, the Darwinian Conditions of Existence, the Lucretian void-with-swerve, and the Stoic providential cosmos.

Decision rules for classifying a motive-statement as scene-featuring (extracted from the chapter): (i) primary appeal to environmental, situational, contextual, or material conditions in explaining acts; (ii) putative agents that are described as derived from the larger ground reclassify as `agent-minus` and the explanation is scene-featuring; (iii) systematic replacement of action-vocabulary by motion-vocabulary signals scene-featuring even where surface terms ("will," "action") survive — the Hobbes test; (iv) the word "situation" can be borrowed by agency-featuring or agent-featuring schools and is not by itself diagnostic; (v) scenic grounds endowed with internal-tendency-to-deviate (clinamen) or with purposive/rational structure (pronoia) classify as scene-featuring-with-pre-agentification rather than as agent-featuring; (vi) ambiguity between scene and agent classifications resolves by tracing the philosophy's `Ausgangspunkt` and the generative direction of its derivation chain.

For the dissertation's game-user-motive analytical apparatus, Scene operationalizes as a stack of layers: game-world (geometry, level design, environmental affordances, level state), room (the player's physical room as outer Scene), and social context (multiplayer scene, streaming/audience scene, community context). Two Burke devices from the chapter are particularly load-bearing for the dissertation. The first is `scenic circumference` (book pp. 131–132): different game-studies theories will draw the player's relevant scene at different radii (the screen alone; the screen plus controller; the avatar's diegetic environment; the player-and-room-and-audience). The second is `pre-agentification of scene` (book pp. 159–161): game-world AI, procedural generation, emergent NPC behavior, and dynamic difficulty all function as pre-agentified scene — the level itself behaves quasi-agentically without being a discrete agent. This is the strongest yield from the Hellenistic-materialism subsection for dissertation deployment.

## viii. Cross-pipeline bridges

The chapter's strongest cross-pipeline mapping is with Jakob von Uexküll's biosemiotic framework. Burke's containing-Scene and Uexküll's `Umwelt` converge structurally on the same insight: motive-locus is a bounded, subjectively-constituted environment that delivers perceptual cues (Uexküll's `Merkwelt` / scene-as-given) and absorbs effector outputs (`Wirkwelt` / scene-as-acted-upon). The convergence is parallel-discovery rather than influence: Phase 0 verified zero Uexküll hits in the *Grammar* (the *Streifzüge* was not English-translated until 1957). Provenance is therefore strictly anticipatory-projection (dissertation), and Manual Section E must flag the convergence as a candidate for forcing if pressed too hard.

The Calleja PIM mapping for Scene is Spatial primarily, Kinesthetic secondarily. The Spatial mapping is strong: both Burke's Scene and Calleja's Spatial-involvement dimension name the bounded subjectively-constituted environment within which player involvement is constituted (game-world, room, social context). The Kinesthetic mapping is secondary because the body-in-space subdimension of player involvement is itself a scene-agent ratio at the player's micro-temporal phase — the controller-handling, perceptually-orienting body inhabits and is partly constituted by the spatial scene. The reciprocal direction (Calleja's Spatial chapter reading Scene) is anticipatory in both senses: Calleja contains zero Burke references, and Burke contains zero Calleja references (anachronistic).

Anticipatory ontological grounding for Scene runs to Heidegger's `In-der-Welt-sein` (being-in-the-world) and `Befindlichkeit` (situatedness/state-of-mind/attunement). Scene's grammatical function as containing-principle for action structurally parallels these existentials as the structure within which Dasein discloses an act. Anticipatory only — Burke 1945 contains zero Heidegger hits per Phase 0 (the Macquarrie-Robinson translation of *Sein und Zeit* is 1962); provenance is anticipatory-projection (dissertation) via the BCAP and *Being and Time* corpus indices. The Spinozistic conatus offers a partial exegetical bridge to the dissertation's existing Aristotelian-substance corpus: the conatus as ground-derived agent-equivalent recalls (without citing) the Aristotelian self-mover and the *psuchē*-as-form-of-the-body discussion in *De Anima*. This exegetical bridge is weaker than the GM-06 Aristotle-Burke bridge but stronger than the Heidegger or Uexküll bridges.

The forward-reference to *Rhetoric of Motives* requires noting that Burke's later addition of Attitude as a sixth pentad term will modify Scene's operationalization: the affective/dispositional coloring with which an agent stands within a scene maps to Calleja's Affective dimension rather than to Scene proper. The dissertation Manual Section A entry for Scene should keep this Attitude-modifier slot open as `forward_reference_to_rhetoric_of_motives_attitude` until the *Rhetoric* pipeline is built. Finally, Rickert's *Ambient Rhetoric* offers a third anticipatory bridge: scene-as-ambient anticipates the ambient-rhetorical environment within which persuasion takes place; this awaits a future Rickert-Burke synthesis at the rhetorical-environment level.

The chapter's six numbered Rhetorical/Symbolic observations also bridge to the dissertation: they demonstrate that a scene-featuring discourse generates Rhetorical-level and Symbolic-level traces that can run counter to its Grammatical commitment. For the dissertation's game-studies deployment, this means that a game's stated design philosophy (Grammatical level), its player-persuasion effects (Rhetorical level), and the latent imagery and identifications its community discourse generates (Symbolic level) can diverge — and that the divergence is itself a diagnostic resource. Lucretius's plague-ending, Aurelius's hatred-of-the-flesh, and Epictetus's slave-to-freedom progress are the chapter's three load-bearing analogues for this dissertation-deployable triple-level diagnosis.
