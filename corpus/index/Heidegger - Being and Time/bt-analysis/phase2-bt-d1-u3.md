- **Unit thesis**: This unit presents a critical confrontation with Descartes' ontology of the "world" as *res extensa*, arguing that Descartes skips over the phenomenon of the world and the Being of entities within-the-world (readiness-to-hand) by interpreting worldhood exclusively through substantiality, extension, and mathematical-physical knowledge — an ontological orientation that presupposes but never interrogates the ancient determination of Being as constant presence-at-hand.

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 The definition of the "world" as *res extensa* (§19)
- Descartes distinguishes *ego cogito* from *res corporea*; this distinction is ontologically determinative for the Nature/spirit opposition, though its foundations remain unclarified.
- The Being of an entity "in itself" is defined as *substantia* — but "substance" functions equivocally, sometimes meaning the Being of an entity (substantiality) and sometimes the entity itself.
- The *res corporea* is explicated through its substance, i.e., substantiality. The single "attribute" that constitutes the real Being of corporeal substance is *extensio* (length, breadth, thickness).
- Shape, motion, hardness, weight, and colour are all derivative *modi* of *extensio*; force and sensory qualities contribute nothing to determining the Being of the corporeal Thing.
- Descartes argues that hardness is experienced through resistance, which is itself reducible to a body's not yielding place — and thus to extension and change of location.

### 2.2 Foundations of the ontological definition of the "world" (§20)
- Substantiality — the idea of Being to which the *res extensa* characterization appeals — is traced to its medieval-scholastic roots: substance is that which needs no other entity in order to be.
- In the strictest sense, only God (*ens perfectissimum*) satisfies this definition; created entities (*ens creatum*) are "substances" only in a qualified sense, since they depend on God for production and sustentation.
- The term "Being" cannot apply univocally to God and created entities: there is an "infinite" difference between uncreated and created Being.
- Descartes inherits and evades the medieval problem of the analogy of Being (*analogia entis*): how the signification of "Being" relates to different kinds of substances. He does not inquire into the meaning of Being that "substantiality" embraces or the character of its "universality."
- Substantiality is itself inaccessible directly (*vorgangig*); it is grasped only through attributes. The *substantia finita* as *res corporea* is primarily "assigned" its *extensio*.
- The ontological grounds for defining the "world" as *res extensa* lie in an unclarified idea of substantiality whose meaning of Being remains unquestioned.

### 2.3 Hermeneutical discussion: what Descartes skips over (§21, first half)
- The critical question: does Descartes' ontology of the "world" seek the phenomenon of the world at all? Heidegger answers decisively: "To both questions we must answer 'No'."
- Descartes equates the Being of the "world" with *extensio*, thereby fixing ontological access to entities through *intellectio* — mathematical-physical knowledge. Entities whose Being is securely grasped are those that "always are what they are": the *remanens capax mutationum*, what constantly remains through change.
- This ontological orientation presupposes Being as permanent presence-at-hand, which in turn dictates the privileging of mathematical knowledge. It is not that Descartes esteems mathematics highly and then derives an ontology; rather, his ontological orientation *toward* constant presence-at-hand makes mathematics the paradigmatic mode of access.
- Descartes' treatment of sensory perception (*sensatio*) as founded on *intellectio* and his dismissal of the senses as ontologically insignificant follow from this prior ontological commitment — not from epistemological neutrality.
- Hardness and resistance, phenomenally understood, presuppose the kind of Being that Dasein has (or at least something living); Descartes translates them into relative velocities of two *res extensae* present-at-hand side by side, obliterating the phenomenal character of touch.

### 2.4 Hermeneutical discussion: the positive counter-argument (§21, second half)
- Descartes not only misses the Being of entities within-the-world (readiness-to-hand) but also the phenomenon of the world itself (*Weltphanomen*) and within-the-world-ness (*Innerweltlichkeit*). He takes the Being of Dasein as substance in the same way he takes the Being of the *res extensa*.
- The "rounding out" strategy — adding value-predicates to Things of Nature to recover equipment — is rejected: values would themselves be present-at-hand, merely presupposing presence-at-hand as their ontological foundation.
- Descartes narrows the question of the world to Things of Nature (*Naturdinglichkeit*), confirming the opinion that knowing an entity provides primary access to its Being.
- Four open questions are posed for the projected but unwritten Division Three: (1) why the phenomenon of the world was passed over; (2) why entities within-the-world were substituted for it; (3) why these entities were found first as "Nature"; (4) why value has been invoked to supplement such an ontology.
- A partial "rescue" of Descartes: his radical establishment of *extensio* as *praesuppositum* for every definite characteristic of the *res corporea* prepared the way for Kant's understanding of the *a priori*.

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **The status of Descartes' ontological "skipping over" (§21)**: Heidegger claims that Descartes does not merely *fail* to discover readiness-to-hand but that his ontological framework makes it structurally impossible to encounter. Yet Heidegger also acknowledges that even within Descartes' framework, the ontological problem of the world "will in some sense get formulated and further advanced" (H.96). The tension between structural impossibility and partial accessibility remains unresolved in this unit. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: BT-D1-U3-P3.

2. **The value-predicate objection and its scope (§21, H.99)**: Heidegger rejects supplementing Thinghood with value-predicates as a way to recover equipment, arguing that values themselves would be present-at-hand. But the argument is brief and directed mainly at an unnamed neo-Kantian position. Whether *all* strategies for enriching a Thing-ontology fall to this objection, or only value-theoretic ones, is left open. [INTERP-high] — Linked to: BT-D1-U3-P5.

3. **Greek ontology as both origin and standard (§§20–21)**: Heidegger criticizes Descartes for being "always far behind the Schoolmen" on the problem of the analogy of Being, and traces the Schoolmen's departure to Aristotle and Greek ontology. Yet the entire Destruktion aims to show that Greek ontology itself determined Being as presence-at-hand (*Vorhandenheit*). The evaluative force of "behind the Schoolmen" is therefore ambiguous: is Descartes worse because he is further from an origin that is itself problematic? [INTERP-high] — Linked to: BT-D1-U3-P2.

4. **The partial "rescue" of Descartes (§21, H.101)**: Despite the thoroughgoing critique, Heidegger concedes that Descartes' radicalization of *extensio* as *praesuppositum* prepared the way for Kant's *a priori*. This concession sits uneasily with the claim that Descartes' ontology "passes over" the phenomenon of the world entirely. The relationship between ontological deficiency and historically productive contribution is acknowledged but not theorized. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: BT-D1-U3-P3.

5. **The four unanswered questions (§21, H.100–101)**: The unit closes by posing four questions about why the phenomenon of the world was passed over, why entities within-the-world were substituted, why these were found as "Nature," and why value was invoked. Heidegger defers these to Division Three, which was never published. The unit thus performs its critique without being able to ground it in the deeper temporal analysis it calls for. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: BT-D1-U3-P3, BT-D1-U3-P4.
