## 1. Book Overview

*Being and Time* undertakes a single task: to reawaken and concretely elaborate the question of the meaning of Being (*Seinsfrage*). Heidegger argues that this question — once the animating impulse of ancient philosophy in Plato and Aristotle — has been forgotten, trivialized, or declared superfluous by the Western tradition. The treatise proceeds by way of a "fundamental ontology" grounded in the analytic of Dasein, the entity that in each case "we ourselves are" and that is distinguished ontico-ontologically by the fact that its own Being is an issue for it. Because Dasein already operates with a pre-ontological understanding of Being, it serves as the privileged point of access for the question. The method is phenomenological-hermeneutic: a letting-be-seen of that which shows itself from itself, pursued as an interpretation (*Auslegung*) of the structures of Dasein's existence.

The published text covers only Part One of the projected two-part work, and only the first two of Part One's three projected divisions. Division One (§§9-44, "Preparatory Fundamental Analysis of Dasein") works out the basic structures of Dasein's being-in-the-world — worldhood, the "who" of everyday Dasein (das Man), being-in as such (attunement, understanding, discourse, falling), and care (*Sorge*) as the unitary being of Dasein. Division Two (§§45-83, "Dasein and Temporality") deepens the analysis through being-towards-death, conscience and resoluteness, and demonstrates that temporality (*Zeitlichkeit*) is the ontological meaning of care, before tracing the temporal constitution of everydayness, historicality, and within-time-ness. The projected Division Three ("Time and Being"), which was to carry out the "reversal" (*Kehre*) from the temporality of Dasein to the temporality of Being itself, was never published, leaving the treatise structurally incomplete. The equally unwritten Part Two — a phenomenological destruction of the history of ontology through Kant, Descartes, and Aristotle — is previewed in §6 but remains unpublished.

The Introduction (§§1-8) establishes the plan. §§1-4 argue for the necessity, formal structure, and ontico-ontological priority of the question of Being. §§5-8 lay out the methodological apparatus: the analytic of Dasein as horizon (§5), the Destruktion of the history of ontology (§6), the phenomenological method via the concepts of phenomenon, logos, and their synthesis (§7), and the design of the treatise (§8). The Introduction is thus simultaneously a statement of the problem, a justification of the chosen access point (Dasein), and a declaration of method.

## 4. Provisional Claims List (10 items)

These are the major theses signaled in the Introduction and projected across both divisions:

1. **The question of the meaning of Being has been forgotten.** The tradition since Aristotle has treated "Being" as the most universal, indefinable, and self-evident "concept," thereby rendering the question superfluous (§1, H.2-4).

2. **Dasein is the privileged entity for the question of Being.** Dasein is ontico-ontologically prior: it is the entity that has an understanding of Being, and this understanding belongs to Dasein's constitution (§4, H.12-14).

3. **Being-in-the-world is a unitary, basic constitutional state of Dasein**, not a relation between a subject and an external world (previewed §5, H.15; developed §§12-13).

4. **The essence of Dasein lies in its existence.** Dasein is always its possibilities — it is "to-be" rather than a fixed substance (§9, previewed in §4 and §5).

5. **Care (*Sorge*) is the being of Dasein.** The structural totality "ahead-of-itself-being-already-in-(the-world-) as being-alongside (entities encountered within-the-world)" (previewed §5; demonstrated §§39-42).

6. **Temporality (*Zeitlichkeit*) is the ontological meaning of care** and the horizon for Dasein's understanding of Being (§5, H.17-19; the central thesis of Division Two).

7. **The ordinary conception of time (clock time, within-time-ness) is derivative**, not primordial. It arises from the "levelling off" of originary temporality (§5-6; demonstrated §§78-83).

8. **The history of ontology must be "destroyed" (*Destruktion*).** This positive task aims to trace the tradition's hardened concepts back to their primordial experiential sources — through Kant, Descartes, and Aristotle — to free the question of Being (§6, H.19-27).

9. **Phenomenology is ontology.** The method of the treatise is defined as letting that which shows itself from itself be seen; "only as phenomenology is ontology possible" (§7, H.35).

10. **Being is determined as presence (*Anwesenheit*) in the tradition.** The Greeks interpreted Being as *ousia* (presence, being-at-hand), disclosing entities in terms of a specific mode of time — the "present" — without ever thematizing time as such as the horizon of this interpretation (§6, H.25-26).

## 5. Candidate Tensions (6 items)

1. **Dasein-centrism vs. the question of Being itself.** The treatise accesses Being through Dasein's understanding, yet its ultimate question concerns the meaning of Being *in general*. The hermeneutic circle (§§2, 7, 32-33) is both the enabling condition and the persistent vulnerability of this approach. The unwritten Division Three ("Time and Being") was meant to perform the "reversal" from Dasein's temporality to the temporality of Being itself.

2. **Authenticity vs. sociality.** The Introduction presents the "they" (*das Man*) as an essential existentiale of Dasein's being, not merely a deficiency (§5, H.16: "in its average everydayness"). Yet the language of "falling" (*Verfallenheit*), "idle talk," and "ambiguity" carries evaluative weight. Whether authentic existence is radically individual or can be genuinely communal is a tension the text itself does not fully resolve.

3. **Historicality vs. radical individuation.** Being-towards-death isolates Dasein in its ownmost possibility (Division Two, Ch I), while historicality (Ch V) binds Dasein to its "heritage" (*Erbe*) and communal "destiny" (*Geschick*). The relationship between these two moments — radical singularity and historical belonging — is philosophically fraught.

4. **Incompleteness of the project.** §8 announces six divisions; only two are published. The Introduction promises a Destruktion through Kant, Descartes, and Aristotle (§6) that never appears. The treatise's own framework declares that temporality must be shown to be the horizon of Being in general, not merely of Dasein — but Division Three, where this was to occur, was suppressed. The question is whether the published text can stand on its own or structurally demands a continuation it cannot provide.

5. **Ontological vs. ontic register.** Heidegger insists on a strict distinction between ontological inquiry (into the structures of Being) and ontic description (of beings). Yet the analyses of everydayness, fallenness, anxiety, and death inevitably draw on ontic characterizations. §§43-44 engage directly with ontic topics (reality, worldhood as problem). The line between existential analytic and anthropological observation is a recurrent site of interpretive dispute.

6. **The status of presence (*Anwesenheit*).** §6 identifies the traditional determination of Being as presence (Greek *ousia*) and traces it through the parousia/ousia etymology (§6, H.25-26). The Introduction claims that the treatise will demonstrate how this determination arises from a specific — and unthematized — temporalization. Yet within the published text itself, the analysis of within-time-ness (§§78-83) reaches Aristotle's *Physics* IV discussion of time, while the more fundamental question of *why* Being was interpreted as presence receives only a promissory note. This tension is especially relevant for BCAP pipeline integration.

## 8. Key Observations for Phase 2

- **The Introduction heavily previews Division Two.** §5 already announces temporality as the meaning of care and the horizon for the understanding of Being. §6's Destruktion through Aristotle's essay on time directly connects to §§78-83 (within-time-ness). This means Phase 2 analyses of Division Two units can expect extensive forward-references from the Introduction.

- **German terminology is philosophically loaded from §1 onward.** The M/R translators' notes (especially on pp.15-16, 19 fn.3 on *Zeitlichkeit/Temporalität*, and the extensive notes in §7) provide a rich resource for Phase 3's German terminology appendix. The distinction between *Zeitlichkeit* (temporality of Dasein) and *Temporalität* (temporality of Being) is structurally central and must be tracked.

- **Aristotle appears in the Introduction more than the TOC suggests.** §1 opens with a *Sophist* quotation, §6 discusses the path from Greek ontology through Suarez to Descartes and Kant, §7 extensively discusses *logos* (*apophansis*), *phainomenon*, and *aletheia* in their Greek senses, and the Destruktion plan names Aristotle's essay on time as its third station. This gives the BCAP pipeline significant integration surface beyond just §§78-83.

- **The M/R glossary (p.503) will be an essential Phase 3 resource.** It collects the translation choices in one place and can be cross-referenced against the German appendix built from text analysis.

- **§7's treatment of phenomenon/logos/truth prefigures §44.** The preliminary conception of phenomenology (§7) establishes truth as *aletheia* (unconcealment), which §44 will revisit as the culmination of Division One. This creates a bookend structure within the published text.
