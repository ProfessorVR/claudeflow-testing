# AES-03 — Jessica Moss, "Akrasia and Perceptual Illusion" (*Archiv für Geschichte der Philosophie* 91.2 [2009], 119–156)

> **Cluster role**: the **non-judgmental / phantastic** pole of the Aristotelian-Emotion cluster. The clearest short statement of the thesis that the *motivating* evaluative cognition is a quasi-perceptual *phantasia*-appearance, not a belief (*doxa*), and can run *contrary* to belief. This article is the published kernel of **AES-04** (Moss, *Aristotle on the Apparent Good*, ch. 5). Content paraphrased throughout; page anchors to the journal pagination (119–156).

## § Master thesis

*De Anima* III.9–10 contains a neglected account of *akrasia* (incontinence) as a conflict between **two cognitions, each representing something as good**: an *appearance* to *phantasia* on one side, a *rational judgment* by intellect on the other (122, 127–28). The akratic agent is torn between an **appetite** for what *appears* good to her *phantasia* (the present pleasure) and a **rational wish** (*boulēsis*) for what her intellect *judges* good. Because the conflicting cognition is a (false) appearance set against a true rational judgment, *akrasia* turns out to be "the practical equivalent of certain cases of being taken in by perceptual illusion" (122) — the structural twin of seeing the sun as a foot wide while believing it larger than the earth.

Moss then uses the *de Insomniis*' account of how a *pathos* (emotion, appetite, sleep, disease) "covers over" intellect to reconcile two accounts of *akrasia* that have seemed incompatible: the **struggle** account (*akrasia* = victory of non-rational over rational desire; *EN* I.13, *EE* II.8) and the **ignorance** account (*EN* VII.3, the "Socratic" claim that *akrasia* involves ignorance). Her reconciliation (122, 153–55): non-rational desire wins **not by brute overpowering but by undermining the cognitive basis of rational desire** — by impairing intellect's function of *noticing and contradicting false appearances*, so the value-appearance gains acceptance "by default" and the rational motivation that depended on the now-occluded knowledge lapses. *Akrasia* therefore *necessarily* involves a temporary, physically-induced ignorance; the ignorance account is not a rival to the struggle account but the *explanation of how* the struggle is lost.

## § Section-by-section synthesis

**Introduction (119–122).** Two puzzles frame the paper. (i) Given Aristotle's "Platonic" resources for a struggle account, why did he want the "Socratic" claim that *akrasia* involves ignorance at all? (ii) How can an ignorance account even be *compatible* with a struggle between opposing desires (Ross's sharp objection, 121: the standard minor-premise reading leaves "no moral struggle")? Moss's move is to look *outside* the ethical works, to *DA* III.10, for a third account that dissolves both puzzles.

**§1 — *Akrasia* in *de Anima* III.9–10 (122–131): the un-Humean reading.** *DA* III.9 (passage 2, 432b26–433a8) seems to deny that either intellect or desire is *the* mover, citing *akrasia* and *enkrateia* as cases where each overrides the other. But the fuller picture in III.10 (passage 3, 433a9–b12) corrects this: action results from **desire combined with some form of cognition — either intellect *or* phantasia** (273–75). Against the Humean division of labor (desire sets the end, cognition finds means), Moss argues that practical cognition is *in the first place* cognition of things **as good or bad, worthy or unworthy of pursuit** (380–82): (3e) ties "intellect always correct, *phantasia* sometimes not" to "desire is for the good *or the apparent good*," so the object of desire is *either* what intellect judges good *or* what appears good to *phantasia*. The present pleasant "appears (*phainetai*) both without qualification (*haplōs*) pleasant and good without qualification, from a failure to look to the future" (3f, 433b8–10). Moss defuses the worry that "good without qualification" is too sophisticated for non-rational *phantasia*: the *kai* in "pleasant *and* good" is epexegetical (for the non-rational part, to desire as pleasant just *is* to desire as good, *DA* 431a8–11), and "without qualification" means only that this part **fails to notice the factors that qualify** the pleasure — "thus the qualifiedly pleasant appears pleasant and good without qualification *just as the sun appears a foot wide*" (127). Belief (*doxa*) is ascribed to rational animals alone, and the *doxastikon* is equated with the *logistikon* (*EN* VI.5 1140b25–28, n.14), so desire-in-accordance-with-belief is desire specially linked to reasoning. The upshot (passage 4, *EE* VII.2 1235b25–29; passage 5, *Met.* XII.7 1072a27): the pleasant is desired as an *apparent* good — "to some it appears so even though they do not believe it so, **for *phantasia* and belief (*doxa*) do not reside in the same part of the soul**." This is the cluster's load-bearing sentence.

**§2 — Following appearances against knowledge (131–136).** It would be unintelligible to act on an appearance one *actively* recognizes as illusory ("I know those are cracks, but they look like animals, that's why I'm swatting them" — 131). So if one "follows *phantasia* contrary to knowledge" (3b), her intellect must be impaired — "covered over" by a *pathos* (passage 8, *DA* III.3 429a5–8). The mechanism comes from the *de Insomniis*: the faculty by which the ruling part (*to kurion*) **judges (*krinein*) is not identical with that by which *phantasmata* arise**, the proof being the sun-illusion (passage 7, *Insomn.* 460b16–20; cf. passage 6, *DA* III.3 428b2–4). When all goes well something in us "contradicts the *phantasia*" (passage 9, 459a6–8: belief sometimes says the appearance is false, but at other times is "held in check" and follows the appearance). The finger-on-eyeball and dreamer cases (passage 10, 461b29–462a8) and, decisively, the *pathē* case (passage 11, **460b3–16**: the coward sees enemies, the lover the beloved, the feverish see animals in wall-cracks; "the stronger the *pathos*, the less similarity is needed") show that a strong *pathos* makes the appearance's falsity "escape one's notice" (*lanthanei*) so that one even "moves oneself in accordance with the appearances." **Crucial non-doxastic point (135):** the agent does *not actively assent*; her rational faculty "simply fails to oppose" the appearance, which gains "acceptance by default" (Gallop 1991; Pickavé/Whiting 2008 for the fever case). Moss flags the Stoic parallel (136): the rational animal has reason that *judges* (*krinonta*) *phantasiai*, accepting some and rejecting others — Aristotle's distinction between *being subject to* an appearance and *rationally accepting* it prefigures Stoic assent (*synkatathesis*).

**§3 — Applying the account to *akrasia* (136–140).** The temperate person is **not even subject** to the false value-appearance (*EN* X.5 1176a15–22), and so has no conflicting appetite. The ethically inferior agent does: appetite (a bodily *pathos*) and rational wish vie for a time; then the appetite's physical alterations impair intellect's contradicting-function, "now there is no longer anything to contradict the *phantasia*, and so she follows it" — "default yielding rather than active rational assent" (138). The akratic "pursues harmful pleasures *without ever believing* that they are good" (*EN* VII.3 1146b22–24; VII.8; IX.4) — i.e. *without doxa*. A possible complication (139): *pathē* also **generate** false appearances (*Rh.* II.1 1377b31–1378a5: things appear different to the friendly vs. hostile, the appetitive vs. the unemotional). Moss construes the appetite→appearance→appetite loop not as a vicious circle but as a **causal chain**, distinguishing a *general* state of craving (which distorts appearances) from the *specific* appetite for this object that the focused appearance then produces.

**§4 — *EN* VII.3 (140–153).** The famous "dry-food" syllogism (1147a1–10) is *not* akrasia — it involves no struggle (141). The real explanation begins at passage (14) (1147a10–18): the akratic "both has and doesn't have" knowledge, like the mad/drunk/asleep, because *spirited passions and appetites alter the body* — confirmed by *Physics* VII.3 247b (passage 15: while affected, one is *literally incapable of using* knowledge). The hard passage (16)–(17) (1147a31–b3) says the appetite "leads" and the agent acts "by *logos* and belief," but the belief is opposed only *accidentally*. Moss treats the syllogistic, belief-grounded picture as **one species, a difference of emphasis** (146): *EN* VII.6 1149a34–b1 (passage 18) explicitly says appetite can be guided by "*logos or perception*," and *EN* VII.7 (impetuous akratics "follow the *phantasia*," don't wait for *logos*) confirms the *phantasia*-grounding. She answers the worry that an impaired intellect would also knock out the beliefs grounding appetite with two replies (146–47): (a) *phantasia* can continue to apprehend the cake as pleasant once alerted, even when reason is silent; (b) the impairment is **localized/"surgical"** — it knocks out the ability to register "this appears good but is not," leaving "this is sweet" intact. Beasts are not akratic (passage 19, 1147b3–5: only *phantasia* and memory of particulars, no universal) — *akrasia requires the rational faculty whose contradicting-function can fail*. On the disputed "last *protasis*" (passages 20–21): the illusion account favors the **conclusion reading** (the agent loses "this is to-be-avoided," the contradictory of the value-appearance "this is to-be-gone-for"), or a minor-premise reading only if the middle term is evaluative ("unwholesome," "one too many"). On the *nature* of the ignorance (153): she is **literally, physically unable to access** the relevant knowledge — not a mere failure to combine (Joachim, Irwin), to use (Broadie), or to integrate into character (Dahl, McDowell, Wiggins). The cost (153): Aristotle's *akrasia* applies **only to cases of strong, body-altering passion** — not to "clear-eyed" weakness (Davidson's bedtime tooth-brusher).

**§5 — Ignorance and struggle revisited (153–155).** The two answers: (1) because motivational conflict *is* a variety of perceptual illusion, and no one acts on an appearance recognized as illusory, Aristotle had **substantive reason to insist that akrasia must involve temporary ignorance** — a "clear-eyed akratic" would be as impossible as a clear-eyed swatter of illusory animals. (2) The ignorance account is not merely compatible with but a **constitutive part of** the struggle account: appetite wins precisely by knocking out the rational cognition on which rational desire depends.

## § Positioning within the cluster

Moss anchors the **non-judgmental pole**. Where Fortenbaugh (AES-01) and Dow (AES-05) make the cognition constitutive of *pathos*/desire a *belief* or an *affirmation* of the representation as true, Moss makes the *motivating* evaluative cognition a **non-doxastic quasi-perceptual appearance** that is causally and structurally independent of belief — "*phantasia* and belief do not reside in the same part of the soul" (127). She shares with Konstan (AES-02) the view that desire/emotion is cognition-laden, but differs on the *kind*: Konstan's is interpersonal *judgment/construal*; Moss's is perception-like *appearing-good*. The decisive contrast is with the judgmental camp's reading of the recalcitrance passages: where they read the sun-illusion and the merely-imagined-fearful as cases that still involve (or lapse from) belief, Moss reads them as the paradigm proof that motivation tracks *phantasia*, not *doxa*. Cross-pipeline, her *DA* III.3 *phantasia*-as-quasi-perception bears directly on the **Phantasia Secondary** cluster (Caston's "common currency" and Frede's "cognitive role" are congenial; the "image-view" she resists less so).

## § Significance for the dissertation's Section 5

- **T1-2 (the *doxa*-gate vs. the *phantasia* rival).** This is the strongest single source against a *doxa*-requirement for affect. Moss's value-appearance is exactly what moves the agent, and it can stand *against* belief; the dissertation's own recalcitrance examples (the rope-snake startle 1217, the foot-wide sun 1030, fear at fiction 1120 fn) are *her* paradigm cases (the sun, the wall-cracks, the swatter). The dissertation must either (a) answer Moss by showing the higher-order civic emotions require something her appetite-case does not, or (b) adopt the **two-tier reconciliation**: concede the lower tier (basic hedonic tonality / *resonant epithymia* / the apparent good) to Moss's phantastic model, and reserve *doxa* for the higher civic emotions. Moss strengthens option (b): she explicitly restricts her phantastic appetite-case to the *non-rational* register and grants that *doxa*/*logos* is the rational overlay.
- **The "acceptance by default" point sharpens a problem for the dissertation's *doxa*-concept.** Moss/Gallop argue the appearance is accepted *by default* when reason fails to contradict — *not* by a positive act of assent. The dissertation's "*doxa* = involuntary *positive* taking-as-true" (§4 1016ff) is a *stronger* commitment than the text may support; Moss's default-yielding model is the more deflationary rival the dissertation should engage.
- **T2-1 (the *pathetic* loop's synchronic/diachronic assignment).** Moss reads *de Insomniis* 460b3–16 — the dissertation's own loop passage (1144–1146) — as operating **within a single akratic episode**: the appetite vies, *then* impairs intellect, *then* one acts, all in one pass. That is the **synchronic** reading. It is direct evidence against the dissertation's decision (1150) to file this mechanism wholly in the *diachronic* register; the dissertation needs to defend that assignment against Moss's within-episode reading or concede a synchronic feedback.
- **Animal-*pathos* (1079 fn).** Moss's (19) (beasts not akratic: only *phantasia*, no universal) and her treatment of non-rational *phantasia* supply analytic support for the dissertation's claim that the higher, rational-articulated affects are unavailable to animals — but on a *phantasia*-based, not *doxa*-based, rationale.
