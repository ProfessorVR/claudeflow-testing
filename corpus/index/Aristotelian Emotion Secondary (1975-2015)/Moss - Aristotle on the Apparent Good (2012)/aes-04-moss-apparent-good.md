# AES-04 — Jessica Moss, *Aristotle on the Apparent Good: Perception, Phantasia, Thought, and Desire* (Oxford University Press, 2012)

> **Cluster role**: the **non-judgmental / phantastic** pole, in its fullest monographic statement. The book that turns the cluster's load-bearing thesis — that the *motivating* evaluative cognition is a quasi-perceptual *phantasia*-appearance, not a belief (*doxa*) — into a systematic reading of Aristotle's whole moral psychology ("Practical Empiricism"). **Chapter 4, "Passions and the Apparent Good" (pp. 69–99), is the single most important secondary text in the cluster for the dissertation's T1-2**: it argues by name that the passions are explained on the *phantastic*, not the *doxastic*, model, and supplies the **accepting-vs-believing distinction** that the dissertation's *doxa*-requirement must answer. Chapter 5 (pp. 100–136) is the book-form of AES-03 (Moss 2009) and is not re-analyzed here, only referenced. Content paraphrased throughout; page anchors to the printed pagination (xi–xv, 48–99); Bekker numbers given where Moss cites Aristotle.

## § Master thesis

The desired is "either the good or the apparent good (*phainomenon agathon*)" — and the apparent good is to be taken **literally**: things appear good to us, just as they appear large or small, in virtue of *phantasia*, the same quasi-perceptual capacity responsible for dreams, after-images, visualization, and optical illusions (Introduction, xi–xii). Moss's two epigraphs fix the parallel that organizes the book: the pleasant is desired as an apparent good, and the *EE* notes that to some it appears so although they do not believe it so — "**for *phantasia* and belief do not reside in the same part of the soul**" (*EE* VII.2 1235b26–29); and the optical case is the model, since "the sun appears only a foot in diameter, though something else contradicts the *phantasia*" (*de Insomniis* 460b16–20). An appearance of goodness is **a pleasurable, essentially motivating representation through *phantasia*, derived from a previous pleasurable perception of its object**, which then forms the basis for thoughts about goodness (xiii–xiv, 63–64). Just as ordinary perception is the basis of all theoretical cognition, *practical* perception — pleasurable/painful perception of things as good or bad — is the basis of all practical cognition; *phantasia* preserves and reproduces it (xiii–xiv). This makes pleasure the efficient cause (not necessarily the object) of all desire, and greatly restricts the role of intellect, since *phantasia* — a *non-rational* form of cognition shared with animals — does work others reserve for *nous* (xiv–xv, 65–66).

On this foundation the book's centerpiece, Chapter 4, defends the **phantastic account of the passions against the doxastic account**. Fear, anger, shame, pity, envy and the rest are pleasurable or painful responses to *literal* quasi-perceptual evaluative appearances — the very sort one has in a vivid memory or a dream (69, 76–84). Moss's decisive move against the doxastists (Fortenbaugh, Nussbaum 1996, Dow 2009) is the **accepting-vs-believing distinction** (90–94): the doxastist insists that emotion requires not a mere appearance but the subject's *taking* the appearance to be true, hence a belief. Moss concedes the premise but denies the inference. For a *rational* subject there is a real gap between being subject to an appearance "as of *p*" and accepting that *p*; but the passions belong to the **non-rational part of the soul** (the *pathētikon* / *aisthētikon* / *phantastikon*, identified via *de An.* 431a13–14), and for that part — "as for a non-rational animal" — there is, in Moss's words, no meaningful distinction between being subject to an appearance and accepting it (93). Being appeared-to *just is* a kind of default, automatic, unreflective acceptance — what the Stoics would call mere assent-by-default and refuse to dignify as a *pathos*, but which Aristotle calls a passion. The passions are therefore based on *phantasiai* **alone**; evaluative beliefs are their normal *accompaniments*, not their basis, because in standard cases a *phantasia* "as of *p*" automatically triggers the belief that *p* (the finger-on-eye proof, *Insomn.* 461b31–462a2) — a proto-Stoic account of appearance-based belief (96–99). Hence passions can in special cases run *against* belief (the caged-man at the precipice; *akrasia*), which is exactly what a doxastic account cannot allow.

## § Section-by-section synthesis (Introduction + Ch. 3 + Ch. 4)

**Introduction (xi–xv).** Moss frames the whole book by juxtaposing the *EE* VII.2 and *de Insomniis* passages and insisting that the "apparent" in "apparent good" be read *literally*, on the model of optical appearance (xi–xii). She distinguishes two explanatory roles the apparent good plays across the corpus — explaining motivation *in general*, and explaining *mistaken* motivation — and matches them to two roles of *phantasia*: representing absent objects (necessary for goals) and *mis*representing them (perceptual illusion, ethical error) (xii–xiii). The payoff is "Practical Empiricism": just as there is nothing in the (theoretical) intellect not first in the senses, so practical thought derives its content from practical perception via *phantasia* (xiii–xiv). She flags the two "radical" consequences — pleasure becomes central to motivation and virtue, and intellect's role is sharply restricted (xiv) — and previews the three-part structure: Part I derives evaluative *phantasia* from the psychological works (Chs. 1–3); Part II applies it to *non-rational* motivation (Ch. 4 passions, Ch. 5 *akrasia*); Part III extends it to *rational* motivation — decision and wish (Chs. 6–8). She situates the view between Plato (precedent) and the Stoics (afterlife), and aligns it with the British empiricists and with Gendler's "alief" (xv, fn. 5).

**Ch. 3, "*Phantasia* and the Apparent Good" (48–67).** This is Moss's *phantasia* theory, and the engine for everything in Part II. (1) *§3.1 Phantasia in action* (49–51): the *MA* and *de An.* imply *phantasia* is specially necessary for locomotion (*MA* 702a17–19; *de An.* 432b15–16), because it is through *phantasia* that creatures apprehend objects *as goals* — as good (50). (2) *§3.2 The basic conception* (51–57): against Nussbaum (*phantasia* "interprets" perceptions, 1978), Modrak, Lorenz ("envisaging prospects"), Moss defends a **minimalist** account: *phantasia* is just "a movement arising from actual perception" (*de An.* 428a1–2, 429a1–2; *Insomn.* 459a17–18), exhaustively characterized by three claims — it always arises *from* perception, it is *independent* of present objects, and *phantasmata* are *similar* to the *aisthēmata* they derive from and so have similar psychological effects (*de An.* 428b10–17, 429a4–6; *Mem.* 450a27–32). It is simply "a faculty of preserving and reproducing perceptual experiences" (xiii, 51–57). (3) *§3.3 Practical phantasia* (57–64): the crucial similarity is **affective** — a *phantasia* preserves the *pleasurableness or painfulness* of the perception it copies, not merely its narrowly representational content (*MA* 701b13–19, 701b33–35, 702a2–7; *Rhet.* 1370a27–35; *Phys.* 247a7–14). *Phantasia* of an object of pursuit is itself heating/chilling — pleasant or painful — and thereby itself motivating, exactly as perception is. It follows that a pleasurable, desire-inducing *phantasia* **is** an evaluative *phantasia*, a cognition of something *as good*: "the appearance of goodness is the representation through *phantasia* … of perceptible goodness" (63). This *is* the analysis of the apparent good (63–64). (4) *§3.4 Phantasia and thought* (64–67): turning to distinctively human motivation, Moss lays out the three relations *phantasia* can bear to thought — it can exist *without* relevant thought (as in animals, or in humans when reason is impaired by sleep/passion), it can *conflict* with thought (perceptual illusion; *akrasia* — *Insomn.* 460b19–20), and it can *agree* with thought; and since there is no thought without a *phantasma* (*de An.* 431a14–17, 432a8–9), even *practical* thought (judging something good) **presupposes** a corresponding evaluative *phantasia*, from which it takes its content (65–66). This is the hinge that lets *phantasia* underlie phenomena "traditionally thought purely intellectual."

**Ch. 4, "Passions and the Apparent Good" (69–99)** — *the cluster centerpiece.* The chapter's two aims (70): (i) show *against the doxastists* that the passions involve *literal phantasiai*, the same quasi-perceptual appearances found in memories and dreams; (ii) show — where prior phantastic readers (Cooper, Achtenberg, Nieuwenburg) have not — how that reading *accommodates* the textual and philosophical evidence that has seemed to favor belief.

- *§4.1 Doxastic vs. phantastic accounts of the passions* (70–71). Moss names the debate. The *Rhetoric*'s definitions are studded with *phantasia* and *phainesthai* (e.g. "Fear is a pain … arising from the *phantasia* of a destructive or painful future evil," *Rhet.* 1382a21–22). But the bare presence of appearance-language is "far too quick" an argument, since *phainesthai* often just means "believe." The **doxastists** (whom she names: Fortenbaugh, Nussbaum 1996, Dow 2009) argue that people fear what they *believe* dangerous, that Aristotle freely uses *dokein*/*oiesthai* in the definitions, and that *doxa* — not *phantasia* — is the distinctively human, *logos*-dependent state (*de An.* 428a20–24); so the appearance-language is non-technical (vividness/subjectivity). Moss's reply-strategy: the **ethical works** assign the passions to a non-rational soul-part incapable of belief (§4.2), so *if* cognition-laden they must rest on *perception or phantasia*; the ***Rhetoric*** shows the cognition is *phantasia* in particular (§4.3); Plato gives precedent (§4.4); and the two doxastic objections fail (§§4.5–4.6).

- *§4.2 The passionate part of the soul* (71–75). The tripartite soul (by relation to *logos*: rational / merely-*logos*-obeying / vegetative; *EN* I.13, *EE* II.1) locates the passions in the **middle part**, the *pathētikon* (*Pol.* 1254b8). Crucially this part is *not* capable of reason but *does* exercise perception and *phantasia*: the *orektikon* is the same as the *aisthētikon* (different only "in being," *de An.* 431a13–14) and as the *phantastikon* (*Insomn.* 459a16–17), confirmed by the *EE*'s "perceptive and desiderative" (*EE* 1219b23). Against **Fortenbaugh** (2002), who makes the passionate part a division of *nous* ("alogical intellect") on the strength of the "peculiarly human" passages (*EE* 1219b37–38; *EN* function argument 1097b33–1098a5), Moss replies that the human perceptive part may be called "peculiar" precisely because of its interaction with reason, that the *EE* passage contrasts only with the *nutritive* part (and elsewhere assigns the perceptive part a role in virtue, *EE* 1219b21–25), and — decisively — that Aristotle plainly grants animals *some* passions (anger *Rhet.* 1380a24; *thymos* *EN* 1111b12; envy *HA* 608b10), which the doxastist must downgrade to "only analogous" (Fortenbaugh 2002, 69). *De An.* 403a5–18 ("in general perceive … the *pathē* all seem to be with the body") clinches that the passions are body-involving and so not functions of intellect (cf. *EN* X.8 1178a14–16).

- *§4.3 Passions in the Rhetoric* (75–84). The *Rhetoric*'s definitions yield three essential features of the passion-class: they are **pleasurable/painful**, they involve **evaluative representations**, and those representations are the work of ***phantasia***. Moss canvasses the definitions (anger 1378a30–31; shame 1383b12–13/1384a22; confidence 1383a16–19; pity 1385b13–16; indignation 1387a8; envy 1387b23–24; rivalry 1388a32) and the *I.11* pleasure-descriptions (victory/honor/being-liked, 1370b32–1371a20). The argument: (i) all pleasure/pain is in perception or quasi-perception (the "weak perception" passage, *Rhet.* 1370a28–35; pains 1382a10; *Phys.* 247a7–14); (ii) the passions are essentially pleasurable/painful (general definition 1378a19–22; *EN* II.5 1105b21–23; *EE* II.2 1220b12–14) — so they must involve perception or *phantasia*; (iii) the passions are directed at **non-present** objects (memory/expectation/imaginative embellishment) — so *phantasia* in particular. The "most unequivocal" evidence is the anger passage explicitly comparing the *phantasia* of revenge to "the *phantasia* that occurs in dreams" (1378b1–10). Where a passion (anger, pity) seems present-directed, it still involves memory/expectation (1370b1–11; 1386a1–3); and the remaining passions involve **putting things "before the eyes"** (*pro ommatōn*) — orators induce pity/shame by vivid visualization (1386a29–b7; Cydias on Samos 1384b32–35), which *de An.* III.3 (427b18–19), *Mem.* (450a5) and *Insomn.* (458b20) make the signature of *phantasia*. We induce passions in *ourselves* the same way (pride/ambition/envy as imaginatively embellished self-representations).

- *§4.4 Platonic precedent* (85–87). Plato makes quasi-perceptual imaginative embellishment a *necessary condition* of the passions, anticipating Aristotle. The *Philebus* scribe-and-painter (39a–b, 40a): hope is a response not to a mere belief that one might get a fortune but to a vivid *painted phantasma* of oneself "beside himself with delight" — an image of the future *as good* (40a10–12). The *Timaeus* liver-mirror (71a–d): the rational part cannot reach the appetitive part by *logos*, so its thoughts reflect off the liver as *phantasmata* that "make the course of action simply look good or bad, the way something can look good or bad in a picture" — quasi-perceptual, evaluative, and (per *Tim.* 71d–72b) dream-involving, exactly as in Aristotle's anger/dreams parallel.

- *§4.5 First doxastic objection: the contents of perception* (87–90). Objection (Dow 2009): the passions' representations are **propositional** and predicate **value-properties** (a slight, undeserved well-doing) that *cannot be perceived*, so they exceed *phantasia*'s range. Reply: this misreads Aristotle's view of perception. Aristotle standardly treats perception of common/incidental perceptibles as propositional — we perceive *that* the white thing is a man (*de An.* 418a21–22, 430b29–30; Sorabji 1996; Tuozzo 1994) — so propositional structure is no obstacle. And value *is* literally perceptible (Ch. 2): since all pleasant/painful things are perceptible and the passions are pleasure/pain responses, their objects must be perceptible. Direct confirmation: ***EN* VII.6 1149a32–34** contrasts *logos* and *phantasia* as alternative sources of the awareness of a slight — "*logos* **or** *phantasia* has shown that something was *hubris* or a slight" — which "belies a doxastic interpretation": one can have a *phantasia* *of* something *as* an insult, and it induces anger. Aristotle may think intellect *expands* the perceptual range (we perceive insults as insults the way we perceive roses as roses), and the Stoics and Epicureans follow him in treating value-impressions (*phantasiai*) as perceptual without argument (Plutarch on Chrysippus; Cicero on Epicurus).

- *§4.6 Second doxastic objection: accepting vs. believing* (90–92) — *the dissertation's pivotal section.* Two sub-claims. **(a)** *Phantasia* gives "mere appearances" and is affectively inert (*de An.* 427b17–24: imagining something terrible is like seeing it in a picture; "believing is not up to us"). Reply: that passage is about the *voluntary* species of *phantasia* ("up to us"); dreams and illusions are *not* up to us and *do* move us — and the *Poetics* has us feel real pity and fear at representations. Read as a blanket claim it would flatly contradict *de An.* 429a4–8 and the *MA* (*phantasia* "has the power of the actual things," 701b16–19; 701b33–35; 702a17–19; *de An.* 433b11–12). **(b)** The deeper objection (Nussbaum 1996; Dow 2009; cf. Cooper 1996a): passions respond to how we *take* things to be (acceptance), not to how they merely appear, so they need belief. Moss's answer is the **accepting-vs-believing distinction**, rephrased in Stoic terms: introduce "**acceptance**" as a genus covering both *active rational assent* and *passive failure-to-question*. For a rational being, being subject to an appearance "as of *p*" is distinct from accepting *p*; **but for the non-rational soul-part there is no such gap — being appeared-to *is* a default, automatic, unreflective acceptance** (91–93). So being subject to an evaluative appearance of something as terrible "will not always entail *rational* acceptance, i.e. belief … but it will always entail *non-rational* acceptance — i.e. fear" (93). The advantage: passions become *independent of belief* — one can feel a passion against one's belief (Hume's caged man at the precipice, "his imagination runs away with its object"; cf. Gendler's alief) — which the doxastist must deny but common sense affirms (94). The residual *de An.* III.3 difficulty (one can be subject to the appearance *without* fear) Moss handles with two options: either we do feel *some* fear, or the *voluntary*/deliberate entertaining of an appearance differs from being struck by it (deferred to Ch. 6).

- *§4.7 Aristotle and the Stoics on phantasia, assent, and passion* (92–94). Moss uses the Stoic *phantasia*/*synkatathesis* (assent) framework as a lens. On the Stoic view all animals have *phantasiai*; rational creatures can question them and *assent* (→ *doxa*) or reject; animals, lacking that ability, "simply go with the appearances, by default." Some *phantasiai* are "impulsive" (*hormētikai*) and evaluative; assent to these yields passions, which are therefore *rational* and denied to animals — yet animals have *hormai* very like fear/appetite (Origen, *On Principles* 3.1.2; Epictetus *Disc.* 3.3.4). The "most striking difference": Stoic passions are *active rational assents* (rational-only); Aristotelian passions are exactly what the Stoics attribute to *animals* and refuse to call passions — **default, passive acceptances of appearances** (93). This is what dissolves the second doxastic objection.

- *§4.8 Aristotle and the Stoics on passions, phantasia, and belief* (94–99). The remaining task: accommodate the substantial evidence that passions *correlate* with evaluative beliefs (e.g. *de An.* 427b21–22; *EN* VII.6's "*logos*"; *Rhet.* 1382b33, 1380b16–17). Moss establishes two theses: **(a)** all thought requires *phantasia*, so *phantasia* is **necessary** for belief and hence for any belief-mediated passion (*de An.* 432a3–9, 431a14–17; *Mem.* 449b30–450a9) — thinking one will suffer entails a *phantasma* of the suffering; and **(b)** in standard cases a *phantasia* "as of *p*" automatically **triggers** the belief that *p*, so *phantasia* is (in belief-capable creatures) **sufficient** for belief — the **finger-on-eye proof** (*Insomn.* 461b31–462a2: the doubled finger is *believed* double unless one rejects the appearance). This yields a **proto-Stoic account of "appearance-based beliefs"** (most of them automatic/unreflective assents to appearances; cf. Sextus, *AM* 7.225–6; she restricts the claim against Sextus). Moss explicitly **declines the strongly-Stoicizing reading** on which the passion is based on the *belief* (that would just be the doxastic view; Andronicus/Aspasius, *in EN* 44.33–45.10): that clashes with the non-rationality of the passions. Her conclusion (98–99): **the passions are based on *phantasiai* alone, but those *phantasiai* normally prompt corresponding beliefs.** So one can usually characterize passions via beliefs (as the *Rhetoric* does) and predict that appeal-to-passion sways the *kriseis* (judgments) of jurors — yet anger "does not already entail a judgment." The analogy that closes the chapter: feeling anger "can result from, and will normally entail, and yet is essentially independent of, the belief that they have done you wrong," just as seeing something *as round* results-from/entails/yet-is-independent-of the belief that it is round (99). Chapter 5 (= AES-03) then supplies the divergence-cases (*akrasia*) that prove the independence.

## § Positioning within the cluster

Moss-AG is the **anchor of the non-judgmental / phantastic pole**, and Chapter 4 makes the pole's claim *by name* and against *named* opponents. On the five cluster axes:

1. **DOXASTIC ↔ PHANTASTIC (central).** Moss is the cluster's most explicit **phantastic** voice. Where **Fortenbaugh** (AES-01) makes the passions essentially involve a *belief/judgment* and **Dow** (AES-05) defends, against the "mere appearance" reading, that the passions require the subject's *affirming* the representation as true, Moss argues that the passions are based on *phantasiai* **alone**, with belief a mere normal accompaniment (94–99). She targets all three judgmental figures explicitly: Fortenbaugh on the passionate part (§4.2), Nussbaum 1996 on the non-technical reading of *phantasia*-language, and **Dow 2009 as the chief named opponent** ("presses the doxastic view forcefully," 70 n.3; both objections in §§4.5–4.6 are "developed in Dow 2009").
2. **Cognitive-evaluative ↔ quasi-perceptual.** Decisively **quasi-perceptual**: things appear good "the way something can look good or bad in a picture" (87), on the literal model of the foot-wide sun (*de An.* 428b2–4; *Insomn.* 460b16–20). The evaluative content is an *appearing-good*, not a judgment-*that*.
3. **Social-relational ↔ individual-psychological.** **Individual-psychological** in mechanism, though Moss handles the *social* emotions (anger at a slight, shame before observers, envy/indignation) — she construes their *toward-whom* content as imaginatively-embellished quasi-perceptual representation (§4.3), not as **Konstan**'s (AES-02) interpersonal *judgment/construal*. She thus shares Konstan's cognitivism but parts company on the *kind* of cognition.
4. **Reason-governed ↔ pleasure/appearance-governed.** Strongly **pleasure/appearance-governed**: "Practical Empiricism" makes pleasure the efficient cause of all desire and restricts intellect (xiv–xv), the polar opposite of Fortenbaugh's reason-answerable emotion.
5. **Animals have *pathē* ↔ *pathē* are rational-only.** Moss **lowers the bar**: animals genuinely have passions because the passionate part is non-rational and shared with animals (§4.2), explicitly contra Konstan/Fortenbaugh's "only analogous" line and contra the Stoics' rational-only passions (§4.7).

Relation to **AES-03**: this is the same author and the same pole; Ch. 5 of this book *is* the published Moss 2009 article (xi, "A version of Chapter 5 is published as 'Akrasia and Perceptual Illusion'"). But Chapter 4 is *new* relative to AES-03 and is the cluster's strongest standalone case for the phantastic reading of the *passions* (AES-03 made the case via *akrasia*/appetite). Cross-pipeline, Moss's minimalist *phantasia* (§3.2) explicitly engages the **Phantasia Secondary** cluster: she argues *against* Nussbaum 1978 ("interpretation"), Schofield 1978, Modrak, Lorenz 2006, and largely *with* Caston 1996/1998 (the "indefinite singular content" / quasi-perceptual common-currency reading) and **Frede 1992** (supervenience-congenial; she cites Frede for the merely-metaphorical reading of *de An.* III.3, 91 n.39).

## § Significance for the dissertation's Section 5

- **T1-2 (the *doxa*-gate vs. the *phantasia* rival) — primary rival, centerpiece text.** Chapter 4 is the most direct and sustained scholarly case *against* a *doxa*-requirement for the **passions specifically** (AES-03 ran the case through *akrasia*/appetite; this runs it through fear, anger, shame, pity, envy by name). The dissertation's catalog emotions are *Moss's* examples, and her conclusion — passions are based on *phantasiai* alone, belief a normal accompaniment (98–99) — is the thesis T1-2 must defeat or absorb. The two-tier reconciliation is *invited* by Moss's own structure: she confines the phantastic account to the **non-rational part** of the soul (§4.2) and grants that evaluative beliefs normally accompany the passions, so the dissertation can (a) **concede the lower tier** (basic hedonic tonality / *resonant epithymia* / the apparent good) to her phantastic model while (b) arguing the **higher civic emotions** add a *doxa*-condition her non-rational-part argument cannot reach. Note the leverage point: Moss's wedge is that the passionate part is *incapable of belief* (§4.2); if the dissertation's civic emotions are seated (even partly) in a *logos*-sharing capacity, her central argument does not bind them.
- **The accepting-vs-believing distinction (90–94) — bears directly on whether "*doxa*" is the right model.** This is the sharpest tool Moss hands the dissertation's opponent *and* its most useful concession. Moss's claim is that emotion requires **acceptance** but that, for the non-rational part, acceptance collapses into mere being-appeared-to (default, automatic, unreflective) — so "acceptance" need *not* be belief (93). This (i) confirms that the dissertation cannot get *doxa* for free from the intuition that "emotion involves taking things to be so" (that intuition is satisfied by sub-doxastic acceptance), and (ii) supplies the dissertation's most precise way to *characterize* the gap between a weak "acceptance" and full *doxa* (= taking-as-true with *logos*-grounded conviction, *pistis*). The dissertation's stronger commitment (*doxa* = involuntary *positive* taking-as-true) must show that civic emotions require the *active/rational* species of acceptance that Moss reserves for belief, not the default species she assigns to the *pathē*.
- **The two-tier reconciliation is *textually staged by Moss herself* (§4.8).** Moss's thesis-(b) — *phantasia* normally triggers a corresponding *doxa* — is *already* a two-level picture: a non-rational phantastic basis plus a normal doxastic overlay. The dissertation can adopt exactly this architecture and then argue that for the *civic* emotions the overlay is *constitutive* rather than a mere "normal accompaniment." Moss's round/round analogy (99) is the hinge to contest: she says the belief is *separable* from the seeing-as-round; the dissertation's claim is that for higher emotions the belief is *not* separable.
- **Animal-*pathos* (1079 fn).** Moss is a strong source for the *opposite* of the dissertation's instinct here: she argues animals genuinely have passions (§4.2, §4.7) on *phantastic* grounds, against Konstan/Fortenbaugh/the Stoics' "only analogous." The dissertation's claim that higher, rational-articulated affects are unavailable to animals must therefore be pitched at the *rational overlay* (the *doxa*-involving civic emotions), conceding Moss's point about the *non-rational* basal *pathē* — which is precisely the two-tier move.
- **T1-4 (emotion toward non-realizable goods).** Moss's apparatus bears indirectly: because the apparent good is *phantasia*-based and can represent absent/non-present and even never-perceived objects (Caston's dragons; *de An.* 432a3–9), emotions toward non-realizable or counterfactual goods are unproblematic on her model — they need only an evaluative *phantasia*, not a belief in attainability. The dissertation's grief/non-realizable-good cases can borrow this, or contest it via the *boulēsis*/wish material she defers to Part III (Ch. 7).
