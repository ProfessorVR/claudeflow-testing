# AES-05 — Jamie Dow, *Passions and Persuasion in Aristotle's Rhetoric* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2015)

> **Cluster role**: the **judgmental / affirmational** pole of the Aristotelian-Emotion cluster, and the dissertation's decisive **ally**. The most sustained defense of the thesis that, for Aristotle, the passions are *pleasures and pains* whose representational contents (supplied by *phantasia*) are **affirmed by the subject herself** as the way things are — against the "mere appearance, uncommitted" (non-judgmental) reading shared by Cooper, Sihvola, Striker, and (qualifiedly) Moss. This is the resource that **defuses the recalcitrance objection**: the rope-snake / foot-wide-sun / fear-at-fiction cases. Scope = chs. 8–10 (pp. 131–225), with ch. 10 ("Feeling Fantastic Again — Passions, Appearances, and Beliefs in Aristotle," pp. 182–225) the keystone. Paraphrased throughout (copyrighted monograph); anchored to Bekker numbers and to chapter/page ranges from the table of contents. Ch. 10 reuses Dow's *OSAP* articles (2009, 2014); ch. 9 reuses Dow 2011.

## § Master thesis

The book's principal claim is that, for Aristotle in the *Rhetoric*, **arousing the passions of an audience can amount to giving them proper grounds for conviction (*pistis*)**, so that a skill in doing so is genuinely part of the expertise of rhetoric (Introduction, "Principal Claims"; ch. 6; ch. 10.1). This rests on two foundations. First (Part 1), a *normative* interpretation of rhetoric centred on its role in the state: rhetoric just is the skill of producing "proper grounds for conviction," and its three "technical proofs" (*entechnoi pisteis*) — argument, character (*ēthos*), and the passions of the listeners (*pathos*) — are each ways of supplying such grounds. Second (Part 3), and the part load-bearing for this cluster, a *particular view of the passions*: they must be **representational states in which the subject takes things to be the way they are represented**. For if a passion involved *no* affirmation of its content — if it were a "mere appearance" to which the subject is wholly non-committal — it could not serve as the acceptance of a premise, and so its arousal could not be a technical proof of anything (Introduction, "Synopsis"; ch. 10.1–10.2).

Aristotle accommodates this representational aspect within a **substantive theory of the passions as pleasures and pains** (ch. 9). Two claims: (Claim 1) to have a passion *is* to experience pain, pleasure, or both; (Claim 2) that pain/pleasure is *intentional and representational* — it is pain/pleasure *at* the passion's "target," and it involves the target's being represented in ways that give the "grounds" for the particular passion (ch. 9.1; 1378a19–22). Being afraid of the bear is pain *at* the bear, *at* its being a source of future harm. The representational capacity exercised is *phantasia* (ch. 10.3) — but, crucially, the *attitude* the subject takes toward *phantasia*'s evaluative content, in having a passion, is one of **affirmation**: the animal as a whole treats the representation as giving the way things actually are (ch. 10.4; 10.8).

Dow announces in ch. 10 that he has **changed his mind** since Dow 2009: he now holds that a passionate state constitutes "an affirmation by the subject *herself* (not only by a part of her soul)" of *phantasia*'s representations (10.1). This affirmation is the hinge of his anti-recalcitrance argument. Because passions involve the subject's *affirming* their content, recalcitrant passions (fear of the spider one believes harmless) are genuine cases of the subject's holding **inconsistent attitudes** — which is exactly what a good theory of emotion should deliver, and exactly what the "uncommitted appearance" reading cannot (10.2; 10.4.4). The foot-wide sun, by contrast, is *not* a case of inconsistency: there the subject remains entirely uncommitted to the small-sun appearance and keeps a "wholehearted endorsement" of the belief that the sun is huge (10.4.4). The disanalogy *between* the sun-illusion and recalcitrant passion is therefore the engine of Dow's case: a belief-/affirmation-based account is *bad* for the sun (it would make the sun-case irrational, which it is not) but *good* for the passions (it makes irrational fear irrational, which it is).

## § Section-by-section synthesis

**Ch. 8 — "The Passions in Aristotle's *Rhetoric*" (pp. 131–144).** A close reading of *Rhet.* 2.1, 1378a19–22: the passions are "those on account of which people change and differ *in relation to their judgements* (*pros tas kriseis*), and which are accompanied by pain and pleasure" (anger, pity, fear, and their opposites). Dow argues (8.2) this is a *definition* of the passions' essential features — since pleasure/pain is plainly essential across the corpus (*EN* 2.5 1105b21–3; *EE* 2.2 1220b12–14; *Phys.* 7.3 247a3–9), and by parity the "difference in *kriseis*" clause is too. **On *krisis* (8.5):** against Sihvola — who reads the deliberately general *krisis* as a *studied neutrality* between belief- and appearance-readings (and as leaving room for animal *pathē*) — Dow argues the word is chosen because rhetoric operates in *courts and assemblies*, where listeners render *verdicts* (*kriseis*); Aristotle flags the usefulness of emotion-arousal, not psychological neutrality. The examples (1377b31–1378a5: things "appear" (*phainetai*) different to friendly vs. hostile; the friendly listener "believes" (*dokei*) the accused innocent) show Aristotle switching *indifferently* between "appears" and "believes" — evidence the passion's outlook is something the subject *takes to be so*. **Adequacy (8.6):** apparent counterexamples (enmity, which involves *no* pain, 1382a12–13; the hungry diner) are met by an *integrated* reading: a passion is a representational state of the subject's overall outlook that *is itself* pleasure/pain *at* the represented circumstances — so hunger and headache-thoughts fail the definition, and the friend-as-innocent worry dissolves (passions always affect the *overall* outlook, even when considered judgement is unmoved).

**Ch. 9 — "Aristotle's Theory of the Passions — Passions as Pleasures and Pains" (pp. 145–181).** Against Cooper (the *Rhetoric* offers only a "preliminary, dialectical" clarification), Striker (no theory of Aristotle's *own*; results imported from the Academy), and Frede (Aristotle endorses the *Philebus* view that each emotion is a *mixed* pleasure-and-pain), Dow argues that *Rhet.* 2.1–11 expresses **Aristotle's own theory** of the emotions, which is moreover *still credible* (9.1). The theory is Claim 1 + Claim 2 (above). **Claim 1 (9.2):** a philological study of *hepetai* ("accompanies / follows," 1378a21) shows that, although the bare word asserts no more than concurrence, Aristotle's dominant usage marks a connection in which one term is *part of the essence* of the other (cf. *An. Pr.* 56a28–9; the *Rhet.* 1.7 1363b28ff. simultaneous/subsequent/potential distinction). Six *Rhet.* 2 passions are explicitly *defined* as pains (fear 1382a21f.; shame 1383b12–14; pity 1385b13f.; indignation 1386b9–11; envy and emulation 1386b18–21, 1387b23–5), three as pleasures (incl. *epichairekakia* / *Schadenfreude*); on a generous count up to eleven of seventeen emotion-types are pleasures or pains. Anger is "a desire *with* pain" — a desire that is also a pain — so even where pleasure/pain is not the *genus*, it can be essential (n.13; Aspasius's "most generic passions" view, *CAG* 19.1, is rejected as un-Aristotelian, *pace* Sorabji). **Claim 2 (9.3):** the pains/pleasures are *intentional* — taken *at* a "target" and *on* "grounds," following Aristotle's own three-part schema (1378a22–4: how disposed, at whom, on what grounds). Dow shows (via the *epi* + dative, *peri* + accusative, *dia* + accusative, *hoti* constructions) that targets and grounds rarely come apart: the passion just *is* representing facts about its object. **The pleasure-account (9.4–9.5):** for the passions to be representational pleasures/pains, Aristotle needs an account of pleasure that is representational. Against the "restoration of nature" reading of *Rhet.* 1.11 (1369b33–1370a18) — on which pleasure is just a perceived natural restoration, with no role for *how* the object is viewed — Dow argues that the definition's first clause ("some kind of motion/event of the soul," b33f.) would be near-superfluous on that reading, and that Aristotle's own examples (habits; the unforced) are pleasant *not* because they restore one's nature but because they produce an experience in which we *take ourselves* (sometimes mistakenly) to be undergoing such a restoration. Read against the *Philebus* (31b–36c) and especially the *Timaeus* (64c–e), pleasure and pain emerge as *perceptual, representational, and falsifiable* events with a "double object." This grounds the passions-as-representational-pleasures thesis. **Merits (9.6):** that pleasure/pain is *not the genus* of the emotions is itself a philosophical advantage.

**Ch. 10 — "Feeling Fantastic Again — Passions, Appearances, and Beliefs in Aristotle" (pp. 182–225).** The keystone. Dow distinguishes **four questions** usually run together (10.1): (1) by what *faculty* do passions get their content? (2) what *attitude* toward that content does a passion involve, for the subject *herself*? (3) how do passion-*phantasmata* fit Aristotle's general psychology of *phantasia* in humans and animals? (4) what passion–belief *conflicts* does Aristotle recognize, and with what resources? Conflating these has, he argues, muddied the *doxa*-vs.-*phantasia* debate. His own view: the passions involve *phantasia* (question 1), but the subject **affirms** *phantasia*'s content (question 2).

- *10.2 Significance + the recalcitrance desiderata.* Locating the passions' representations in *phantasia* yields three fits (with Aristotle's biology, ethics, and rhetoric) and a philosophical payoff for **recalcitrant emotions** — cases where an emotion arises or persists in recognized conflict with the subject's better belief/knowledge. Dow sets out **five desiderata** any theory of the emotion-attitude must meet: (1) not render recalcitrance impossible/exceptional; (2) explain why emotions *usually* track better beliefs; (3) account for the *conflict/inconsistency* implicating the subject; (4) account for the *failing* in recalcitrant response; (5) not *overstate* that failing. He flags immediately that a *phantasia*-based account meets (1), since *phantasia* and *doxa* are distinct capacities and so can conflict. The chief crucial datum (10.1): Aristotle thinks adult humans do *not*, except in highly unusual cases, have persisting passions whose contents they *wholly repudiate* — passions are aroused either where belief affords *some* support, or where reason is *disabled* (sleep, drink, disease). Where one believes a fear *wholly* unwarranted, one will *not* feel the fear, despite *phantasmata* of fearsome things (*DA* 3.3, 427b21–4).
- *10.3 Passions involve* phantasia. Two arguments (each sufficient, together decisive). (i) *Parts of the soul (10.3.1):* the passions belong to the *non-reasoning* part, whose representational resources are limited to *aisthēsis* and *phantasia*, not *doxa*/*hypolēpsis*/*pistis* (*DA* 1.1 403a3–10, where anger/appetite/perception are body-involving and "all the passions" are added at a16; *EN* 1.13; *EE* 2.1; *Rhet.* 1.10 1369a4, anger and appetite as *alogos orexis*). Since most passions' objects are not currently sensed, the representation must be *phantasia*. (ii) *Pleasure/pain (10.3.2):* passions essentially involve (sensory) pleasure/pain, and pleasure/pain itself involves *phantasia* ("a kind of weak perception," *Rhet.* 1.11 1370a27–32; confirmed at *Phys.* 7.3 247a7–18). Dow *sets aside* (10.3.3) the standard inference *from* Aristotle's use of *phantasia*/*phainomenos* in the *Rhet.* 2.2–11 definitions to the faculty-claim (Cooper, Sihvola, Striker): that terminology, he will argue (10.4.2), shows something else — how the subject *takes things to be*.
- *10.4 The attitude: affirmation (the heart of the chapter).* Dow specifies **"affirming"** carefully (10.4.1). To "affirm" *phantasia*'s content is **not** a further psychological act of assent *over and above* exercising *phantasia* and *nous*. It is a **relational, person-level** matter: an animal is "simply disposed to treat the representational contents of *phantasia* as giving the way things are, *unless nous is operating effectively*," in which case the *nous*-derived beliefs are what get affirmed (paraphrasing 429a4–8). Whether *phantasia* is affirmed thus depends on whether *something else in the animal's psychology* (a more authoritative faculty) takes over the role. A second distinction (the crossed-fingers case, *Insomn.* 461b2–5): contents can be affirmed (a) by the *capacity* that presents them, or (b) by *the person as a whole*. Dow's thesis is the *latter*: in a passion, the *subject as a whole* affirms — and this is precisely where his account goes *beyond* Moss's (n.48: Moss's "acceptance" speaks only to *sub-personal* parts; Dow addresses the whole subject). Two qualifications: the subject can simultaneously affirm something contrary *by a reasoned belief* (then she is *conflicted* — this is recalcitrance); and reason can be *inhibited by degrees* (10.5), so the behavioral weight of the affirmed *phantasma* varies. **Evidence (10.4.2):** the *phantasia*/*phainesthai* terminology in the *Rhet.* 2.2–11 definitions does *not* signal the faculty but signals that *this is how the subject takes things to be*. The orator could not arouse anger merely by getting the audience to *entertain* an uncommitted thought of insult; anger *abates* (calmness, 1380a8–12) precisely when the subject *affirms* that the accusation was false or the deed involuntary. "Apparent" (*phainomenon*) flags only that the subject *may be wrong* — a false belief in innocence quells anger as well as a true one, but a *mere unendorsed thought* does not. **Second argument (10.4.3, *DA* 3.3 427b16–24):** *believing* something fearsome immediately produces a passion, whereas with *phantasia* "we are as if looking at fearsome things in a painting." This shows *doxa* suffices and uncommitted *phantasia* does not — best explained by the passions' being *responses to states of affairs taken to obtain*, i.e. involving affirmation.
- *10.4.4 The sun, the mixture view, and the limits of the visual-illusion model.* Dow rereads *DA* 3.3 428a18–b9 (the foot-wide sun). Aristotle's target is the Platonic **"mixture view"** (*Sophist* 263d–264b): *phantasia* = *doxa* + *aisthēsis*, with belief explaining how appearances can be false. The sun case is a *problem for that view*: a false appearance (sun a foot wide) sits alongside a true belief (sun huge), and the mixture theorist must say either that the true belief is *lost* (with none of the usual belief-changing processes occurring) or that "the same [belief] is at once true and false" (b7–8). Surveying the readings (Hamlyn/Lycos; Hicks/Everson; his own preferred "in his view" reading), Dow's payoff is the **disanalogy**: the sun-illusion does *not* implicate the subject in inconsistency — her "wholehearted endorsement" of "the sun is huge" is *uncompromised*, and nothing is irrational about the sun's looking small. So a belief-/affirmation-account is a *bad* theory of the sun-appearance. **But that does not transfer to the passions.** If affirmation is involved in a passion, then fearing the spider one believes harmless *is* irrational and conflicted — exactly right ("irrational fears"). Hence the *DA* 3.3 arguments, far from motivating a *non-doxastic* account of the passions, actually *tell against* the "wholly unendorsed appearance" reading, which cannot capture the conflict and irrationality of recalcitrant passions. Dow concludes the passions involve "*some level of* affirmation."
- *10.5 Regulation of the passions.* Two features of *phantasia*: (a) whether a *phantasma*'s content is affirmed is governed by whether a *more authoritative* faculty is active (the crossed-fingers and sun cases are *uncommitted* because sight/reason overrules; in brutes and reason-disabled humans, *phantasia* is affirmed and acted on, *DA* 3.3 429a5–8); (b) *evaluative phantasmata* of the pleasant/painful have **necessary motivational consequences** *regardless* of conflicting belief (*De Motu* 8, 701b33–702a2). The "covering-over" of reason (10.5.2) is explicitly a matter of *degree* (*Insomn.* 460b11–16), and passions *themselves* can cover over reason (citing Moss 2009 for the *EN* 7.3 mechanism). Feature (b) explains (10.5.3) why Aristotle uniquely demands that *evaluative* *phantasia* "listen to reason" and "completely concur" in the virtuous (*EN* 1.13 1102b13–33) while imposing no such demand on *non-evaluative* *phantasia* (memory, dreams, the sun). The painting passage (*DA* 3.3 427b23–4), via *Rep.* 10 596d–e, is reread as evaluative *phantasia* *successfully concurring with reason*: recognizing image-*as*-image subtly shifts the content (to "what fearful things *look like*"), so we are unmoved.
- *10.6 Resources for conflict.* On Aristotle's view recalcitrant passions involve **two malfunctions**: (1) the passion's evaluative representation is *not* regulated by reason; (2) the less-authoritative *phantasma* nonetheless remains *affirmed by the subject* and keeps exerting motivational pressure, when the subject *should* be comparatively uncommitted to it (as with the known sun-illusion). This is a *failing* — a strength of the theory, per desideratum (4). The conflicts Aristotle *actually* foregrounds, though, are *not* cases where reason contradicts the passion's representation directly: in *thumos*-akrasia (*EN* 7.6, 1149a21–b26; Odysseus's anger, *Od.* 20; cf. *Rep.* 4 440e–441b) reason agrees a slight occurred and grants *some* impulse to vengeance, disputing only the all-things-considered "one should fight"; in appetite-akrasia (*DA* 3.10, 433b5–10) reason does not repudiate the object's being *pro tanto* pleasant/good but objects that appetite treats it as *unqualifiedly* so. *De Mem.* 2 (453a26–8: anger/fear that won't "settle down") is read, on balance, as about *bodily* persistence rather than persisting misrepresentation; *DA* 1.1 (403a19–25) supplies the body-involvement cases. Such recalcitrant conflicts are *unusual* deviations from the normal functioning in which passions occur *only* in the absence of conflict with reason.
- *10.7 Philosophical merits.* The account meets all five desiderata, and notably *avoids overstating* the failing (5): the failing in a recalcitrant emotion is *less severe* than holding outright inconsistent *beliefs*, because the reasoning part is the "ruling" part and it is more important that *it* be evidence-regulated. Dow even allows (via *EN* 7.6 1151b17–22) that *occasionally* the passion may track the evidence *better* than reasoned belief (the well-founded but inarticulate suspicion of a fraud), in which case its persistence is a *merit*.
- *10.8 Conclusion + the fiction problem.* The passions are exercises of *phantasia* with evaluative content that the subject *affirms* — which is why their arousal can be a *legitimate* rhetorical proof, and why the *virtuous* (who make an *unconflicted* affirmation of the correct appraisal) are better than the merely self-controlled (who carry a "dissenting voice"). The **Conclusions** chapter (pp. 226–8) leaves *fiction explicitly unresolved*: passion at known fiction forces a dilemma — either it is a psychological *malfunction*, or the subject in some way takes the fictional objects to be real. Dow gestures (via *Poetics* 1451a36–b10) at a possible escape: in pitying/fearing tragic figures we may "really" be responding to universal truths about the human condition, so we *take something* to be the case without compromising our grasp of the fiction's fictionality — but he does not adjudicate.

## § Positioning within the cluster

Dow anchors the **judgmental/affirmational pole**, *contra* Moss (AES-03/AES-04). The two share substantial ground: both hold that the passions' representational capacity is *phantasia*, both accept that recalcitrant passions resemble visual illusions in *some* respect, both read *DA* 3.10 and *EN* 7.3 as a complementary account of appetite-akrasia (Dow repeatedly cites "the justly celebrated Moss 2009"). The split is over **question 2 — the *attitude***. Moss makes the motivating evaluative cognition a *non-doxastic, default-accepted* appearance that the subject does *not* positively endorse ("reason simply fails to oppose"; "*phantasia* and *doxa* do not reside in the same part of the soul"). Dow makes it an *affirmation by the subject as a whole* — and presses precisely the *sub-personal*-vs.-*personal* distinction Moss's "acceptance" leaves untheorized (10.4.1 n.48). The cluster's sharpest disagreement is therefore over the **recalcitrance passages**: Moss reads the foot-wide sun and the fevered wall-cracks as the *paradigm* of non-doxastic, default-yielding motivation; Dow reads the *sun* as *disanalogous* to passion (no inconsistency, no irrationality) and uses that very disanalogy to argue that the passions, *unlike* the sun-illusion, involve genuine subject-level affirmation and so genuine conflict. Toward **Fortenbaugh (AES-01)**, Dow is a near-ally with one explicit correction: where Fortenbaugh ties the passions to *belief/judgment*, Dow agrees the subject "takes things to be" as represented but insists the faculty is *phantasia* not *doxa*, and rejects Fortenbaugh's claim that it is the passions' *causes*, not the passions themselves, that carry representational content (Dow 2011, 58–9; ch.10 n.1). Toward **Konstan (AES-02)** Dow is congenial on animals: his common-*phantasia* account lets human and non-human passions differ only "by the more and the less" (*HA* 8.1 588a18–30), a *softer* line than Konstan's denial of *pathē* "in the proper sense" to animals. Cross-pipeline, Dow leans on the **Phantasia Secondary** debate (Caston 1996/1998 "needs imagination"; Everson; Lorenz 2006, whose "prospects" reading of *phantasia* Dow resists; Nussbaum 1978 on *De Motu*) and bridges to the **rhetorical-phantasia** literature; his "proper grounds for conviction" framing connects to Heideggerian *doxa*-as-yes-saying and Gross's situated-emotion adjacency.

## § Significance for the dissertation's Section 5

- **T1-2 (the *doxa*-gate vs. the *phantasia* rival) — Dow is the primary ALLY.** He is the strongest single source *for* a "taking-as-true" requirement on affect: a passion that involved no affirmation of its content could not be a *pistis*, so for Aristotle (in the rhetorical context the dissertation cares about) the passions *do* involve the subject's taking things to be as represented. This directly arms the dissertation against Moss's non-doxastic reading and underwrites the claim that aroused civic emotions can constitute *reasonable acceptance of a speaker's premises*.
- **CRITICAL — is Dow's "affirmation" the dissertation's *doxa* / "taking-as-true"?** *Not identical, and the difference is load-bearing.* Dow's affirmation is **weaker and distinct** from full *doxa*/*pistis* in three respects the dissertation must register. (1) *Different faculty*: affirmation is the *non-reasoning* part's treating *phantasia*-content as veridical; *doxa*/*hypolēpsis*/*pistis*/*conviction* belong to the *reasoning* part. This is *why* a passion's affirmation can stand *against* a contrary belief (recalcitrance) — they are different capacities, so this is not (*pace* a naïve belief-theory) a flat self-contradiction within one faculty. (2) *Not a separate act*: affirmation is a **relational, person-level disposition** to "treat as giving the way things are," constituted by the *absence of override* by a more authoritative faculty — *not* an extra act of assent (so it is *not* Stoic *synkatathesis*, and not a positive volitional endorsement). (3) *Gradable*: Dow speaks of "*some level of* affirmation" and of reason's being inhibited "by degrees," whereas *pistis*/conviction reads as all-or-nothing. **Upshot for the dissertation**: Dow vindicates the *taking-as-true* core of the *doxa*-gate (the subject really does take her passion's content to be so), but **not** the *conviction/pistis* strength the dissertation may load onto "*doxa* = involuntary positive taking-as-true." The dissertation should either (a) recast its "taking-as-true" as **Dovian affirmation** (non-doxastic, relational, gradable, person-level) rather than full *doxa*, OR (b) argue that the *higher civic* emotions specifically engage the *reasoning* part (*doxa* proper) in a way Dow's general account does not require. Option (a) maps cleanly onto the **two-tier reconciliation**, with Dow supplying the *attitude* (affirmation) at *both* tiers and Moss supplying the non-doxastic *mechanism* at the lower.
- **How Dow defuses recalcitrance (the rope-snake / sun / fiction cases) — flag exactly.** (i) *Sun (foot-wide) and crossed-fingers/eyeball*: these are **uncommitted** *phantasia* — the subject explicitly rejects the content; *no inconsistency*, *no irrationality*; they are **disanalogous** to passions and so do *not* threaten the affirmation reading (10.4.4; 10.5.1). (ii) *Genuine recalcitrant emotion (rope-snake startle; fear of harmless spider)*: a **double malfunction** — evaluative *phantasia* unregulated by reason, *plus* the *phantasma* remaining affirmed and motivationally live when reason should override it; this is a real *failing*, which is a *desideratum met*, not a problem (10.6). The persistence of the pull even against belief is explained by evaluative *phantasmata*'s **necessary** motivational consequences (*De Motu* 8) and by reason's degree-graded inhibition — *not* by Moss's "default yielding." (iii) *Fiction (fear at tragedy)*: handled *twice and differently* — in 10.5.3 the **painting** case (*DA* 3.3 427b21–4) is a *success*: recognizing image-as-image shifts the content so the appearance is *not* affirmed and we are unmoved; but in the **Conclusions** Dow concedes fiction is **unresolved**, offering only the *Poetics*-based suggestion that tragic pity/fear may really target *universal* features of the human condition (so something *is* taken-as-true). **For T1-4 (emotion toward non-realizable / non-actual objects, incl. grief and fiction): Dow's account is admittedly incomplete here** — the dissertation can borrow his "universals" gesture but must supply the resolution Dow withholds.
- **The single most important point for T1-2.** Dow's **disanalogy argument** is the keystone: the foot-wide sun is the wrong model for emotion *because* it involves no subject-level inconsistency, whereas recalcitrant passions *do* — therefore the passions, unlike the sun-illusion, involve the subject's **affirming** their content. This is the exact inversion of Moss's use of the same sun passage, and it is the dissertation's most economical reply to the recalcitrance objection: concede the sun/wall-cracks to Moss as *non-doxastic illusion*, and insist (with Dow) that *emotions proper* are categorially different precisely in carrying subject-level affirmation — which is what makes their arousal a legitimate *pistis*.
- **Animal-*pathos* (1079 fn).** Dow's common-*phantasia*, "more-and-less" account (*HA* 8.1 588a18–30) supplies a *graded-continuity* alternative to a hard rational-only line: animals have *simpler* affirmations of *simpler* contents, not merely analogical *pathē*. The dissertation can use this to soften any claim that higher affects are *categorially* unavailable to animals — the difference is in the *complexity of the affirmed content* (deploying notions like "undeserving," "slight"), not in the *kind* of attitude.
