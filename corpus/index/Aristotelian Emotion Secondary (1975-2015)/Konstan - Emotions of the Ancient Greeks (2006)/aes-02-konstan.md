# AES-02 — David Konstan, *The Emotions of the Ancient Greeks: Studies in Aristotle and Classical Literature* (University of Toronto Press [Robson Classical Lectures], 2006)

> **Cluster role**: the **judgmental / social-cognitive** pole, supplied by the classicist rather than the philosopher of mind. Konstan reads the Greek *pathē* as **cognitive acts** — construals of the words, acts, and intentions of others insofar as they bear on one's **social standing** — and therefore as *changeable by persuasion* and *denied "in the proper sense" to animals and small children*. He is the dissertation's principal witness for the **toward-whom / social-relational** structure of emotion (five-fold element 2) and, via his **Grief** chapter, its strongest source for the claim that some affects (grief, sadness, loneliness) **do not fit Aristotle's conception of a *pathos*** at all. Content paraphrased throughout; page anchors to the printed pagination; Aristotle anchored to Bekker (*Rhetoric* II.1–11 supply the per-emotion definitions). No ≥25-word verbatim from the copyrighted book.

## § Master thesis

The emotions of the ancient Greeks differ in significant, partly *systematic* respects from our own, and the difference is grounded in the **social world** of the classical city-state (5). Against the neo-Darwinian / "basic-emotions" program of Ekman and the evolutionary psychologists — who treat a small set of affects as innate, universal, and legible from stripped-of-context facial expressions (8–20) — Konstan argues that *pathē* are not "internal states of excitation" but **reactions elicited by our interpretation** of the words, acts, and intentions of others (xii, 23–24). The emotions Aristotle catalogues in the *Rhetoric* are directed **principally at agents, above all human agents**: they "involve an awareness of other subjectivities" (39), which is why moods (melancholy, awe at nature), appetites (hunger, sexual desire), and morally-neutral losses fall outside the category (39–40).

Konstan builds the thesis on the **cognitive turn** in emotion studies (Lyons, Lazarus, Solomon, Nussbaum) and on Aristotle's own definition of *pathos* in *Rhetoric* II.1 (1378a20–23): emotions are "all those things on account of which people change and differ in regard to their judgments, and upon which attend pain and pleasure" (27, 33). Two features of that definition do the work. (i) Because the *pathē* are picked out by their **effect on judgment** (*krisis*), and judgment is shifted by altering how one **construes** the precipitating event, emotions are intrinsically **rhetoric-responsive** — one can dissolve another's anger by showing the slight was unintended (xii, 35). (ii) Because the constitutive cognition is an **appraisal of social position** — a slight that *diminishes one's standing*, an undeserved success of a peer, a hostile agent with the power to harm — the emotions are, in the strong sense, **social transactions**, not private feelings disclosed through bodily symptoms (24, 30–31, 248).

Two corollaries are load-bearing for the dissertation. First, **the animal/child restriction**: because the *pathē* "depend on the capacity for symbolization" (xii–xiii), and because persuasion cannot reach a dog or an infant, Aristotle (and the Greek philosophers generally) held that **animals and small children do not have emotions "in the proper sense"** — they may be said to have *pathē* only by *analogical* usage (xii, 22, citing Fortenbaugh 2002: 94). Second, **the grief problem**: grief (*lupe*, *penthos*) is conspicuously **absent from the *Rhetoric*'s catalogue**, and Konstan argues this is not an oversight but symptomatic — grief lacks the relational/intentional structure (judgment of an agent's act, appraisal of desert or status, an answering "action readiness") shared by the genuine *pathē*, so that "grief might not qualify as a *pathos* at all" (245–248, 258).

## § Chapter-by-chapter synthesis

**Preface (ix–xiii) — the thesis statement.** Konstan states the premise (Greek emotions differ from ours; recognizing this illuminates Greek literature and our own emotional theory, ix) and previews the cases that do *not* map cleanly onto modern categories: romantic jealousy has **no** corresponding Greek term (Ch. 11), and grief is one Aristotle "omits to discuss" because, Konstan argues, "grief did not fit Aristotle's conception of an emotion" (xi). The Preface closes with the chapter's most-cited claim (xii): Greek philosophers held that animals and small children "did not have emotions — or rather *pathē* … — in the proper sense," because the emotions "are elicited by our interpretation of the words, acts, and intentions of others," so that one can **alter** an emotion "by changing [a person's] way of construing the precipitating event" — something impossible with dogs or infants. "The emotions, as opposed to drives and appetites, depend on the capacity for symbolization" (xiii); persuasion was therefore "central to the idea of an emotion."

**Ch. 1 — *Pathos* and Passion (3–40): the theory.** Konstan begins lexically: *pathos* (from *paschein*, "suffer/experience"; cognate via Latin *patior* with "passion"/"passive") covers a wide field — what befalls a person, a secondary quality (*Met.* 1022b15–21), a mental event such as remembering (*De Mem.* 449b4–7) — and acquires the specific sense "emotion" perhaps only late (Harris: the 420s or later) (3–4). He then mounts the **anti-universalist** argument by analogy with **colour terminology** (blue/indigo/*glaukos*/Welsh *glas* carve the spectrum differently across languages, after J. Lyons): the emotional repertoire is likewise "preeminently cultural" (Lutz on Ifaluk *fago*), and the Greek *pathē*-list exhibits "mergings, separations, hiatuses, and intersections" against modern catalogues — Aristotle splits anger into two emotions, collapses guilt into shame, includes *zēlos* and pity but **omits grief** (5–16). Darwin's *Expression of the Emotions* (1872) and Ekman's basic-emotions program are the tradition he resists: their fixation on the *terminus* of the process (the expressive face), abetted by photography and a post-Romantic/Cartesian picture of the self as a private inner locus, obscures the **stimulus** and its **evaluation** (8–20, 28–31).

The constructive core runs from p. 23. Modern **appraisal theory** (Lazarus, Roseman & Smith) and the recognition that emotions involve "a substantial cognitive component" converge on Aristotle, whose *Rhetoric* (Lazarus concedes) anticipated cognitive-mediational theory (23, 31). Konstan endorses **Fortenbaugh's** cognitivist reading and quotes his epilogue: humans can *believe* an insult has occurred or a danger threatens; "animals lack this cognitive capacity and therefore cannot experience emotions as analyzed by Aristotle," and emotion-words applied to beasts are **analogical usage** (22). He adds a key **demurral from Nussbaum**: a difficulty with her extreme cognitivism is that "she ascribes emotions, and hence judgment, to animals" (23, n. 32) — Konstan sides with the rationality-restriction. He flags but defers the dissertation-relevant question whether *human* startle-responses (recoiling from a snake-like stick, after LeDoux's dual-pathway model) are **"pre-emotions"**: genuinely cognitive fear requires the autonomic shying-away to be "in synch with a particular learned response," and in a culture where snakes are a delicacy the final response need not be fear at all (25–27). The **social-relational** strand follows: appraisal is "more fundamentally social" (Manstead & Fischer); the classical self was "interdependent" rather than "independent"; Elster describes Aristotle's world as one "in which everybody knows that they are constantly being judged" (23–24, 31).

Konstan then reaches the **definition of *pathos*** (*Rhet.* II.1, 1378a20–23): "all those things on account of which people change and differ in regard to their judgments, and upon which attend pain and pleasure" (33). He glosses both clauses. The **pain/pleasure** clause is the hedonic/valence dimension, but Aristotle does *not* sort emotions by valence: he writes "pain *and* pleasure," allowing mixed cases, and opposite emotions (pity / indignation) can *both* be painful (34). The **effect-on-judgment** clause is "more remarkable": it defines the *pathē* not by their cause but by their power to alter *krisis*. Against Fortenbaugh (who reads the *Rhetoric* definition as **local** to the rhetorical context, not a general definition of emotion, 2002: 114), Konstan argues the courtroom is "a concentrated form" of how emotions work "in the daily negotiation of social roles" (35). He defends the definition with Aristotle's own form/matter distinction (*De Anima* I.1, 403a29–b2): the *phusikos* (natural scientist) defines anger by the boiling of blood around the heart (matter), the *dialektikos* by "a desire to return pain for pain" (form / *logos*); Aristotle's *Rhetoric* definition is of the latter kind — it states the emotion's **purpose**, "what they are used for," on analogy with defining a house by the protection it affords (35–37). Konstan extracts the dissertation-relevant **feedback structure**: a belief ("she insulted me," "he intends me harm"), once charged with pleasure/pain, modifies *further* beliefs (a juror's verdict, a rival's motives) and may reinforce the original judgment — emotions are "self-validating," generating "a closed or circular cognitive system" (Aeneas's *furor* manufactures the belief that it is beautiful to die in arms) (37–38). Finally the **scope** consequence (39–40): the catalogue's emotions target *agents* and "involve an awareness of other subjectivities," so generalized moods, aesthetic feelings, bodily appetites, and "such sentiments as sadness, loneliness, or grief" (which arise from circumstances beyond anyone's control, not from a hostile intention) fall *outside* the *pathē* — a "Greek habit of thought" treating emotions as "responses not to events but to actions … that entail consequences for one's own or others' relative social standing."

**Ch. 2 — Anger (41–76).** Aristotle's definition (*Rhet.* II.2, 1378a31–33): anger is "a desire, accompanied by pain, for a perceived revenge, on account of a perceived slight (*oligoria*) on the part of people who are not fit to slight one or one's own" (41). Konstan stresses the **centrality of evaluation**: a slight is "a complex social event, which takes a considerable measure of judgment to recognize" — an appraisal of social roles, intentions, and consequences, not an instinctive response to a hostile gesture (43). "Accompanied by pain" distinguishes anger from **hatred** (*misos*), which is *not* painful (II.4, 1382a12–13) and aims at the other's *harm*/destruction, whereas anger aims at the other's *pain*, requires the offender to **perceive** the revenge (*antipathein*), is **personal** (at Cleon, not at a class, where hatred can target a group), and heals with time (42, 47–48). The restriction to *oligoria* (belittlement, from *oligos*) is "extraordinary": anger is **not** a response to harm as such, even intentional harm — that yields hatred — but only to a *slight*, of three species: **contempt** (*kataphronēsis*), **spite** (*epēreasmos*, thwarting another's wishes for no gain of one's own), and **insolence/abuse** (*hubris*, shaming another "for the sheer pleasure of it," from superiority) (45–46). The analysis presupposes a **hierarchical, honour-saturated** world: one is angered above all by a slight from an inferior or "from people of no account," before those whose esteem one values (73–74). Summary (75): Aristotle envisages "a world in which self-esteem depends on social interaction"; once another's low opinion is "actualized publicly in the form of a slight, you have lost credit," and "anger is precisely the desire to adjust the record" by a compensatory revenge that restores social position. (The chapter's bulk — Homeric *mēnis*/*cholos*, Achilles, the tragedians — is the literary case-study, not summarized here.)

**Ch. 4 — Shame (91–110).** Konstan distinguishes **aidōs** (Cairns: an inhibitory emotion of self-image; in Homer "always prospective and inhibitory," "not shame") from **aiskhunē**, which has *both* a prospective and a retrospective face and is the term Aristotle analyses as a *pathos* in the *Rhetoric* (93–96). Aristotle's definition makes *aiskhunē* a pain concerning those **ills — past, present, or future — that tend to bring disgrace** (*adoxia*) (98); Konstan reads it as a *single, unitary* emotion in which "what varies is simply the timing of the perceived ills," dissolving the shame/sense-of-shame split the lexicographers impose (99–100). Shame has **three elements**: the particular act (throwing away one's shield), the **character-flaw** it reveals (cowardice), and the **loss of esteem before the community** (101) — by tying shame to disgraceful acts *and* the vice they expose, Aristotle "bridges" the modern shame/guilt divide (101–102). Greek lacks a basic term for **pride** as shame's positive opposite — plausibly because in the competitive *polis* people were "struggling to preserve their status under the critical gaze of their fellow citizens" rather than "basking in their admiration" (100). Shame is thus, like anger, "symptomatic of a society in which one's reputation in the eyes of others is crucial" (260).

**Ch. 5 — Envy and Indignation (111–128).** A contrasting pair. **To nemesan / nemesis** ("being indignant") is "feeling pain at someone who appears to be succeeding undeservedly" (II.9, 1386b8–9) — the opposite of pity (pain at *undeserved misfortune*); both mark **good character** because they rest on a **judgment of desert** (and so are ascribed to the gods) (112). **Phthonos** ("envy") is "a kind of pain, in respect to one's equals, for their apparent success in things called good" — and crucially "not so as to have the thing oneself but [solely] on their account" (II.10, 1387b23–25), i.e. indifferent **both** to desert **and** to one's own need; it is the *only* emotion in the *Rhetoric* Aristotle treats as "unqualifiedly negative," the mark of the *mikropsukhos* and *philodoxos* (113). Konstan's distinctive historical thesis: **"indignation" (*to nemesan*) was not commonly an independent emotion in Aristotle's time** — *nemesis* was already archaic, and the pity/*phthonos* contrast (not pity/*nemesis*) prevailed afterward (Stoics, Cicero). Aristotle, he argues, **introduced and systematized** *to nemesan* "for reasons that have more to do with his own systematic exposition than with actual usage," appropriating an archaic word to supply a noun answering to the vice *phthonos* (the *Eudemian Ethics* mean *nemesetikos* between *phthonos* and *epikhairekakia*) (111–115). This is the book's cleanest case of the catalogue **diverging from ordinary usage under philosophical pressure** — directly relevant to how the dissertation should weigh the *Rhetoric* list as evidence.

**Ch. 6 — Fear (129–155).** Fear *looks* like the universal, animal-shared emotion (we ascribe it to a deer in flight), so it tests the cognitivist thesis (129). Aristotle's definition (*Rhet.* II.5, 1382a21–25): fear is "a kind of pain or disturbance deriving from an impression (*phantasia*) of a future evil that is destructive or painful," and only of near, impending evils (so all know they will die, but do not fear it, being far off) (130). Konstan insists **fear involves knowledge and inference**: it is not pain that frightens but the things that *portend* it (a poisonous snake, a poised spear, a snake-track in the sand), so fear tracks **signs** (*sēmeia*) (131). He stages the ancient debate that maps onto the dissertation's central axis: **Aspasius** held that "certain emotions are generated simply by impressions (*phantasiai*)" — by perception, prior to any *supposition* — whereas **Andronicus** held that "an emotion arises because of a *supposition* (*hupolēpsis*) of good or bad things"; Konstan judges that for fear (and the other *Rhetoric* emotions) "the truth lies more with **Andronicus** than with Aspasius" (132). Aristotle's fear is **supposition-laden**, not bare *phantasia* — a *judgmental* verdict, though Konstan grants the definition's own wording uses *phantasia* and a *future* evil. Fear is then shown to be **socially conditioned**: the frightening par excellence are agents who are hostile **and** have the power to harm — so fearing presupposes understanding anger and hatred, "which themselves depend on complex judgments" (132). Fortenbaugh's frightened soldier (the dust-cloud inferred a hostile army; "fear makes men ready to deliberate," *Rhet.* 1385a5) exemplifies the role of *logos* "in which animals have no share" (133); confidence (*to tharsos*) likewise rests on inference about relative strength (133). The literary cores (Thucydides' Phormio, Aeschylus's *Seven*) show fear as both *grounded in* and *colouring* judgment, and as **rhetorically manipulable** — generals alter their men's "perception of the enemy's capabilities," explicitly enacting the II.1 definition (143–145).

**Ch. 10 — Pity (201–218).** *Eleos* (Greek pity) is "not an instinctive response to another person's pain, but depended on a **judgment of whether the other's suffering was deserved or not**" — "both cognitive and social," involving "moral appraisals" (201). Aristotle's definition (*Rhet.* II.8, 1385b13–16): pity is "a kind of pain" at an apparent (*phainomenon*) "destructive or painful harm" of one "not deserving to encounter it," which one might expect oneself "or one of one's own, to suffer," when it "seems near." Konstan stresses the structural **"distance"** condition: to pity one must recognize a *resemblance* to the sufferer yet *not* share their plight — where identification is complete one feels the other's emotion, not pity (201–202). The chapter's move is to **widen** *eleos* slightly, finding room in Aristotle's scheme for humane sensibility toward even an *undeserving* sufferer via the "common laws of the Greeks" that limit legitimate suffering (the Plataean appeals in Thucydides/Isocrates) (202–203). Pity thus retains the **toward-whom / for-whom** structure (one pities for "oneself or one of one's own") that anchors the social reading.

**Ch. 12 — Grief (244–258): the *pathos*-status problem.** Konstan's "classificatory problem." Grief (*lupe*, *penthos*) was named and ritualized (lamentation, the consolation genre), but the question is "**whether grief was conceived of as an emotion**" — and his point of departure is its **absence from the *Rhetoric*'s catalogue** (244–245). A merely lexical explanation: *lupe* is just Aristotle's word for **"pain"** — one of the two *sensations* (*aistheseis*) that are *components* of a *pathos*, not a *pathos* — so it was "awkward to treat *lupe*, even in its significance as 'grief,' as a phenomenon on the same level as other *pathē*" (245–246). But Konstan presses a **substantive** argument (247–248). Other emotions are laden with appraisal — anger with a judgment of intentions and one's capacity for revenge, pity and indignation with desert, fear with a reckoning of relative power, even envy with a "sense of equivalent entitlement" among equals. **"Grief seems different in this respect"** (247): it involves "no judgment of intentions, no reckoning of relative power, no reference to desert or to social status." The loss of a loved one "strikes us with greater immediacy, as if the fact itself were painful," irrespective of intentions or "consequences for our social position" — "in this sense, the grieving is like physical pain" (247). Grief also lacks **"action readiness"**: where anger seeks revenge, love seeks the other's good, envy/*zēlos* seek equality, grief "invites no compensatory act … There is no agent who bears responsibility for one's grief, no one to get even with"; even when a *killing* caused the loss, the resulting anger/hatred targets the killer, while the *bereavement* targets the dead — "nothing will bring that person back … Time alone heals such a wound" (247–248). The chapter's keystone (248): "The emotions that Aristotle identifies and analyses are social, in the strong sense that they are responses to behaviour by **conscious agents** … deeply enmeshed in a world of competitive honour and esteem (*doxa*) … a *pathos* is part of a **social transaction**. The natural fact of death stands outside this system." Konstan reads **Sophocles' *Electra*** as a deliberate confrontation of the two sentiments: Electra's driving passion is not the "passive grief elicited by a morally neutral event" but **anger and a desire for vengeance** (her father was *murdered*, not merely dead) — anger has the agency, action-readiness, and social dimension grief lacks, which is why it, not grief, can sustain a drama (249–252). He closes (258): on the classical conception, "which looked more to agency and effect on social standing than to one's inner state," grief and anger "look radically different" — **"even to the point that grief might not qualify as a *pathos* at all."**

**Conclusion (259–262).** A précis of the social reading: Aristotle's emotional world is "highly competitive," everyone "jockeying to maintain or improve their social position"; *zēlos*/emulation, anger, shame, fear, pity, and indignation are each tuned to *relative standing, power, desert, and the gaze of others*. The Greek "ideological construction of the emotions" differs from ours partly because we now treat emotions "as interior states of feeling rather than responses to social interactions" (259–261).

## § Positioning within the cluster

Konstan anchors the **judgmental pole alongside Fortenbaugh and Dow**, but from **classics / history of emotions** rather than philosophy of mind, and with a distinctive **social-relational** inflection. Where Fortenbaugh (AES-01) locates the constitutive cognition in a **belief** internal to the agent's psychology, Konstan locates it in a **construal of another agent's act bearing on one's social standing** — the cognition is irreducibly *interpersonal* (debate-axis 3: social-relational vs. individual-psychological). He explicitly **endorses Fortenbaugh's** animal-restriction (quoting the 2002 epilogue at p. 22) and **parts from Nussbaum** precisely where she extends emotion (and judgment) to animals (p. 23 n. 32). Against the non-judgmental pole, Konstan's verdict on the fear debate is the cluster's clearest *witness* against a *phantasia*-only reading: he sides with **Andronicus** (*hupolēpsis* / supposition) over **Aspasius** (bare *phantasia*) for the *Rhetoric* emotions (132) — the mirror image of **Moss** (AES-03/04), for whom the motivating evaluative cognition is exactly a non-doxastic *phantasia*-appearance. The nuance the dissertation must record: Konstan frames the constitutive cognition as **social appraisal / judgment of standing**, not as *doxa* in Aristotle's technical sense; he is an ally of the *judgmental* side on the *that*-it-is-a-judgment, while supplying a *social-construal* rather than a *propositional-doxa* model of *what* the judgment is. Relative to **Dow** (AES-05), who defends the passions as requiring the subject's *affirming* a representation as true, Konstan's "appraisal/construal" is congenial but pitched at the level of cultural-semantic content rather than the assent-vs-appearance metaphysics Dow contests. Cross-pipeline, his treatment of fear's *phantasia* and the Aspasius/Andronicus dispute bears directly on the **Phantasia Secondary** cluster (the *phantasia*-as-appearance vs. *phantasia*-as-supposition question), and his social/situated reading is the classicist correlate of **Gross's** *Uncomfortable Situations* and the Heideggerian ***Mitsein*** frame of BCAP (*pathos* as being-with-others, *doxa* as the public taking-as).

## § Significance for the dissertation's Section 5

- **The toward-whom / *Mitsein* dimension (five-fold element 2) — Konstan is the classicist witness.** This is his strongest single contribution. The *pathē* "involve an awareness of other subjectivities" (39) and are "responses to behaviour by conscious agents … part of a social transaction" (248); each emotion has a built-in **for-whom and against-whom** (anger's offender-to-be-revenged-upon and the self-or-one's-own that was slighted; pity's "oneself or one of one's own"; shame's community-before-whom; fear's hostile-agent-with-power). The dissertation's element 2 (the toward-whom / *Mitsein* structure of the higher emotions) can cite Konstan as the **independent, source-critical confirmation** that Aristotle's catalogue is constitutively relational — emotion is directed at others' acts and intentions as they impinge on one's standing, not at impersonal events. Anchor passages: *Rhet.* II.2 1378a31–33 (anger's whole structure), II.8 1385b13–16 (pity's for-whom), and Konstan pp. 24, 30–31, 39–40, 248.

- **Animal-*pathos* (1079 fn) — strong, named support for the rational-only restriction.** Konstan is the cluster's most explicit ally here. The Preface (xii) and Ch. 1 (22–23) state flatly that Aristotle held animals and small children lack emotions **"in the proper sense,"** that beast-applications of *orgē*/*phobos* are **analogical** (quoting Fortenbaugh 2002: 94), and — crucially — Konstan **rejects Nussbaum's** ascription of emotions/judgment to animals (23 n. 32). For the dissertation's footnote restricting *pathē* "in the proper sense" to rational, judging, social beings, Konstan supplies the doctrine, the rationale (emotions "depend on the capacity for symbolization," hence are unreachable by persuasion in beasts/infants), and a contrast partner (Nussbaum) — on a **judgmental/social** rationale complementary to Moss's *phantasia*-based "beasts-not-akratic." Single most important point: the criterion of a genuine *pathos* is responsiveness to the construal of an agent's act — a capacity beasts and infants lack — which is why persuasion (and only persuasion) can change a *pathos* but not a drive.

- **T1-4 (grief / emotions toward non-realizable goods) — Konstan is the *directly* relevant source; capture in detail.** The Grief chapter is the cluster's center of gravity for T1-4. On **textual** grounds (grief's absence from the *Rhetoric*) and **structural** grounds (no judgment of intentions, desert, or relative power; **no action-readiness**; no responsible agent — "no one to get even with"), Konstan argues that **grief did not fit Aristotle's conception of a *pathos*** and "might not qualify as a *pathos* at all" (247–248, 258). It is the most precise scholarly statement available that an affect oriented to an **irreversible, non-realizable** state of affairs (the dead cannot be brought back; "time alone heals") falls *outside* the relational-intentional schema. Single most important point: Konstan supplies the explicit exclusion-criterion — relational structure plus an answering action toward a responsible agent — giving the dissertation a ready-made test for "emotions toward non-realizable goods."

- **T1-2 (the *doxa*-gate vs. the *phantasia* rival) — Konstan is broadly an ALLY of the judgmental side, with a nuance.** Konstan reads the *Rhetoric* emotions as **judgments/appraisals** and, on the fear case, sides with **supposition (*hupolēpsis*) over bare *phantasia*** (132, the Aspasius/Andronicus verdict) — direct comfort against Moss's *phantasia*-only model. The nuance: Konstan frames the constitutive cognition as **social appraisal / construal of standing**, not as *doxa* in Aristotle's technical taking-as-true sense — an ally on *emotion = judgment*, but with a *social-construal* rather than *propositional-doxa* analysis of the judgment's content. For the **two-tier reconciliation** he strengthens the *upper* tier: the civic emotions (anger, shame, indignation, pity) are exactly the cases where appraisal of *agents, intentions, desert, status* is constitutive — the register the dissertation reserves for *doxa* — while the *phantastic* lower tier (basic hedonic tonality, startle/"pre-emotions") is one Konstan himself flags as possibly **sub-emotional** (the snake-stick recoil, 25–27). He also independently supplies the dissertation's **self-validating-emotion / *pathetic*-loop** intuition (a pleasure/pain-charged belief modifies and reinforces further beliefs — "a closed or circular cognitive system," 37–38), though he leaves its synchronic/diachronic status unanalysed. Single most important point: Konstan ranges the social-appraisal model on the judgmental side and rejects the *phantasia*-only reading for the *Rhetoric* emotions, but his "judgment" is social construal, not technical *doxa* — so he arms the upper tier of the reconciliation without by himself settling the *doxa*-gate.
