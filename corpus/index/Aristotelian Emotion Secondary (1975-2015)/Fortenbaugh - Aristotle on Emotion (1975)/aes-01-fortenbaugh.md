# AES-01 — W. W. Fortenbaugh, *Aristotle on Emotion: A Contribution to Philosophical Psychology, Rhetoric, Poetics, Politics and Ethics* (London: Duckworth, 1975)

> **Cluster role**: the **judgmental / doxastic** pole of the Aristotelian-Emotion cluster — its *locus classicus* and chronological origin point. Fortenbaugh's foundational thesis is that Aristotle made a decisive advance over Plato by giving a *cognitive* account of emotion: an emotion (*pathos*) **essentially involves a thought, belief, or judgment** built into its very definition as the *efficient cause* (anger = pain on account of an *apparent unjust slight*), so that emotions are (a) open to reason and rhetorical persuasion and (b) sharply distinct from non-rational bodily drives (hunger, thirst, sexual appetite). This is the belief/cognitivist pedigree from which the dissertation's *doxa*-thesis descends. **Crucial finding for T1-2: Fortenbaugh's constitutive cognition is *doxa*/*krisis* (belief/judgment), not *phantasia*.** He uses "thought," "belief," "opinion," "judgment," and "propositional judgment" throughout; *phantasia* and "imagination" appear nowhere in his conceptual apparatus or his subject index, whose relevant entry is "opinion (thought, belief involved in emotion)." Content paraphrased throughout; page anchors to the printed pagination (Duckworth 1975, pp. 9–92); Aristotle anchored by Bekker.

## § Master thesis

During Aristotle's years in the Academy a problem was under investigation that Plato had left unresolved in the *Philebus*: how exactly is cognition *involved* in emotional response (9–11)? Plato had grouped the emotions — anger, fear, longing, lament, love, emulation, envy (*Phlb.* 47e1–2) — as "pleasures and pains of the soul itself," marked off from bodily sensations (itches, tickles) and bodily drives (hunger, thirst), and had glimpsed that opinion is "intimately connected" with them; but his prepositions ("with," "follows," "fills up") were metaphors that named the connection without explaining it (11, 229–34). Aristotle's solution, Fortenbaugh argues, was to construe the thought as the **efficient cause** named in the *essential definition* of each emotion. In *Rhetoric* II Aristotle defines anger as a desire for revenge accompanied by pain *on account of* (*dia*) an apparent unjustified slight to oneself or one's own (*Rhet.* II.2 1378a30–32), and fear as a pain or disturbance arising from the appearance of impending destructive or painful evil (*Rhet.* II.5 1382a21–22). The thought is not a contingent accompaniment that merely happens to occur *together with* (*meta*) the disturbance; it is *causally necessary* and therefore part of the essence (11–12, 261–73).

This single move, on Fortenbaugh's reading, is the hinge of the whole book. Because the efficient cause is cognitive, four consequences follow, one for each field of the subtitle. (1) *Demonstrative science*: questions of essence and of cause are one (*An. Post.* 90a14–15), so each emotion now admits a syllogistic explanation with the cognition as middle term — "replace 'war' with 'anger' and 'unprovoked aggression' with 'apparent insult'" (13–15). (2) *Rhetoric*: since emotion is grounded in a belief that "may be criticised and even altered by argumentation," persuasion *through the hearers* (emotional appeal) is no longer an extra-rational "charm" or "enchantment" (so Gorgias, Thrasymachus) but intelligent behaviour, raised to a footing coordinate with persuasion *through demonstration* (16–18, 474–537). (3) *Poetics*: tragedy's pity and fear and comedy's laughter become *intelligent* responses compatible with reason, dissolving Plato's *Republic* X charge that drama "plays to the unintelligent part," and grounding the restriction of tragic plots (a good man falling, a vicious man prospering, arouse neither pity nor fear: *Poet.* 1452b34–1453a1) on purely poetic grounds (18–22). (4) *Politics and ethics* (the book's longest payoff, Chs. 2–4): the cognitivist analysis licenses a **bipartite psychology** — soul divided into an *alogical* (emotional) half and a *logical* (calculative) half, the former obedient to the latter — which replaces Platonic tripartition and undergirds the distinction between *moral virtue* (perfection of the alogical, emotion-bearing half) and *practical wisdom* (*phronēsis*, perfection of the logical half) (23–44, 63–92).

The deepest point for the cluster is *which* cognition Fortenbaugh makes constitutive. His answer is unwavering: it is **belief / judgment / opinion** — a *doxa* with propositional content, "open to the persuasion of reasoned *logos*" (48 n.1). Emotions "are grounded upon opinions or assessments" (25); emotion "involves judgment and therefore is open to reasoned persuasion" (30); the alogical half is "cognitive" precisely in being "associated with certain opinions or assessments" (86 n.1). Where the later non-judgmental reading (Moss, AES-03/04) makes the motivating evaluation a quasi-perceptual *phantasia*-appearance that can run *against* belief, Fortenbaugh runs the dependence the other way and entirely in the register of *doxa*: the belief grounds the emotion, and changing the belief changes the emotion. He does not thematize *phantasia* at all.

## § Section-by-section synthesis

**Ch. 1 §1 — Emotion and cognition (9–12).** The Academic backstory. Plato's *Philebus* gathers the emotions as soul-pleasures/pains distinct from bodily sensations and drives but fails to clarify how opinion is "involved." The *Topics* shows Aristotle already preferring a *causal* definition: anger is pain "with" the thought of outrage only in the sense of pain *on account of* (*dia*) such a thought — not "and," not "made up out of," not "in the same receptacle/place/time" (*Top.* 150b27–151a19; cf. 156a32–33). The *Rhetoric* then fixes the answer: the thought is the *efficient cause* written into the essence. Fortenbaugh frames this as a "striking anticipation of much modern controversy," siding Aristotle with Bedford (cognition essential to emotion) against Pitcher (cognition merely characteristic) and against James's bodily-feeling theory (12, 275–83).

**Ch. 1 §2 — The study of emotion and demonstrative science (12–16).** Building the efficient cause into the definition brings emotion under the *Posterior Analytics*: knowing the essence is knowing the cause (90a14–15, 31–32; 93b38). Unlike eclipses, the cause here is not recondite — ordinary Greeks already half-define fear as "expectation of evil" (*EN* 1115a9), so the scientist need only "tighten up" the everyday notion. Emotional response is then explained *syllogistically*, the cognition as middle term (94b16–118). Two corollaries: anger is necessarily directed *at a particular individual* (Cleon, Callias), because belief has an object (1378a33–34, 1382a5–6); and anger is logically distinguished from kindred emotions (hate, fear, shame) by *which* thought is built in — personal insult vs. a person's general bad character vs. impending harm vs. impending disgrace (1382a3–7, 1383b13–18). Fortenbaugh stresses that Aristotle's is an *inclusive* analysis: not the efficient cause alone but also the *material* cause (blood boiling round the heart) and *final* cause (return of pain / revenge) belong in the definition (*De An.* 403a25–b2), enabling demonstrations of why angry men flush and act.

**Ch. 1 §3 — A contribution to rhetoric (16–18).** The cognitivist analysis is rhetoric's great prize. (i) *Method*: Aristotle imposes a deductive ("let-it-be," *estō*) form on the treatment of emotions in *Rhetoric* II just as on the topics of Book I, meeting Plato's *Phaedrus* demand for a philosophical rhetoric. (ii) *Substance*: by making belief the efficient cause, Aristotle shows that emotional response "is intelligent behaviour open to reasoned persuasion" — the angry are "not victims of some totally irrational force" but respond "in accordance with the thought of unjust insult," a thought that argument can criticize and alter. Emotion thus ceases to be the diseased "charm/enchantment/drug" of Gorgias (*Helen*) and Thrasymachus (*Phaedrus* 267c) and earns a dignified, *coordinate* place beside demonstration; appeals need no longer be confined to prooemium and epilogue.

**Ch. 1 §4 — A contribution to poetics (18–22).** Symmetrical gains for poetics. Mimetic poetry is tied to *cognitive* emotions: tragedy to fear (*phobos*) and pity (*eleos*), comedy to the laughable (*geloion*). Once fear is "essentially an expectation of destructive or painful evil" and pity is referred to "the thought of unmerited suffering" addressed to men "who reason well" (1385b13–16), Plato's *Republic* charge that drama erodes reason loses force. The tragic emotions become *controls on plot construction* (the good-to-bad and vicious-to-good plots fail to arouse pity/fear and so are deficient on poetic, not merely moral, grounds: 1452b34–1453a1). Comedy is pruned of Plato's link to envy and reduced to harmless mistakes/deformities (*Poet.* 1449a34–35), pointing toward a Hobbesian "sudden glory"/superiority theory; "educated insolence" replaces crude *psogos*. Finally, the *inclusive* (cognitive + bodily) analysis lets Aristotle also meet Plato's demand for a *benefit*: the homoeopathic theory of *katharsis* — purgation of pity and fear (*Poet.* 1449b27–28; *Pol.* 1341b32–1342a15) — works because fear is also a bodily alteration (*De An.* 403a16–19), so the spectator undergoes a "quasi-medical treatment."

**Ch. 2 — A New Political-Ethical Psychology (23–44).** The pivot from emotion-theory to moral psychology. Plato's *Laws* already works with an *implicit bipartite* psychology: the puppet-image's golden cord of *logismos* against the iron cords of pleasure/pain and fear/confidence (645a), where fear and confidence are introduced explicitly *as opinions* (*doxai*) and expectation (*elpis*) is "a common name for opinions concerning the future" (644c9–d1). Fortenbaugh insists — against Görgemanns' "thrusts vs. cognition" reading — that the *Laws* dichotomy is **not** sensation/drive vs. thought but rather *two kinds of cognition*: emotional response vs. reasoned reflection (24–25, 780–815). Aristotle formalizes this latent bipartition (alogical vs. logical halves, the alogical "obedient" to reason: *EN* 1102b13–1103a3) and — the chapter's central polemic — argues it is an *advance over tripartition* and must **not** be conflated with the *De Anima*'s *biological* psychology (sensation vs. intellect). Emotion has no single biological-faculty locus: fear brings *nous*, *aisthēsis*, and *orexis* into play at once (28 n.1, 926–29), which is partly *why* the *De Anima* "generally ignores" emotion — a telling admission that Aristotle's *psychology of mind* lacks a unified treatment of *pathos*, so the *Rhetoric* must supply it.

**Ch. 3 — Consequences for Political Theory (45–61).** Applications: (1) *Moral education* (*paideia*) trains the alogical half's "loves and hates" *before* the logical half matures (after Plato, *Laws* 653b); the young "live by *ēthos*, not *logismos*" (*Rhet.* 1389a33–34). (2) The *imperfection of the young*: they have value-laden, action-guiding opinions but lack the deliberative excellence of *phronēsis*. (3) *Natural slaves* and (4) *women*: the natural slave shares in reason enough to apprehend but not to possess it; the cognitivist, bipartite frame supplies the analytic for these status-distinctions. Throughout, the animal/human boundary is policed by the *doxa* requirement: animal discrimination is *aisthēsis* + *krisis* but only a "quasi-affirmation"/"quasi-denial" (*De An.* 431a9–10), never a *doxa* "open to persuasive *logos*" (48 n.1, 2681–86) — animals cannot, strictly, have emotions, moral virtue, or *akrasia*.

**Ch. 4 §1 — A new conception of human virtue (63–70).** Because emotion is cognitive and human-specific, *moral virtue* is a *hexis* perfecting man's *alogical-but-cognitive* half — "a peculiarly human virtue" (*EN* 1102a14–16) unavailable to beasts. Even courage is "a cognitive disposition" of men who have learned to *despise* (*kataphronein*) danger and to *see* their endurance "as instances of noble action" (66–67). Animals are temperate/courageous only *metaphorically* (1149b31–32; 1145a25–27).

**Ch. 4 §2 — Virtuous action without calculation (70–75).** A pivotal refinement (important for the dissertation). Moral virtue can operate *without any exercise of practical wisdom*. The case of the "more courageous man" (*EN* 1117a18–22): in *sudden* alarms there is no time for deliberation, so response issues "entirely from the alogical half"; if that half is well-trained the agent "assesses the situation correctly and responds courageously" without *logismos*. Hitting the mean is "a critical [cognitive] act" but need not be assigned to *phronēsis* — *eustochia* is "something quick and without *logos*" (1142a2–3). The repeated dictum that the mean is "determined by right reason" commits Aristotle only to reason's *authority* in *justifying* what counts as virtuous, not to reason's being *in play* in every virtuous act (74–75). **This is the seat, in Fortenbaugh, of an unreflective-but-cognitive evaluation that grounds correct emotional response** — the nearest his account comes to a sub-deliberative value-cognition (cf. T1-2 below), though he names it judgment/assessment, never *phantasia*.

**Ch. 4 §3 — Moral virtue and the goal of action (75–79).** *Moral virtue makes the end correct; practical wisdom makes the means correct* (*EN* 1144a7–9; 1145a4–6). The morally virtuous man's habituated opinions *set the goal* (the noble), and his *phronēsis*/cleverness selects means. Against Allan, Fortenbaugh insists moral virtue *can* furnish a true judgment of the end, *because* the alogical half is itself cognitive — this is the doctrinal core of his anti-Humean, judgment-first ethics.

**Ch. 4 §4 — Practical and non-practical emotions (79–83).** A taxonomy with direct dissertation relevance. *Practical* emotions (fear, anger) build a *goal* into their essence — fear aims at safety (and so "makes men deliberators," *Rhet.* 1383a5–8), anger at revenge (and is *absent* when revenge appears impossible, *Rhet.* 1370b13; "no one aims at the apparently impossible," 1378b1–4) — and so can be perfected by an action-involving *hexis* (courage, good temper). *Non-practical* emotions (shame, indignation, pity) carry no essential goal/action and so support *no* corresponding moral virtue — explaining (a famous Fortenbaugh point) why Aristotle has no virtue of *righteous indignation* and no analogue to the Christian virtue of *mercy*. Shame is "pain concerning bad things which *appear* likely to involve discredit" (*Rhet.* 1383b12–14) — note again *appears*, but glossed by Fortenbaugh as the *thought* of disgrace.

**Ch. 4 §5 — Temperance and human appetites (83–87).** The cleanest statement of the *emotion-vs-drive* distinction that underwrites the whole cognitivist program. *Emotions* have a cognitive efficient cause; *bodily drives* (hunger, thirst, sex) have a *physiological* efficient cause and are shared with animals. Aristotle nonetheless makes *temperance* a moral virtue by exploiting the distinction between **non-rational appetite** (originating "through the body," *not* from judgment) and **rational appetite** ("attributed to persuasion") (*Rhet.* 1370a18–25). The temperate man's "loves and hates" are *acquired*, judgment-caused rational appetites "obedient to reasoned argument," not retuned stomachs; he "feels disgust at unseemly pleasures" because educated, not weak-stomached (1119a13). Crucially, even *sexual desire* "is often caused by thoughts and beliefs" — desire "awakened and directed by the thought that someone is beautiful and worth having," so that "judgment arouses the body and not vice versa" (87, 3483–88). Bare bodily appetite, by contrast, is persuasion-proof: one "does not reason one's hunger away" (1113b26–30).

**Ch. 4 §6 — Non-emotional modes of social interaction (87–92).** A sophisticated coda: friendliness, wittiness, and truthfulness are moral virtues *dissociated from* both emotions and appetites — perfections of the alogical half exhibited "in speech and action" rather than in feeling. This shows the alogical half is broader than the emotions, and that not every *hexis* of it is emotion-grounded — a nuance the dissertation can use when locating *which* affects carry rational/civic articulation.

## § Positioning within the cluster

Fortenbaugh **defines the judgmental pole** and is, chronologically, the cluster's headwater: every later unit writes in his wake. He shares with **Konstan (AES-02)** the thesis that emotions are cognition-laden judgments denied (in the strict sense) to animals and children — but Konstan's cognition is an interpersonal *social construal* (toward-whom, standing), whereas Fortenbaugh's is an *individual-psychological* propositional belief about the efficient-causal situation (insult, danger). He is the direct ancestor of **Dow (AES-05)**: Dow's "affirmation" reading (the passions require the subject's *affirming* the representation as true, *against* the "mere appearance" view) is a refinement and defense of Fortenbaugh's belief-thesis under the pressure of the recalcitrance cases that Moss exploits. The decisive opposition is with **Moss (AES-03/04)**, the non-judgmental/phantastic pole: where Moss makes the *motivating* evaluation a quasi-perceptual *phantasia*-appearance that resides in a *different part of the soul* from belief and can run *contrary* to it, Fortenbaugh recognizes only *doxa*/*krisis* and treats the emotion as straightforwardly *tracking* belief (alter the belief, alter the emotion). Notably, on the central debate axis — **DOXASTIC ↔ PHANTASTIC** — Fortenbaugh sits at the *extreme doxastic* end: he does not merely privilege belief over appearance, he **does not deploy *phantasia* as a category at all**. The Aristotelian definitions he leans on are full of *phainomenon* ("apparent slight," "appearance of evil"), but he uniformly *reads* these as the *thought*/*belief* that p, never pausing over the gap between *its appearing* that p and *believing* that p — the very gap Moss prises open. Cross-pipeline, his near-total silence on *phantasia* is a striking contrast to the **Phantasia Secondary** cluster (Nussbaum, Caston, Frede), and his De-Anima-marginalizes-emotion observation (28 n.1) is a useful datum for that cluster's account of where the *pathē* live in Aristotle's psychology.

## § Significance for the dissertation's Section 5

- **T1-2 (the *doxa*-gate vs. the *phantasia* rival) — the foundational ally, and a precise verdict on its purity.** Fortenbaugh is the historical pedigree of the dissertation's *doxa*-thesis: he is the *locus classicus* for "emotion essentially involves a belief," and he grounds it in *doxa*/*krisis* (belief/judgment), not *phantasia*. **His subject index has no entry for *phantasia*, "imagination," or even "appearance"; the relevant entry is "opinion (thought, belief involved in emotion)," and he glosses the constitutive cognition as "propositional judgments open to the persuasion of reasoned *logos*" (48 n.1).** So the dissertation's *strongest historical ally is purely doxastic* — which cuts both ways. It *confirms* the cognitivist/belief lineage the dissertation claims; but it also means the ally **pre-dates and does not engage the *phantasia* problem**, so it offers no defense against Moss's recalcitrance cases (the foot-wide sun, the rope-snake, fear-at-fiction). Fortenbaugh simply did not see the gap between *appearing-good* and *believing-good*; he reads every *phainomenon* in the *Rhetoric* definitions as a *doxa*. The dissertation therefore inherits from him a belief-thesis that is *exegetically confident but dialectically unarmed* against the phantastic reading — exactly the gap **Dow (AES-05)** later tries to close with "affirmation."
- **The "virtuous action without calculation" doctrine (Ch. 4 §2, 70–75) is the dissertation's best Fortenbaughian resource for a sub-deliberative value-cognition.** His "more courageous man" who, in sudden alarms, "assesses the situation correctly" via a *quick, logos-less* critical act of the well-trained alogical half is the closest cognitivist analogue to the dissertation's *basic hedonic tonality* / *resonant epithymia* tier. But Fortenbaugh names this *judgment/assessment*, not *phantasia* — so the dissertation can cite him for the *existence* of an unreflective evaluative cognition grounding correct emotional response, while noting he does not (cannot, lacking the category) identify it with *phantasia*. This is precisely the **two-tier** seam: where the dissertation might concede the lower tier to Moss's phantastic model, Fortenbaugh stands as the witness that *even the lower, unreflective tier was classically read as cognitive/judgmental* — strengthening the case that the lower tier is *proto-doxastic assessment* rather than belief-independent appearance.
- **The emotion-vs-bodily-drive distinction (Ch. 4 §5, 83–87) directly arms the dissertation's *epithymia* discussion.** Fortenbaugh's rational-vs-non-rational appetite distinction (*Rhet.* 1370a18–25) — and his striking claim that even *sexual desire* is "often caused by thoughts and beliefs," with "judgment arous[ing] the body and not vice versa" — supports the dissertation's claim that some appetitive affects are cognitively articulated and persuasion-responsive, while bare somatic drives are not. This is the cognitivist counter-pressure to a purely phantastic/somatic reading of *epithymia*: it shows the *judgmental* camp already had a principled way to *raise* (not lower) appetite into the cognitive register.
- **Animal-*pathos* (1079 fn) — the *doxa*-based denial.** Fortenbaugh denies emotions, moral virtue, and *akrasia* to animals on the ground that animals lack *doxa* "open to persuasive *logos*" (animal pursuit is mere "quasi-affirmation," *De An.* 431a9–10). This is the **belief-based** rationale for restricting higher affect to rational agents — the exact complement to Moss's **phantasia-based** rationale (beasts have *phantasia* but no *universal*). The dissertation can present these as the two competing grounds (doxastic vs. phantastic) for the same animal-exclusion, and must choose which it adopts.
- **The "effect of emotion on judgment" axis — note the *direction*.** Fortenbaugh's emphasis runs *judgment → emotion* (belief grounds and directs the emotion), not the later literature's *emotion → judgment* (emotion distorts appearance/belief). He registers the reverse direction only glancingly — emotions "upset a man's ability to reason well and judge correctly" (25) and "make men deliberators" (about means: *Rhet.* 1383a5–8). So for the dissertation's *pathetic loop* (1144–1146) and any **emotion-generates-appearance** mechanism (Moss's *Rhet.* II.1 1377b31 reading), Fortenbaugh supplies the *grounding* leg (cognition → emotion) but not the *feedback* leg — confirming that the loop's emotion-distorts-cognition arc is a contribution of the *phantastic* camp, not the founding doxastic one.
