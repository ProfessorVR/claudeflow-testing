# ped-sec-28 — Richardson & Swan, "Examining Social Presence in Online Courses in Relation to Students' Perceived Learning and Satisfaction" (2003)

**Type:** empirical journal article (correlational survey) · **Discipline:** educational technology / distance & online learning (social-presence theory, CMC tradition) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F's foundational empirical instance of the social-presence → perceived-learning/satisfaction predictor claim; the earliest primary study this cluster catalogues in what becomes, fourteen years later, Richardson's own pooled meta-analysis (ped-sec-32).

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Richardson and Swan set out to test, empirically and for the first time in an asynchronous online-course setting, whether students' perceptions of "social presence" — the degree to which participants in mediated communication are experienced as psychologically "real" — relate to and predict their perceived learning and their satisfaction with the instructor (FE, p.68–70). The paper's organizing move is to reroute a standing complaint against online education (that it lacks face-to-face co-location, and is therefore inferior) away from physical presence and toward social presence: the authors argue that what critics actually miss is not bodies in a room but a perceived interpersonal salience that can, in principle, be "cultured" within a purely text-based, asynchronous medium (FE, p.69, citing Gunawardena & Zittle 1997). Four hypotheses operationalize this thesis: (1) social presence relates to perceived learning and instructor satisfaction; (2) social presence predicts perceived learning; (3) gender, age, and credits earned relate to perceived social presence; (4) course activities with higher perceived social presence also show higher perceived learning (FE, p.70).

## 2. Structure

The article runs six named sections. **I. Introduction** (pp.68–70) frames online learning's promoted advantages against the face-to-face critique, then reviews social-presence theory's lineage: Short, Williams & Christie's original medium-salience definition: "the degree of salience of the other person in the (mediated) interaction and the consequent salience of the interpersonal relationships" (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.69, quoting Short/Williams/Christie 1976); Gunawardena & Zittle's "culturing" revision, which relocates part of social presence from the medium itself to communicators' perceptions and behavior within it (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.69); and the adjacent teacher-immediacy literature (Wiener & Mehrabian's psychological-distance construct), which the authors treat as a related, possibly subsumed strand (FE, p.69–70). **II. Methodology** (pp.70–73) states the purpose, four hypotheses, subject pool, survey instrument, procedures, and correlational design. **III. Analyses** (pp.73–76) reports the overall correlations, the predictor regression, the demographic correlations, the per-activity correlations, and the open-ended qualitative data. **IV. Discussion** (pp.76–80) walks each hypothesis in turn, flags a measurement-overlap caveat, and situates the predictor finding against Gunawardena & Zittle and Boverie et al.; a subsection lists limitations. **V. Conclusions** (p.81) argues for a "better" online-course design model that builds in social aspects of learning rather than only delivering content, and calls for further research on the mechanisms and instructor behaviors that convey social presence.

## 3. Constructs

**Social presence / co-presence** is the paper's central and most fully operationalized construct, measured as students' average agreement across a bank of Likert items adapted from Gunawardena & Zittle's scale (e.g., feeling comfortable conversing, forming a sense of online community, having one's point of view acknowledged by other participants) (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.71–72, 85–86). The construct is explicitly treated as a joint property of medium and communicators rather than a fixed technological feature: the authors' review foregrounds Gunawardena & Zittle's finding that social presence "could 'be cultured'" among participants regardless of the medium's inherent capacity (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.69).

**Learning outcomes: retention & transfer**, here operationalized narrowly as *perceived* learning (a self-report Likert composite — "my level of learning that took place in this course was of the highest quality," and per-activity analogues), not measured achievement or retention (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.72, 85–86). This is a load-bearing scope limitation the unit carries forward into its concordance cell and its "Not treated" line below.

**Affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation** appears only via the proximate construct of *instructor satisfaction* — a single overall Likert item, "overall the instructor for this course met my expectations" (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.72, 86) — which the authors themselves flag as a thin, single-item operationalization that may have partially absorbed the social-presence construct given the survey's asymmetric item allocation (two social-presence items directly probe instructor interaction, versus the single satisfaction item) (FE, p.77–78).

**Engagement / flow** is not separately instrumented but is implicated through the qualitative finding that "interaction, feedback, and other students' perspectives" is the most-cited reason (59% of coded responses) students give for finding a course activity beneficial (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.76, 79). The authors read this as evidence that social presence, and the engagement it fosters, "permeates" even activities nominally classified as individual (written assignments, self-tests) rather than only the overtly social ones (discussions, group projects) (FE, p.79).

## 4. Conditions & design

Design: correlational survey, cross-sectional, single administration at course completion; no manipulation, no randomization, and no control condition (an "intact group," per the authors' own limitation statement) (FE, p.80). Setting: Empire State College's (ESC) fully online, asynchronous, text-based courses delivered on the SUNY Learning Network (SLN) template, spring 2000 — a single medium throughout, with no comparison arm against face-to-face, video, or any richer-media condition (FE, p.70–71). Sample: n=97 completers (of 369 enrolled, 26% response rate after a second mailing), 63% female, majority age 36–45 (range 19–63), 57% junior/senior standing by credits earned, and a nontraditional/returning-student population that the authors explicitly flag as limiting generalizability to traditional undergraduates (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.71, 80). Instrument: a modified Gunawardena & Zittle social-presence scale plus demographic items, 16 overall six-point Likert items (social presence, perceived learning, instructor satisfaction), 12 per-activity variables across six course-activity types (class discussions/Q&A, group projects, individual projects, self-tests/module tests/final exams, written assignments, lectures/notes/readings), and two open-ended questions (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.71–72). Analysis: zero-order Pearson correlations (overall and per-activity), one standard direct-entry regression (social presence → perceived learning), and frequency/percentage coding of the open-ended responses (FE, p.73). Per plan §5/task instruction, Strand F is not scoped to populate the media-condition axis: this is a within-medium (asynchronous online-text) study with no media contrast built into the design, so no media-condition cell genuinely applies regardless of scope — no media-condition axis content.

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Overall perceived social presence correlates .68 with overall perceived learning (r²=.46) and .60 with instructor satisfaction (r²=.36); instructor satisfaction correlates .73 with perceived learning (r²=.53); all p<.05 (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.73).
- A standard direct-entry regression confirms social presence as a significant predictor of perceived learning: F=78.83, df=1,93, p<.05, R²=.46 (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.74). ****** UNVERIFIED: the source text prints "df=1, 931" for this regression, which is very likely an OCR/typesetting artifact of "df=1, 93" given n=95 in the correlation table two paragraphs above; carried here as printed in the extraction, flagged rather than silently corrected.
- Gender correlates .219 with overall perceived social presence (R²=.047, p<.05), with women reporting higher social presence than men; age and college credits earned show no significant correlation with social presence (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.74).
- Per-activity social-presence/perceived-learning correlations range from .83 (class discussions/Q&A, r²=.69) and .80 (group projects, r²=.64) down through .55 (individual projects), .50 (self-tests/module tests), .46 (written assignments), to .40 (lectures/notes/readings, r²=.16) — all significant at p<.01 (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.74–75). The authors read the persistence of a significant, if attenuated, correlation even in nominally "individual" activities as evidence that social presence permeates the whole course rather than only its overtly collaborative segments (FE, p.75).
- Qualitative open-ended data converge with the quantitative pattern: written assignments (31%) and class discussions (25%) are the two most-cited "most beneficial" activities, and 59% of stated reasons for that benefit name interaction, feedback, or other students' perspectives (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.76).
- The authors triangulate their predictor finding against Gunawardena & Zittle's 58%-of-satisfaction-variance figure and against Boverie, Nagel, McGee & Garcia's finding that, among learning-style, emotional-intelligence, and social-presence predictors, only social presence significantly predicted distance-education satisfaction (FAITHFUL-Richardson&Swan, p.78–79).
- A measurement-overlap caveat is registered but not resolved: because the survey carried a single instructor-satisfaction item against multiple social-presence items (two of which directly probe instructor interaction), the authors cannot rule out that their social-presence score partly absorbed the satisfaction construct rather than merely correlating with it (FE, p.77).
- Conclusion: sociocognitive theory (Vygotsky; cooperative-learning literature) plus these findings jointly argue for a "better" online-course model integrating social aspects of learning into design and instruction, not only content delivery (FE, p.81).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit is Richardson's own early, single-study precursor to the pooled synthesis the cluster catalogues at ped-sec-32 (Richardson, Maeda, Lv & Caskurlu, 2017): where this 2003 study establishes the social-presence-predicts-perceived-learning claim on n=97 within one institution's asynchronous course template, ped-sec-32 revisits and quantifies that same claim across the intervening fourteen years of accumulated studies, and Part III should read the two units as a before/after pair on the same predictor relationship rather than as independent findings *(anticipatory-application)*. This unit also directly triangulates against, and adapts the instrument of, ped-sec-25 (Gunawardena & Zittle, 1997): the 58%-satisfaction-variance figure this unit cites as corroboration is ped-sec-25's own headline finding, and the social-presence scale used here is ped-sec-25's scale modified for the SLN template — Part III citing either unit for "the" social-presence-predicts-satisfaction claim should note the direct methodological lineage rather than treating them as independently converging studies *(anticipatory-application)*. Structurally, this unit bridges to `vle-01-deployment-survey`'s F7 finding ("Presence & the WE"), which reports a parallel pattern in Part III's own survey — self-reported presence-of-others tracking engagement/satisfaction-adjacent outcomes — but the bridge is a conceptual, not a media, match: vle-01 is IVR/desktop-3D deployment-survey data, while this unit is asynchronous, text-based, entirely non-immersive online coursework, so any citation joining the two should hold the media gap explicit rather than implying equivalence *(anticipatory-application)*. Finally, the paper's own measurement-overlap caveat (social presence and instructor-satisfaction items may not be cleanly separable given the survey's item-allocation asymmetry) is a methodological caution worth carrying into any Part III instrument design that pairs a rich multi-item construct against a single-item outcome measure *(anticipatory-application)*.

**Not treated:** presence (spatial/physical), immersion (technological), agency/interactivity, embodiment/self-representation, cognitive load (extraneous vs. germane), situational interest, self-efficacy, cybersickness & physical discomfort, and representational fidelity — none of these constructs appear in this pre-VR, text-based asynchronous CMC study, which predates the desktop-3D/IVR media-comparison literature this cluster's Strand C catalogues by more than a decade.
