# ped-sec-25 — Gunawardena & Zittle, "Social Presence as a Predictor of Satisfaction within a Computer-Mediated Conferencing Environment" (1997)

**Type:** journal article (empirical, correlational) · **Discipline:** distance education / computer-mediated communication (educational technology) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F foundational hub — origin of the Gunawardena Social Presence Scale (SPRES), a CMC-context alternative to Short, Williams & Christie's (1976) semantic-differential presence measure, and the cluster's primary grounding source for the "social presence / co-presence" construct-axis row.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Gunawardena and Zittle argue that "social presence" — the degree to which a communicator is perceived as a "real person" in mediated interaction — is not merely a property that text-based computer-mediated communication (CMC) categorically lacks relative to richer media, but an empirically measurable predictor of learner satisfaction *within* a text-only medium, provided the construct is operationalized with an instrument built for the CMC context rather than borrowed wholesale from face-to-face or broadcast-media research (FE, pp. 8–9). The paper's stated purpose is narrower and more instrumental than a general theory of presence: to test whether social presence predicts overall satisfaction in an inter-university academic computer conference, and, in doing so, to introduce and report reliability data for a new fourteen-item social-presence scale designed specifically for group-based CMC rather than dyadic broadcast communication (FE, p. 11–12). A secondary, more exploratory aim — introduced almost as an afterthought in the purpose statement but developed at length in the results — is to examine whether participants' use of emoticons interacts with perceived social presence to shape satisfaction (FE, p. 12).

## 2. Structure

The article follows a standard empirical-report shape, but its introduction does substantial theoretical labor before any data appear.

1. **Introduction** (pp. 8–9) — situates CMC research within a "socio-psychological perspective," and adopts Spears and Lea's (1992) taxonomy of three competing "social cues perspective" frameworks — Short, Williams & Christie's (1976) social presence model, Rutter's (1987) cuelessness model, and Kiesler, Siegel & McGuire's (1984) reduced-social-cues approach — selecting social presence as the most influential and the one this study will operationalize (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, p. 8).
2. **Definition of Social Presence** (pp. 9–10) — presents Short, Williams and Christie's original definition and its grounding in Argyle and Dean's (1965) "intimacy" and Wiener and Mehrabian's (1968) "immediacy," treating social presence there as chiefly a property of the medium's bandwidth for nonverbal cues (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, p. 9).
3. **Research on Social Presence** (pp. 9–11) — reviews face-to-face and interactive-television (ITV) immediacy-outcome literature, then turns to CMC-specific findings (Walther 1992; Baym 1995) that complicate a simple bandwidth-determines-presence story, and introduces Rafaeli's (1988, 1990) distinction between social presence (subjective) and interactivity (an actualized property of a communication sequence) (FE, p. 10).
4. **The Need for this Study / The Purpose of this Study** (pp. 11–12) — identifies the gap the paper fills: no prior work had measured social presence's effect on satisfaction specifically within CMC, nor built a CMC-context social-presence scale; argues that because CMC decenters the instructor into a facilitator role, presence should be measured from a group, not teacher-centered, perspective (FE, p. 11).
5. **Method** (pp. 11–16) — describes the GlobalEd inter-university computer conference, participants (N=50), the 61-item GlobalEd Questionnaire and its nine Likert subscales, and the fourteen-item Social Presence scale and ten-item Satisfaction scale used as the study's key measures.
6. **Results** (pp. 16–20) — reports the bi-polar convergent-validity correlations, the stepwise regression predicting satisfaction, and two ancillary findings (a nonsignificant Equal–Satisfaction correlation and an emoticon-by-presence interaction pattern).
7. **Discussion / Conclusion** (pp. 21–24) — interprets social presence's predictive strength as extending Hackman and Walker's (1990) ITV finding into the CMC context, situates the study within broader European CMC research programs, and closes with design recommendations and calls for future research on cognitive learning outcomes and cross-cultural replication.

## 3. Constructs

**Social presence / co-presence** is this paper's central and namesake construct, and the paper is the origin point of an instrument (the Gunawardena Social Presence Scale, "SPRES") still cited across the CMC-education literature. Short, Williams and Christie's (1976) original definition — "the degree of salience of the other person in the interaction and the consequent salience of the interpersonal relationships" (quoted at p. 9, original p. 65) — is retained as the theoretical anchor, but Gunawardena and Zittle depart from it operationally: where the 1976 model measures presence via bi-polar semantic-differential ratings of the medium itself (personal/impersonal, warm/cold), SPRES instead asks participants fourteen Likert-scaled questions about their own experience of "immediacy" within a specific group conference — comfort conversing and introducing oneself, sense of "online community," feeling one's viewpoint was acknowledged, and ability to form distinct impressions of other participants through text alone (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, pp. 13–15, Table 2). The paper treats the two operationalizations as convergent rather than competing: six of the original bi-polar items are retained specifically to correlate against SPRES as a validity check (r = .52–.87, p < .001), with the strongest correlation on the Personal/Impersonal pair (r = .87) (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, pp. 16–17, Figure 1).

Three further constructs receive lighter but non-trivial treatment. **Self-efficacy** appears narrowly as the single-item "Capable" subscale — participants' confidence in mastering CMC (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, p. 13) — which is measured but does not survive into the final predictive model. **Agency / interactivity** is treated both instrumentally, via the single-item "Equal" subscale (perceived equal opportunity to participate), which does enter the final regression model, and theoretically, via Rafaeli's (1988, 1990) distinction between social presence as a subjective perception and interactivity as an actualized, structural property of a communication sequence — a distinction the paper cites but does not itself operationalize as a separate scale (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, p. 10). Finally, the study's actual dependent variable, **satisfaction** (the ten-item "Sat" scale), has no dedicated row in the cluster's §3 construct axis; its nearest controlled-vocabulary neighbor is "affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation," and that mapping should be read as adjacent rather than exact — satisfaction here bundles perceived learning, motivation to continue participating, and valuation of the conference as a whole, not enjoyment alone (P/anticipatory-application; flagged, not resolved, in this unit).

## 4. Conditions & design

This is a Strand F (CMC social-presence tradition) unit, and its design does not populate the cluster's §3 media-condition axis: that axis's columns (IVR/HMD, desktop-3D, 2D video, screen-based simulation, conventional-instruction control, home-vs-classroom, and so on) were built for the media-comparison and simulation literatures of Strands C/D, and none genuinely fits a single-medium correlational CMC study. There is no media contrast in this design — no group used a different modality — so no cell of that axis is populated by this unit, and that omission is deliberate rather than an oversight (per plan scope, Strand F units are not required to populate the media-condition axis).

What the study does offer, described here rather than mapped to a column: the empirical setting is the Fall 1993 "GlobalEd" inter-university computer conference, a text-based, asynchronous, learner-centered collaborative CMC forum running the length of a semester across (at core) six graduate-education programs — San Diego State, Texas A&M, University of New Mexico, University of Oklahoma, University of Wisconsin-Madison, and University of Wyoming, with informal participation from two further institutions — totaling approximately ninety participants (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, p. 11–12). The analytical sample, however, was N=50 respondents across five of those universities (San Diego State n=8, Texas A&M n=11, New Mexico n=14, Wisconsin-Madison n=7, Wyoming n=10), 62% female, mean age 40, roughly 88% Caucasian, drawn from a post-conference paper-and-pencil questionnaire (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, pp. 12–13). The instrument was the 61-item GlobalEd Questionnaire (developed by Gunawardena), of which fifty-two five-point Likert items across nine subscales/single items were used for this analysis (Spres, Active, Attitude, Barriers, Capable, Equal, Trained, Tech, Sat), plus six bi-polar items retained for the SPRES validity check (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, pp. 12–14). Reported internal-consistency reliabilities (Cronbach's alpha) were strong for the scales this cluster cares most about — SPRES α=.88 (14 items), Sat α=.87 (10 items), Tech α=.83 (3 items), Attitude α=.74 (2 items), Barriers α=.72 (2 items) (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, p. 14, Table 1). The analytic method was stepwise multiple regression (SPSS 7.5) predicting Sat from the eight subscale/single-item predictors, correlational rather than experimental throughout — there is no manipulated independent variable and no control condition.

## 5. Findings/Claims

- The bi-polar convergent-validity check strongly supports SPRES as a valid operationalization of Short, Williams and Christie's (1976) social-presence construct: correlations between SPRES and six "intimacy" bi-polar indicators ranged r=.52–.87 (all p<.001) (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, pp. 16–17).
- An initial eight-predictor stepwise regression converged on a four-predictor model (Spres, Equal, Tech, Attitude) explaining over 75% of variance in satisfaction, but the authors flag this model as compromised by response dropout shrinking the effective sample (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, p. 18).
- Re-running the regression restricted to the three strongest predictors from that first pass yields the paper's reported final model (Table 4): Spres, Equal, and Tech jointly explain 70.8% of variance in satisfaction (R=.841, R²=.708, F(3,30)=24.22, p<.001), with social presence alone contributing approximately 60 percentage points of that variance, Equal roughly 7, and Tech roughly 4 (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, pp. 18–19, Table 4). ****** UNVERIFIED: the paper's own abstract rounds this final model's variance to "about 68%," which does not match the R²=.708 reported in Table 4 for the same three-predictor model; this is an inconsistency internal to the source text itself, not a transcription choice made in this unit.
- An exploratory, non-significant negative correlation between Equal and Sat (r=-.20) is examined via scatterplot: at low social presence, respondents who rated equal-participation-opportunity low nonetheless rated satisfaction higher than those who rated Equal high; this difference disappears at higher social presence, which the authors read as a possible interaction effect worth future study rather than a settled finding (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, pp. 19–20).
- Emoticon use shows no relationship to satisfaction at low levels of social presence, but an increasingly positive relationship as social presence rises — glossed as a "rich-get-richer" compensatory pattern, following Steinfield's (1986) finding that frequent social users of e-mail did not perceive it as low in social presence (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, pp. 21–22).
- Consistent with Walther's (1992) social-information-processing perspective, GlobalEd participants agreed that later-conference messages were more social and personal than earlier ones (Mean 4.15/5), supporting the view that CMC's depersonalizing reputation may reflect only its initial interactions (FAITHFUL-Gunawardena-Zittle, p. 22–23).
- The authors conclude social presence is a strong satisfaction predictor even in a low-bandwidth, text-only medium, extending Hackman and Walker's (1990) ITV-context immediacy finding into CMC, and they close with a design recommendation: academic CMC conferences should be deliberately designed to cultivate social presence, since — per Johansen, Vallee and Spangler (1988) — presence "can be cultured" rather than being fixed by medium bandwidth alone (FE, p. 23).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* As a foundational hub, this unit's most direct value to Part III is definitional: it supplies a validated, group-perspective operationalization of "social presence" that can be cited whenever the dissertation needs to distinguish perceived co-presence of *other people* from the spatial/physical presence (of an environment) that dominates the cluster's Strand A sources. The bridge to the VLE entry, however, must be stated carefully rather than overstated. Vle-01's finding F7 ("Presence & the WE": a 4.3 vs. 1.2 per-100 gap in presence-coded language between app-users and video-only respondents, alongside optative statements like "seemed isolating" and "watch with others" as a stated pro) is thematically adjacent to what SPRES measures — but the VLE's own fourteen-item instrument analogue does not exist: Q7/Q8 are a forced-choice able-to-use / immersion item, not a validated multi-item social-presence scale, and F7's evidence is open-text coding of unprompted remarks, not scale responses. This unit's own instrument therefore identifies a genuine gap in the VLE's design rather than a match to it: Gunawardena and Zittle's fourteen SPRES items (sense of online community, feeling one's viewpoint was acknowledged, ability to form distinct impressions of co-participants) would be a natural template for a future VLE presence instrument, but no such instrument currently exists in the corpus, and vle-03's "watch with others" and "seemed isolating" exemplar quotes should be read as suggestive, not confirmatory, of an SPRES-type effect *(anticipatory-application)*. As a citation hub, this paper is confirmed cited within this cluster by ped-sec-26 (Garrison, Anderson & Archer 1999), whose own Social Presence section cites "Gunawardena & Zittle (1997) on emoticon use" directly and lists Gunawardena among its pre-1999 interlocutors (FE, per ped-sec-26). Given the shared CMC/online-learning social-presence lineage this cluster's Strand F was built to trace, this unit is also expected to be cited by later Strand-F units still pending build at the time of this writing — plausibly Tu (2002, ped-sec-27) and Biocca et al. (2003, ped-sec-29) — a probable intra-cluster hub role to be confirmed at the Phase 3 citation-network wave *(anticipatory-application)*. Finally, the paper's own primary interlocutor is Short, Williams and Christie (1976): Gunawardena and Zittle do not contest that model's core definition of social presence, but they do relocate its site of measurement — from the medium's bandwidth properties to participants' self-reported group experience — a methodological shift this cluster should track wherever later Strand-F sources (which may themselves depart further from Short/Williams/Christie toward Garrison et al.'s context-dependent view) are read against this paper.

**Not treated:** presence (spatial/physical) in the Strand A/immersive-VR sense; immersion (technological); embodiment/self-representation; cognitive load; situational interest; engagement/flow as a named construct; learning outcomes of any kind (the authors explicitly flag cognitive/objective learning outcomes as unaddressed and reserved for future research); cybersickness and physical discomfort; representational fidelity. This is a text-only CMC study with no visual, spatial, or embodied dimension of any kind.
