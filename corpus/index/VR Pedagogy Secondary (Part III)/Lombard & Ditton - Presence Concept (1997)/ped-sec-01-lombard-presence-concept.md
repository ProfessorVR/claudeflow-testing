# ped-sec-01 — Lombard & Ditton, "At the Heart of It All: The Concept of Presence" (1997)

**Type:** theory/foundations review article (media psychology / communication) · **Discipline:** communication (presence research) · **Depth:** deep (OCR-LOW; read directly from PDF in ≤10pp chunks, no `pdftotext`) · **Corpus role:** Strand A's founding definitional source — the cluster's single most cited-by-others presence paper, supplying the six-conceptualization taxonomy and the "perceptual illusion of nonmediation" definition against which every later presence-measurement and media-comparison unit in this cluster (Witmer & Singer, Chow, and the entire Strand-C battery) implicitly or explicitly positions itself.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Lombard and Ditton's aim is definitional and synthetic rather than empirical: presence — the perceptual illusion that a mediated experience is not mediated — is, in their diagnosis, both practically and theoretically important yet remains fragmentary and unsystematic as a research object because scholars in communication, psychology, computer science, engineering, philosophy, and the arts have each approached it from within a separate literature, using separate vocabularies, with no shared operational core (FE, pdf p.2–4). The article's stated three-part task is to (a) review the field's several conceptualizations of presence and provide a single unifying explication, (b) synthesize existing evidence and speculation about presence's causes and effects, and (c) recommend the attributes of a systematic future research program (FE, pdf p.5). The practical warrant offered is that an enhanced sense of presence is central to the usefulness and profitability of emerging technologies (virtual reality, simulation rides, home theater, HDTV, videoconferencing); the theoretical warrant is that researchers routinely use mediated stimuli as a convenience substitute for nonmediated stimuli when studying psychological and physiological processes, an assumption whose validity depends on understanding presence (FE, pdf p.3–4).

## 2. Structure

The article runs seven unlabeled-by-number but internally announced movements across its 47 pages of body text (pp.48–69 of the printout are the reference list and are not treated as source content here).

1. **Introduction** (pdf p.2–4) — catalogues the presence-producing technologies of the mid-1990s (VR, simulation rides, IMAX, videoconferencing, "talking" computers); states the practical and theoretical rationale for studying presence; previews the article's three-part task.
2. **Concept Explication — six conceptualizations** (pdf p.4–14) — reviews, in turn, presence as (1) social richness, (2) realism, (3) transportation (with three subtypes: "you are there," "it is here," "we are together"), (4) immersion, (5) social actor within medium, and (6) medium as social actor, citing each conceptualization's own operational measures where they exist.
3. **Presence Explicated** (pdf p.14–16) — synthesizes the six conceptualizations into the article's formal unifying definition: the perceptual illusion of nonmediation, occurring via one of two mechanisms (invisible/transparent medium, or medium transformed into a social entity); frames presence as a property of the person, occurring in discrete instants rather than continuous degree.
4. **Causes of Presence as Invisible Medium** (pdf p.16–35) — the article's longest section; surveys form variables (sensory-output breadth/depth, visual-display characteristics, aural characteristics, stimuli for other senses, interactivity, obtrusiveness, live-vs-recorded status, number of people), content variables (social realism, media conventions, task/activity nature), and media-user variables (willingness to suspend disbelief, knowledge/experience, other user traits).
5. **Causes of Presence as Transformed Medium** (pdf p.35–38) — the parallel, shorter causal survey for the second mechanism: form variables (interactivity, use of voice, medium size/shape), content variables (social realism, media conventions, task/activity), and user variables (knowledge/experience, age, gender, personality).
6. **Effects of Presence** (pdf p.38–45) — physiological effects of presence as invisible medium (arousal; vection and simulation sickness; other physiological changes), psychological effects of presence as invisible medium (enjoyment, involvement, task performance, skills training, desensitization, persuasion, memory and social judgment, parasocial interaction), and briefly, psychological effects of presence as transformed medium (perceived computer "personality," gender-stereotyped responses to computers).
7. **Future Presence Research** (pdf p.45–47) — the article's conclusion; calls for standardized conceptual/operational definitions, a combined subjective (questionnaire) and objective (physiological/behavioral) measurement instrument, and standardized manipulation of causal variables; closes with a set of open, deliberately unresolved philosophical questions about whether presence is a good thing, whether it can be excessive, and what evolutionary or social function the human desire for presence serves.

## 3. Constructs

Lombard & Ditton is a definitional hub: it either originates or gives an early, citable synthesis of ten of this cluster's fourteen construct-axis rows (§3 of the plan). Each entry below normalizes the source's own term to the canonical axis label.

- **presence (spatial/physical)** — the article's core object. Its own "you are there" and "it is here" transportation subtypes are the clearest instances of the axis's spatial sense: the user transported to a mediated place, or objects/people from elsewhere transported into the user's own environment (FAITHFUL-Lombard&Ditton, pdf p.7–9).
- **social presence / co-presence** — treated under the article's own labels "presence as social richness" (Short, Williams & Christie's social presence theory; media richness theory) and the "we are together" (shared space) transportation subtype, in which two or more communicators are jointly transported to a place they share (FAITHFUL-Lombard&Ditton, pdf p.5–6, 10).
- **immersion (technological)** — the article's own "presence as immersion" conceptualization, split into a perceptual component (the objective degree to which a medium submerges the user's sensory system — HMD, headphones, gloves) and a psychological component (involvement, absorption, engrossment) (FAITHFUL-Lombard&Ditton, pdf p.10–11).
- **agency / interactivity** — treated at length as a primary causal variable for both mechanisms of presence, via five subsidiary dimensions: number of user inputs accepted, number/type of presentation characteristics modifiable, range of possible change, correspondence between input and response, and response speed (FAITHFUL-Lombard&Ditton, pdf p.27–29, 35).
- **representational fidelity** — the article's own "presence as realism" conceptualization, explicitly split into perceptual realism (objects/people look, sound, or feel like the real thing) and social realism (the portrayal is plausible or "true to life," independent of perceptual fidelity) (FAITHFUL-Lombard&Ditton, pdf p.6–7, 31–32).
- **cybersickness & physical discomfort** — treated under "vection and simulation sickness," a physiological effect of presence combined with the illusion of self-motion: dizziness, eyestrain, disorientation, dysphoria, standing/walking unsteadiness, nausea (FAITHFUL-Lombard&Ditton, pdf p.39–40).
- **affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation** — "enjoyment" is named "perhaps the most prominent psychological impact of presence," linked to the commercial popularity of presence-evoking technologies, though the article notes remarkably little research had directly tested the presence→enjoyment link at time of writing (FE, pdf p.40).
- **engagement / flow** — treated as "involvement": Heeter's (1995) factor-analytic survey of 312 users of the BattleTech VR-entertainment system is reported as finding involvement (fun, exciting, competitive, addictive, intense) the highest-rated factor (8.7/10) among several (FAITHFUL-Lombard&Ditton, pdf p.40–41).
- **learning outcomes: procedural / skills** — treated under "skills training": flight-simulator training and an Army Research Institute virtual-navigation study are cited as evidence that high-presence media (VR) improve trained performance over lower-presence alternatives (photographs, textbooks), though the article is careful to flag open questions about whether this beats conventional training and about simulator-to-real-world transfer risk (FAITHFUL-Lombard&Ditton, pdf p.42).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — treated under "memory and social judgment": presence-enhancing viewing conditions (larger screen, Dolby surround sound) are linked in Ditton's own 1997 study to a greater rate of source-monitoring error, in which information from fictional mediated scenes is misattributed and unknowingly applied to real-world social judgments (FAITHFUL-Lombard&Ditton, pdf p.43–44).

Two further conceptualizations are treated but sit outside the concordance's construct rows as theory-models/terms rather than constructs in their own right: **parasocial interaction** (presence as "social actor within medium," after Horton & Wohl 1956 — users respond to social cues from personas within a medium as though the interaction were mutual) and **CASA, "computers are social actors"** (presence as "medium as social actor," after Reeves & Nass's Stanford research program — users respond to social cues from the medium itself). Both are foundational to later human-computer-interaction and social-presence literatures but are recorded here as concepts rather than concordance-axis rows.

## 4. Conditions & design

No media-condition axis content — conceptual/definitional paper. Lombard & Ditton conduct no original data collection; the article is a literature synthesis, and its own empirical contribution is limited to two prior studies the authors briefly summarize (Lombard, Reich, Grabe, Campanella & Ditton 1995 on screen size and vection; Ditton 1997 on screen size/sound and source-monitoring memory errors), neither of which is a VR-vs-desktop-vs-2D media-comparison design of the kind Strand C later runs. The article does, however, discuss specific media forms extensively as illustrative loci for its causal-variable taxonomy — head-mounted-display VR, videoconferencing/Distributed Virtual Environments, television, IMAX, and simulation rides recur throughout the causes-of-presence sections — and these are recorded in the concordance feed below as the closest approximation to media-condition content this source offers, with the caveat that they are the authors' synthesis of others' scattered findings rather than a designed comparison.

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Presence is formally defined as "the perceptual illusion of nonmediation": a person's failure to perceive or acknowledge the existence of a medium in the communication environment, responding as though the medium were not there (FE, pdf p.14–15).
- This illusion occurs via one of two mechanisms: the medium becomes perceptually invisible or transparent, functioning as an "open window" onto content that shares the user's environment; or the medium is perceptually transformed into an independent social entity (FE, pdf p.15).
- Presence is a property of the person rather than the medium, though it results from an interaction of media-form characteristics, media-content characteristics, and media-user characteristics; it does not occur in continuous degree but either does or does not occur at any given instant of media use, with subjective "greater or lesser" presence attributable to the number of such instants during an experience (FE, pdf p.16).
- All six conceptualizations of presence reviewed in the literature — social richness, realism, transportation, immersion, social actor within medium, and medium as social actor — are claimed to be subsumed by this unifying definition, each representing one or more aspects of the perceptual illusion of nonmediation (FE, pdf p.14–16).
- Causes of presence divide systematically into form, content, and user-characteristic variables, and this three-way division is applied separately to each of the two mechanisms (invisible medium; transformed medium) — yielding a six-cell causal framework (FE, pdf p.16–38).
- Effects of presence are both physiological (arousal, vection/simulation sickness, flinching/ducking reflexes, transient after-effects such as reduced eye-hand coordination) and psychological (enjoyment, involvement, task performance, skills-training benefit, desensitization to violent/sexual content, enhanced persuasion, memory/source-monitoring effects, and intensified parasocial interaction) (FE, pdf p.38–44).
- The article closes by explicitly declaring the research program to be in its infancy and specifying what a systematic program requires: standardized conceptual and operational definitions; a combined subjective (self-report questionnaire) and objective (physiological, behavioral) measurement instrument; and standardized manipulation of causal variables — illustrated by a cautionary example in which prior image-size studies used inconsistent, even contradictory, size labels across studies, undermining comparability (FE, pdf p.45–46).
- The article ends not with a claim but with a deliberately open set of questions: is presence necessarily good; can technologies be designed to let users control its intensity; can presence contribute to dissociative or psychotic symptoms; is the desire for presence rooted in an evolved threat-detection response; and will increasingly presence-rich mediated interaction with real people break down social stereotypes or merely extend them (FE, pdf p.46–47).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* As Strand A's foundational hub, this unit's chief service to the dissertation is vocabulary discipline: whenever Part III or the VLE entry invokes "presence" as if it were one construct, this source supplies the resources to specify which of the six conceptualizations is actually in play — spatial transportation, social richness/co-presence, immersion, or parasocial/CASA responses to the medium itself — rather than letting the term do undifferentiated work *(anticipatory-application)*. The "we are together" transportation subtype and the presence-as-social-richness conceptualization together supply the theoretical vocabulary for vle-01's F7 finding (Presence & the WE: 4.3 vs 1.2 per-100 for APP vs VID strata; the E-SHA optative "watch with others" requests): in this vocabulary, what the video-only stratum lacks is not presence in general but specifically the co-presence/shared-space subtype, a more precise diagnosis than "less immersive" alone would give *(anticipatory-application)*. Separately, the article's "obtrusiveness of medium" cause — installer glitches and malfunctions that remind the user the experience is mediated — offers a presence-theoretic reading of vle-01's F1 (threshold foreclosure) and F2 (friction scorecard) findings: install/lag/crash frictions are, in this vocabulary, obtrusive-medium events that foreclose presence before it can be established, rather than merely usability defects incidental to the pedagogical content *(anticipatory-application)*. The six-cell causal taxonomy (form/content/user × invisible/transformed medium) also gives Part III a structured checklist for explaining why a desktop-3D deployment should be expected to produce weaker presence effects than a hypothetical IVR deployment — sensory breadth/depth, field-of-view, obtrusiveness, and interactivity all differ systematically between the two conditions, and each is individually named here *(anticipatory-application)*. Finally, the article's own closing ambivalence — open questions about whether presence is unconditionally desirable, whether it can be excessive, and its association with desensitization and source-monitoring memory errors — is available as an early caution against treating presence-maximization as an unqualified pedagogical good when Part III weighs the design tradeoffs of higher- versus lower-presence VLE deployment *(anticipatory-application)*.
