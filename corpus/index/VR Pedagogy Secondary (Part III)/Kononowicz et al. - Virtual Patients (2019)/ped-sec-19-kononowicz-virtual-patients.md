# ped-sec-19 — Kononowicz et al., "Virtual Patient Simulations in Health Professions Education: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis" (2019)

**Type:** systematic review + random-effects meta-analysis (health professions education) · **Discipline:** medical/health-professions education, Digital Health Education Collaboration (DHEC) series · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand D's screen-based simulation-outcomes anchor; the review-level counterweight to Strand D's single-study units, and the primary in-cluster source for debate axis 6 (simulation-vs-no-intervention strength vs. sim-vs-sim negligibility).

## 1. Thesis/Aim

This Cochrane-methodology systematic review and meta-analysis, produced by the Digital Health Education Collaboration (DHEC), evaluates the effectiveness of screen-based virtual patient (VP) simulations in pre-/post-registration health professions education across four comparisons: VP versus traditional education, VP-blended-learning versus traditional education, VP versus other digital education, and VP design-variant comparisons (FE, p.1–2). Its central methodological commitment, sustained through the closing Outlook section, is to redirect the field's evidentiary appetite away from media-comparative research — comparing VP against traditional-education formats — which the authors judge, following Friedman and Cook, to be largely uninterpretable because such comparisons cannot control for the many confounds bundled inside "format," and toward theory-grounded, adequately powered design-variant studies asking which internal features of a virtual patient (feedback type, narrative versus problem-solving structure, scaffolding, spacing) actually drive learning (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.13).

## 2. Structure

- **Background & Introduction** (p.1–2) — defines virtual patients as interactive digital simulations of clinical scenarios; situates their pedagogy in Kolb's experiential learning theory; explicitly excludes 3D immersive virtual-learning-environment VP variants as out of scope, deferred to a companion DHEC review on virtual reality (cited internally as ref [13]) (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.2).
- **Methods** (p.3–4) — Cochrane methodology, PRISMA reporting; searched 7 databases from 1990 to September 2018, no language restriction; RCTs and cluster RCTs only, crossover trials excluded; a priori excludes 2-arm VP-versus-no-intervention comparisons because an earlier meta-analysis had already established a large effect there (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.3).
- **Results** (p.4–11) — study characteristics (51 trials, N=4,696); outcome-by-outcome synthesis for knowledge, skills, attitudes, and satisfaction; secondary outcomes (cost, patient outcomes, adverse effects); subgroup analysis and albatross plots; risk-of-bias assessment.
- **Discussion** (p.12–13) — principal findings interpreted through Bloom's taxonomy; limitations; comparison with prior meta-analyses (Cook et al., Consorti et al.); Outlook, joining the call to abandon media-comparative research.
- **Conclusions** (p.13–14).

## 3. Constructs

- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — treated via the "knowledge" outcome category: pooled SMD=0.11 (95% CI −0.17 to 0.39, I²=74%, n=927), indicating no significant advantage for VP over traditional education (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.6).
- **learning outcomes: procedural / skills** — treated via the "skills" outcome category: pooled SMD=0.90 (95% CI 0.49 to 1.32, I²=88%, n=897), a moderate-to-large advantage, concentrated in clinical reasoning, procedural skills, and mixed procedural/team skills (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.6–7).
- **self-efficacy** — treated within "attitudes" (confidence, preparedness, comfort, perceived ability across topics from history-taking to opioid therapy); mixed evidence, and notably one study found self-assessed confidence *lower* in the VP group despite no corresponding deficit — and sometimes a surplus — in objective performance (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.8, 12).
- **affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation** — treated within "satisfaction," specifically its "comfort in use" sub-dimension (engagement/motivation, positive climate or safety, enjoyment/pleasure); most item-by-item satisfaction comparisons were indifferent between VP and control groups rather than clearly favorable (FE, p.8–9).
- **representational fidelity** — treated obliquely through the design-variant literature the review aggregates: Friedman et al.'s [96] contrast of a "pedagogic" (guided, menu-based) VP format against a "high-fidelity" (free-text, unguided) format found the *less* maximally realistic, more guided format winning on both knowledge and satisfaction — a counter-intuitive result against a naive "more fidelity is better" prior — while Dankbaar et al.'s [88] discussion section separately hypothesizes that high-fidelity VP may raise motivation while also proving more distracting for novices (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.7, 9, 11).

Constructs from the cluster's row axis that this source does *not* operationalize are named in the closing "Not treated" line.

## 4. Conditions & design

The review's four comparison arms map directly onto the cluster's media-condition columns, all instantiating **screen-based virtual patient / sim**: 25 studies compared VP with traditional education [53–77]; 11 compared VP-blended-learning with traditional education [50,52,78–86]; 5 compared VP with other digital education [51,87–90]; and 10 compared different VP design variants [91–100] — 51 RCTs/cRCTs total, N=4,696, drawn from 44,054 initially screened citations (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.1, 4–5).

The **conventional instruction (control)** column is itself internally differentiated: traditional-education controls were reading assignments (6 studies), lectures (4), group assignments (4), mannequin-based training (4), standardized patients (1), ward-based education (1), and mixed forms (5) (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.5). This internal differentiation becomes analytically load-bearing: a post hoc split of controls into "active" (group discussion, mannequin-based training) versus "passive" (lectures, reading) forms, visualized via albatross plots after none of the fifteen pre-registered subgroup analyses explained the study heterogeneity, showed VP producing large positive effects against passive controls but small or even negative effects against active controls (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.10, 12).

The **review / meta-analytic aggregate** column is the review's own apparatus: random-effects pooling in Review Manager 5.3, I² heterogeneity statistics, GRADE-based quality-of-evidence downgrading, and Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment across seven bias domains, with an overall verdict of low-to-modest, mixed-quality evidence (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.4, 11).

Two exclusions bound the review's scope and are analytically consequential for this cluster. First, VP-versus-no-intervention comparisons were deliberately excluded because an earlier VP-specific meta-analysis (Cook, Erwin & Triola — cited internally as ref [38], **not** the technology-enhanced-simulation meta-analysis at ped-sec-15, which is a distinct paper by an overlapping but not identical Cook-led author group) had already shown a large effect there (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.3). Second, 3D-immersive virtual patients are explicitly excluded and deferred to a companion DHEC review on virtual reality, meaning this unit's own construct-under-condition cells never populate the IVR (HMD) or desktop-3D columns directly (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.3) — bridges from this unit into Strand C's immersion literature are therefore necessarily indirect (P/anticipatory-application).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Knowledge outcomes show no significant SMD difference (0.11) between VP and traditional education (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.6).
- Skills outcomes show a moderate-to-large advantage for VP (SMD=0.90), specifically for clinical reasoning, procedural skills, and mixed procedural/team skills (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.7).
- Blended VP-plus-traditional-education outperforms traditional education alone: knowledge SMD=0.73 (moderate), skills SMD=0.60 (moderate, CI crosses zero) (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.6–7).
- VP-versus-other-digital-education comparisons remain too sparse (2 knowledge studies, 3 skills studies) for firm conclusions; most non-significant, with an exception where VP beat a mixed traditional-plus-web-content control on clinical skills (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.7).
- **Design-variant (sim-vs-sim) comparisons — the review's own answer to "does internal VP design matter":** in the knowledge domain, of 8 studies only Friedman et al. [96] found a significant difference (pedagogic/guided design beating problem-solving/high-fidelity design, P<.01); the remaining seven contrasts (linear/branched, scaffolded/nonscaffolded, worked/unworked examples, usability extensions, self-determined/mandatory access, spaced/nonspaced case release, solving-versus-constructing VPs) were non-significant (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.7). In the skills domain, the picture is more mixed across 4 studies: empathic feedback beat no feedback (P<.03), narrative design beat problem-solving design for communication skills (P=.03), and representational scaffolding improved diagnostic efficiency (P=.045), while solving-versus-constructing VPs again showed no difference (P=.54) (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.7).
- Subgroup analysis: none of the pre-registered analyses explained heterogeneity; a balanced 6-versus-6 study split on narrative-versus-problem-solving VP design showed no subgroup difference for knowledge outcomes (P=.97) (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.10).
- Interpreting the knowledge/skills split via Bloom's taxonomy: little added value from VP at lower cognitive levels (remember, understand — the knowledge outcome), greater value where knowledge is applied within problem-solving (the skills outcome) (FE, p.12).
- The review's explicit methodological verdict — echoing Friedman and Cook — is that media-comparative research (VP versus a traditional format) should be abandoned in favor of adequately powered, theory-grounded design-variant research (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.13).
- Attitudes and satisfaction are generally positive but with a documented undertow: self-reported confidence was sometimes lower in VP groups even where objective performance was equal or better, a pattern the authors attribute tentatively to disbelief in computer-based methods or anxiety about reduced direct-patient contact (FE, p.8, 12). Cost data are inconsistent across studies but several report VP as cheaper than mannequin-based simulation (e.g., a cost-utility ratio of US$1.08 versus US$3.62 in one study) (FAITHFUL-Kononowicz, p.9).
- Positive effects were documented in both high-income (44/51 studies) and low-/middle-income countries (7/51), supporting a claim of global applicability (FE, p.1, 5).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit bridges to `vle-01-deployment-survey`: the review's finding that VP's advantage is contingent on what it is compared against — large against passive instructional formats, negligible against active ones — is directly relevant to any future comparison of the VLE's APP versus VID (video-only) strata on a learning-outcome measure, since the VID stratum's played-back-video condition sits closer to Kononowicz's "passive" pole while the APP condition sits closer to its "active" pole; a naive APP-beats-VID reading should be checked against this asymmetry before being attributed to immersion or presence rather than to the passive/active axis alone *(anticipatory-application)*. The review's own methodological verdict — that media-comparative research is largely uninterpretable given uncontrolled confounds — extends as a caution to Part III generally: the VLE's own APP-versus-VID comparison bundles interactivity, self-pacing, and presence together in exactly the way Kononowicz (following Friedman and Cook) warns against, so any outcome gap Part III observes should be decomposed rather than attributed to a single construct without further evidence *(anticipatory-application)*.

Most consequentially for this cluster's debate map, this unit is the strongest available in-cluster test of debate axis 6 ("simulation beats no-intervention strongly" versus "sim-vs-sim differences negligible"), read against `ped-sec-15` (Cook et al., *Technology-Enhanced Simulation for Health Professions Education*, 2011). Ped-sec-15's own conclusion — that simulation-versus-no-intervention effects are consistently large-to-moderate and that further such studies are of low value, with future research redirected toward simulation-design comparisons — is precisely the research program Kononowicz's design-variant arm executes seven years later. Kononowicz's results offer *qualified, outcome-dependent* support for the "sim-vs-sim differences negligible" pole: strongly so for knowledge (7 of 8 design-variant contrasts non-significant), more equivocally so for skills (3 of 4 significant, though each with a narrow, specific mechanism — feedback type, narrative structure, or scaffolding — rather than a generic "more fidelity/more interactivity" effect). This connection is this cluster's own construction, not a citation either source makes about the other: Kononowicz's own explicit citation for the "prior VP meta-analysis it extends" (ref [38], Cook, Erwin & Triola) is a *different*, VP-specific 2010 meta-analysis, not the general technology-enhanced-simulation meta-analysis that is ped-sec-15 — the two Cook-led papers must not be conflated when this edge is used downstream *(P/anticipatory-application)*.

**Not treated:** presence (spatial/physical or social), immersion (technological), cognitive load as an instrumented construct, situational interest, engagement/flow, embodiment/self-representation, and cybersickness — none of the cluster's VR-specific constructs appear in this review, which deliberately confines its scope to screen-based, non-3D-immersive virtual patients and defers the immersive variant to a companion review outside this cluster.
