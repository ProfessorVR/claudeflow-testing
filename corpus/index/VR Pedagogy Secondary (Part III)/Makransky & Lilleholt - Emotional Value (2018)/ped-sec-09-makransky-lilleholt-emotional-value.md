# ped-sec-09 — Makransky & Lilleholt, "A structural equation modeling investigation of the emotional value of immersive virtual reality in education" (2018)

**Type:** empirical journal article (educational technology / educational psychology) · **Discipline:** empirical-quantitative · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand C media-comparison SEM study; the cluster's PRIMARY SOURCE for debate axis 4's affective-route pole and direct empirical scaffolding cited by CAMIL (ped-sec-06).

## 1. Thesis/Aim

The paper pursues two stated aims (FE, p.1145): (A) determine whether an immersive-HMD version of a VR learning simulation carries greater "emotional value" than a desktop-VR version of the identical content, and (B) use structural equation modeling (SEM) to trace the process by which the level of immersion affects non-cognitive and perceived-learning outcomes. Its guiding claim is that immersion's contribution to learning-relevant outcomes runs indirectly, through two parallel process paths rather than a single direct effect: an *affective path* (immersion → presence → enjoyment/motivation → outcomes) and a *cognitive path* (immersion → cognitive benefits → outcomes), the former grounded in the control-value theory of achievement emotions (CVTAE; Pekrun 2000) and the latter extending Lee et al.'s (2010) desktop-VR causal framework (FE, p.1141, 1145–1146).

## 2. Structure

- **Introduction** (p.1141–1142) — frames VR's projected educational role; argues the emotional/non-cognitive impact of immersion is under-researched relative to cognitive outcomes; states aims A and B.
- **Existing definitions and types of VR** (p.1142–1143) — defines VR (Burdea & Coiffet; Biocca); distinguishes CAVE, HMD, and desktop VR; adopts Cummings & Bailenson's account of immersion as an objective, system-driven property.
- **Existing research about the impact of immersive VR** (p.1143–1144) — surveys a mixed prior literature: some studies (Alhalabi; Webster; Bric et al.) find immersive VR outperforming traditional/desktop instruction cognitively; others (Moreno & Mayer 2002; Stepan et al.; Makransky et al.'s own "presence but less learning" study, cited here as 2017b — cross-cluster: ped-sec-10) find null or negative cognitive effects paired with higher cognitive load, motivating the paper's pivot to non-cognitive outcomes.
- **Virtual simulations in training and education** (p.1144–1145) — positions VR simulations as a cost-effective training supplement/alternative, citing meta-analytic support for simulations generally (including Merchant et al. 2014 — cross-cluster: ped-sec-16) while flagging open questions specific to immersive VR's added value.
- **Theory and predictions** (p.1145–1149) — grounds the study in CVTAE combined with Lee et al.'s (2010) desktop-VR causal model to build an a-priori path model: platform (immersive/desktop) → VR features & usability → affective variables (presence, motivation, enjoyment, control/active learning) and cognitive variables (cognitive benefits, reflective thinking) → outcomes (behavioral intention, satisfaction, perceived learning).
- **Materials and Methods** (p.1149–1151) — sample, crossover procedure, 13-construct survey instrument, the Labster DNA-profiling simulation, apparatus.
- **Results** (p.1151–1154) — paired t-tests across platforms; SEM of the a-priori model, trimmed to a final two-path model.
- **Discussion — empirical, theoretical, practical contributions** (p.1154–1156).
- **Limitations and future directions** (p.1156–1157) — novelty effect, self-report-only outcomes, small N relative to model complexity, unmeasured side effects.

## 3. Constructs

Thirteen constructs are measured via adapted 5-point Likert scales (FE, p.1146–1149), organized into four families that the paper's own model treats as ordered stages rather than a flat list:

- **VR features** — *representational fidelity* (3 items, adapted from Lee et al. 2010) and *immediacy of control* (4 items, adapted from Lee et al. 2010): the objective realism and control affordances of the platform (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1146–1147).
- **Usability** — *perceived usefulness* and *perceived ease of use* (Davis 1989 items): mediating variables between VR features and the affective/cognitive factors (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1147).
- **Affective factors** — *presence* (10 items, Sutcliffe et al. 2005, operationalized per Witmer & Singer's involvement + immersion account — cross-cluster: ped-sec-02), *intrinsic motivation* (7 items, Lee et al. 2010), *perceived enjoyment* (3 items, Tokel & İsler 2015), *control and active learning* (5 items, Lee et al. 2010) (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1148).
- **Cognitive factors** — *cognitive benefits* and *reflective thinking* (4 items each, Lee et al. 2010; the latter after Dewey's 1933 definition) (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1148).
- **Outcomes** — *behavioral intention to use*, *satisfaction*, and *perceived learning* (self-report only, not objective retention/transfer) (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1148, qualified p.1156).

Constructs the study treats but does not name in the cluster's own vocabulary: *situational interest* surfaces only in the discussion, via Renninger & Hidi's four-phase interest-development theory used to explain why the novelty-driven results might not persist (FE, p.1156) — analogical rather than a measured construct. *Engagement/flow* likewise appears only as an interpretive gloss (Csikszentmihalyi 2000) on why enjoyment predicts the outcomes, not as an instrument (FE, p.1155).

## 4. Conditions & design

**Design:** within-subjects crossover repeated-measures; every participant used both platforms, order counterbalanced across two groups (FE, p.1149). **N:** 104 university students (39 female, 65 male; mean age 23.8) from a large European university, informed consent per Danish Health Research Ethics Committee regulations (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1149). **Conditions:** *IVR (HMD)* — Samsung Gear VR + Galaxy S6, stereoscopic, head-tracked field-of-view, touchpad-on-headset object selection; *desktop-3D / non-immersive VR* — identical Labster DNA-profiling crime-scene biology simulation on a 15-inch laptop, standard touchpad input (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1149–1150). Each session capped at 20 minutes per platform; survey administered after each platform use (FE, p.1149). **Instruments:** the 13 adapted Likert scales above (Cronbach's α 0.69–0.91); CFA to validate factor structure (two presence items dropped for non-significant loading; final CFA fit CFI=0.96, TLI=0.95, RMSEA=0.05); paired-samples t-tests (Bonferroni-corrected α=0.004) for platform comparison; Mplus SEM with ordinal-indicator estimation for the process model, fit criteria CFI/TLI≥0.90 and RMSEA≤0.06 (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1149–1151). **Effect direction:** immersive VR scored significantly higher on 11 of 13 constructs; largest effects — presence *d*=1.67, motivation *d*=1.28, immediacy of control *d*=0.99, enjoyment *d*=0.94 (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1151).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- **Emotional-value contribution:** immersive VR is preferred over desktop VR on nearly every measured dimension, with the largest margins on presence, motivation, enjoyment, and immediacy of control (FE, p.1154).
- **Two-path SEM model:** the trimmed final model (CFI=0.91, TLI=0.91, RMSEA=0.06) resolves into an *affective path* — platform → presence & VR features (β=−0.52/−0.31) → usability (β mediating) → presence (β=0.36 from usability, β=0.21 from features) → motivation (β=0.68) & enjoyment (β=0.63) → perceived-learning outcomes (motivation β=0.41, enjoyment β=0.15); control-and-active-learning also predicts outcomes directly (β=0.23) — and a *cognitive path* — VR features/usability → cognitive benefits (β=0.51/0.45) → outcomes (β=0.35); reflective thinking, though strongly predicted by VR features (β=0.83), does not itself predict outcomes (FAITHFUL-Makransky-Lilleholt, p.1152–1154).
- **Presence as mediator, not terminal predictor:** presence does not directly predict the outcome variables; instead it mediates between platform/features/usability and motivation/enjoyment — a result the authors explicitly contrast with Lee et al.'s (2010) desktop-VR finding that presence directly predicts perceived learning (FE, p.1155).
- **Theoretical interpretation:** the affective path is read through CVTAE — enjoyment follows from appraised control plus intrinsic value — while the cognitive path is read through the cognitive-affective theory of learning with media and cognitive load theory, the latter cited (Sweller et al. 2011) only to note that immersive VEs could either foster generative processing or risk extraneous processing depending on design (FE, p.1155).
- **Practical upshot:** two levers for designers — (1) build enjoyable, motivating, high-usability, high-fidelity VR to raise presence and downstream affect; (2) support autonomy/control and make cognitive benefits legible to raise the cognitive path's contribution (FE, p.1155–1156).
- **Self-reported limitation:** results likely partly reflect novelty effects, since most participants had never used immersive VR before; all outcome measures are self-report, not objective retention/transfer or physiological affect measures; the SEM's sample size is small relative to its complexity; motion sickness and other side effects were not assessed (FE, p.1156–1157).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit is the cluster's primary empirical grounding for debate axis 4's *affective route* — the claim that immersion's benefit to learning-relevant outcomes travels through presence-driven enjoyment and motivation rather than acting on cognition directly — which Part III can cite whenever it wants a quantified (SEM path-coefficient) rather than merely theoretical account of why felt engagement with a virtual environment might matter independently of content mastery. It bridges to `vle-01-deployment-survey`: the survey's F7 finding that the APP stratum reports markedly higher presence than the VID (video-only) stratum (4.3 vs 1.2 per-100 F1-PRESENCE codes) is structurally the same platform-presence gap this study quantifies experimentally (immersive vs desktop, *d*=1.67), though the VLE's own comparison is 3D-app vs 2D-video rather than HMD vs desktop-3D — a coarser-grained version of the same affective-route claim *(anticipatory-application)*. It also bridges to `vle-04-mark-attention-span`: this study's motivation/enjoyment/control-and-active-learning cluster maps loosely onto Mark's engagement × challenge quadrant (the "Focus" cell — high engagement, high challenge — versus "Bored," low/low), suggesting that VLE deployment context (lab/APP vs deployment/VID) could be read as pushing students toward different quadrants of that framework, though the correspondence is this cluster's own construction, not either source's claim *(anticipatory-application)*. Finally, this unit is one of CAMIL's (`ped-sec-06`) own cited empirical foundations: CAMIL's "General Overview" section names this study directly as source scaffolding for its affective/cognitive dual-path structure (CAMIL, p.941–942), so the dependency here runs *from* ped-sec-06 *to* this unit, not the reverse — this 2018 paper predates and empirically motivates CAMIL's 2021 theoretical generalization, and should be read as CAMIL's raw material rather than as an application of CAMIL.

**Not treated:** cybersickness/physical discomfort (mentioned only as an unassessed limitation, p.1157), social presence/co-presence (single-user simulation, no collaborative condition), self-efficacy (no scale for it), and cognitive load as an instrumented construct (only "cognitive benefits," an appraisal measure, not a load measure, is used).
