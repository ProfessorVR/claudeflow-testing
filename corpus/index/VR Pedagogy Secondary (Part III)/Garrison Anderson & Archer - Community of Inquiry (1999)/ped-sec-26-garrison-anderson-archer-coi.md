# ped-sec-26 — Garrison, Anderson & Archer, "Critical Inquiry in a Text-Based Environment: Computer Conferencing in Higher Education" (1999)

**Type:** journal-article-length secondary reading (educational technology / computer-mediated communication) · **Discipline:** education / educational technology · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F's foundational theory paper — the keystone article introducing the Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework and its three constitutive elements (cognitive presence, social presence, teaching presence). This is very likely the single most-cited external interlocutor within Strand F: it supplies the definition of "social presence" that the row-axis construct of the same name, in every later Strand-F unit, either adopts, refines, or contests.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

The article's stated purpose is to provide conceptual order and an analytic tool for computer-mediated communication (CMC), particularly asynchronous computer conferencing, in higher education (FE, p.87). Its central thesis is that a worthwhile educational transaction requires a Community of Inquiry composed of teachers and students interacting through three interdependent elements — cognitive presence, social presence, and teaching presence — and that a transcript-derived coding template of textual indicators can operationalize each element for research and practice (FE, p.87–90). The authors are explicit that this piece is "the keystone of a series of publications reporting the results of this ongoing research project" (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.87) — the article's own framing already anticipates the companion papers (Rourke et al., 1999, on methodology; Anderson, Rourke, Garrison & Archer, 1999, on social presence in more depth) that would, within a few years, seed the broader CoI literature this cluster's later Strand-F units inherit. A closing claim, offered tentatively rather than demonstrated empirically in this article, is that computer conferencing "has considerable potential to create a community of inquiry for educational purposes" (FE, p.87, 104–105).

## 2. Structure

The article proceeds in eight unlabeled-by-number but internally signposted movements:

1. **Abstract and introduction to the three-element model** (pp.87–90) — states the study's purpose; introduces the Community of Inquiry (Fig. 1) as teachers and students interacting through cognitive, social, and teaching presence; previews the transcript-derived indicator template (Table 1).
2. **"Oral and Text-based Communication"** (pp.90–92) — contrasts oral communication (fast, spontaneous, rich in paralinguistic cues) with text-based communication (a "lean" medium lacking non-verbal cues but affording time for reflection); surveys literature (Applebee 1984; Fulwiler 1987; White 1993; Chafe & Danielewicz 1987) associating writing with more explicit, higher-order thinking, while cautioning the connection is "far from straightforward" (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.91).
3. **"A Conceptual Framework"** (pp.92–94) — grounds the model in Lipman's (1991) account of a community of inquiry as context for critical thinking, Ramsden's (1988) emphasis on negotiating meaning, and Dewey's (1959) claim that education has inseparable psychological and sociological sides; introduces the "collaborative constructivist perspective" (Garrison & Archer, in press) and positions teaching presence as the element responsible for designing and integrating the cognitive and social elements.
4. **"Cognitive Presence"** (pp.94, 94–99 for the extended treatment) — defines cognitive presence and reviews prior empirical transcript-coding work: Newman, Johnson, Cochrane & Webb's (1996) face-to-face/computer-conference comparison; Gunawardena, Lowe & Anderson's (1997) five-phase knowledge-construction model; Bullen's (1997, 1998) methodologically troubled critical-thinking study; introduces the practical inquiry model (triggering event, exploration, integration, resolution) built on Dewey's (1933) pre-reflection/reflection/post-reflection phases (pp.98–99).
5. **"Social Presence"** (pp.94–96, 99–101 for the extended treatment) — defines social presence, explicitly departs from media-richness theorists, and sets out three indicator categories (emotional expression, open communication, group cohesion), including a discussion of emoticons and self-disclosure as compensations for lost visual cues.
6. **"Teaching Presence"** (pp.96–98, 101–103) — identifies two functions (design, facilitation) and three indicator categories (instructional management, building understanding, direct instruction); cites Tagg & Dickenson (1995) and Fabro & Garrison (1998) on tutor/moderator effects on student activity.
7. **"A Template" / "Coding"** (pp.103–104) — describes the development of the coding template (Table 1) and flags the unit-of-analysis problem as a major methodological challenge deferred to the companion paper (Rourke et al., 1999).
8. **"Conclusion"** (pp.104–105) — recaps the framework and template as the study's contribution; restates the potential-but-unproven claim about computer conferencing's capacity to create an educational community of inquiry.

## 3. Constructs

The article's payload for this cluster's row-axis vocabulary is narrow but load-bearing: it is the single canonical source for the **social presence / co-presence** construct that anchors Strand F, and it packages that construct inside a larger three-element THEORY-MODEL whose other two elements (cognitive presence, teaching presence) are related-but-distinct components rather than themselves instances of the §3 concordance rows.

- **social presence / co-presence** (the concordance row this unit is built to fill) — defined as participants' capacity to project their "full personality" as "real" people, socially and emotionally, through whatever communication medium is in use (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.94, restated p.99). Its function is framed dually: primarily as *support* for cognitive presence ("indirectly facilitating the process of critical thinking"), and, "when there are affective goals for the educational process," as a *direct* contributor to educational success by keeping learners in the cohort for the program's duration (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.89). Three indicator categories operationalize it: emotional expression (emoticons, self-disclosure, humor — citing Gunawardena & Zittle 1997, Kuehn 1993, Gorham & Christophel 1990, Cutler 1995), open communication (mutual awareness, recognition, reply/quoting behaviors), and group cohesion (activities building group commitment) (FE, p.99–101).
- **cognitive presence** (THEORY-MODEL component, related but distinct from the social-presence row) — "the extent to which participants…are able to construct meaning through sustained communication" (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.89), framed as "the element in this model that is most basic to success in higher education" and tied to critical thinking as higher education's "ostensible goal" (FE, p.89). Operationalized via the practical inquiry model's four phases — triggering event, exploration, integration, resolution — mapped onto Dewey's pre-reflection/reflection/post-reflection sequence (FE, p.98–99).
- **teaching presence** (THEORY-MODEL component, related but distinct) — "the binding element in creating a community of inquiry," with two functions (design of the educational experience; facilitation) and three indicator categories (instructional management, building understanding, direct instruction) (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.96). The article states plainly that "when education based on computer conferencing fails, it is usually because there has not been responsible teaching presence" (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.95, citing Gunawardena 1991; Hiltz & Turoff 1993).
- **community of inquiry** (THEORY-MODEL, the overarching framework) — the composite structure within which the three presences interact; Figure 1 depicts a worthwhile educational experience as "embedded within" this community, composed of teachers and students as the key participants (FE, p.88).

Two further §3 rows are touched only analogically, not as the article's own operationalized constructs: **engagement / flow** — approached obliquely through "group cohesion" and an explicit hypothesis linking strong social presence, plus high commitment and participation, to the development of higher-order thinking and collaborative work (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.94), which gestures at sustained engagement without naming or measuring it as a distinct construct; and **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — touched via the framing of critical thinking as higher education's "ostensible goal" and via Newman et al.'s (1996, 1997) finding that computer-conferencing students showed higher-quality critical-thinking indicators (linking outside material, applying solutions) while being "less interactive" than face-to-face seminar students (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.93) — a finding this article reports secondhand, not as its own data (P/anticipatory-application: treat as analogical-only in the cluster's concept-matrix, not a full treatment).

## 4. Conditions & design

This unit populates the `conditions[]` field only with an explicit gap-note, not with study data. The article is a conceptual/theoretical framework paper: it proposes a model and a transcript-coding template but reports no sample, no N, no instrument validation, and no statistical findings of its own — its authors describe the coding template as still at the stage of "preliminary application," to be verified "through data gathered from student interviews and surveys" in future work (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.104, 744–751/txt). The methodological apparatus it does describe — a category-and-indicator coding template (Table 1) applied to computer-conference transcripts, with the "vexing problem" of unit-of-analysis explicitly deferred to a companion paper (Rourke et al., 1999) (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.103) — is a qualitative content-analysis methodology, not an experimental media-comparison design.

**No media-condition axis content.** All communication the article describes is asynchronous, text-based computer conferencing (CMC); there is no IVR, desktop-3D, or 2D-video contrast, and no "conventional instruction (control)" arm. Per the cluster plan, Strand F is not required to populate the §3 media-condition column, and nothing in this source genuinely fits any of its eight canonical labels — this is stated explicitly rather than forced. The nearest the article comes to a medium-contrast claim is definitional rather than empirical: oral communication is characterized as fast, spontaneous, and paralinguistically rich, text-based communication as "lean" but reflection-affording (FE, p.90–91) — a medium-theoretic aside, not a tested condition.

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Computer conferencing shows "considerable potential to create an educational community of inquiry," though the authors caution that substantial work remains before anyone truly understands how to optimally design and deliver a worthwhile educational experience in a text-based environment (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.104–105) — offered as the article's own tentative, forward-looking claim rather than a demonstrated result.
- Cognitive presence, while "most basic" to educational success, is "not sufficient to sustain a critical community of learners" on its own; it must be nurtured within a broader socio-emotional environment (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.94, 360–364/txt).
- The authors explicitly hypothesize that strong social presence, together with a high degree of commitment and participation, is necessary for developing higher-order thinking skills and sustaining collaborative work (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.94) — an explicit hypothesis, not (in this article) a tested claim.
- Social presence is located in the *communication context* — familiarity, skills, motivation, organizational commitment, activities, and duration of media use — rather than in properties of the medium itself, "unlike earlier communications theorists (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Short, Williams, & Christie, 1976; Sproull & Kiesler, 1986)" (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.94–95). This is the article's single most consequential theoretical move for this cluster: it is an explicit, named departure from media-richness theory that lands directly on the plan's debate axis 3 (presence as technology-determined ↔ presence as psychologically/individually determined) — the position later occupied, on the technology-determined side, by Witmer & Singer's control/sensory factors (ped-sec-02) and, on the psychologically-determined side, echoed and extended by Chow's SEM determinants (ped-sec-03) (P/anticipatory-application).
- Teaching presence is "the binding element," integrating cognitive and social presence toward educational outcomes; when computer-conferencing-based education fails, the diagnosis offered is usually a deficit of teaching presence rather than of the medium itself (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.95–96).
- Text-based CMC participants develop compensating strategies — emoticons, self-disclosure, humor — for the loss of visual/paralinguistic cues; Gunawardena & Zittle (1997) are cited directly for the finding that conference participants "enhanced their socio-emotional experience by using emoticons to express missing nonverbal cues in written form" (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.99, quoting Gunawardena & Zittle 1997, p.8) — a direct, named intra-cluster citation to ped-sec-25.
- Newman et al. (1996, 1997) found computer-conferencing students "adopted a more serious, worthier, style" with higher-quality critical-thinking indicators but were "less interactive" and generated fewer creative contributions than face-to-face seminar students (FAITHFUL-Garrison-Anderson-Archer, p.93) — reported by Garrison, Anderson & Archer as prior evidence, not their own finding.
- The coding template (Table 1) — cognitive presence's four phase-categories (triggering event, exploration, integration, resolution), social presence's three categories (emotional expression, open communication, group cohesion), and teaching presence's three categories (instructional management, building understanding, direct instruction) — is presented as a preliminary but usable analytic tool, with unit-of-analysis reliability flagged as its chief unresolved methodological problem (FE, p.103–104).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit is the strand's foundational hub: it is the source most likely to be cited, directly or via its companion papers, by every later Strand-F unit that treats social presence empirically. **Flag: Terry & Doolittle's "Re-examining Social Presence" (ped-sec-31, 2019) is the single most probable direct citer**, given its explicit re-examination framing of the very construct Garrison, Anderson & Archer define here; Richardson & Swan (ped-sec-28, 2003) and Richardson et al.'s meta-analysis (ped-sec-32, 2017) are the next most probable, since both sit downstream of the CoI research program this article inaugurates; Tu (ped-sec-27, 2002), Biocca, Burgoon & Harms (ped-sec-29, 2003), and Kreijns, Kirschner & Jochems (ped-sec-30, 2003) are plausible citers insofar as any early-2000s "measuring/theorizing social presence" piece in this literature routinely cites the CoI framework as a reference point, though this is this unit's own extrapolation rather than a citation confirmed in this article's own (backward-looking) reference list. The article's own citations run the other direction — it cites Gunawardena & Zittle (1997, ped-sec-25) directly on emoticon compensation (p.99) — which is the one intra-cluster citation this 1999 article could actually make, since every other Strand-F unit postdates it.

Toward the VLE anchor: vle-01's F7 finding ("Presence & the WE" — F1-PRESENCE indicator rate 4.3 vs 1.2 per-100 postings, APP vs VID strata; E-SHA optative language such as "seemed isolating"; "watch with others" cited as a main pro) and vle-03's exemplar quotes on presence, carry-over, and social watching plausibly instantiate this article's social-presence construct — participants "project[ing] themselves socially and emotionally, as 'real' people" — inside the VLE's own text data, even though the VLE's medium (a 3D application versus solo video) differs from this article's medium (asynchronous text conferencing); the construct transfers across media, the operationalization does not (P/anticipatory-application). Toward Part III directly: the article's central, named departure from media-richness theory — that social presence is a function of communication context (familiarity, skills, motivation, commitment) rather than medium properties per se — offers Part III a ready-made theoretical resource for interpreting why the VLE's video-only stratum reports lower presence-indicator rates than its 3D-app stratum without needing to attribute the gap to the video medium's technological "leanness" alone; contextual factors (group assignment, prior familiarity, task structure) identified by Garrison, Anderson & Archer as social presence's true determinants may be doing comparable work in the VLE's own APP/VID split (P/anticipatory-application). This reading should be held provisionally and refined once the remaining Strand-F units (especially ped-sec-29's "robust theory," which explicitly engages the technology-vs-context debate from the communication-theory side) are built.

**Not treated:** immersion (technological), agency / interactivity, embodiment / self-representation, cognitive load (extraneous vs germane), affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation, situational interest, self-efficacy, cybersickness & physical discomfort, representational fidelity — none of these constructs, nor any IVR/desktop-3D/2D medium contrast, appear in this article, which is confined throughout to asynchronous, text-based computer conferencing in higher education.
