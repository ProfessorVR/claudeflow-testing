# ped-sec-10 — Makransky, Terkildsen & Mayer, "Adding immersive virtual reality to a science lab simulation causes more presence but less learning" (2019)

**Type:** empirical journal article (experimental, within-subjects media comparison) · **Discipline:** educational psychology / learning sciences · **Strand:** C — media-comparison & process experiments · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** the cluster's primary media-comparison datapoint for debate axis 1 (immersion → presence but not learning) and its principal EEG-based evidence for the cognitive-load explanatory mechanism feeding debate axis 4.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Makransky, Terkildsen & Mayer set out to determine what happens when a virtual learning simulation designed for a low-immersion desktop (PC) environment is ported, unmodified, to a high-immersion head-mounted-display (HMD) environment (FE, p.225–226). Their central empirical claim, stated in the title itself, is that adding immersive VR increases self-reported presence but decreases learning relative to the PC version of the identical simulation (FE, p.225, 233). A secondary aim tests whether Mayer's redundancy principle from the cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML) generalizes across immersion levels (FE, p.227). A third, methodological aim is to use electroencephalography (EEG) as a direct, non-self-report index of cognitive workload during learning, answering a National Research Council (2011) call to examine the mediating cognitive/affective processes inside simulation-based science learning (FE, p.225).

## 2. Structure

Five numbered sections: (1) Introduction, with subsections on objective/rationale, virtual learning simulations, theoretical background, the redundancy-principle question, and the study's two research questions (pp.225–227); (2) Method, covering participants/design, materials (simulation, tests, survey, apparatus), and the EEG workload measure (pp.227–230); (3) Results, opening with an instrument-validity check (Rasch fit, Cronbach's alpha) then reporting media effects and method effects across eight outcome measures (pp.231–233); (4) Discussion, subdivided into empirical, theoretical, practical, and methodological implications plus limitations (pp.233–235); (5) Conclusion (p.235).

## 3. Constructs

- **Presence (spatial/physical).** Measured with a 10-item scale adapted from Schubert, Friedmann & Regenbrecht (2001; e.g., "the virtual world seemed real to me"), five-point Likert (FE, p.228–229). The paper's own theoretical framing ties presence to Slater & Wilbur's (1997) immersion-drives-presence account and to interest theories descending from Dewey (1913) (FE, p.226).
- **Cognitive load (extraneous vs. germane).** Framed via CTML's three-way processing distinction — extraneous, essential, generative — and Sweller's cognitive load theory (CLT); operationalized both as a theoretical lens and as a continuous EEG workload metric from an Advanced Brain Monitoring 9-channel system, classified into boredom (≤0.4), optimal (0.4–0.7), and overload (>0.7) bands (FE, p.226–227, 229–230).
- **Learning outcomes: retention & transfer.** Two multiple-choice instruments — a 10-item knowledge test (essential/procedural content) and a 10-item transfer test (application to novel scenarios), each Rasch-trimmed before scoring (FE, p.228–229, 231).
- **Affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation.** Operationalized as "learning beliefs" (8 items) and "satisfaction" (7 items), both adapted from Lee, Wong & Fung (2010) (FE, p.229).
- **Situational interest.** Treated only theoretically, not as a separate measured scale: the paper frames added immersion as a candidate trigger of situational interest (citing Renninger & Hidi, 2016) that may not itself produce deep learning (FE, p.227) — this construct row is fed by discussion, not by a dedicated instrument (flagged in the concordance feed below).

Not treated as separate constructs here, though named elsewhere in the cluster: social presence/co-presence, embodiment/self-representation, self-efficacy, cybersickness, engagement/flow, and representational fidelity.

## 4. Conditions & design

A 2×2 mixed experiment, N=52 (22 male, 30 female; ages 19–42, M=23.8, SD=4.5) from a large European (Danish) university (FE, p.227). **Between-subjects factor (Method):** 28 participants received text-only (T) versions of the simulation; 24 received text-plus-narration (T+N) versions — the redundancy-principle manipulation (FE, p.227). **Within-subjects factor (Media):** each participant experienced both a **desktop-PC (mouse-controlled monitor) version** and an **immersive-VR (Samsung GearVR HMD, touchpad-controlled) version** of an identical Labster mammalian-transient-protein-expression lab simulation, order counterbalanced in a cross-panel design (VR-then-PC or PC-then-VR), each intervention lasting 15 minutes (FE, p.227–228, 230–231). Instruments: presence, learning-beliefs, and satisfaction self-report scales; knowledge and transfer post-tests (with pre-test covariate); continuous EEG workload and percent-overload-time measures (FE, p.228–230). Rasch-model fit and Cronbach's alpha (α=0.72–0.91 for the self-report scales; α=0.68 knowledge, 0.32–0.55 transfer) established instrument reliability before analysis, with several misfitting items trimmed (FE, p.231).

Key statistics (ANOVA/ANCOVA, first vs. second intervention): presence, VR > PC, d=1.30 / d=2.20; knowledge-test gain, PC > VR, d=0.48 / d=0.80; transfer-test gain, ns both interventions; learning beliefs and satisfaction, ns both interventions; EEG workload, VR > PC, ns first / d=0.59 second; percent overload-time, VR > PC, ns first / d=0.62 second (FE, p.231–232). Method (T vs. T+N) effects were largely null on learning, presence, and affect measures; one workload effect ran counter to the redundancy prediction (T > T+N, d=0.61, first intervention only), which the authors attribute to a confound with the modality principle — some VR participants listened to narration rather than reading on-screen text (FE, p.233).

## 5. Findings/Claims

The paper's own headline finding, restated almost verbatim from its title, is that immersive VR produced significantly higher presence but significantly lower knowledge-test learning gains than the desktop-PC version of an identical simulation (FE, p.233). The authors gloss this as "liking is not learning" (FE, p.235) — instructional media that increase the enjoyment or immediacy of an experience do not thereby guarantee better learning. The EEG results supply what the authors call preliminary brain-based evidence that the VR condition produced greater cognitive workload and more time spent in the overload band than the PC condition, particularly by the second intervention (FE, p.232–233). Interpretively, the discussion favors the "pessimistic" CTML/CLT route over the "optimistic" one: added immersion is read as functioning like a seductive detail or a set of "bells and whistles" (echoing Moreno & Mayer's 2002 term) that increases extraneous processing rather than generative processing, an account reinforced by Van Der Heijden's (2004) hedonic-vs-utilitarian systems framing — students may treat the HMD version as a hedonic (fun-focused) system and disengage from its instrumental (learning) value (FE, p.233–234). The result is explicitly positioned as agreeing with the earlier, lower-immersion-favoring findings of Moreno & Mayer (2002) and Richards & Taylor (2015), and as diverging from newer studies reporting learning benefits for high-immersion VR (Alhalabi, 2016; Passig et al., 2016; Webster, 2016) (FE, p.233). On the secondary research question, the redundancy principle did not clearly generalize across immersion levels — no consistent learning or affect differences between text-only and text-plus-narration groups, and where a cognitive-load difference did appear, it ran opposite to the redundancy prediction (FE, p.233). Practically, the authors caution against a technology-centered ("port it and see") approach to VR adoption, urging instructional designers to weight immersion's costs and benefits against specific learning goals rather than assuming immersion is intrinsically beneficial (FE, p.234).

## 6. Concordance feed

- **presence (spatial/physical) × IVR (HMD):** VR significantly exceeds PC, d=1.30 (1st intervention) / d=2.20 (2nd) (FE, p.231–232).
- **presence (spatial/physical) × desktop-3D / non-immersive VR:** PC is the lower-presence comparison condition in the same contrast (FE, p.231–232).
- **cognitive load (extraneous vs germane) × IVR (HMD):** EEG workload d=0.59 and overload-time d=0.62, both VR > PC, second intervention only (FE, p.232–233).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer × IVR (HMD) vs desktop-3D:** knowledge-gain advantage to PC, d=0.48/0.80; transfer-gain difference non-significant both interventions (FE, p.231–232).
- **affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation × IVR (HMD) / desktop-3D:** learning-beliefs and satisfaction ratings show no significant media effect in either direction (FE, p.231–232).
- **situational interest × IVR (HMD):** treated as a candidate theoretical mechanism (added immersion as an interest-trigger that may not deepen into durable learning) rather than as a directly measured cell; recorded here as a discussion-level, not instrument-level, concordance entry (FE, p.227) — ****** UNVERIFIED: no dedicated situational-interest scale was administered in this study, so any numeric cell for this row×column pair would be fabricated.

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* As the cluster's title-bearing source for "presence but less learning," this unit is the primary evidentiary anchor for debate axis 1 (more immersion → more learning ↔ immersion adds presence but not learning) and supplies the most mechanistically explicit case for debate axis 4's cognitive-load route: the EEG overload finding operationalizes, rather than merely asserts, the claim that presence's motivational pull can translate into extraneous processing and distraction instead of generative processing *(anticipatory-application)*. This bridges to the VLE entry's `vle-04-mark-attention-span` unit, whose attentional-states framework (engagement × challenge, with a "Frustrated" quadrant of high challenge/low engagement and a documented link between pupil diameter and cognitive load) offers Part III a parallel, non-EEG vocabulary for describing the same overload phenomenon this study measures directly *(anticipatory-application)*. For Part III's own lab=VR ↔ deployment=2D-PC poles, this unit is a load-bearing caution: it demonstrates, in a controlled experiment with an identical simulation ported across media, that the higher-immersion pole is not automatically the higher-learning pole, and that self-report engagement/presence measures can dissociate sharply from objective learning and workload measures — a dissociation Part III should anticipate rather than assume away when triangulating survey and behavioral data *(anticipatory-application)*. The study's own practical conclusion — that content should be designed for its medium rather than ported across media — is directly relevant to how Part III frames its own VLE's desktop-3D and 2D-video conditions as deliberately distinct designs rather than mere immersion-degree variants of one another *(anticipatory-application)*.
