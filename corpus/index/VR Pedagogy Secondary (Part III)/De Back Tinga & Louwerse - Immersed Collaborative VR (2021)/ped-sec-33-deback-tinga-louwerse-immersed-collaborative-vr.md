# ped-sec-33 — De Back, Tinga & Louwerse, "Learning in Immersed Collaborative Virtual Environments: Design and Implementation" (2021)

**Type:** empirical/design-research article (cognitive science, educational technology) · **Discipline:** cognitive science / educational technology · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F unit whose actual content is VR-collaborative-learning design guidance illustrated by a CAVE-based case study, not social-presence theory. Its placement in Strand F as a "CMC-to-VR social-presence bridge" is this cluster's own construction (P/anticipatory-application) — the paper itself never performs that bridge (see §7).

## 1. Thesis/Aim

De Back, Tinga and Louwerse argue that immersive virtual reality's potential to scaffold learning remains under-realized in educational practice because existing pedagogical frameworks (Dalgarno & Lee, cited here as 2010; Fowler, cited here as 2015; Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning, 2009/2014) are general and offer few concrete recommendations for immersive settings, especially collaborative ones (FE, p.5364–5366). Their aim is twofold: first, to extend these frameworks into specific, actionable design recommendations organized around cognitive load, collaborative learning, platform-specific affordances and limitations, and additional strategies; second, to demonstrate the recommendations' feasibility via a concrete, working example — a CAVE-based collaborative neuroanatomy environment already shown to produce learning gains in two prior empirical studies — plus a novel method of automated behavioral-performance logging that extends the value obtainable from any such environment (FE, p.5364, 5382). The paper is explicitly a design/implementation contribution, not a theory paper: its own stated payoff is removing barriers to adoption of immersive VLEs by giving designers and educators a worked template (FE, p.5382).

## 2. Structure

Seven unlabeled but internally signposted movements. **Introduction** (p.5364–5366) distinguishes non-immersive desktop VR from immersive VR (HMD- or CAVE-delivered) and states the paper's aim. **Theoretical framework** (p.5365–5369) reviews Dalgarno & Lee's and Fowler's frameworks, then opens three subsections — cognitive load, collaborative learning, and gamification — as the aspects judged "critical for IVR settings" but under-specified by the general frameworks. **Recommendations for designing immersive VLEs** (p.5368–5371) translates the framework into five categories of design guidance, condensed in the paper's Table 1. **Implementation** (p.5371–5375) details the two-year-developed CAVE neuroanatomy environment (Table 2, Figures 1–2). **Empirical support and automated performance analysis** (p.5375–5378) reports the two prior studies (n=40; n=158) and introduces the dynamic-logging model (Figures 3–4). **Discussion** (p.5378–5380) frames the contribution as an IVR-specific operationalization of existing frameworks and gestures toward physiological/eye-tracking extensions. A brief **Conclusion** (p.5382) closes.

## 3. Constructs

The construct axis this paper actually populates is narrower, and differently labeled, than Strand F's other members. Two points bear directly on how it must be read against the cluster's frozen §3 vocabulary.

**"Social presence" is absent.** A full-text check (confirmed at Phase 1 and rechecked here) finds the term never used, and none of the CMC-tradition Strand-F sources (Short et al. 1976; Gunawardena & Zittle, ped-sec-25; Garrison, Anderson & Archer, ped-sec-26; Tu, ped-sec-27; Biocca, Burgoon & Harms, ped-sec-29; Richardson et al., ped-sec-32) are cited anywhere in the reference list (FE). "Presence" tout court appears exactly once, glossed generically via Heeter (1992) and Steuer (1992) as "the feeling of existing inside a virtual environment" (FAITHFUL-De Back, p.5369) — the general/spatial sense of the term, invoked to justify pairing pre-training with a practice period, not a social or co-presence construct.

**Cognitive load (extraneous vs. germane)** is the paper's best-developed canonical construct. It adopts the standard three-load model (intrinsic, extraneous, germane) and Mayer's coherence, segmenting, and generative-processing principles as applied to IVR by Parong and Mayer (FAITHFUL-De Back, p.5366–5367), adding pre-training as a further germane-load-management strategy (FE, p.5367). The paper's own worked case violates one of these principles deliberately: it presents instructions in both audio and text simultaneously, "in violation of the redundancy principle of Mayer," justified post hoc by informal piloting showing that CAVE visibility constraints (a learner's body can occlude a wall-mounted text panel) made the violation net-beneficial rather than harmful (FAITHFUL-De Back, p.5373, 5378) — a design trade-off the paper itself flags as illustrative of "weighing the pros and cons of individual design decisions," not a general license (FE, p.5378).

**Embodiment / self-representation** and a bespoke, non-canonical **"collaborative learning"** construct (the paper's own term, following Dillenbourg 1999) carry the paper's second load of content. Embodiment is treated as a feature of learner interaction within Dalgarno and Lee's framework — natural motions, avatar realism, and (non-)verbal communication are argued to amplify collaborative learning specifically because they are richer under IVR than under desktop VR (FAITHFUL-De Back, p.5367–5368). "Collaborative learning" itself is operationalized procedurally (equal access, active/interdependent participation, visible interactions, turn-taking to limit social loafing; FE, p.5368–5369) rather than measured as a construct with an instrument — there is no collaboration or co-presence scale administered anywhere in the paper. Phase-2 judgment: this is conceptually adjacent to, but not codeable as, "social presence / co-presence" in the cluster's canonical sense; it is filed here as its own THEME rather than forced into that row (P/anticipatory-application).

**Engagement / behavioral proxies.** The automated-logging model's five metrics — leader time, leaders observed, distance walked, full head rotations, and face time — are framed as indicators of "behavioral engagement, reported to be positively related to academic achievement" (FAITHFUL-De Back, p.5376, citing Lei et al. 2018), and face time specifically as "an indicator of the level of social interaction" (FAITHFUL-De Back, p.5376). These are the paper's closest approach to anything resembling co-presence, but they remain unlabeled behavioral proxies, not a named construct, and the paper does not claim they measure social presence.

## 4. Conditions & design

The source's own platform is a **CAVE** (Cave Automatic Virtual Environment; walls, not an HMD), which the plan's §3 media-condition axis has no dedicated column for. Following the plan's own conditionsNote, this unit normalizes CAVE to **IVR (HMD)** for concordance purposes while flagging the mismatch — CAVE and HMD share "surrounding, embodied, immersive" status but differ in single- vs. multi-user visibility and in facial-expression transmission (FE, p.5364, 5373).

**Study 1** (De Back, Tinga, Nguyen & Louwerse 2020, cited not reprinted; n=40, participant-pool recruits unfamiliar with neuroanatomy) contrasts two conditions: collaborative CAVE use in two- to four-person groups vs. textbook learning (conventional instruction, control). Learning gains favored the CAVE condition, with the largest advantage among learners with low spatial ability (FE, p.5375, citing Carroll 1993 and Höffler 2010 for the spatial-ability construct).

**Study 2** (De Back, Tinga & Louwerse 2021, a companion paper cited not reprinted; n=158, undergraduate cognitive-science course) replicates the learning-gain finding and varies two design parameters within the IVR condition only, with no non-VR comparison arm: group size (one-, two-to-four-, five-to-six-person groups — all showed gains, smaller groups larger gains) and timing of application relative to the course (before/during/after — no effect found) (FE, p.5375–5376). These two variables do not map onto a single §3 condition column; they are logged here as within-condition design parameters rather than separate cells.

The **automated performance-logging model** (Figures 3–4) is not itself a media condition but an instrumentation contribution: it dynamically adapts to the feature set of different immersive technologies and outputs both a metrics dashboard and a visual replay of learner behavior, explicitly extending Chodos et al. (2014, fixed-category logging for non-immersive 2D environments) and implementing what Fardinpour and Reiners (2014) had only conceptualized (FAITHFUL-De Back, p.5375–5377).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Collaborative CAVE-based learning produced greater gains than textbook instruction in Study 1, with the benefit concentrated among learners with lower spatial ability (FE, p.5375).
- Study 2 replicated the gain at scale (n=158) across all tested group sizes, with an inverse group-size gradient (smaller groups, larger gains), and found no effect of when in the course the environment was used (FE, p.5375–5376).
- The redundancy-principle violation (simultaneous audio+text instruction) was judged net-beneficial in this specific CAVE context based on informal piloting, not a controlled A/B test — the paper is explicit that this is a context-dependent trade-off, not a general recommendation (FE, p.5373, 5378).
- Gamification elements were selectively retained: direct feedback on correctness benefited learners, but visible score displays were removed after piloting suggested they were "largely ignored," and a per-stage time limit was removed as potentially disadvantaging slower groups — both decisions justified via the coherence principle rather than via a formal comparative test (FAITHFUL-De Back, p.5374).
- The automated-logging model is presented as feasible and low-cost ("little effort is required to enable logging"), with the paper explicitly proposing — not yet demonstrating — its use to investigate relationships between behavioral metrics (e.g., distance walked, leader time) and learning gains, and between motion metrics and self-reported motion sickness (FE, p.5376–5377).
- The paper's own limiting claim: immersive technologies are "no universal panacea," to be adopted only where a genuine match exists between intended learning outcomes and IVR's specific affordances (FAITHFUL-De Back, p.5378).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit's most consequential analytical fact is negative: **it does not perform the CMC-to-VR social-presence bridge that Strand F's design originally assumed for it.** The strand was built around a genuine gap in the cluster's §3 construct axis (no dedicated source grounded "social presence / co-presence"), and this paper's title and abstract made it look, before full-text checking, like the natural VR-side bridge to the CMC-tradition sources (Gunawardena & Zittle, Garrison/Anderson/Archer, Tu, Biocca/Burgoon/Harms, Richardson et al.). Full-text verification shows otherwise: the paper's interlocutors are entirely the VR-pedagogy-framework literature already elsewhere in this cluster (Dalgarno & Lee/ped-sec-04, Fowler/ped-sec-05, Parong & Mayer 2018/ped-sec-08, Merchant et al./ped-sec-16, plus Mayer's multimedia-learning theory as an external, non-cluster interlocutor), and it treats "presence" only in Heeter and Steuer's general/spatial sense. Any statement that this paper "bridges" CMC social presence into VR is therefore **this cluster's own construction (P), not a connection De Back, Tinga and Louwerse make themselves** — the honest analytical role is to cluster this unit primarily against **Strand B/C content** (VLE design frameworks, cognitive load, media-specific affordances), while retaining its Strand-F folder placement as a bibliographic/administrative fact rather than a substantive claim about its argument.

Where this unit *does* offer genuine, non-manufactured bridge value is design-practical: its CAVE case study's mandatory small-group collaboration and its automated engagement metrics (face time, leader time, distance walked, head rotations) resonate with the VLE (King–Salvo) entry's own mandatory group-assignment design — relevant to the VLE-proximity-voice correction bearing on `vle-01-deployment-survey`'s F7 finding — and with `vle-03-exemplar-bank`'s co-watching exemplars *(anticipatory-application)*. Its behavioral-logging approach (a non-self-report, continuous engagement measure) is a useful design precedent for `vle-04-mark-attention-span`'s attention-span analysis, though no direct empirical overlap exists between the two *(anticipatory-application)*. The stronger genuine Strand-F bridge candidate, per the cluster's own manifest correction, is ped-sec-34 (VanderMeer et al. 2023), which at least cites a De Back companion paper directly and surveys VR-collaborative learning more broadly — though it too does not engage the CMC-tradition canon.

Two non-blocking citation-year discrepancies carry over from Phase 1: this paper cites Dalgarno and Lee as "(2010)" against ped-sec-04's manifest year of 2009, and Fowler as "(2015)" against ped-sec-05's manifest year of 2014 (both likely online-first/print-year artifacts, unresolved, flagged not corrected). A further correction discovered at this phase: the task's suggested cluster-author list named ped-sec-13 (Parong & Mayer 2020) as a likely citation target, but the paper in fact repeatedly and exclusively cites Parong and Mayer's **2018** paper (coherence/segmenting/generative-processing principles, two experiments) — that is ped-sec-08, not ped-sec-13. Ped-sec-13 (2020) is not cited anywhere in this source. Likewise, ped-sec-07 (Mayer et al., "Promise and Pitfalls," 2022) is not cited; the paper's extensive Mayer citations are all to Mayer's 2009/2014 multimedia-learning-theory works, an external (non-cluster) interlocutor distinct from ped-sec-07.

**Not treated:** social presence / co-presence as a named, theorized, or measured construct (see §3, §7 — the central methodological caution for this unit); situational interest; self-efficacy as a measured outcome (mentioned once in passing via Merchant et al., not operationalized here); cybersickness as a measured outcome (raised only as a suggested future-work variable, p.5377); any SEM/process model of the immersion→presence→learning chain; and the CMC-tradition social-presence canon (Short et al., Gunawardena & Zittle, Garrison/Anderson/Archer, Tu, Biocca/Burgoon/Harms, Kreijns/Kirschner/Jochems, Richardson et al./Richardson & Swan) — none of it appears in this paper's reference list.
