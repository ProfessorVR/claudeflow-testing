# ped-sec-21 — Potkonjak, Gardner, Callaghan, Mattila, Guetl, Petrović & Jovanović, "Virtual laboratories for education in science, technology, and engineering: A review" (2016)

**Type:** journal review article (engineering education) · **Discipline:** engineering / educational technology · **Strand:** E — reviews, agendas & constraints · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** the cluster's technology-side survey of what a "virtual laboratory" concretely is (platforms, criteria, projects) — a bill-of-materials companion to Strand B's affordance/pedagogy theory and Strand D's professional-education simulation studies, and an explicit self-declared *non*-pedagogy paper.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Potkonjak et al. argue that Science-Technology-Engineering (STE) education lags other fields in distance/e-learning uptake because laboratory exercises resist remote delivery, and that of the two available fixes — remote-access to a physical lab, or a fully software-based virtual lab — the virtual-lab route is preferable on cost, scalability, flexibility, and safety grounds (FE, p.310–311). The paper's stated aim is a technology-focused critical survey: it derives four evaluation criteria from the requirement that operating a virtual lab must "feel like" working with real, authentic devices in a real, authentic space, then scores roughly nineteen existing virtual-lab systems and six virtual-world platforms against those criteria, closing with a trends section and an explicit disclaimer that "the important issues of Learning Theories and Pedagogy" are reserved for a separate publication (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.310, 325). The paper is therefore best read as an inventory-and-criteria document, not a learning-outcomes study — no participants, no measures, no statistics appear anywhere in the text (FE).

## 2. Structure

Seven sections. §1 Introduction situates virtual labs within distance/e-learning trends in STE and states the technology-not-pedagogy scope (p.310–311). §2 Advantages and drawbacks of virtual laboratories lists benefits (cost savings, flexibility, multiple simultaneous access, reconfigurable parameters, damage resistance/learning-from-mistakes, "making the unseen seen") against drawbacks (computational/modeling burden; a weakened sense of seriousness because "nothing really bad" can happen; the eventual need for real equipment) (p.311–312). §3 Requirements/criteria derives four evaluation criteria (C1–C4, defined below) from a single authenticity requirement, and notes haptic feedback as a plausible fifth criterion excluded because no reviewed system supports it (p.312–313). §4 State of the art in virtual laboratories — "the heart of this paper" — surveys roughly nineteen systems grouped as General Initiatives, Science-Physics, Process Technology, Engineering-non-robotic, and Engineering-Robotics, scored against C1–C4 in a summary table (p.313–320). §5 State of the art in virtual worlds reviews six platforms (Second Life, RealXtend, OpenSimulator, Open Wonderland, Open Cobalt, Unity 3D) as candidate substrates for the C4 collaborative-space criterion, and supplies the paper's operational definitions of virtual reality (VR), virtual environment (VE), multi-user virtual environment (MUVE), and virtual learning environment (VLE) (p.321–325). §6 Trends and future advances argues C2 (dynamics) and C4 (virtual space) are rarely satisfied together, proposes a two-level object-library-plus-shared-space architecture, and gestures toward a future "mixed" lab combining physically- and virtually-present students, flagging consumer HMDs (Oculus Rift) as an emerging development (p.324–325). §7 Conclusion recaps the five-group classification (20 systems total), reaffirms virtual labs as an initial-step complement to hands-on training rather than a full replacement given the current state of the art, and re-brackets pedagogy as future work (p.325).

## 3. Constructs

- **representational fidelity** — the paper's central organizing construct, operationalized directly as two of its four criteria: C1 (interface fidelity — must be "identical to the corresponding real devices") and C3 (visualization — must "feel like… looking at a real authentic thing") (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.312).
- **agency / interactivity** — operationalized as C2, behavioral/dynamic equivalence: the virtual system's state and control variables must behave like the physical system, distinguished at a "lower level" (operator training — kinematics only) and a "higher level" (why the system behaves as it does — full dynamics) (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.312–313). Also treated concretely across the surveyed systems as user-adjustable parameters (motor replacement, controller design, custom control blocks) (FE, p.320–321).
- **social presence / co-presence** — operationalized as C4, the 3D collaborative laboratory space "for communication and collaboration among students and with the lab supervisor," and elaborated via avatars, text/voice chat, and shared-workspace features across the virtual-world platforms in §5 (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.312, 321–324).
- **embodiment / self-representation** — treated via the avatar concept threaded through §5's platform survey (Second Life "Residents," realXtend avatars, Open Wonderland headset/mic-driven avatars) and via the Stevens Institute system's user-designed avatars (FE, p.316, 321–324).
- **immersion (technological)** — treated definitionally rather than empirically: the paper adopts Pan, Cheok, Yang, Zhu & Shi's (2006) definition of VR as computer graphics plus display/interface devices producing "the effect of immersion in the interactive 3D computer-generated environment" (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.321, quoting Pan et al.), and flags consumer HMDs (Oculus Rift) only as an emerging future-trends note, not as a system under review (FE, p.325).
- **learning outcomes: procedural / skills** — the paper's implicit outcome target throughout §4: virtual labs are consistently framed as vehicles for skill acquisition (robot programming, kinematics/dynamics understanding, control-system tuning) rather than declarative knowledge, though the paper reports no outcome measurement of its own (FE, p.311, 320–321).

## 4. Conditions & design — review of virtual-lab technologies for STE

This unit is itself a **review / meta-analytic aggregate** (no primary data collected); its "design" is the four-criterion rubric applied to a structured technology survey.

**Criteria (C1–C4).** C1 interface fidelity; C2 dynamic-behavior equivalence (lower level = kinematics/operator training; higher level = full dynamics/why-questions); C3 visualization (3D CAD modeling, synchronized motion); C4 collaborative 3D space (avatars, communication). A fifth candidate criterion — haptic/force feedback — is explicitly excluded because zero surveyed systems support it (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.312–313).

**Systems surveyed, by group (Table 1, p.313–314; twenty systems total per the paper's own tally):**
- *General initiatives (2):* LiLa (Library of Labs, EU-funded, Univ. Stuttgart) and the Go-Lab Project (EU-funded, Univ. Twente; portals to ESA/CERN/NUCLIO online labs).
- *Science-physics (2):* VccSSe (multi-country EU project; Boyle's/Charles's/Gay-Lussac's-law simulations) and TEALsim (MIT; electromagnetism, e.g. the "Falling Coil" simulation).
- *Process technology (2):* the RWTH Aachen "Ironmaking" blast-furnace VSM system and Oregon State's Virtual CVD Learning Platform.
- *Engineering — non-robotic (5):* a Slovak University of Technology control-systems lab (tank/heat-exchanger/reactor plants); a Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya multiplatform lab (magnetic levitator, inverted pendulum-cart); TriLab (Loughborough, LabVIEW, triple-access hands-on/virtual/remote); a Firat University Virtual Electric Machine Laboratory; and the Stevens Institute of Technology machine-dynamics lab (avatars, MMO-style environment, pre/post quizzes).
- *Engineering — robotics (9), the paper's declared emphasis:* RoboUALab (Univ. Alicante; virtual + tele-operated real robot); a Tec de Monterrey mobile-robotics lab with an intelligent tutoring system built on a probabilistic student model; ROBOMOSP (Pontificia Javeriana Univ., Colombia; multibody dynamics, cross-platform); VCIMLAB (Eastern Mediterranean Univ.; CIM/manufacturing-cell simulation scored C1–C4 = yes/yes/yes/yes, the review's only full-marks system); VirtualRobot (DISA-UPV, Spain; general robotics platform since 1998); VLR (the authors' own system, Univ. Belgrade; five industrial-robot models, exposed inner mechanisms); USARSim (Carnegie Mellon/Pittsburgh; built on the Unreal Engine game engine, originally for search-and-rescue); and two commercial packages, COSIMIR (Univ. Dortmund/industry) and RoboLogix (Logic Design Inc.).

**Virtual-world platforms (§5, evaluated as C4 substrates, not scored in Table 1):** Second Life (launched 2003, Linden Lab, 37M+ registered users, education use surveyed via Warburton 2009), RealXtend (2007, own viewer since 2010, C#/Python), OpenSimulator (open-source, SL-protocol-compatible, standalone/grid modes), Open Wonderland (Java, ex-Sun-Microsystems "Project Wonderland," shared applications), Open Cobalt (Squeak/Smalltalk, peer-to-peer, no central server), and Unity 3D (Denmark, 2005, commercial game engine, client-based).

**Governing definitions adopted (not this paper's own coinages, but load-bearing for it):** VR = computer graphics + display/interface devices producing immersion in an interactive 3D environment (Pan et al., 2006); VE = the resulting environment; MUVE = a 3D environment supporting multiple avatar-represented users (e.g., Second Life); VLE = the education-purposed subtype of MUVE (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.321–322).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- No existing system in the survey satisfies C2 (dynamics) and C4 (collaborative virtual space) simultaneously and well; the two criteria trade off against each other across nearly the entire twenty-system sample, with VCIMLAB the sole full-marks exception (FE, p.324).
- Virtual-world platforms remain "still rarely used" as an integral part of virtual laboratories despite offering the richest C4 affordances, indicating an unrealized integration opportunity rather than a solved problem (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.321, 324).
- The paper's own methodological/practical caveat: criterion relevance is application-dependent — training robot *operators* privileges C1 (interface) and kinematics, while teaching robot *theory* privileges C2 (dynamics), so no single scoring is universally "best" (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.314, Table 1 note).
- Robotics is treated as the STE sub-field of choice for demonstrating virtual-lab affordances generally, on the stated ground that its multidisciplinary character (mechanical, electrical, programming, physics) makes it representative of engineering education broadly (FE, p.309, 315).
- The future direction the authors themselves propose is architectural (a two-level object-library-plus-shared-space system) and social (a "mixed" lab co-presencing physically- and virtually-attending students via avatars and, potentially, consumer HMDs), rather than pedagogical (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.324–325).
- The paper's closing normative claim is explicitly hedged: virtual labs are currently best understood as an initial-step complement to, not full replacement for, hands-on training with real equipment — a position the authors "agree with… based on the current state of the art" while anticipating it may narrow over time (FAITHFUL-Potkonjak, p.325).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit supplies Part III's construct-x-media concordance with a technology-side vocabulary — the C1–C4 criteria — for describing *what kind of desktop-3D system* the dissertation's own VLE is, independent of any learning-outcome claim. Because the VLE deployment survey (`vle-01-deployment-survey`) documents its own APP-stratum friction profile as substantially an install/hardware-threshold problem (F1) before any content or presence question arises, Potkonjak et al.'s C1 (interface fidelity) and computational-burden drawback (§2) offer an independent technology-literature basis for that reading: friction at the installer is a known, general cost of the fully-software-based virtual-lab route this cluster's Strand-C/D sources otherwise treat as pedagogically settled *(anticipatory-application)*. The paper's C4 criterion and its six-platform virtual-world survey (§5) is the most direct technology-vocabulary match to the VLE's own co-watching/spatial-proximity-voice design (per the corrected F7 reading in `05-findings.md`): Potkonjak et al.'s own finding that C2 and C4 are rarely jointly satisfied is a caution worth carrying into any claim that the dissertation's VLE achieves both dynamics and collaborative space at once — that claim would need independent verification, not an assumption from this review *(anticipatory-application)*. The paper's explicit, self-declared bracketing of pedagogy is itself analytically useful: it marks a genuine boundary between the technology-adequacy literature (this unit, and Strand E generally) and the learning-affordance literature (Strand B) that the dissertation should keep distinct when citing "virtual laboratories" research, rather than treating a technology review as if it made a learning-outcome claim *(anticipatory-application)*. Finally, the paper's own drawback about "nothing really bad can happen" in a virtual lab — producing a video-game attitude that weakens seriousness/responsibility (§2) — bears directly on Part III's own recurring theme (Strand C's affective/cognitive-load debate axis) of whether low-stakes virtuality helps or hinders engaged learning; this is a technology-literature version of a debate this cluster documents from the psychology side in Strand C, and the two should be cross-referenced rather than treated as independent findings *(anticipatory-application)*.
