# ped-sec-18 — Dubovi, Levy & Dagan, "Now I Know How! The Learning Process of Medication Administration Among Nursing Students with Non-Immersive Desktop Virtual Reality Simulation" (2017)

**Type:** empirical journal article (quasi-experimental) · **Discipline:** nursing / health-professions education (educational technology) · **Strand:** D — simulation-based & desktop-3D learning in professional education · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** the cluster's clearest single-source demonstration that a non-immersive desktop-VR simulation can produce both strong procedural learning gains and presence scores matching or exceeding published immersive-VR norms — directly germane to Part III's desktop-3D vs. video-only strata.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Dubovi, Levy & Dagan report on PILL-VR (Pharmacology Inter-Leaved Learning Virtual Reality), a desktop, non-immersive VR simulation built in OpenSim to teach nursing students the medication-administration procedure, and argue that such a low-cost, low-immersion platform can close part of the theory-practice gap in professional education by supporting significantly greater, longer-retained conceptual and procedural learning than a matched lecture-based curriculum (FE, p.16–17). Their second, process-oriented aim is to characterize *how* PILL-VR facilitates that learning — via participants' sense of presence in the environment and their in-environment performance across two practice scenarios — rather than merely certifying an outcome difference (FE, p.19). The paper's most conceptually loaded claim, developed fully only in the discussion, is that presence in VR learning environments is "not a unique attribute of the environment" produced by immersive hardware, but is instead "induced by the fidelity of representation and the high degree of interaction and user control" (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.26) — a claim the authors use to explain why a desktop system nonetheless generated presence scores comparable to Witmer & Singer's (1998) original immersive-VR benchmarks.

## 2. Structure

1. **Introduction (§1–1.4, p.17–19)** — frames the theory-practice gap in nursing education (medication-administration error rates, limited clinical placement access), reviews non-immersive vs. immersive VR and Witmer & Singer's (1998) four presence factors (control, realism, distraction, sensory input), surveys inconsistent meta-analytic evidence on non-immersive VR in health-care education (Cook, Erwin, & Triola, 2010 vs. Consorti et al., 2012), and states two research questions: (1) PILL-VR vs. lecture-based learning gains at post-test and a five-month delayed post-test; (2) how PILL-VR facilitates learning via sense of presence and in-environment performance.
2. **Methods (§2.1–2.5, p.19–23)** — quasi-experimental pretest-intervention-posttest comparison-group design (non-randomized, diffusion-motivated); experimental group (n=82 of 104 enrolled) trained 3 hours with PILL-VR, comparison group (n=47 of 73 enrolled, drawn from the prior academic year) received an equivalent-duration lecture from two pharmacists; describes the PILL-VR architecture (hospital-ward island, nurse-avatar, 45-minute acclimation phase, two scenarios A/B) and the three instruments (MAP knowledge questionnaire, Witmer & Singer Presence Questionnaire, worksheets + n=10 screen-video recordings).
3. **Results (§3.1–3.2, p.23–25)** — reports significantly higher MAP learning gains for the PILL-VR group across the overall score and all subscales, retention sustained to five months, video/worksheet evidence of an in-VR practice/error-correction effect between scenarios A and B, high Presence Questionnaire scores matching or exceeding Witmer & Singer's immersive-VR norms, and a small but significant positive correlation between the Involved/Control presence subscale and learning gains.
4. **Discussion (§4–4.2, p.25–27)** — interprets the findings through Fitts & Posner's (1967)/Anderson's (1983, 2009) three-stage skill-acquisition model and dual-channel/multimedia-learning theory (Baddeley et al., 2009; Clark & Paivio, 1991; Mayer, 2014), reframes presence as fidelity/control-induced rather than immersion-induced, states limitations (non-randomization, dropout, an instrument asymmetry between groups, small video subsample), and concludes that VR simulation functions as a safe, low-cost mechanism for "deliberate practice."

## 3. Constructs

- **Presence (spatial/physical).** Measured via the Hebrew-validated (Kizony, 2006) Witmer & Singer (1998) Presence Questionnaire, three subscales (Involved/Control, Natural, Interface Quality). All three subscale means (5.2±0.7; 4.7±1.03; 5.0±1.1) were higher than or comparable to Witmer & Singer's own immersive-VR norms (5.2±0.8; 4.1±1.1; 4.8±1.1) (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.24). The discussion generalizes this into the paper's central presence claim: presence is induced by representational fidelity and interactive control rather than by immersive hardware per se (FE, p.26), explicitly citing Lee, Wong & Fung (2010) as a companion desktop-VR finding.
- **Agency / interactivity (operationalized as "sense of control").** The Involved/Control PQ subscale is treated as the presence factor most proximate to learning; the discussion frames the 45-minute acclimation phase (free exploration: moving, opening cabinets, picking up equipment) as the mechanism by which students gain this control before the graded scenarios begin (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.20, 24, 26).
- **Cognitive load (extraneous vs. germane).** Explained via dual-channel/multimedia-learning theory: PILL-VR's multisensory (visual + auditory) cueing is argued to reduce extraneous cognitive load and thereby support the "cognitive stage" of skill acquisition, drawing on Mayer's (2014) signaling/cueing principle and Baddeley, Eysenck & Anderson (2009) working-memory theory (FE, p.25).
- **Learning outcomes: procedural / skills.** The MAP questionnaire's Medication Administration Sequence subscale shows the largest learning gain of the three subscales; video-coded performance in scenario A shows a gap between complete declarative knowledge (9/10 students answered the worksheet item correctly) and partial procedural performance (9/10 nonetheless selected the wrong medication in-VR), narrowing to near-zero errors by scenario B (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.23–24).
- **Learning outcomes: retention & transfer.** Learning gains held from the two-month post-test to a five-month delayed post-test (repeated-measures ANOVA, Greenhouse-Geisser corrected, F(1.62,113.4)=251.8, p<.001; Bonferroni post hoc: pre-post significant, post/delayed-post non-significant, p=.14) (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.23).
- **Representational fidelity.** Named directly as one of two sources (alongside interaction/control) of the paper's presence account, displacing immersion/hardware as the operative variable (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.26).
- **Embodiment / self-representation.** Present only thinly: students "enter the PILL-VR environment as a nurse-avatar" and are invited to "explore his/her avatar-body" during acclimation (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.20); the paper does not theorize avatar embodiment beyond this descriptive mention — treat as instances-in-text, not a developed construct (P/anticipatory-application).

## 4. Conditions & design

**Design.** Quasi-experimental pretest-intervention-posttest comparison-group design; non-randomized by explicit choice, to reduce diffusion of the PILL-VR treatment into the comparison cohort and to avoid resentful demoralization — the comparison group was drawn from the prior academic year (2014–2015) rather than run concurrently with the experimental group (2015–2016) (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.19).

**Sample.** Experimental group: 104 enrolled, 82 (78%) completers (both pre- and post-test). Comparison group: 73 enrolled, 47 (65%) completers. No significant demographic or baseline-achievement differences between groups (Table 1); no significant pre-test differences between completers and non-completers in either group (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.19, 23).

**Intervention conditions.**
- **Desktop-3D / non-immersive VR (PILL-VR):** OpenSim-built hospital-ward environment; nurse-avatar; two rooms (patient room, medication room); 45-minute free-exploration acclimation phase; two graded scenarios (A, B) requiring assessment → order interpretation → medication preparation → administration → monitoring; 3 hours total training; embedded video feedback and worksheets (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.20).
- **Conventional instruction (control):** lecture-based curriculum, two pharmacists, PowerPoint, 3–4 hours (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.19).

**Instruments.** MAP (Medication Administration Procedure) knowledge questionnaire — study-built, 13 multiple-choice items, three subscales (Medication Administration Sequence; Basic Procedural Concepts; Reasoning and Accountability, the last added mid-study and thus absent from the comparison group's pre-test), Cronbach's α=0.78 (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.21). Presence Questionnaire (Witmer & Singer, 1998; Hebrew validation Kizony, 2006, α=0.89), three subscales (Involved/Comparison [sic, = Involved/Control], Natural, Interface Quality), 7-point Likert, this study's α=0.88 (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.21–22). Worksheets and Camtasia-recorded screen video for a randomly selected n=10 experimental-group subsample, used to code declarative-vs-procedural performance, error type/count, and completion time across scenarios A and B (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.22).

**Key statistics.** Overall MAP learning gain: PILL-VR group Mdn=62 vs. comparison Mdn=0 (Mann-Whitney U=576, p<.001, effect size r=0.58) (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.23, Table 2). Reasoning/Accountability subscale (post-test only, asymmetric pretest coverage): unpaired t(127)=3.8, p<.001, Cohen's d=0.91 (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.23). Scenario A→B error reduction: median errors 3.0→0 (Wilcoxon Z=2.5, p<.05); time-on-task 4.0→0.75 min (Z=2.3, p<.05) (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.24). Presence–learning correlation: overall MAP score × Involved/Control subscale, Pearson r=0.211 (p<.05); Medication Administration Sequence subscale × Involved/Control, r=0.231 (p<.05); all other PQ-subscale × MAP-subscale correlations non-significant (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.24, Table 3).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- PILL-VR produced significantly higher conceptual/procedural learning gains than lecture-based instruction on the overall MAP score and on every subscale, with the largest gain on Administration Sequence — the most procedurally sequenced subscale (FE, p.23).
- Learning gains were retained without significant decline from the two-month post-test to a five-month delayed post-test (FE, p.23).
- Declarative and procedural knowledge dissociate within the VR environment itself: near-complete worksheet-based declarative knowledge co-occurred with frequent in-VR procedural errors (checking brand name, checking expiry date) on first attempt, which the authors read through an "error recovery" lens as valuable, self-correcting feedback rather than mere failure (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.23–25, citing Henneman, Blank, Gawlinski, & Henneman, 2006).
- A non-immersive desktop system produced presence scores matching or exceeding Witmer & Singer's (1998) own immersive-VR benchmarks, extending Witmer & Singer's own unconfirmed 1998 hypothesis that presence increases learning (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.24, 26, quoting Witmer & Singer's admission "we have no direct evidence to support our contention," p.239) and aligning with Tüzün & Özdinç (2016).
- The presence–learning correlation, though small (r≈0.21–0.23), is offered as a design principle: "ensuring that participants gain sufficient control of the environment is an important condition for learning" (FAITHFUL-Dubovi, p.26).
- The authors explicitly relocate presence's causal source from immersion/hardware to representational fidelity plus interactive control — the paper's most transferable theoretical claim for lower-cost VR deployment (FE, p.26).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit gives Part III its clearest single-source empirical warrant that a non-immersive desktop-3D system — structurally the same media register as the VLE's APP stratum — can generate presence scores at or above published immersive-VR benchmarks, directly bearing on `vle-01-deployment-survey`'s F7 finding (APP stratum presence 4.3 vs. VID stratum 1.2 per-100) *(anticipatory-application)*. Where F7 documents a presence gap between the VLE's app-users and video-only users from self-report codes, Dubovi et al. supply an independent mechanism for why the app side of that gap should be expected: presence tracks representational fidelity and interactive control, not immersion tier, so a desktop-3D deployment is not structurally disadvantaged relative to head-mounted VR on presence grounds *(anticipatory-application)*. The paper's declarative/procedural dissociation — complete worksheet knowledge alongside erroneous first-attempt in-VR performance — offers Part III a cautionary vocabulary for interpreting any future learning-outcome or task-completion channel drawn from the VLE or the (as-yet-unbuilt) Boredom Experiment dataset: apparent task success or failure on a single pass may reflect the associative stage of skill acquisition rather than settled competence or its absence *(anticipatory-application)*. The correlation finding (control-subscale presence predicting learning, r≈0.21–0.23) is worth citing cautiously alongside `bor-sec-13`'s Slaby-derived caution against over-reading affective/attunement signals: the correlation here is real but small, and the paper's own design-principle claim ("ensure sufficient control") should not be inflated into a strong causal presence→learning warrant, since Witmer & Singer's parallel 1998 hypothesis remained similarly unconfirmed for sixteen years *(anticipatory-application)*. The `grounds-measure` edge from this unit to the Boredom Experiment dataset was **retired 2026-07-16** (rescope): this unit's warrant serves Part III's VLE evaluation (its `bridges-to-part-iii`/`bridges-to-vle` edges), not the boredom *lab* dataset.

**Not treated:** social presence / co-presence, situational interest, self-efficacy, engagement / flow, cybersickness & physical discomfort, home vs. classroom setting, and any IVR (HMD) condition — the study contrasts desktop-3D VR only against lecture-based instruction, never against head-mounted-display VR.
