# ped-sec-27 — Tu, "The Measurement of Social Presence in an Online Learning Environment" (2002)

**Type:** journal-article secondary reading (educational technology / distance-education measurement) · **Discipline:** educational technology, computer-mediated communication (CMC) instrument development · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F's methodological corrective — a direct, named critique of both prior social-presence instruments in the cluster (Short, Williams & Christie 1976 and Gunawardena & Zittle 1997, ped-sec-25) and the source of a new 59-item measure (the Social Presence and Privacy Questionnaire, SPPQ) built to fix their gaps.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Tu's article argues that no existing instrument adequately captures the complexity of social presence in a computer-mediated-communication (CMC) learning environment (FE, p.34). Its target is explicitly two-fold: nearly all prior studies adopted Short, Williams & Christie's (1976) four-item semantic-differential scale (personal-impersonal, sensitive-insensitive, warm-cold, sociable-unsociable), and the sole prior departure — Gunawardena & Zittle's (1997) instrument, in-cluster as ped-sec-25 — is judged an improvement that nonetheless still omits key variables (privacy, recipients, topics) and includes some items tailored to specific student groups (FE, p.34). Tu's guiding claim is that social presence theory was never designed for CMC in the first place — it was originally studied in face-to-face, audio, and closed-circuit-television encounters — and that CMC's distinctive characteristics (multiple identities, anonymity) demand a purpose-built measure rather than a borrowed one (FE, p.34-35). The article's own contribution is methodological rather than theoretical: it develops and validates the Social Presence and Privacy Questionnaire (SPPQ), a 59-item instrument spanning social presence and a related privacy dimension, and reports its factor structure from an empirical validation sample (FE, p.38-43).

## 2. Structure

The article runs continuously without numbered sections but is internally organized into seven functional movements.

1. **Overview of the problem** (p.34-35) — states the instrument-adequacy thesis; introduces the three difficulties with Short et al.'s (1976) scale and the residual gaps in Gunawardena & Zittle's (1997) alternative; frames CMC as requiring its own measure.
2. **Literature review: Social Presence** (p.34-38) — surveys prior findings on social presence's instructional significance (Hackman & Walker 1990; Gunawardena & Zittle 1997; Perse et al. 1992), tracing three developmental stages: the theory's emergence, its application to CMC generally, and its application within education specifically.
3. **Online Privacy sub-review** (p.35-36) — a parallel review of five privacy-adjacent constructs (feeling of privacy, illusion of privacy, risk-taking behavior, nonchalant attitude, system privacy) that feed directly into the SPPQ's privacy subscale.
4. **Existing Studies on Social Presence** (p.36-38) — chronicles the theory's three stages in more empirical detail: emergence (Champness 1972a/b; Christie 1973/1974, all lab studies separating a "social presence" factor from an "Aesthetic Appeal" factor); application to CMC (Garramone, Harris & Anderson 1986 on political bulletin boards; Steinfield 1986 on organizational e-mail); application in education (Perse et al. 1992; Gunawardena 1995; Gunawardena & Zittle 1997).
5. **Weakness of Existing Studies** (p.38-39) — names five limitations motivating the SPPQ: unclear definition, inadequate instruments, non-education samples, single-CMC-system designs, and artificial laboratory time constraints.
6. **Method** (p.38-40) — reports SPPQ development (adapted from Steinfield's 1986 CMC-attitude instrument and Witmer's 1997 perceived-privacy instrument), content validation (5-expert item-matching panel with three "lemon" control items), and construct validation (N=310, exploratory factor analysis).
7. **Results and Discussion** (p.40-44) — reports the five-factor solution, item-level descriptive findings for each factor across three CMC channels (e-mail, bulletin board, real-time discussion), and the social-presence/privacy correlation.

## 3. Constructs

- **social presence / co-presence** — the article's central and namesake construct, here explicitly re-operationalized rather than merely theorized: Tu retains Short et al.'s (1976) definition ("the degree of salience of another person in an interaction and the consequent salience of an interpersonal relationship," p.38) as the field's inherited starting point, but treats it as *empirically incomplete* — his own factor-analytic results resolve social presence into three components (social context, online communication, interactivity) rather than a single undifferentiated construct (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.38, 40-43). This tripartite decomposition is this source's principal contribution to the construct's operational definition within the cluster.
- **agency / interactivity** — treated as one of the three constituent factors of social presence itself, not a separate construct: Tu's "interactivity" factor (pleasant, immediate-reply, responsive, comfortable-with-familiar-topics) measures users' perceived quality of exchange within a CMC channel, closest in the controlled vocabulary to agency/interactivity though narrower than an affordance-theoretic sense of agency (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.41, qualified by P/anticipatory-application: Tu's interactivity factor is a perceived-communication-quality measure, not a manipulated experimental condition).
- **online privacy (adjacent, non-§3 construct)** — not one of the fourteen canonical §3 rows, but load-bearing in this source: five privacy sub-constructs (feeling of privacy, illusion of privacy, risk-taking behavior, nonchalant attitude, system privacy) are reviewed and then instantiated as two of the SPPQ's five emergent factors (system privacy, feeling of privacy) (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.35-36, 42-43). Recorded here as a flagged non-canonical construct rather than force-mapped onto an ill-fitting §3 row (P/anticipatory-application).
- **measurement-instrument validity (methodological construct, non-§3)** — the article's actual object of study is not a substantive finding about learners but the adequacy of measurement itself: content validity (item-objective matching, "lemon" items), construct validity (exploratory factor analysis, Cronbach's alpha), and criterion correlation (social presence × privacy) are all treated as first-order objects of the argument, not incidental method detail (FE, p.38-40).

Not treated (in the §3 sense): presence (spatial/physical), immersion (technological), embodiment/self-representation, cognitive load, situational interest, self-efficacy, engagement/flow, learning outcomes (retention/transfer or procedural/skills), cybersickness, and representational fidelity — none of Tu's constructs concern spatial or embodied media at all; the article's entire object is text-based asynchronous/synchronous CMC (e-mail, bulletin board, real-time discussion), not IVR, desktop-3D, or 2D-video learning environments.

## 4. Conditions & design (measurement-critique methodology)

Strand F is not required to populate the §3 media-condition axis (IVR-HMD / desktop-3D / 2D), and this source confirms why: its own comparative conditions are three **CMC channel types** — e-mail, bulletin board, and real-time discussion — not visual/spatial media conditions, so **no §3 media-condition-axis cells are populated by this unit** (P/anticipatory-application, per plan scope).

The source's actual design is instrument-development-and-validation, reported in two stages (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.38-40):

- **Instrument construction.** The SPPQ (59 items total, including 12 demographic items) was assembled from two prior instruments — Steinfield's (1986) 16-item semantic-differential CMC-attitude scale (developed for business users) and Witmer's (1997) 32-item Likert perceived-privacy scale (developed for an online-newsgroup population discussing a specific topic) — plus additional items drawn from the literature review, then pruned of items specific to either donor population (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.38-39).
- **Content validation.** Eight content objectives (social presence, privacy, utility, ease of use, interactivity, language, CMC experience/competence, demographics) were rated by a five-expert panel performing an item-objective matching task, with three "lemon" items inserted as a check on panel accuracy; no panelist correctly flagged all three lemons, so no panelist was excluded. Match rates ranged 44%-87% across the eight objectives; items below 60% match were revised or dropped, and Objectives 3 (utility) and 4 (ease of use) — carried over from Steinfield's donor instrument but outside this study's intent — were discarded outright. A chi-square test of independence among the five experts' matching patterns, χ²(28) = 13.51, p < .05, fell below the reported critical value of 41.337, so the null hypothesis of no significant difference among experts was retained — i.e., the panel matched items consistently (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.39-40).
- **Construct validation sample.** N=310 in-service and pre-service teachers (166 responding online, 112 by paper-and-pencil), voluntary participation; majority aged 18-35, 82.6% Caucasian, 75.2% female, self-rated computer expertise mostly intermediate (64.5%) or novice (26.7%); accessed computers primarily at home (87.5%), classrooms (58.3%), and computer labs (55.7%) (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.40).
- **Analysis.** Exploratory factor analysis (orthogonal and oblique rotation, varimax for the reported solution) on 30 items, retaining factors with eigenvalues > 1.00 and confirmed by scree-plot inspection; three items (numbered 15, 17, 29 in the instrument) were dropped for loadings below a .45 cutoff (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.40, 43).

No manipulated experimental conditions, no control group, and no medium-comparison design in the §3 sense are present; this is a correlational, cross-sectional survey-validation study.

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Factor analysis of the 30 retained items yielded a **five-factor solution** accounting for 82.33% of variance, with Cronbach's alpha ranging .74-.85 across factors (social context .75, online communication .85, interactivity .78, system privacy .84, feeling of privacy .79) (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.40, 43).
- Three of the five factors — **social context, online communication, and interactivity** — are treated as the constituent dimensions of social presence proper; the remaining two — **system privacy and feeling of privacy** — as the instrument's privacy dimensions (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.42).
- Respondents rated CMC generally as social (79%), informal (78%), personal (59%), and comfortable with familiar people (84%), but only slightly sensitive (38%); e-mail was consistently rated highest on social-context, communication, and interactivity items relative to bulletin board and real-time discussion, with bulletin board rated lowest on conveying feeling/emotion and stimulation (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.40-42).
- The **social presence × privacy correlation was significant but weak**, r(319) = .30, p < .01; all five factors were significantly inter-correlated at p < .01, ranging from r = .146 (interactivity × feeling-of-privacy) to r = .596 (interactivity × system privacy) (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.43, Table 2).
- Tu's own interpretive conclusion: social presence in an online learning environment is a joint function of **media attributes (online communication), user perception (social context), and user activity/engagement (interactivity)** — not media attributes alone, contra a purely technology-determinist reading of the construct (FE, p.43).
- The privacy-social-presence correlation's weakness leaves privacy's status as a bona fide social-presence dimension **unresolved** rather than confirmed — Tu explicitly declines to claim privacy belongs inside the construct (FE, p.43).
- A "controversial finding": respondents rated CMC as not private and not confidential overall, yet still rated privacy as important to them and were willing to share personal/sensitive topics online regardless — a pattern Tu links to Witmer's (1997) Risk-Taking-Behavior finding (users feel secure despite low actual privacy) (FAITHFUL-Tu, p.43-44).
- Limitations acknowledged by the source itself: alpha values of .74-.85 are characterized as "medium-low"; the SPPQ is offered only as "a first step" toward measuring social presence, not a finished instrument; the three items dropped for low loading may conflate more than one variable and warrant further qualitative scrutiny; and the validation sample (in-service/pre-service teachers) should be extended to non-education populations (FE, p.43-44).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* Within the cluster, this unit's chief value is methodological-cautionary rather than substantive: it is the sharpest available demonstration that social-presence measurement is itself contested terrain, with Tu explicitly naming ped-sec-25 (Gunawardena & Zittle 1997) as an incomplete corrective to Short et al. (1976) and then proposing a third, still-provisional instrument in turn. Any dissertation use of "social presence" as a settled construct should route through this article's caution that even validated instruments here report only medium-low reliability (alpha .74-.85) and that the field's leading scales disagree about what belongs inside the construct (privacy notably unresolved) *(anticipatory-application)*.
