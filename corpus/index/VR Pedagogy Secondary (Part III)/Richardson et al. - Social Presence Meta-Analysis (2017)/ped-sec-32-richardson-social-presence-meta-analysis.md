# ped-sec-32 — Richardson, Maeda, Lv & Caskurlu, "Social Presence in Relation to Students' Satisfaction and Learning in the Online Environment: A Meta-Analysis" (2017)

**Type:** quantitative meta-analysis (random-effects) · **Discipline:** educational psychology / online-learning research (CMC tradition) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F's capstone synthesis — the cluster's single pooled, quantified answer to the social-presence-predicts-outcomes question that ped-sec-25 through ped-sec-28 each establish only at the level of one primary study.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Richardson, Maeda, Lv and Caskurlu set out to give a "holistic view" of social presence in fully online higher-education courses by pooling, for the first time, the accumulated CMC-tradition literature on social presence's relationship to two student outcomes — satisfaction and perceived learning — across the contexts, disciplines, and measurement scales that individual primary studies had never been jointly compared against (FE, p.402). The paper's organizing claim is that although social presence — defined here simply as "the ability to perceive others in an online environment" — has repeatedly been shown, study by study, to bear on motivation, participation, satisfaction, and learning, "very few researchers have attempted to look across contexts, disciplinary areas, or measures of social presence" (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.402), leaving the true strength and stability of the relationship unknown. Three research questions structure the synthesis: how strong is the social-presence/satisfaction correlation and how much does it vary across studies; how strong is the social-presence/perceived-learning correlation and how much does it vary; and what study-level conditions (scale type, audience, discipline, course length) moderate that variation (FE, p.406–407).

## 2. Structure

The article runs five numbered sections. **1. Introduction** (1.1–1.7, pp.402–407) frames online learning's growth and its face-to-face-lack critique, then traces social presence's lineage from the social-psychology nonverbal-communication literature (Argyle & Dean; Mehrabian) through Short, Williams & Christie's 1976 medium-salience coinage, Walther's CMC-era critique of face-to-face/CMC comparison methodology, and Gunawardena's 1995 relocation of social presence to communicators' perception rather than medium alone (FE, p.403). Table 1 catalogues fourteen competing definitions of social presence from Short et al. (1976) through Garrison (2011); Table 2 catalogues the self-report instruments in use, including the studies later pooled into the meta-analysis (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.404–406). Subsections 1.5.1–1.5.3 survey, narratively rather than quantitatively, social presence's reported relationships to satisfaction, to the satisfaction/perceived-learning pair jointly, and to traditional graded performance — the last of these explicitly bracketed as too thinly studied (only two primary studies) to synthesize (FE, p.405–406). **2. Method** (2.1–2.5, pp.407–410) details the search, inclusion criteria, coding scheme, effect-size extraction, dependent-effect-size handling, and the random-effects analytic model. **3. Results** (3.1–3.3, pp.410–413) reports the pooled correlations, moderator analyses, and sensitivity/publication-bias checks for both outcomes. **4. Discussion** (4.1–4.4, pp.413–416) interprets the findings, situates the course-length and discipline moderators against outside literature, discusses measurement heterogeneity across social-presence scales, and states limitations and strengths. **5. Conclusion** (p.416–417) closes with implications for course design and calls for improved reporting practices.

## 3. Constructs

**Social presence / co-presence** is the paper's organizing construct, defined for study purposes as "the ability to perceive others in an online environment" — a deliberately theory-agnostic gloss chosen to accommodate the fourteen competing definitions Table 1 catalogues, from Short, Williams & Christie's original medium-salience formulation through the CMC-era communicator-perception revisions (Walther; Gunawardena) to the Community of Inquiry framework's participant-projection language (Garrison, Anderson & Archer) (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.402, 404). The construct is not itself pooled as an effect size; rather, it is the fixed predictor side of both correlations the meta-analysis synthesizes, operationalized differently across the twenty-five primary studies by four scale families: Gunawardena & Zittle (1997, k=8 for satisfaction), Richardson & Swan (2003, k=3 for satisfaction), the Community of Inquiry survey (k=4), and single-study "other" scales (k=9) (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.410).

**Learning outcomes: retention & transfer**, operationalized here narrowly as self-report *perceived* learning (not measured achievement, retention, or grades), is one of the two pooled outcomes: fourteen of the twenty-five included studies reported a social-presence/perceived-learning correlation, contributing thirty extracted effect sizes reduced to twenty-six independent effect sizes after dependent-effect averaging (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.408–409). The authors explicitly justify this narrower scope: traditional academic performance (grades) was examined in "only a few studies" and is treated in §1.5.3 as background narrative rather than folded into the quantitative synthesis, because too few primary studies reported it comparably (FE, p.405–406).

**Affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation** appears via the paper's second pooled outcome, *satisfaction* — nineteen of the twenty-five studies reported this correlation, contributing twenty-eight extracted effect sizes reduced to twenty-six independent effect sizes (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.408–409). The authors frame satisfaction and perceived learning together as "affective" outcome measures, citing their own earlier methodological argument (Richardson, Maeda & Swan 2010) that such affective self-report measures are not intended as substitutes for cognitive/traditional learning outcomes but are frequently the outcome the primary literature actually targets (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.406).

**Engagement / flow** and **learning outcomes: procedural / skills** are not separately pooled; motivation and participation are named repeatedly in the introduction's literature survey as constructs social presence has been shown to influence (FE, p.402) but are not among the study's own extracted effect sizes — this meta-analysis's quantitative apparatus is confined to the satisfaction/perceived-learning pair.

## 4. Conditions & design

Design: random-effects meta-analysis (Hedges & Vevea's framework), chosen over a fixed-effect model on the grounds that the located studies are treated as a sample from a broader, only partially accessible target population rather than an exhaustive census (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.409). Search: systematic, 1992 (the year social presence was first applied to online-learning research) through May 2015, across EBSCO, PsycINFO, ERIC, Education Full Text, digital dissertations, Google Scholar, the CoI project website, and major education-conference archives (Sloan-C, AECT, AERA, AACE), supplemented by manual reference-list checking (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.407). This identified 98 candidate studies; three inclusion criteria (fully online higher-education setting; social presence as the explicit theoretical frame; reportable quantitative correlational data) narrowed the pool to 25 studies — 10 journal articles, 6 conference papers, 9 dissertations — after excluding 73 for insufficient statistical detail or fit (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.407–408). Sample: 3,862 participants across nineteen satisfaction-reporting studies; 3,051 participants across fourteen perceived-learning-reporting studies (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.408). Coding covered publication type, target audience (graduate/undergraduate/other), course length (6/8/16 weeks), discipline area (Education/Business/Other), and social-presence scale used, plus a six-index study-quality rubric; inter-coder agreement was 95.68% (satisfaction) and 96.43% (perceived learning) before full-team reconciliation to 100% (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.408). Effect-size handling: Fisher's r-to-z transformation with inverse-variance weighting; dependent effect sizes from the same or nested samples (six studies, including Richardson & Swan 2003 itself, reported multiple correlations) were averaged within study before pooling, yielding 52 total effect sizes — 26 independent per outcome (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.408–409). Heterogeneity was assessed via Q, I², and tau-squared; moderator analysis via Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) v2.0; publication bias via funnel plots, Orwin's Failsafe N, and Duval & Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.409–410). This is the concordance grid's "review / meta-analytic aggregate" media-condition column in its purest instance: no single medium or immersion level is contrasted — the "condition" being synthesized is variation *among* fully-online, asynchronous or blended course designs, not a media-comparison in the desktop-3D/IVR sense the cluster's Strand C catalogues.

## 5. Findings/Claims

- The weighted average correlation between social presence and satisfaction is r=0.56 (SE=0.02, k=26 independent effect sizes), a strong positive relationship, with significant heterogeneity: Q(25)=187.64, p<.001, I²=86.68%, tau²=0.048 (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.410).
- The weighted average correlation between social presence and perceived learning is r=0.51 (SE=0.05, k=26), also strong and positive, with even larger heterogeneity: Q(25)=345.77, p<.001, I²=92.77%, tau²=0.123 (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.411).
- Satisfaction correlation is significantly moderated by course length (Q(2)=18.26, p<.001: 6-week r=0.48/k=3, 8-week r=0.72/k=2, 16-week r=0.53/k=9), discipline area (Q(2)=11.93, p=.004: Education r=0.42/k=7 < "other" disciplines r=0.62/k=8, but > Business r=0.32/k=3), and scale type (Q(3)=15.89, p=.001: Richardson & Swan 2003 scale strongest at r=0.73/k=3, versus Gunawardena & Zittle r=0.58/k=8, CoI r=0.62/k=4, other scales r=0.39/k=9); publication type and target audience are non-significant moderators, and the correlation is statistically identical for graduate and undergraduate courses (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.410–411).
- Perceived-learning correlation is significantly moderated by course length (Q(2)=7.19, p=.027: 16-week r=0.58/k=11 > 8-week r=0.49/k=2 > 6-week r=0.45/k=3), discipline area (Q(2)=11.92, p=.003: Education r=0.42/k=7 < other r=0.62/k=8, > Business r=0.32/k=3), and target audience (Q(2)=7.69, p=.021: certification/mixed r=0.59/k=9 > graduate r=0.47/k=5 > undergraduate r=0.35/k=4); publication type and scale type are non-significant here (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.411–412).
- Publication-bias diagnostics indicate minimal threat: the satisfaction funnel plot is nearly symmetric (Orwin's Failsafe N=134); the perceived-learning funnel plot is mildly asymmetric (Failsafe N=82), but Duval & Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill, imputing three missing cases, would raise rather than lower the pooled estimate (to r=0.613), so the authors read the asymmetry as not undermining the finding (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.412).
- Discussion interprets the course-length pattern via Akyol & Garrison's (2008) finding that social presence "develops" unevenly over a course's duration — affective expression indicators are strongest early and wane as group-cohesion indicators rise — and via Akyol, Vaughan & Garrison's (2011) 6-week/13-week comparison showing significant differences in affective communication and group cohesion by course duration (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.413–414). Discipline-area differences are tentatively linked to Arbaugh, Bangert & Cleveland-Innes's (2010) hard/soft and pure/applied paradigm distinctions and to Gorsky et al.'s (2010) finding of higher science-course interaction rates than humanities-course rates (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.414).
- No two social-presence scales are equivalent: differing item coverage and reliability partly explain the differing correlations, and the Richardson & Swan (2003) scale — the strongest satisfaction-moderator category — also showed the highest reliability among the scales examined, a result the authors treat as consistent with, not merely coincidental to, its stronger correlation (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.415).
- Limitations: the small per-outcome sample (k=26 effect sizes) limits both generalizability and the power to fully explore heterogeneity; instructor behavior and course-design elements could not be examined as moderators because only 7 of 25 studies reported them; 57 of the original 98 identified studies had to be excluded for missing statistical detail, which the authors read as a field-wide reporting-quality problem rather than a flaw specific to this synthesis (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.415–416).
- The study positions itself as "the first attempt to synthesize the relevant studies to quantify the common effect size and the disparity among effect sizes" in the social-presence-and-outcomes literature (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.415).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* As Strand F's capstone, this meta-analysis is the cluster's single most citable quantitative summary of the social-presence-predicts-satisfaction/learning claim: where the strand's primary studies (ped-sec-25 through ped-sec-28, each treated separately below) each establish the relationship once, in one course setting, this unit hands Part III a pooled effect-size pair (r=0.56 satisfaction, r=0.51 perceived learning, both k=26) with documented moderators and documented heterogeneity, appropriate for citation whenever the dissertation needs "the" magnitude of this relationship rather than a single study's instance of it *(anticipatory-application)*.

This unit's citation network within Strand F is worth stating precisely rather than assumed wholesale. Direct textual verification against this extraction confirms the meta-analysis cites, and in three cases pools as primary data, four of this cluster's other Strand F units: **ped-sec-25** (Gunawardena & Zittle 1997) — cited in Table 1's definitions, Table 2's instrument catalogue, and §1.5.1's satisfaction-prediction narrative, and pooled as one of the 25 included studies (its scale is the second most-used, k=8 for satisfaction) (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.403–406, 410); **ped-sec-26** (Garrison, Anderson & Archer 1999) — cited in Table 1 as the source of the Community-of-Inquiry definition of social presence (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.404); **ped-sec-27** (Tu 2002, "The measurement of social presence in an online learning environment") — cited in Table 2 as the source of the SPPQ instrument (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.405); and **ped-sec-28** (Richardson & Swan 2003) — the same lead author's own fourteen-years-earlier primary study, cited repeatedly and pooled as one of the 25 included studies, contributing multiple perceived-learning correlations (flagged in §2.4's dependent-effect-size handling) and, in the moderator analysis, the scale category with the strongest satisfaction correlation (r=0.73) and the highest scale reliability of any measure examined (FAITHFUL-Richardson, p.408–410, 415). ****** UNVERIFIED: two units the calling instruction flagged as part of this strand's CMC-tradition literature — ped-sec-29 (Biocca, Burgoon & Harms 2003) and ped-sec-30 (Kreijns, Kirschner & Jochems 2003) — do NOT appear, under those specific titles/years, in this source's reference list (only the distinct papers Biocca & Harms 2002, Biocca, Harms & Gregg 2001, and Kreijns, Van Acker, Vermeulen & Van Buuren 2014 are cited); this unit therefore records no direct citation edge to ped-sec-29 or ped-sec-30, and Part III should not assume this meta-analysis synthesizes their specific findings.

This unit and ped-sec-28 form the cluster's clearest before/after pair on a single claim: ped-sec-28 establishes the social-presence-predicts-perceived-learning relationship on n=97 within one institution's asynchronous course template in 2003; this unit revisits and quantifies that same relationship, and Richardson & Swan's own scale, fourteen years later across the accumulated literature, with the Richardson & Swan scale emerging as the strongest and most reliable of the scale families this meta-analysis compares *(anticipatory-application)*. Structurally, this unit's pooled finding bridges to `vle-01-deployment-survey`'s F7 finding ("Presence & the WE"), for the same reason and with the same caution ped-sec-28 already registers: vle-01 is IVR/desktop-3D deployment-survey data, while every study this meta-analysis pools is asynchronous or web-based CMC coursework predating the desktop-3D/IVR media-comparison literature entirely, so any citation joining the two must hold the media gap explicit rather than implying equivalence *(anticipatory-application)*. For Part III's own instrument and analysis design, three of this unit's own findings transfer as methodological cautions: first, the course-length moderator (longer courses, stronger correlations for both outcomes) suggests that any Part III measure of presence-outcome relationships should record and, where possible, control for exposure duration before comparing conditions *(anticipatory-application)*; second, the discipline-area moderator, and its citation of hard/soft and pure/applied paradigm explanations, is a caution against generalizing a presence-outcome correlation found in one course type to Part III's own (VLE) setting without argument *(anticipatory-application)*; third, the finding that no two social-presence scales are equivalent, and that scale reliability itself partly drives correlation strength, reinforces ped-sec-28's own measurement-overlap caveat and argues for reporting Part III's own instrument's reliability alongside any presence-outcome correlation it yields *(anticipatory-application)*.

**Not treated:** presence (spatial/physical), immersion (technological), agency/interactivity, embodiment/self-representation, cognitive load (extraneous vs. germane), situational interest, self-efficacy, engagement/flow (named narratively as an outcome social presence influences, but not itself pooled as an effect size), cybersickness & physical discomfort, and representational fidelity — none of these constructs appear in this literature, which pools exclusively pre-VR, text-based and web-based CMC coursework studies (1992–2015) and predates the desktop-3D/IVR media-comparison literature this cluster's Strand C catalogues. Traditional academic performance (grades) is discussed narratively in §1.5.3 but explicitly excluded from the quantitative synthesis for insufficient comparable primary-study data.
