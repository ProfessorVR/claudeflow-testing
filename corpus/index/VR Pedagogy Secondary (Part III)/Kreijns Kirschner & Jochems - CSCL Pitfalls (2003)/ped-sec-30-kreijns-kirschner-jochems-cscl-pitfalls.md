# ped-sec-30 — Kreijns, Kirschner & Jochems, "Identifying the Pitfalls for Social Interaction in Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning Environments: A Review of the Research" (2003)

**Type:** review article (educational technology / computer-supported collaborative learning) · **Discipline:** educational technology, CSCL · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F's skeptical anchor — the cluster's clearest source-authored counter-voice to the CMC social-presence tradition's affirmative predictor-of-satisfaction literature (Gunawardena & Zittle, Richardson & Swan), and the direct textual origin of debate axis 9's "assumed-not-demonstrated" pole.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Kreijns, Kirschner and Jochems argue that asynchronous distributed learning groups (DLGs) in computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) environments routinely underperform their promise not because collaborative-learning pedagogy is flawed but because two field-wide pitfalls go unaddressed (FE, p.335–336). The first pitfall is taking for granted that social interaction will occur automatically merely because a CSCL environment makes it technologically possible. The second is restricting whatever social-interaction interventions are designed to cognitive, task-oriented processes while neglecting the social-psychological dimension — trust, warmth, belonging — needed to sustain a working learning community (FE, p.335–336). The article's aim is explicitly a review-and-diagnosis, not an empirical test: it surveys the collaborative-learning literature to establish social interaction as collaborative learning's "conditio sine qua non," then surveys the community-building and social-presence literatures to explain why CSCL's distinctive features (text-based media, task-only use, zero-history groups) make the two pitfalls more consequential there than in face-to-face settings, and closes by cataloguing proposed remedies and calling for a still-undeveloped genre of "sociable" CSCL environment design (FE, p.349–350).

## 2. Structure

The article runs five numbered sections plus an unheaded introduction.

1. **Unheaded introduction** (p.335–336) — motivates the review from field observations (Hallett & Cummings; Gunawardena; Hobaugh) that CSCL environments often fail to produce the dialogic interaction needed for deep learning; names the two pitfalls; attributes their salience to CSCL's distinctive media, task-only orientation, and zero-history groups.
2. **"1. Collaborative learning"** (p.336–340) — surveys competing collaborative/cooperative-learning definitions, concludes to treat them as equivalent (following Kirschner 2001); establishes social interaction as the field's shared key variable; catalogs three structuring approaches (cognitive/epistemic-fluency, direct/technique-based, conceptual/interdependence-based) and the negative dynamics (free-riding, social loafing, the sucker effect) structured collaboration is meant to counter.
3. **"2. Social interaction in CSCL environments"** (p.340–342) — states the thesis in the two named pitfalls: 2.1 Pitfall 1 (taking interaction for granted; media-richness and communication-apprehension barriers unique to text-based CMC); 2.2 Pitfall 2 (restricting design to cognitive/task processes; introduces Gilroy's adapted formula, Valued Learning Experience = F[Pedagogy, Content, Community]).
4. **"3. Community building in CSCL environments"** (p.342–346) — defines the "affective structure" (affiliation, impression formation, interpersonal attraction → cohesion, trust); 3.1 argues non-task/off-topic contexts foster this structure more than task contexts do, but that contemporary CSCL environments lack such spaces; 3.2 reviews social-presence and reduced-social-context-cues research (Short, Williams & Christie's original theory; Sproull & Kiesler) against Walther's competing, time-based account.
5. **"4. Avoiding the pitfalls"** (p.346–349) — four categories of proposed remedies: importing face-to-face collaborative-learning methods; building interactivity into web-based CSCL environments; changing instructor/learner roles; deliberately increasing social presence (Gunawardena; Tu).
6. **"5. Summary, discussion and conclusions"** (p.349–350) — restates the thesis; critiques purely "functional" CSCL environments for forgetting participants are social beings, not only information processors (quoting Sproull & Faraj's "gathering" metaphor); proposes "sociable CSCL environments" as the needed complement and calls for future design-oriented research.

## 3. Constructs (treated critically)

- **social presence / co-presence** — the article's central construct, but it is never itself operationalized or measured here; instead the article treats social presence theory (Short, Williams & Christie 1976) as one explanatory account among competing ones for *why* CSCL's low-cue, text-based media impede impression formation, and immediately relativizes it via Walther's counter-claim that reduced social-presence effects may be an artifact of insufficient interaction time rather than a fixed property of the medium (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.346–347). This unit treats "social presence" here as a contested explanatory variable under active dispute within the source's own citation network, not as a settled predictor — the skeptical framing debate axis 9 targets (P/anticipatory-application).
- **Pitfall 1 — automatic-interaction assumption** — the claim, attributed to Kearsley, Rourke, and Olson & Olson, that groupware technology creates an unrealistic expectation of easy, automatic connection; "availability of communication media is necessary, but not sufficient" for social interaction to occur (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.340–341).
- **Pitfall 2 — cognitive-only restriction** — the claim that even educators who do design for interaction typically restrict it to task/cognitive purposes, neglecting the social-psychological prerequisites (trust, warmth, belonging) Rourke and Wegerif identify as necessary before learners will engage willingly in collaboration (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.341–342).
- **affective structure** — a constructed sequence of affiliation → impression formation → interpersonal attraction, argued to produce group cohesion and mutual trust; explicitly positioned as what CSCL environments' task-only, low-cue design fails to cultivate (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.342–343).
- **media richness / reduced social-context cues** — the technology-determined account (Daft & Lengel; Rice; Sproull & Kiesler) of why text-based CMC constrains socio-emotional information transfer relative to face-to-face communication (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.340, 345–346).
- **community of learning / sense of community** — treated via Wegerif's claim that feeling treated sympathetically by fellow group members is a necessary first step for collaborative learning, formalized in the adapted Gilroy formula (Valued Learning Experience = F[Pedagogy, Content, Community], where any factor near zero collapses the whole) (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.341–342).
- **negative group dynamics (free-riding, social loafing, sucker effect)** — a catalog of failure modes in unstructured collaboration, offered as the problem structured-collaboration methods are meant to solve; tangential to the social-presence thread but part of the article's broader "social interaction as prerequisite" argument (FE, p.339–340).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — asserted, not measured, at the article's outset: dialogic collaborative processes are claimed (via Van der Linden & Renshaw 2001; Garrison, Anderson & Archer 2001; Johnson & Johnson 1999) to support deeper learning, critical thinking, and long-term retention (FE, p.335, 337–338).

Not treated as instruments: the article proposes no scale, coding scheme, or measurement protocol of its own for any construct it discusses; every construct here is inherited from cited literature and used argumentatively, not operationalized.

## 4. Conditions & design (review methodology)

This is a narrative literature review, not an empirical study, and Strand F is not scoped to populate the media-condition axis for this unit — accordingly, **no media-condition axis content** applies; the article contains no IVR/desktop-3D/2D comparison, no sample, no instrument, and no reported effect size. Its "design" is argumentative: it establishes a premise (social interaction as collaboration's conditio sine qua non, drawn from Hooper & Hanafin, Hiltz, Gilbert & Moore, Gunawardena, Liaw & Huang, Northrup, Wagner), diagnoses two pitfalls against that premise, and evaluates four categories of remedy proposed elsewhere in the literature (collaborative-learning-method transplantation; interactivity-building; instructor/learner-role change; deliberate social-presence cultivation), each drawn from a distinct sub-literature and none tested against another within this article (FE, p.346–349). The nearest thing to a formal model is Gilroy's adapted multiplicative formula, which is presented as an illustrative heuristic, not a fitted statistical model — it is not a SEM/process model in the sense the cluster's Strand-C concordance column intends, so it is not mapped to that column either.

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Social interaction is collaborative learning's key mechanism: collaboration is present if and only if social interaction is present, and vice versa (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, citing Garrison 1993; Johnson, Johnson & Stanne 1985; Soller et al. 1999, p.338).
- Social interaction does not automatically follow from technological affordance; media availability is necessary but not sufficient, and text-based CMC introduces media-richness and communication-apprehension barriers absent from face-to-face settings (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.340–341).
- Interactivity built into a CSCL environment (forums, labeled "café" spaces) is likewise necessary but not sufficient: it enables social interaction without causing it (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.348).
- Social presence theory's technology-determinism is not the field's settled account: Walther's competing, time-based explanation holds that CMC's apparent deficits in impression formation may reflect insufficient interaction time rather than an intrinsic medium property, implying the deficits should attenuate with extended engagement (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.346–347).
- The article's own closing position is a critique of "purely functional" CSCL environments, which support cognitive/task processes but forget participants are social beings seeking affiliation and affirmation as well as information — quoting Sproull & Faraj's "gathering" metaphor for what such environments neglect (FAITHFUL-Kreijns, p.349–350).
- The article's constructive proposal is "sociable CSCL environments": designs that deliberately provide non-task, off-topic communication opportunities and persistent presence/awareness of co-members across time and space, offered as a future-research agenda rather than a demonstrated design (FE, p.350).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit is Part III's clearest available source for a skeptical, mechanism-level reading of social presence: rather than reporting that social presence predicts satisfaction or learning (the register of ped-sec-25 Gunawardena & Zittle and ped-sec-28 Richardson & Swan), Kreijns et al. explain *why* social presence and the community it depends on so often fail to materialize in mediated learning environments even when the technology nominally supports interaction — a mechanism, not merely a correlational finding, for reading survey signals of felt social absence *(anticipatory-application)*. This bears directly on vle-01-deployment-survey's F7 finding ("Presence & the WE"), where APP-stratum respondents report social-presence language at markedly higher rates than VID-stratum respondents, and several open responses (curated in vle-03-exemplar-bank's "shared dimension" cluster — "seemed isolating," "watch with others," "collaboration, such as class discussions") register an unmet want for co-presence *(anticipatory-application)*. Because the VLE in fact provisions spatial-proximity voice co-watching and assigns every student to a group, a naive Pitfall-1 reading (interaction failed because the technology didn't permit it) does not fit the deployment; Kreijns et al.'s Pitfall 2 — that designing the technological affordance for interaction is necessary but not sufficient, and that neglecting the social-psychological scaffolding (deliberately cultivated affective structure, non-task off-topic space, persistent co-member awareness) leaves an available social channel unactivated — is the better-fitting mechanism, relocating the "WE unmet want" reading from a design/grouping gap to a student-side activation gap, exactly as the VLE unit's own correction states *(anticipatory-application)*. The same mechanism cautions Part III generally against treating "students didn't report feeling co-present" as evidence that co-presence affordances are absent; Kreijns et al.'s own argument is that affordance and activation are distinct, and that only the latter, not the former, predicts a socially rich learning experience *(anticipatory-application)*. Finally, as required by this cluster's debate-axis 9 seed, this unit is positioned as the explicit skeptical pole against ped-sec-25 (Gunawardena & Zittle) and ped-sec-28 (Richardson & Swan)'s affirmative predictor-of-satisfaction findings: the contrast is not that Kreijns et al. deny social presence has effects, but that — true to the article's own title — they argue its emergence in CSCL is systematically *assumed* rather than demonstrated, and that where studies do find predictive effects, those findings describe environments that happen to have solved (deliberately or not) the very design problem this article diagnoses as usually unsolved; the "inconsistency" pole of axis 9 is thus best read here as an unaddressed moderator (design adequacy for social-psychological scaffolding) rather than a direct denial of the predictor literature's results *(anticipatory-application)*.

Not treated: cybersickness, embodiment/self-representation, cognitive load (extraneous vs. germane), representational fidelity, and any IVR/desktop-3D/2D media contrast — this is a pre-VR, text-based-CMC-tradition review with no immersive-media content; self-efficacy and situational interest also do not appear in the article's own vocabulary, though its community-building argument is adjacent to both.
