# ped-sec-17 — Makransky, Bonde, Wulff, Wandall, Hood, Creed, Bache, Silahtaroglu & Nørremølle, "Simulation Based Virtual Learning Environment in Medical Genetics Counseling" (2016)

**Type:** empirical journal article (single-arm pre/post intervention study) · **Discipline:** medical education / educational psychology · **Depth:** map · **Corpus role:** Strand D's shortest and most narrowly clinical unit — a single-session, desktop-based Labster simulation evaluated for learning, motivational, and self-efficacy gains, with no media-comparison design.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

The article's stated aim is to test whether a case-based, desktop simulation bridges the gap between theoretical medical-science instruction and clinical practice by measuring pre-to-post change in four outcomes: (1) students' understanding of the translation from laboratory analysis to clinical diagnosis/decision-making, (2) knowledge of medical genetics, (3) intrinsic motivation for the topic, and (4) self-efficacy for genetics-counseling activities (FE, p.3). The paper frames simulation technology generally as a response to cost, time, and safety barriers restricting hands-on laboratory access in undergraduate medical curricula (FE, p.2).

## 2. Structure

Standard IMRaD: Background (theory-practice gap in translational medicine; prior digital-simulation literature) → the Labster genetics case description → Method (sample; training exposure and measures) → Results (translation-to-practice appraisals; knowledge gain; motivation; self-efficacy, each broken out by prior-knowledge tertile) → Discussion → Recommendations for practice → Limitations and future research → Conclusion.

## 3. Constructs

- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — knowledge rose 67%→79% correct on an 18-item MCQ test, *d* = 0.73 (FE, p.4); a 4-item Likert battery on transfer to "everyday clinical practice" showed 90% agreement the lab procedures improved understanding and 78% greater counseling confidence (FE, p.4).
- **affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation** — five items adapted from the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory's Interest/Enjoyment subscale; overall *d* = 0.24, significant for medium/high-knowledge tertiles but not the low tertile (FE, p.5).
- **self-efficacy** — five items adapted from the Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire; overall *d* = 0.46, largest for the low (*d* = 0.61) and high (*d* = 0.52) tertiles (FE, p.5–6).
- **situational interest** — 82% found medical genetics "more interesting" post-simulation (FE, p.4); a single appraisal item, not a validated scale.
- **learning outcomes: procedural / skills** — operationalized loosely via in-simulation laboratory tasks (amniocentesis, array CGH, karyotyping) and the counseling-confidence item, not a separate skills assessment (FE, p.3–4).

## 4. Conditions & design

Single-arm pre-test (30 min) / simulation (2 hr) / post-test (30 min) design, participants as their own controls; **no comparison condition** (FE, p.3, acknowledged as a limitation, p.7). Setting: mandatory Medical Genetics course, University of Copenhagen, fall 2014, N = 300 first-year students (84% medicine, 16% molecular biomedicine; 60% female), preceded by a 1-hr lecture (FE, p.3). The simulation (built with Labster; co-author M. Bonde is Labster's CEO, disclosed p.8) is **desktop, non-immersive**: students perform amniocentesis and karyotyping on a virtual fetal-DNA sample, then counsel a virtual pregnant couple — combining a *desktop-3D / non-immersive VR* laboratory condition with a *screen-based virtual patient / sim* counseling condition in one session (FE, p.2–3). Only 54%/40% completed the optional self-efficacy/motivation scales; completers and non-completers did not differ significantly on pre/post knowledge (FE, p.5–6).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- All three primary outcomes (knowledge, motivation, self-efficacy) increased significantly pre-to-post (FE, p.4–6).
- Effect size was strongly moderated by prior knowledge: the low-knowledge tertile showed the largest knowledge gain (*d* = 3.35) but non-significant motivation gain; the high-knowledge tertile showed the smallest knowledge gain (*d* = 0.36, attributed to a ceiling effect at 85% pre-test correct) but the strongest motivation/self-efficacy gains (FE, p.4–6).
- The authors read the counseling-confidence finding as corroborating Cook et al.'s meta-analytic conclusion that virtual-patient interaction improves outcomes relative to non-computer instruction or no intervention, arguing their design adds value by pairing virtual-patient counseling with virtual-laboratory procedure practice (FE, p.6, citing Cook, Erwin & Triola 2010 — companion review to this cluster's ped-sec-15 Cook et al. 2011 meta-analysis).
- In passing, the discussion cites Pekrun's control-value theory of achievement emotions to explain why weaker students "often report feeling bored, and subsequently demotivated, by not being able to meet high academic demands," framing hands-on simulation as a compensating format for this population (FE, p.6).
- Limitations acknowledged: no control group; identical knowledge items pre/post (repetition-bias risk); low completion of the optional non-cognitive scales (FE, p.7).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit's chief contribution to the cluster is a compact, validated battery — knowledge MCQ, IMI-derived motivation items, MSLQ-derived self-efficacy items — matching the kind of learning-outcome channel the VLE entry's own deployment survey (`vle-01-deployment-survey`) flags as absent ("F8 Limits… no learning-outcome channel"); pointing Part III at this battery as an instrument model is this cluster's own construction, not a claim Makransky et al. make about the VLE *(anticipatory-application)*. Separately, the discussion's brief invocation of Pekrun's control-value theory to explain weaker students' boredom and demotivation under high academic demand is a direct, incidental point of contact with Part III's own boredom framing — external corroboration that hands-on simulation is proposed elsewhere as a compensating response to the disengagement pattern Part III investigates *(anticipatory-application)*. The title's own use of "virtual learning environment" is a naming coincidence with this cluster's VLE anchor, not a substantive overlap — Makransky et al.'s "VLE" is a single-session Labster case, not the multi-term Part III deployment; no bridge rests on that basis alone.

Not treated: presence/immersion measurement (no PQ/IPQ or comparable instrument), cybersickness, media-comparison (IVR vs. desktop vs. 2D — the study has no comparison arm), social presence / co-presence, cognitive load, and engagement/flow.
