# ped-sec-23 — Cossio et al., "Cybersickness and discomfort from head-mounted displays delivering fully immersive virtual reality: A systematic review" (2025)

**Type:** systematic review (PRISMA/PROSPERO-registered) · **Discipline:** nursing/health-professions education, human factors · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand E's dedicated review of adverse physiological effects; the cluster's primary source for the "cybersickness & physical discomfort" construct row and the anchor unit for Debate Axis 7 (cybersickness as substantive moderator of IVR learning vs. ignorable side-effect).

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Cossio et al. synthesize 25 primary studies (2013–2024) on cybersickness and physical discomfort arising from head-mounted-display (HMD) delivered fully immersive VR in educational contexts, arguing that these adverse effects have been historically neglected as secondary outcomes relative to learning-effectiveness measures (FE, p.1). Their explicit review question asks which "negative effects (cybersickness, discomfort)" (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.2) arise from HMD-delivered fully immersive VR in education, operationalized through a PICO framework — population (healthy and non-healthy, all ages), intervention (fully immersive HMD VR for education), comparison (other VR modalities or traditional simulation), outcome (cybersickness/discomfort occurrence) (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.2). The review's payoff is practical rather than theoretical: educators who deploy HMD VR incur a disclosure and documentation duty toward learners, distinct from — and, the authors imply, more physiologically consequential than — the boredom or stress produced by disengaging conventional pedagogy (FE, p.16).

## 2. Structure

The article runs seven sections. **Introduction/Background** (p.1–2) frames VR-HMD adoption in education, distinguishes the competing terminologies — simulator sickness, motion sickness, cybersickness, VR sickness — and their associated instruments (SSQ, MSAQ, FMS, VRSQ/VRSQ-G), then separates discomfort as an ergonomic construct independent of VR content. **Methods** (p.2–3) sets out the PROSPERO-registered PRISMA design, PICO question, four-database search (CINAHL, Cochrane, PubMed, Scopus), inclusion/exclusion criteria, and JBI quality-appraisal checklists for RCTs and quasi-experimental studies. **Results — Study characteristics and quality** (p.3–4) reports the PRISMA flow (5,423 records → 25 included) and describes the corpus's designs, samples, settings, and methodological quality. **Results — Educational content** (p.3) and **data extraction/synthesis** describe the intervention types, HMD models, and exposure conditions. **Results — Outcome metrics and timing** (p.5) and the extended **Table 2** (p.6–13, one row per study) catalogue each study's instrument, timing, and findings. **Results — Outcome data** (p.14) aggregates the cybersickness and discomfort findings across the 25 studies. **Discussion** (p.15–16, presented out of numeric order in the extracted text — subsections 4.1–4.5 covering study characteristics, educational content, outcome metrics/timing, outcome data, and limitations) interprets the aggregate pattern and flags methodological gaps. **Conclusions** (p.16) close with the disclosure-duty argument.

## 3. Constructs

**Cybersickness & physical discomfort** is this unit's primary and most heavily elaborated construct, treated with a precision the rest of the cluster does not attempt. The review first disentangles four overlapping terms the field uses interchangeably: *simulator sickness*, originating in military flight-simulator research and measured by the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ, 16 items across disorientation/nausea/oculomotor-disturbance categories) (FAITHFUL-Cossio, citing Kennedy et al. 1993, p.2); *motion sickness*, a broader category not HMD-specific, measured by the Motion Sickness Assessment Questionnaire (MSAQ, 16 items/four categories) or the shorter Fast Motion Sickness (FMS) scale (FAITHFUL-Cossio, citing Gianaros et al. 2001; Keshavarz & Hecht 2011, p.2); *cybersickness* proper, coined to describe adverse effects specific to virtual-environment exposure and attributed to a mismatch among visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive senses (FAITHFUL-Cossio, citing McCauley & Sharkey 1992; LaViola 2000, p.2); and the *Virtual Reality Sickness Questionnaire* (VRSQ), a nine-item, two-category (disorientation/oculomotor) instrument the review's own cited psychometric literature judges better suited to HMD VR than the SSQ or its French variant SSQ-F (FAITHFUL-Cossio, citing Sevinc & Berkman 2020, p.2). *Discomfort* is defined as analytically separate from cybersickness: a range of physical/psychological symptoms tied to device ergonomics — weight, uneven gravity distribution, localized pressure, restricted head mobility, inadequate thermal regulation — that can occur "regardless of the VR content" (FAITHFUL-Cossio, citing Rupp 2024, p.2). This construct-vs-instrument disentanglement, absent from every other Strand-E/C source in the cluster, is this unit's chief conceptual contribution and the reason it anchors the concordance row rather than merely populating a cell in it.

**Immersion (technological)** appears as framing rather than as an operationalized outcome: the review adopts a tripartite non-/semi-/fully-immersive taxonomy (FAITHFUL-Cossio, citing Martirosov et al. 2022, p.1) and, in its discussion, treats greater immersion as the presumed mechanism generating "greater sensory conflict" (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.14) and hence worse cybersickness. This causal claim is stated but not independently tested by the review itself; it is inherited from the aggregated primary literature.

**Presence (spatial/physical)** receives only a single definitional gesture — VR is glossed as producing "the sensation of 'being there'" (FE, p.1) — and is never revisited as an outcome variable anywhere in the results or discussion. It functions purely to distinguish VR from other educational technologies at the outset.

## 4. Conditions & design

The review's own design is itself the "review/meta-analytic aggregate" condition: a PRISMA-2020-compliant systematic review (Page et al. 2021), pre-registered at PROSPERO (CRD42024583656), searching CINAHL, Cochrane Library, PubMed, and Scopus for English/Italian studies from January 2013 (the release date of the first consumer-generation HMD) to September 2024 (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.2–3). Screening via Rayyan reduced 5,423 records to 25 included studies after duplicate removal (n=348), title/abstract exclusion (n=4,917), and full-text exclusion (n=133), conducted by two reviewers with a third arbiter (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.3). Quality was appraised with JBI checklists for RCTs and quasi-experimental designs, again by two reviewers plus arbiter (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.3).

The 25 included primary studies constitute the review's aggregated evidence for the **IVR (HMD)** condition: 16 (64%) quasi-experimental, 9 (36%) RCTs; predominantly single-group designs (15, 60%); sample sizes from N=3 (Siivola et al. 2023) to N=133 (Maloca et al. 2022); ages 12–74; 18 studies (72%) enrolling students or recent graduates, 4 (16%) healthcare professionals, 3 (12%) non-healthcare populations (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.3–4). Twenty-one of 25 (84%) were set in health education; HTC Vive (9 studies) and Oculus Quest (7) were the most-used HMDs; interventions were technical simulations (16, 64%) or 360° educational video (9, 36%); exposure ranged 3–74 minutes and 20/25 (80%) kept participants stationary (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.4).

Thirteen of 25 studies (52%) included a comparison arm, populating two further concordance columns: **desktop-3D / non-immersive VR** (5 studies, e.g. Al-Khiami et al. 2023, Darnall et al. 2020) and **conventional instruction (control)** — traditional simulation with mannequins or theory sessions (3 studies: Andersen et al. 2023, Babaita et al. 2024, Pedram et al. 2024) (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.5). Two studies compared augmented or mixed reality instead (Huber et al. 2018; Elsenbast et al. 2024), and two compared different HMD models against each other (Bailey et al. 2022; Mehrfard et al. 2020). Instrumentation across the corpus was itself heterogeneous: ad hoc questionnaires dominated (13 studies, 52%), followed by simplified SSQ variants (6, 24%), with MSAQ, FMS, SSQ-F, VRSQ, and VRSQ-G each appearing once or twice (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.5). Only 23% of studies (7/25) administered the outcome tool to a comparison group as well as the HMD group, weakening between-arm comparability (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.14).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Cybersickness was assessed in 24/25 studies (all but Bailey et al. 2022, which measured only discomfort); 12/25 (48%) reported inconsistent or very low scores, while five reported high scores — a genuinely mixed rather than uniformly alarming aggregate picture (FE, p.14).
- Oculomotor disturbances (eyestrain, blurred vision, headache) were the most frequently reported negative effect (9 studies, 36%; 5 moderate/high), followed by nausea (4 studies, 16%) and disorientation (moderate/high in 3 studies, 12%); only Elsenbast et al. (2024) reported moderate-to-high scores across all three symptom categories simultaneously, and the review notes the symptoms appear to vary somewhat independently of one another (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.14).
- Discomfort was investigated in 8/25 studies (32%); five reported inconsistent/minimal discomfort and three reported moderate/high discomfort, with discomfort tracking device weight/heat more closely than exposure duration (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.14).
- Where comparisons existed, worse cybersickness for HMD VR relative to desktop displays, non-immersive VR, and traditional simulation was the majority pattern, attributed by the authors to "greater sensory conflict" (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.14) from higher immersion (FE, citing Yildirim 2020; Al-Khiami et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2020; Andersen et al. 2023; Elsenbast et al. 2024, p.14) — but Huber et al. (2018) is an explicit counter-case: cybersickness was *less* prevalent in fully immersive VR (3.3% of participants) than in augmented reality (6.6%) (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.14).
- Female participants and older learners may be more susceptible to cybersickness (hormonal factors, smaller interpupillary distance, vestibular/cognitive changes with age), though the review characterizes the explanatory mechanisms as still unsettled (FAITHFUL-Cossio, citing Mittelstaedt 2020; Clemes & Howarth 2005, p.15).
- Measurement is compromised by instrument heterogeneity and by tools (chiefly the SSQ) validated for flight-simulator contexts (fixed seated posture, non-stereoscopic image, no head tracking) rather than HMD VR; the purpose-built VRSQ was used in only two of 25 studies despite the review's own citation that it outperforms the SSQ/SSQ-F psychometrically for this context (FAITHFUL-Cossio, p.15).
- Symptoms can persist beyond the exposure session (minutes to hours), and only a minority of studies assessed post-exposure timing beyond an immediate post-session measure (23/25, 92%) (FAITHFUL-Cossio, citing Kim et al. 2018; Sevinc & Berkman 2020; Biniok et al. 2024, p.15).
- Conclusion: educators must recognize, attribute, and document adverse HMD-VR effects and disclose their possibility to learners — particularly where post-session activities like driving are involved — and should actively solicit learner self-report (FE, p.16).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit is the cluster's designated anchor for the "cybersickness & physical discomfort" construct row and for Debate Axis 7. Its own stance on that axis is deliberately measured rather than alarmist: it documents a genuinely mixed evidentiary base (roughly half the included studies report inconsistent or low cybersickness) while still arguing that educators bear a disclosure and documentation duty precisely because the phenomenon has been chronically under-measured as a secondary outcome — this unit's position should be represented as "take it seriously as a reportable moderator" rather than "cybersickness is a large, uniform effect," a nuance worth preserving rather than flattening when the dissertation cites Axis 7 *(anticipatory-application)*. For Part III, whose lab pole uses HMD-delivered VR, this review supplies a documented basis for two design/reporting cautions: (1) any self-report boredom, attention, or engagement measure collected immediately post-session in the VR condition should be read against the possibility of confounding oculomotor or disorientation symptoms, since these were the most commonly reported adverse effects and were assessed by ad hoc, non-standardized instruments in the majority of the underlying literature *(anticipatory-application)*; (2) informed-consent and debrief materials for any HMD-based data collection should disclose the possibility of transient symptoms and account for post-session activity (e.g., driving), following this review's own explicit recommendation *(anticipatory-application)*. No direct link exists to a specific `vle-0N` node in the Virtual Learning Environments (King–Salvo) anchor entry — the VLE's own instrument suite does not include a validated cybersickness/discomfort scale — so this unit's bridge is recorded to Part III generally rather than to a named VLE unit; this gap is itself worth flagging for Part III's own instrumentation review *(anticipatory-application)*. A prospective bridge to the Boredom Experiment (VR Attention Study) dataset, once built, would let this review's exposure-duration findings (3–74 minutes, with symptom onset and habituation effects noted at p.15) be checked against that dataset's own HMD exposure protocol — left as `****** UNVERIFIED:` pending that entry's construction. Within the cluster itself, this review's sensory-conflict account of why higher immersion may cost more physiologically is a natural counterweight to Strand C's presence-optimistic designs (e.g., ped-sec-10's "more presence but less learning" finding and ped-sec-06's CAMIL, which model immersion's affective/motivational upside without accounting for its physiological cost) and to Strand E's more general affordance reviews (ped-sec-21, ped-sec-22), which catalogue IVR's pedagogical promise with only passing attention to adverse effects.

**Not treated:** social presence/co-presence, agency/interactivity, embodiment/self-representation, cognitive load, affect/enjoyment/intrinsic motivation, situational interest, self-efficacy, engagement/flow, and learning outcomes (retention/transfer or procedural/skills) — none of these appear as outcome variables in this review, whose scope is deliberately restricted to physiological/ergonomic side effects rather than learning or motivational constructs.
