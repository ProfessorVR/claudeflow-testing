# ped-sec-15 — Cook, Hatala, Brydges, Zendejas, Szostek, Wang, Erwin & Hamstra, "Technology-Enhanced Simulation for Health Professions Education: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis" (*JAMA*, 2011)

**Type:** journal article, systematic review and meta-analysis · **Discipline:** medical/health-professions education (evidence synthesis) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand D's principal quantitative aggregate — the cluster's primary source for the "simulation robustly beats no-intervention" pole of debate axis 6, and the reference point that scopes Strand D's virtual-patient-simulation literature (ped-sec-19, Kononowicz et al.) out of its own coverage.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Cook et al. set out to answer two questions across the whole of health professions education: (1) to what extent is technology-enhanced simulation training associated with improved outcomes compared with no intervention, and (2) how do those outcomes vary with instructional-design features (FE, p.979). The paper does not argue a theoretical thesis about learning; its claim is evidentiary — that a comprehensive, PRISMA-compliant synthesis of the entire simulation-versus-no-intervention literature yields a stable, generalizable estimate of benefit, and that this estimate is now large and consistent enough that further studies of the same comparison have diminishing scientific value (FE, p.987). The authors explicitly frame "technology" broadly — computer-based VR simulators, high- and low-fidelity mannequins, plastic models, live animals, animal products, and cadavers all count (FE, p.978) — so the unit's contribution to this cluster is a floor-level base-rate for "simulation training works," not a claim about any specific medium's affordances.

## 2. Structure

The article follows the standard JAMA structured-review format: a structured abstract (Context/Objective/Data Source/Study Selection/Data Extraction/Data Synthesis/Conclusion, p.978); an Introduction motivating the need for quantitative synthesis over prior narrower reviews (p.978-979); a Methods section with four named subsections — Study Questions, Study Eligibility, Study Identification, Data Extraction, and Data Synthesis (pp.979-981); a Results section reporting trial flow, study characteristics, study quality, and then the meta-analysis proper, broken out by outcome category (knowledge, time/process/product skills, time/process behaviors, patient effects; pp.981-987); and a Comment section covering limitations and strengths, comparison with previous reviews, and implications (pp.986-988).

## 3. Constructs

- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — operationalized as "knowledge" outcomes, almost always multiple-choice test scores, reported in 118 studies (n=8,595); pooled effect size 1.20 (95% CI, 1.04-1.35) versus no intervention (FE, p.983).
- **learning outcomes: procedural / skills** — the article's largest and most granular construct, subdivided into time skills (speed of task completion), process skills (global ratings, economy of movement, errors), and product skills (quality of the completed artifact); reported across 210, 426, and 54 studies respectively (FE, p.983-985).
- **behavior and patient-care outcomes** — reported as an adjacent but distinct construct beyond the cluster's frozen learning-outcomes vocabulary: time and process behaviors with real patients (20 and 50 studies) and direct patient effects (32 studies), the last being the study's own "headline" outcome for clinical-translation claims (FE, p.985-986).

Cook et al. do not use the constructs presence, immersion, cognitive load, affect, engagement, or self-efficacy anywhere in the article; the paper's entire construct vocabulary is the Kirkpatrick learning/behavior/results hierarchy (FE, p.980, citing Kirkpatrick 1996).

## 4. Conditions & design

This is a review of reviews' evidence base, not a primary experiment, so its "condition" is the aggregate itself: a PRISMA-compliant systematic search of MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, ERIC, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science, key simulation journals, and reference-list snowballing through May 2011, yielding 10,903 candidate records and, after two-stage independent duplicate screening (title/abstract ICC=0.69), 609 eligible studies enrolling 35,226 trainees (137 randomized, 67 nonrandomized two-group, 405 single-group pretest-posttest; FE, p.981-982). All comparisons are simulation **versus no intervention** — either a genuine no-treatment control group or a pretest-posttest design functioning as its own control (FE, p.979) — never simulation versus an alternative *non-simulation* instructional method; the article itself names that latter, harder comparison as the gap future research should fill (FE, p.987-988, "theory-based comparisons between different technology-enhanced simulation designs"), so no such vs.-non-simulation-instruction effect size exists in this source to report.

Per this cluster's own scope correction (see `manifest.json → planCorrections`), Cook et al. **explicitly exclude** studies of computer-based virtual patients requiring only standard computer equipment, citing their own separate systematic review of that literature for the exclusion (FE, p.980, citing Cook, Erwin & Triola 2010) — the same virtual-patient literature ped-sec-19 (Kononowicz et al.) covers directly. This unit therefore fills the concordance's "review / meta-analytic aggregate" column only, and explicitly does **not** fill "screen-based virtual patient / sim" — a boundary this unit's Phase-2 write-up preserves rather than blurs.

Effect sizes were standardized as Hedges g, pooled with random-effects models given consistently high heterogeneity (I² mostly ≥50%; FE, p.980-981). Methodological quality, graded via MERSQI (mean 11.6/18) and a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (mean 2.1/6), was "relatively low" across the corpus (FE, p.983), with two-thirds of studies single-group pretest-posttest designs. Planned subgroup analyses tested five instructional-design features — curricular integration, distributed practice (>1 day vs. one day), feedback, mastery learning, and range of task difficulty — using a z-test for interaction significance (FE, p.980).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Pooled effect sizes versus no intervention were **large** for knowledge (1.20; 95% CI, 1.04-1.35; k=118), time skills (1.14; k=210), process skills (1.09; k=426), and product skills (1.18; k=54), and **moderate-to-large** for time behaviors (0.79; k=20) and process behaviors (0.81; k=50) with real patients, and **moderate** for direct patient effects (0.50; 95% CI, 0.34-0.66; k=32) (FE, p.983-986).
- Heterogeneity was consistently large across every outcome (I² ranging roughly 66%-96%), and individual-study effect sizes spanned an enormous range (e.g., knowledge outcomes from -0.42 to 9.45) (FE, p.983-985).
- Randomized-only subsets showed smaller but still positive pooled effects (e.g., knowledge 0.63, process skills 0.98, product skills 0.76, patient effects 0.37), and two-group and higher-quality studies were consistently associated with smaller effect sizes than single-group pretest-posttest studies — a pattern the authors attribute to better control for maturation and non-intervention learning (FE, p.986-987).
- Subgroup analyses of the five instructional-design features found **no consistent statistically significant interactions**; distributed practice over more than one day and mastery learning weakly trended toward larger effects, and — counter to expectation — high feedback was sometimes associated with *smaller* effect sizes (FE, p.986, "contrary to expectation").
- Sensitivity analyses (excluding imputed/estimated effect sizes, excluding studies with >200 participants) yielded materially unchanged results, and funnel-plot/Egger asymmetry testing suggested publication bias was unlikely to be driving the conclusions (FE, p.986-987).
- The authors' own headline implication: because only about 4% of outcomes failed to show benefit and confidence intervals consistently excluded small associations, "we question the need for further studies comparing simulation with no intervention"; the field's important open questions instead concern *when and how* to use simulation most effectively, which requires theory-based, within-study simulation-vs-simulation comparisons rather than more simulation-vs-nothing evidence (FAITHFUL-Cook, p.987-988).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit supplies Part III's most citable base-rate figure for "does simulation training work at all" — a pooled, large-sample answer (yes, robustly, versus no intervention) that is useful precisely because it is medium-agnostic: Cook et al.'s "simulation" spans mannequins, cadavers, and computer-based VR indiscriminately, so this source cannot itself adjudicate any of Strand C's IVR-versus-desktop-3D-versus-2D questions, only the prior, coarser question those debates presuppose *(anticipatory-application)*. No `bridges-to-vle` edge is recorded for this unit, consistent with the plan's own scope correction: the VLE's specific desktop-3D deployment is too fine-grained a target for a meta-analysis operating at the level of "simulation vs. nothing" across all health professions *(anticipatory-application)*. Where this unit does bridge productively is methodological caution: its own finding that instructional-design subgroup analyses were almost uniformly non-significant, and that lower-quality single-group studies inflated effect sizes relative to two-group/randomized designs, is a transferable caveat for how strongly Part III should weight any single desktop-3D-versus-2D contrast drawn from a small sample against the kind of large-N aggregate evidence this article represents *(anticipatory-application)*. The article's own closing recommendation — that the field should pivot from simulation-vs-no-intervention studies toward theory-based, within-study simulation-vs-simulation comparisons — describes precisely the kind of comparison Part III's own desktop-3D/2D and lab/deployment poles are designed to make, positioning Part III's design as an instance of the research move Cook et al. call for rather than a repetition of the comparison this meta-analysis has already exhausted *(anticipatory-application)*.

**Not treated:** presence (spatial/physical), social presence/co-presence, immersion (technological), agency/interactivity, embodiment/self-representation, cognitive load, affect/enjoyment & intrinsic motivation, situational interest, self-efficacy, engagement/flow, cybersickness & physical discomfort, and representational fidelity — none of these constructs appear anywhere in this article, which restricts itself entirely to the Kirkpatrick learning/behavior/results outcome hierarchy.
