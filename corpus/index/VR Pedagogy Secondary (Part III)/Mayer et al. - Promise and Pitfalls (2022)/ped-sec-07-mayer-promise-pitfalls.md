# ped-sec-07 — Mayer, Makransky & Parong, "The Promise and Pitfalls of Learning in Immersive Virtual Reality" (2022/2023)

**Type:** narrative self-review (educational psychology / instructional design) · **Discipline:** cognitive theory of multimedia learning · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand B's capstone theory-of-VR-learning statement — the cluster's primary source for the methods-not-media / value-added pole of debate axis 2, and a key voice for the cognitive-load route of debate axis 4.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Mayer, Makransky, and Parong ask whether immersive virtual reality (IVR) can serve as an effective venue for learning, and answer with a deliberately narrow, self-referential review of their own two decades of collaborative research rather than a comprehensive meta-analysis of the field (FE, p.2229). Their organizing claim is that IVR's instructional promise and its pitfall are two faces of the same mechanism: high immersion can raise a learner's motivation to engage in generative processing (making sense of material), but the same perceptual richness can also raise emotional arousal and distraction, diverting capacity into extraneous processing that does not serve the instructional goal (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2229-2230). The paper's thesis, stated most plainly in its conclusion, is that the open instructional-design challenge is not choosing IVR over conventional media (or the reverse) but managing this promise/pitfall trade-off within IVR itself — reducing the distracting aspects of immersion while preserving its motivating aspects and safeguarding essential processing of the core material (FE, p.2236). This is the cluster's clearest statement of the "methods, not media" position: media comparison alone is treated as insufficient grounds for instructional recommendations, and the authors' own program pivots from asking "does IVR work?" to asking "what, added to IVR, makes it work better?" (FE, p.2229, 2235-2236).

## 2. Structure

Eleven pages, seven numbered sections plus a references list and author notes; no formal abstract heading is used in the running text but a structured abstract precedes the numbered sections.

1. **Objective and rationale** (p.2229-2230) — frames the human-bloodstream lesson comparison (passive slideshow vs. active IVR) that motivates the whole paper; states explicitly that the review covers only the authors' own published work, not the broader IVR-education literature (FE, p.2229).
2. **Introduction to learning in IVR** (p.2230) — defines immersion (an objective property of delivery technology) against presence (the learner's subjective feeling of "being there," citing Witmer & Singer 1998, p.225); sets out three immersion levels — no immersion (narrated slideshow), low immersion (2D desktop VR), high immersion (HMD-delivered 3D, which the paper shortens to "IVR") (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2230).
3. **Theoretical foundations** (p.2230-2231) — grounds the review in Mayer's own cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML): limited capacity is divided among extraneous processing (does not serve the instructional goal, driven by distraction), essential processing (represents the material itself, driven by complexity), and generative processing (organizes and integrates the material, driven by motivation). A promise/pitfall table maps high immersion to increased motivation (promise) and increased distraction (pitfall) (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2230-2231).
4. **Media comparison research** (p.2231-2232) — reports 13 IVR-vs-conventional-media experiments spanning three lesson families (Design-a-Plant botany game, The Body VR bloodstream lesson, Kokoda WWII history lesson, Labster science-lab simulation), each scored by Cohen's *d* on transfer-test performance (FE, p.2231-2232).
5. **Value-added research** (p.2232-2235) — reports 12 studies comparing plain IVR to IVR-plus-one-added-feature, organized into instructional-design principles (modality, personalization, pre-training), generative-activity principles (summarizing, answering, enacting), and two additional features (gender-matching, home-vs-school venue) (FE, p.2232-2235).
6. **Future directions** (p.2235-2236) — draws two headline conclusions and proposes further work on exposure effects, content-domain fit, and usability/motion-sickness constraints (FE, p.2235-2236).
7. **Conclusion** (p.2236) — restates the promise/pitfall framing and the managing-the-trade-off challenge (FE, p.2236).

## 3. Constructs

- **presence (spatial/physical)** — defined, following Witmer & Singer (1998, p.225), as the learner's subjective experience of being in an artificially created place rather than the actual physical locale; explicitly distinguished from immersion as subjective-vs-objective, learner-focused-vs-technology-focused (Table 1) (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2230).
- **immersion (technological)** — an objective property of delivery hardware/software, gauged by display quality and rendering engine; operationalized as a three-level scale (no/low/high immersion) that structures every experiment reported in the paper (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2230).
- **cognitive load (extraneous vs germane)** — the paper's own three-way CTML vocabulary (extraneous / essential / generative processing) is normalized here to the cluster's construct row: extraneous processing maps to extraneous load, generative processing maps to germane processing, and essential processing sits closest to intrinsic/essential load in the material itself (p.2230-2231) (FE, terminology-mapping note P/anticipatory-application).
- **affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation** — the "promise" half of the model: high immersion is claimed to raise motivation, which channels into generative processing (p.2230-2231); tested directly once, when Parong and Mayer's (2018) enjoyment ratings for IVR-with-summarizing did not differ significantly from IVR-without-summarizing, i.e., the value-added feature did not cost enjoyment (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2235).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — the paper's sole outcome measure across both tables: post-lesson transfer-test scores, reported as Cohen's *d* (FE, p.2231, 2233).

## 4. Conditions & design

This is a review/position paper, not a single study, so "design" here means the synthesis architecture the authors impose on their own 20-year research program rather than a single sample/instrument/procedure (FE, p.2229). Table 5 aggregates 13 media-comparison experiments: six using the Design-a-Plant botany game against a desktop-tutorial control with adult learners (Moreno & Mayer, 2002, 2004 — narration/printed and conversational/formal manipulations), two using The Body VR bloodstream lesson against a matched slideshow with adults (Parong & Mayer, 2018, 2021a — the latter is **ped-sec-13**, "Cognitive and Affective Processes for Learning Science in Immersive Virtual Reality," cited here as "2021a" — the version-of-record year the cluster now adopts for ped-sec-13, so Mayer's dating and the cluster agree), one using the Kokoda WWII-history VR lesson against an interactive desktop video with adults (Parong & Mayer, 2021b), and four using the Labster science-lab simulation against desktop delivery with adolescents (Makransky, Terkildsen, et al., 2019 — this is **ped-sec-10**, "Adding Immersive Virtual Reality to a Science Lab Simulation Causes More Presence but Less Learning"; plus two further 2019 Makransky studies not built as separate cluster units) (FE, p.2231-2232). Table 6 aggregates 12 value-added studies: O'Neil et al. (2000) on an aircraft-fuel-system IVR trainer (modality); three further Moreno & Mayer (2002) Design-a-Plant modality manipulations; one Moreno & Mayer (2004) personalization manipulation; Petersen et al. (2020) on a climate-change IVR field trip (pre-training); Parong & Mayer (2018 and 2021a, i.e. **ped-sec-13**) on summarizing and answering prompts within The Body VR; Parong & Mayer (2021b) on answering prompts within Kokoda; Makransky et al. (2021) on enacting within Labster; a Makransky, Wismer, et al. (2019) gender-matching study within Labster (not a separate cluster unit); and Makransky, Mayer, Veitch, et al. (2019) on home-vs-classroom delivery of a Labster simulation — this is **ped-sec-12**, "Equivalence of Using a Desktop Virtual Reality Science Simulation at Home and in Class" (FE, p.2232-2235). Distraction and arousal are measured, in the two studies that report them (both **ped-sec-13**), via self-report distraction ratings plus biometric heart-rate and EEG indices (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2232).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Across the 13 media-comparison experiments, the median effect size is negligible-to-negative: IVR outperforms conventional media in roughly half the Design-a-Plant comparisons and underperforms it in the other half; The Body VR and Kokoda comparisons favor the conventional-media condition (one large, one negligible effect); the four Labster comparisons yield a negligible median with IVR worse in one and better in three (FE, p.2231-2232).
- The authors' explanatory hypothesis for these null/negative findings is distraction: students who learned in IVR reported higher distraction and showed higher biometric (heart-rate and EEG) arousal than students who learned with conventional media, evidence drawn specifically from **ped-sec-13** (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2232, citing Parong & Mayer, 2021a/in press).
- Two headline conclusions close the paper: (1) there is no strong evidence that students learn academic material more deeply in IVR than with conventional media; (2) instructional-design principles (modality, personalization, pre-training) and generative-activity principles (summarizing, answering, enacting) can improve IVR's own effectiveness, largely independent of whether IVR itself outperforms other media (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2235-2236).
- Among value-added features, modality (narrated over printed text), personalization (conversational over formal wording), and pre-training (background information before rather than during the IVR lesson) each raised transfer scores, sometimes substantially (Cohen's *d* up to 2.79 for one modality comparison); generative activities (summarizing, answering, enacting) also generally raised scores, though the Kokoda answering-prompt effect was small and not statistically significant (FE, p.2232-2235).
- Gender-matching the onscreen pedagogical agent to the learner and moving delivery from school to home each showed weak or negligible effects on learning, though the home-venue finding is framed positively — IVR-based learning at home can be equivalent to IVR-based learning in school (FAITHFUL-Mayer, p.2235, citing the study built here as **ped-sec-12**).
- Future-work gestures (not executed empirically in this paper) include exposure/familiarity effects on IVR learning, whether IVR is better suited to procedural or dangerous-skill domains (the paper's own examples are surgery and piloting) than to general academic content, and usability constraints — HMD weight, motion sickness, and novelty — as open research challenges rather than resolved findings (FE, p.2236).

## 6. Concordance feed

- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer × IVR (HMD)** — negligible-to-negative median Cohen's *d* across 13 experiments (p.2231-2232).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer × desktop-3D / non-immersive VR** — the recurring control condition (Design-a-Plant desktop tutorial, Labster desktop) that IVR fails to consistently beat (p.2231-2232).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer × 2D video / slideshow / text** — The Body VR vs. slideshow and Kokoda vs. interactive video both favor the 2D/video condition (p.2232).
- **cognitive load (extraneous vs germane) × IVR (HMD)** — self-reported distraction and biometric arousal (heart rate, EEG) elevated under high immersion, offered as the mechanism behind the null media-comparison results (p.2232).
- **affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation × IVR (HMD), generative-activity value-added condition** — enjoyment ratings unaffected by adding a summarizing prompt to an IVR lesson (p.2235).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer × home vs classroom setting** — equivalence between home and school delivery of an IVR science simulation (p.2235, feeding **ped-sec-12**).
- **presence × IVR (HMD) / desktop-3D / 2D video** — the three-level immersion scale (Table 2) is explicitly linked to a presumed presence gradient (no immersion < low immersion < high immersion), citing Parong (2022) for the ordering, though this paper does not itself measure presence directly (p.2230-2231).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit is the cluster's primary carrier for debate axis 2 — media-comparison designs are informative vs. methods-not-media/value-added is the valid paradigm — and its own conclusion states the value-added position as unambiguously as any source in the cluster will: 20 years of media-comparison work yielded no reliable IVR advantage, while value-added work reliably found ways to improve IVR's own effectiveness *(anticipatory-application, shifts-debate-axis)*. It also supplies debate axis 4's cognitive-load-route pole with its clearest mechanism (extraneous processing driven by immersion's perceptual richness and emotional arousal), directly citing **ped-sec-13**'s biometric evidence — though the paper's own promise/pitfall table is explicitly dual-path (a motivation/generative-processing route sits alongside the distraction/extraneous-processing route), so this unit should be read as weighting rather than resolving that axis *(anticipatory-application)*. For Part III, the home-vs-classroom equivalence finding (via **ped-sec-12**) bears directly on the dissertation's own lab=VR ↔ deployment=2D-PC poles, offering an existing empirical precedent for deployment-context comparisons that Part III's own survey (vle-01-deployment-survey) approaches from the self-report side rather than a controlled-outcome side *(anticipatory-application, bridges-to-vle)*. The extraneous/essential/generative processing vocabulary also offers Part III a complementary cognitive-load framing alongside Gloria Mark's attentional-states quadrant (vle-04-mark-attention-span): both concern how distraction competes with goal-directed processing for limited capacity, though the two vocabularies (CTML's processing types vs. Mark's engagement-by-challenge quadrant) should be kept terminologically distinct rather than merged *(anticipatory-application, bridges-to-vle)*. Finally, this paper's own methods-not-media caution is itself a limiting frame for Part III's survey-based findings: because vle-01's own F8 notes the absence of any learning-outcome channel, this unit is a reminder that presence/immersion self-report data (which Part III does have) cannot on its own support learning-effectiveness claims — that inferential gap is exactly what this paper's media-comparison program was designed to close and, on its own account, largely failed to close *(anticipatory-application, bridges-to-part-iii)*.
