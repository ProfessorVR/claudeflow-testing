# ped-sec-22 — Radianti, Majchrzak, Fromm & Wohlgenannt, "A systematic review of immersive virtual reality applications for higher education: Design elements, lessons learned, and research agenda" (2020)

**Type:** journal-length systematic mapping study (educational technology / instructional design) · **Discipline:** empirical, meta-level (systematic review methodology) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand E's larger of two major reviews (alongside ped-sec-07 Mayer et al. 2022); a 38-article systematic mapping of immersive-VR-in-higher-education literature (2016–2018) that supplies the cluster's own taxonomy of VR design elements, learning content, and learning theories rather than a single empirical finding.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Radianti et al. propose that no existing overview combines an immersive-VR-only focus with higher-education specificity, learning-content granularity, VR design-element mapping, learning-theory coverage, and outcome-evaluation methods in one place (FE, pp.5–7), and set out to fill that gap through a systematic mapping study of 38 articles (2016–2018) coded across four analysis frameworks (FE, pp.7–12). Five guiding research questions — later split into nine coded RQs — ask how immersive HMD-based VR is embedded into higher education, what design elements/content/theories/evaluation methods are used, and which application domains apply it (FE, pp.1–2, 9). The paper's own methodological self-description is a "systematic mapping" in Kitchenham et al.'s (2009) sense: broader and less hypothesis-driven than a standard systematic review, aimed at charting sub-topics, methods, and gaps in an active field rather than answering one narrow empirical question (FE, p.7).

## 2. Structure

Seven numbered sections plus three appendices (pp.32+, not analyzed here beyond the article list).

1. **Introduction** (pp.1–2) — frames HMD-based VR as an increasingly attractive educational technology given falling costs and rising market projections; states the five guiding RQs.
2. **Theoretical background** (§2.1 VR and immersion; §2.2 Learning paradigms, pp.2–4) — defines VR via Biocca & Delaney (1995); surveys the immersion-as-technological-attribute (Slater & Wilbur, 1997) vs. immersion-as-psychological-state (Witmer & Singer, 1998) split; adopts a technology-type inclusion criterion; surveys behaviorism, cognitivism, constructivism, experientialism, connectivism as the learning-theory backdrop.
3. **Related work** (§3.1 Literature reviews; §3.2 Gaps, pp.5–7) — digests six prior reviews (Feng et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2018; Chavez & Bayona, 2018; Suh & Prophet, 2018; Jensen & Konradsen, 2018; Merchant et al., 2014) and argues each leaves a combinable gap.
4. **Research design** (pp.7–12) — four-database systematic search (Scopus, IEEEXplore, ProQuest, Web of Science; 2016–2018), semi-automatic filtering (KH Coder 3 word-cluster extraction) plus three manual filtering passes, and the four-framework coding scheme.
5. **Results and analysis** (RQ1–RQ9, pp.12–20) — reports the coded distributions and two cross-tabulations (application domain × learning content; learning content × design elements).
6. **Discussion** (§6.1 Implications; §6.2 Research agenda; §6.3 Recommendations for lecturers; §6.4 Limitations, pp.21–24) — argues the field is of "low maturity"; proposes a six-point research agenda; recommends lecturers begin with declarative-knowledge applications.
7. **Conclusion and future research** (p.24) — restates the low-maturity, theory-thin, usability-over-outcomes picture.

## 3. Constructs

- **immersion (technological)** — the review's own operational fulcrum: adopts Slater & Wilbur's (1997) technology-attribute definition purely to make study *inclusion* tractable (mobile/high-end/enhanced HMDs = immersive; desktop VR and CAVE = non-immersive, explicitly excluded), while explicitly flagging — but not adjudicating — the rival Witmer & Singer (1998) view of immersion as a subjective, person-varying psychological state (FE, pp.2–3). This is a genuinely two-sided treatment of debate axis 3 (§4 of the cluster plan): Radianti takes a side operationally (technology-type sorting) without claiming it resolves the theoretical dispute.
- **agency / interactivity** — defined via Steuer (1995) as "the degree to which a user can modify the VR environment in real-time" (FE, p.2), then operationalized richly through the review's own design-element framework (Table 8): basic interaction with objects, assembling objects, interaction with other users, moving around, and making meaningful choices are five of the fourteen coded design elements, with basic interaction the single most frequent (24% of articles) (FE, pp.13, 20).
- **presence (spatial/physical)** — defined via Witmer & Singer's (1998) "subjective experience of being in one place... even when one is physically situated in another" and named, alongside immersion and interactivity, as one of VR's three "core characteristics" (Ryan, 2015; Walsh & Pawlowski, 2002) (FE, p.2). Presence itself is not one of the review's four coded frameworks — it functions as background definitional apparatus rather than a variable the 38 studies were themselves coded against.
- **learning outcomes: procedural / skills** — the single largest learning-content category coded (33% of articles), exemplified by firefighting procedures and surgical/nursing tasks (Zhang et al., 2017; Farra et al., 2018) (FE, pp.16, 19–20); also the content category associated with the largest average number of design elements per article (FE, p.20).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — maps onto "declarative knowledge" (25% of articles; e.g., planet names, theoretical concepts) (FE, p.16), but Radianti's more consequential contribution here is a *finding*, not a construct definition: almost half the reviewed articles report no learning-outcome evaluation method at all, and most evaluation that does occur targets usability rather than outcomes (FE, pp.15, 21–22).
- **representational fidelity** — coded as the "realistic surroundings" design element (17% of articles, second-most-common after basic interaction) (FE, p.13); Radianti's Discussion explicitly names "realism" as an ambiguous, non-uniform term spanning high-fidelity graphical complexity on one hand and mere environmental "recognizability" on the other (FE, p.22).
- **social presence / co-presence** — touched only via the "interaction with other users" design element (9% of articles: avatars, instant messaging, voice chat, shared virtual spaces) and the "communication, collaboration, soft skills" learning-content category (10%) (FE, pp.13, 16, 20); not theorized as its own construct, only coded as one design feature among fourteen.
- **embodiment / self-representation** — analogical-only, via a single cited example (Parmar et al.'s VEnvI, where users see "a co-located virtual self-avatar that mimics their own movements") offered as an instance of a design pattern, not as an operationalized construct in the review's own coding scheme (FAITHFUL-Radianti, p.19–20).
- **engagement / flow** — cited secondhand: Jensen & Konradsen's (2018) reviewed studies found HMD users "more engaged" and spending "more time on the learning tasks" (FE, p.3); "flow theory" also appears, once, among the theoretical foundations Suh & Prophet (2018) catalogue (FE, p.5) — Radianti's own 38-article coding does not treat flow/engagement as a first-class category.

Not treated (deferred to "Not treated" line below): cognitive load, self-efficacy, situational interest, cybersickness/physical discomfort, and affect/intrinsic motivation are absent or reduced to single passing mentions.

## 4. Conditions & design

Radianti et al.'s "condition" is the systematic-mapping apparatus itself — the review's own categorization scheme is the primary object worth cataloguing here, since this cluster concords sources against media-condition columns and this unit *is* a "review / meta-analytic aggregate" that further sorts every other article under "IVR (HMD)."

**Sample.** From an initial aggregated search of 3,219 articles across four databases (2016–2018 window; peer-reviewed journals + conference papers, English only), semi-automatic filtering (exclusion/inclusion keyword lists derived from KH Coder 3 word-cluster extraction, applied per-database) reduced the pool to 1,864, duplicate removal to 590, and three successive manual filtering passes (abstract screening with double-coding → content screening with a twenty-paper intercoder-reliability calibration set → full coding-stage exclusion) yielded 38 final included articles (FE, Table 3, pp.8–11).

**Radianti's own four-framework coding scheme (its own taxonomy):**
1. *Research design framework* — after Wendler (2012): empirical qualitative, empirical quantitative, conceptual, design-oriented (FE, Table 4, p.10).
2. *Research method / data collection & analysis framework* — ten data-collection categories (development, experimental/usability/user testing, survey, interview/focus group, observation, case study/action research, literature review, mobile sensing, interaction log in VR app, no method applied) crossed with quantitative/qualitative analysis methods emergent from coding (t-test, correlation, ANOVA, chi-square, Fisher's exact, Mann–Whitney U, McNemar's, multilevel linear modeling, ANCOVA, descriptive statistics, qualitative analysis, no method applied) (FE, Table 5, pp.10–12).
3. *Learning framework* — subdivided into (a) learning theory (behavioral, experiential, generative, operational, game-based, contextual, Jeffries simulation theory, cone of learning theory; FE, Table 6, p.15) and (b) learning content (analytical/problem-solving; communication/collaboration/soft skills; procedural–practical knowledge; declarative knowledge; learning a language; behavioral impacts; others; not specified; FE, Table 7, pp.16, 18–19).
4. *Design-element framework* — fourteen categories emergent from the literature and extending Wohlgenannt, Fromm, Stieglitz, Radianti & Majchrzak (2019): realistic surroundings, passive observation, moving around, basic interaction with objects, assembling objects, interaction with other users, role management, screen sharing, user-generated content, instructions, immediate feedback, knowledge test, virtual rewards, making meaningful choices (FE, Table 8, p.13).

**Instrumentation note:** because this is a review-of-reviews-plus-primary-studies, there is no first-order instrument of Radianti's own; the "instruments" are the coding categories above, applied by four coders with an explicit intercoder-calibration step (twenty-paper coding test, consensus discussion rather than a quantitative reliability statistic) (FE, p.11).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- 76% of the 38 studies used high-end HMDs (Oculus Rift, HTC Vive); of 41 total VR-technology counts, eight used low-budget mobile HMDs and only a handful used enhanced VR (HMD + gloves/bodysuit/racing-wheel peripherals) (FE, pp.13–14).
- Development research (26 articles), experimental design/usability/user testing (18), and survey (16) were the leading data-collection approaches; t-test (16 articles) was the most common quantitative analysis method, but 18 articles reported no explicit, recognizable data-analysis method at all (FE, pp.14–15).
- Learning theories were absent — "not mentioned" — in 68% of articles; where present, experiential learning led at 11%, with all remaining named theories at 3% each (FE, p.15).
- Nearly half the articles specified no learning-outcome evaluation method; where present, questionnaires (22%) and logged VR-application user activity (12%) led, with exams, expert judgment, and focus groups at 5% each (FE, p.15).
- Eighteen application domains were identified; engineering led at 24%, followed by computer science (10%) and astronomy (7%); 12% of articles were too generic to assign a specific domain (FE, p.16).
- Procedural–practical knowledge (33%) and declarative knowledge (25%) were the leading learning-content categories, followed by analytical/problem-solving skills (12%) (FE, p.16).
- Basic interaction (24%) and realistic surroundings (17%) were the most frequent design elements, with immediate feedback and instructions tied at 10% (FE, p.17).
- The two cross-tabulations (RQ8 domain × content; RQ9 content × design element) show a sparse but interpretable matrix: astronomy skews declarative; biology/chemistry mix declarative and procedural; engineering spans nearly every content category given its size; nursing, physics, and surgical medicine skew procedural; basic interaction and realistic surroundings recur across every content category, while communication/collaboration content associates with the largest average number of distinct design elements (FE, pp.17–20).
- Discussion's central diagnosis: VR-for-higher-education is a field of "low maturity" — mostly experimental/prototype work, rarely embedded in routine teaching, with evaluation skewed toward usability rather than learning outcomes, and persistent terminological ambiguity around "immersion" and "realism" (FE, pp.21–22).
- Six-point research agenda: (1) terminological consolidation toward a shared taxonomy; (2) build cumulatively on prior work rather than exploring from scratch, aiming for generalizable results; (3) cross-disciplinary best-practice exchange; (4) evaluation procedures that assess learning outcomes, not just usability; (5) continued technological progress toward realism/immersion (borrowing from gaming's faster development pace); (6) eventual curricular inclusion, both VR-as-teaching-tool and VR-as-teaching-subject (FE, pp.22–23).
- Recommendation for lecturers: begin with declarative-knowledge applications using only the two baseline design elements (basic interaction, realistic surroundings) as a low-complexity "soft start"; procedural-knowledge applications carry the largest design-element footprint and require more VR experience and possibly curricular change, but the authors judge VR's "true potential" to lie in procedural "learn by doing," not in better declarative delivery (FE, p.23).
- Stated limitations: HMD-immersive-only inclusion criterion excludes 360-video, desktop VR, mixed reality, and multi-user VR domes; search window capped at 2016–2018 (FE, p.24).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* As the cluster's larger systematic review, this unit is Part III's best available compact source for *what the immersive-VR-in-higher-education literature as a whole looks like methodologically* — its own four-framework taxonomy (research design, data collection/analysis, learning theory/content, design elements) offers Part III a ready-made vocabulary for classifying its own VLE-based interventions without inventing new coding categories from scratch *(anticipatory-application)*. Radianti's central diagnosis — that immersive VR remains mostly experimental/prototype work "rather than being applied regularly in actual teaching" (FE, p.21) — bears directly on `vle-01-deployment-survey`: the VLE King–Salvo entry documents an *actually deployed, routine-use* learning environment, which is precisely the exception this review's own base-rate finding would predict is rare; Part III can cite Radianti to frame the VLE's deployment status as an unusual (and hence noteworthy) case rather than a typical one *(anticipatory-application)*. Radianti's finding that "basic interaction" (24%) and "immediate feedback" (10%) are the most common design elements, paired with the secondhand Jensen & Konradsen finding that immersive-HMD users "spent more time on the learning tasks," is relevant to `vle-04-mark-attention-span`: the review supplies design-element vocabulary for describing *what* interactive features might sustain time-on-task, while flagging that this literature's own evaluation of such claims is weak (usability-favoring, outcome-evaluation-poor) — a caution Part III should carry when citing "more engagement" claims from this tradition *(anticipatory-application)*. Because Radianti's own inclusion criterion sorts studies by *technology type* (HMD-immersive vs. desktop/CAVE non-immersive) rather than by *deployment context* (lab vs. home vs. classroom), this axis must not be silently conflated with Part III's own lab=VR ↔ deployment=2D-PC poles: Radianti's exclusion of desktop VR from this review's own corpus is a scoping decision about equipment, not a claim about deployment setting, and Part III's poles are cross-cutting rather than identical to it *(anticipatory-application)*. Finally, on debate axis 1/2 (§4 of the cluster plan), Radianti and Mayer et al. (ped-sec-07) occupy genuinely opposite poles: where Mayer et al. argue methods/value-added design — not the medium — should guide practice, treating media-comparison research as largely uninformative, Radianti et al. read the field's own evaluation weaknesses (theory-thin, usability-over-outcomes) as a maturity/agenda problem to be *fixed*, not as evidence against immersion's underlying promise — this is affordance-optimism tempered by methodological critique, not naive boosterism, since Radianti documents the identical evaluation-quality gaps Mayer highlights but reads them as a research-agenda item rather than a verdict *(anticipatory-application)*.

**Not treated:** cognitive load, self-efficacy, situational interest, cybersickness/physical discomfort, and affect/intrinsic motivation as a dedicated construct are absent from Radianti et al.'s own four coding frameworks; desktop-3D/non-immersive VR and 2D-video/text conditions are excluded from the reviewed corpus by the paper's own inclusion criteria, not merely under-discussed.
