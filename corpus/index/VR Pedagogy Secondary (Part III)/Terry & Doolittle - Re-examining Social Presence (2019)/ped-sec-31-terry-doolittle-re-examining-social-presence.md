# ped-sec-31 — Terry & Doolittle, "Re-examining Social Presence: Implications for Digital Pedagogies" (2019)

**Type:** journal-article-length secondary reading (educational technology, social-presence theory) · **Discipline:** education / educational technology (computer-mediated communication, digital pedagogies) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F's synthesizing hub — a literature review spanning three generational phases of social-presence theory that directly engages nearly every other Strand F foundational source in this cluster, including a sustained reading of ped-sec-26 (Garrison, Anderson & Archer's Community of Inquiry model), and that proposes re-defining social presence as a non-mediated psychological construct built from five factors.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Terry and Doolittle open from a diagnosis: social presence theory has been "a seminal part of digital pedagogies literature" since the earliest generation of computer-mediated communication (CMC) scholarship, yet across multiple generations of development it still lacks a stable, widely accepted definition (FE, p.121). Definitions range from those tying social presence to the affordances and attributes of a communication medium to those treating it as a phenomenal state experienced by participants regardless of setting (FE, p.121). Following Lee's (2004) distinction between mediated presence (perceptions transported or facilitated by technology) and non-mediated presence (perceptions that are direct, natural, or immediate, purely psychological in character), the authors argue that recasting social presence as a non-mediated psychological construct — rather than as a byproduct of a specific technology — shifts research attention away from media affordances and toward instructional and individual differences in how social presence is generated and how it affects learning (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.121–122). The paper's stated goal is to trace the history and evolution of the concept, synthesize the current literature base to identify the major factors that contribute to social presence, and thereby build the case for testing those factors in a non-mediated setting — the necessary first step, in the authors' argument, toward a stable definition and, downstream, more accurate measurement across both mediated and non-mediated environments (FE, p.121–124).

## 2. Structure

The article runs six untitled-by-number but internally headed movements.

1. **Introduction: mediated vs. non-mediated presence** (pp.121–124) — states the definitional problem, introduces Lee's (2004) mediated/non-mediated distinction, and uses Weidlich & Bastiaens's (2017) SIPS model (sociability, social interaction, social presence, social space) as an example of a study whose own findings beg unanswered questions about the instructional and individual-difference mechanisms linking social interaction to social presence to learning.
2. **Digital pedagogy and social presence** (pp.122–124) — situates social presence within a broader call (Morris, 2013; Wadmany & Kliachko, 2014) for digital pedagogues to move past LMS-bound instruction; uses Bronack et al.'s (2008) 3D virtual-campus project, which coined the term "Presence Pedagogy," as an exemplar; cites Richardson, Maeda, Lv & Caskurlu's (2017) meta-analysis of social presence's variable correlation with satisfaction and perceived learning as motivating evidence.
3. **History of social presence: three phases** (pp.125–127) — following Cui et al. (2013) and Lowenthal (2010), divides the field into a medium-based Phase 1 (1970s–80s, Short, Williams & Christie), a media-appropriateness-critique Phase 2 (1990–1999, Rutter's Cuelessness Theory, Daft & Lengel's Media Richness Theory, Walther's Social Information Processing Theory), and a Community-of-Inquiry-situated online-learning Phase 3 (2000–present).
4. **Definitions and measures: models overview** (pp.127–130) — reviews five foundational models in sequence: Gunawardena & Zittle's (1997) Social Presence Scale, Tu's (2002) Social Presence and Privacy Questionnaire and Tu & McIsaac's (2002) four relational dimensions, the Rourke/Garrison/Archer-derived Community of Inquiry model, Biocca, Harms & Burgoon's (2003) call for conceptual clarity, and Sung & Mayer's (2012) Online Social Presence Questionnaire.
5. **Charting the course: five factors** (pp.130–136) — proposes testing social presence in a non-mediated setting via five factors distilled from the review: group cohesion, co-presence/salience of others, affective association, immediacy, and intimacy.
6. **Conclusion: implications of re-examining social presence** (pp.136–137) — endorses Kim et al.'s (2016) view that social presence occurs independent of any specific technology, calls for testing the construct across instructional environments, pedagogies, populations, domains, and cultures, raises an open question about social presence's independence from the Community of Inquiry's cognitive-teaching-social triad, and closes on a validity chain: valid measurement leads to accurately described impact, which leads to effective instructional interventions.

## 3. Constructs

The article's whole analytic apparatus is organized around a single construct-axis row, **social presence / co-presence**, which it argues has been measured too inconsistently because it has never been isolated from the medium in which it is studied (FE, p.121, 130–131). Rather than adding new constructs alongside social presence, the paper decomposes that one construct into five non-canonical sub-factors, each reviewed with its own literature and each assigned to a distinct psychological "root":

- **Group cohesion** — the capacity of a team or community to work together toward shared tasks and learning goals; drawn from definitions of community in the presence literature (Garrison, Anderson & Archer, 1999; Rovai, 2002) and Whiteside's (2015) social-presence model, whose elements (resource sharing, seeing the group as a cohesive whole, approachability) align with Rourke, Garrison & Archer's (1999) Social Presence Coding scheme (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.131–132).
- **Co-presence / salience of others** — a clear sense of who else is present, "regardless of setting," drawn from Biocca, Harms & Burgoon's and de Greef & IJsselsteijn's (2000) "sense of being together" and Kreijns, Kirschner, Jochems & Buuren's (2010) distinction between immediate/synchronous and delayed/asynchronous communication episodes (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.132–133).
- **Affective association** — warmth and safety that free learners to act and interact, rooted in Short, Williams & Christie's (1976) original bipolar semantic-differential scales (unsociable–sociable, cold–warm, impersonal–personal) and Whiteside's (2015) affective-association expansion of the Rourke/Garrison/Archer coding scale (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.133–134).
- **Immediacy** — verbal and nonverbal closeness behaviors reducing psychological and physical distance, per Mehrabian's (1971) taxonomy and Gunawardena & Zittle's (1997) teacher-immediacy behaviors; Garrison (2011) is cited for the claim that immediacy reduces personal risk and facilitates critical discourse (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.133–135).
- **Intimacy** — personalization and communication equilibrium, rooted in Argyle & Dean's (1965) eye-contact studies and expanded by Lombard & Ditton's (1997) list of intimacy behaviors (posture, gesture, facial expression, touch); Short, Williams & Christie (1976) are also credited with naming eye-contact, smiling, and personal conversation as intimacy-promoting behaviors (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.134–135).

None of these five sub-factors maps cleanly onto other rows in this cluster's §3 construct axis (they are internal components of social presence itself, not distinct constructs such as cognitive load or self-efficacy); they are recorded here as sub-factors of the single row they jointly ground.

The paper also treats one closely related THEORY-MODEL — the **Community of Inquiry (CoI)** — reviewed as one of the five foundational models but explicitly judged, in the authors' own voice, to have "broadened and arguably convoluted definitions and conceptions of social presence" by entangling it with cognitive presence, teaching presence, and (via Shea, 2010) learning presence, even as its "real people" definition of social presence is used elsewhere in the paper as a touchstone (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.129, 133). This is the paper's most direct engagement with ped-sec-26 (Garrison, Anderson & Archer, 2000). A second related construct, **interactivity**, is discussed only to be distinguished from social presence: citing Picciano (2002), the authors note users "may be highly interactive without feeling 'present,'" even though many studies continue to investigate the two as near-synonyms (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.125–126).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Despite multiple generations of theoretical development since Short, Williams & Christie's (1976) foundational definition, social presence still lacks a stable, widely accepted definition (FE, p.121–122).
- Social presence theory divides into three generational phases: a medium-based Phase 1 (1970s–80s), a media-appropriateness-critique Phase 2 (1990–1999, Cuelessness Theory, Media Richness Theory, Social Information Processing Theory), and a Community-of-Inquiry-situated online-learning Phase 3 (2000–present) (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, following Cui et al. 2013 and Lowenthal 2010, p.125–127).
- The Community of Inquiry model's entanglement of social presence with cognitive presence, teaching presence, and (later) learning presence broadened and, in the authors' own assessment, arguably convoluted the construct's definition (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.129).
- Interactivity and social presence are not synonymous constructs, even though many studies continue to treat them as such; users can be highly interactive without feeling "present" (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, citing Picciano 2002, p.125–126).
- Five factors — group cohesion, co-presence/salience of others, affective association, immediacy, and intimacy — are proposed as the most critical contributors to learning environments high in social presence, derived from a literature review undertaken in direct response to Biocca et al.'s (2003) call to address unmediated as well as mediated interaction (FE, p.130–131).
- Richardson, Maeda, Lv & Caskurlu's (2017) meta-analysis found positive but highly variable correlations between social presence and both course satisfaction and perceived learning, with variation attributable to discipline, course length, and audience — cited as motivating evidence for a more precise, stable definition (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.123–124).
- The paper's closing validity chain: once social presence is validly measured as a non-mediated psychological construct, its impact on variables of interest can be accurately described; once accurately described, effective instructional interventions can be created (FAITHFUL-Terry-Doolittle, p.136–137).
- An open question is left unresolved rather than answered: whether social presence is independent of the Community of Inquiry's cognitive-teaching-social presence triad, and, if so, whether cognitive and teaching presence are independent of one another as well (FE, p.136).
