# ped-sec-29 — Biocca, Burgoon & Harms, "Toward a More Robust Theory and Measure of Social Presence: Review and Suggested Criteria" (2003)

**Type:** theoretical review/critique (communication theory) · **Discipline:** communication theory / human-computer interaction (social presence theory) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F's most theoretically rigorous critique of prior social-presence measurement — a multi-author review that classifies, critiques, and proposes remedial criteria for the entire CMC-tradition literature this strand documents, rather than reporting a new empirical study of its own.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Biocca, Burgoon & Harms argue that networked and collaborative media need a more robust, detailed theory of social presence — the mediated "sense of being with another" (human or artificial intelligence), distinguished from spatial/telepresence's "sense of being in the place" — in order to guide prediction, measurement, and interface design (FE, p.456–457). The article states three explicit goals: review, classify, and critique current definitions of social presence; review and assess current measures; and propose criteria and scope conditions for a more usable theory and measure (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.457). Its provisional working definition — social presence as the "sense of being with another" — is flagged from the outset as inadequate shorthand, a placeholder the article's own later sections (§5.1) explicitly diagnose as vague and circular (FE, p.456, 469). The piece is thus not an empirical contribution but a foundation-laying critique: it aims to diagnose why the CMC-tradition measurement lineage this cluster's Strand F documents (Short, Williams & Christie 1976; Gunawardena & Zittle 1997/ped-sec-25) has stalled, and to propose scope conditions a future robust measure would need to satisfy (FE, p.476–477).

## 2. Structure

The article runs eight numbered/titled sections across pp.456–477, plus three tables (Table 1: definitions taxonomy; Table 2: measures taxonomy; Table 3: proposed criteria, scope conditions, and example scenarios).

1. **Introduction: the experience of "being with another"** (pp.456–457) — distinguishes social presence from spatial presence; states the provisional definition and the article's three goals.
2. **Examples where a more robust theory can advance research** (pp.457–460) — three problem areas: (2.1) explaining the design goals and social motivations behind "social presence technologies" (teleconferencing, mobile/wireless, agent-based e-commerce, 3D social virtual environments); (2.2) assessing how well such technologies perform, including Bob Lucky's imagined "social presence dial" caution that more social presence is not always desirable; (2.3) using social presence research as a route into theory-of-mind, social cognition, and interpersonal-communication questions.
3. **Current definitions and conceptualizations** (pp.460–465, Table 1) — traces the term to Short, Williams & Christie's (1976) social psychology of telecommunications (itself indebted to Goffman and Mead) and organizes existing definitions into three clusters: copresence, psychological involvement, and behavioral engagement.
4. **Measures of social presence** (pp.465–469, Table 2) — catalogs three measurement families: subjective self-report (dominated by Short et al.'s semantic-differential scale), behavioral indicators, and psychophysiological measures.
5. **Limitations of current theories and measures** (pp.469–472) — five recurring problems, discussed below.
6. **Toward a theory: fundamental questions and operationalization** (pp.472–474) — proposes a joint "technology question" and "psychological question," and argues the theory's scope should be technologically mediated interaction specifically.
7. **Criteria, scope conditions, and example scenarios** (pp.474–476, Table 3) — four proposed scope-condition criteria, each paired with stress-test example scenarios.
8. **Summary and conclusions** (pp.476–477) — restates the diagnosed limitations and the proposed remedy; frames the contribution as foundation-laying rather than a finished theory or instrument.

## 3. Constructs

**Social presence / co-presence** is the article's central, namesake construct, and the piece treats it almost entirely as a *critical object* rather than an operationalized variable of its own. Table 1 sorts existing definitions into three clusters: **copresence** (colocation and mutual sensory awareness, after Goffman 1959's account of the embodied other and Heeter's 1992 "apparent existence... of the other"); **psychological involvement** (Biocca's 1997 "access to another intelligence" — the degree to which a user feels access to another's intelligence, intentions, and sensory impressions — plus Short et al.'s "salience of the other," and intimacy/immediacy/mutual-understanding variants); and **behavioral engagement** (Palmer's 1995 "interdependent, multichannel exchange of behavior"), each family illustrated with several named example studies (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.461–465). The article's own critique of prior *measurement* is organized around Table 2's parallel three families: subjective self-report (Short et al.'s four-item semantic-differential scale — "unsociable-sociable, insensitive-sensitive, cold-warm, impersonal-personal" — remains the most widely used instrument despite the authors' judgment that it measures attitudes toward the medium rather than a state of the interaction; involvement/intimacy/immediacy scales, e.g. Burgoon & Hale; social-judgment measures such as Nowak's homophily), behavioral indicators (rare as explicit social-presence measures — Heeter's media-choice study, Bailenson et al.'s approach-avoidance/proxemics work), and psychophysiological measures (heart rate, skin conductance, fMRI — reported as effectively unused for social presence despite precedent elsewhere in social psychology, and cautioned against via Blascovich's 2000 warning that "invariant indexes... of social psychological constructs often prove illusive") (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.465–469).

**Presence (spatial/physical)** appears principally as the construct social presence is defined *against*: telepresence is the "sense of being there," social presence the "sense of being together with another"; the article treats this bifurcation (following Heeter 1992 and Biocca 1997) as settled background rather than as something it must itself defend (FE, p.456, 460).

**Agency / interactivity** surfaces in two registers: as part of the behavioral-engagement definitional cluster (interdependent multichannel behavior exchange), and, more substantively, in the article's own proposed "psychological question" — what properties of human cognition attribute minds, intentions, and agency to representations (linking to Dennett's intentional stance) — which the authors treat as one of the two pillars (alongside the "technology question") a robust theory must jointly address (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.472–473).

**Embodiment / self-representation** is treated principally through the recurring claim that the body is "a key medium for communicating both presence" and for modeling another's internal states (Goffman-derived), extended to mediated cases where the other is embodied by "some avatar, agent, or simpler representational device" (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.461–462), and via the article's own example scenarios in Table 3 (a user continuing "to feel an avatar is interacting when the human controlling the avatar is no longer connected to his embodied shell") that stress-test whether a robust measure survives representational/embodiment edge cases (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.476).

## 4. Conditions & design

**No media-condition axis content applies to this unit as an empirical study, because this is a theoretical review article, not a manipulated experiment.** Its own "design" is a conceptual synthesis with three methodological components, each worth recording as the source's actual apparatus: (1) a **taxonomic literature review** (Table 1) sorting roughly two dozen prior published definitions of social presence into the three clusters above, each entry keyed to its original source quotation and page citation; (2) a **parallel taxonomic review of instruments** (Table 2), organized by measurement modality (self-report / behavioral / psychophysiological) rather than by construct, with named exemplar studies for each cell; and (3) a **prescriptive criteria-and-scenario table** (Table 3) the authors themselves construct as the article's positive contribution — four scope-condition criteria (span different generations of communication technology; accommodate diverse interaction types from casual to hostile; span human and nonhuman others; apply to real and illusory social interactions), each paired with 2–4 concrete stress-test scenarios (e.g., "an individual feels social presence while observing sculpture"; "a child feels terror at the presence of a monster in a computer game"; "an individual continues to feel an avatar is interacting when the human controlling the avatar is no longer connected") meant to probe whether any resulting measure would survive edge cases (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.474–476). This is the review's operative methodology: classification, critique, and criteria-generation, not sampling, manipulation, or statistical estimation. Strand F is not required to populate the §3 media-condition axis, and this unit genuinely has no cells to contribute there — the "technology question" and "media generations" the authors discuss function as prescriptive scope conditions for a *future* theory, not as tested experimental arms.

## 5. Findings/Claims

The article's substantive contribution is a diagnosis-plus-remedy pair. The **diagnosis** (§5) identifies five recurring problems across the reviewed literature: (1) definitions that are vague, overly broad, or circular — "sense of being together" as mere restatement of the term itself, providing no operationalizable dimensions (FE, p.469); (2) confounding of social presence as a psychological state with its own correlates or effects, illustrated by the ambiguous status of homophily/similarity measures (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.469–470); (3) conceptual drift between social presence as a fluctuating phenomenal state versus a fixed property of the medium, with Short et al.'s measure singled out as explicitly medium-oriented — the authors note it was shaped by its original funders' (UK Post Office, GE) interest in rating media channels rather than by a theoretical commitment to measuring users' transient states (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.470–471); (4) measurement items that embed unstated technology assumptions (about display type, input modality, or "the virtual room"), precluding valid cross-media comparison (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.470–471); and (5) measures tailored to narrow interaction classes (collaboration, impression formation) that break down for atypical cases such as hostile or competitive mediated encounters — the authors' own example: someone "five feet from you in an immersive virtual environment, sneering and pointing a gun directly at your head" plainly has high social presence despite scoring low on "liked them" or "would repeat this interaction" items (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.471–472).

The **remedy** (§6–§7) is twofold. First, a robust theory must jointly address a "technology question" (what interface properties elicit social responses — the traditional design-research starting point) and a "psychological question" (what properties of human cognition attribute minds to representations, linked to Dennett's intentional stance and theory-of-mind research), and its scope should be technologically mediated interaction specifically, not all social interaction generally (FE, p.472–474). Second, because social presence appears irreducibly multidimensional across the reviewed literature, a workable measure must support multidimensional operationalization; the authors propose four scope-condition criteria (technology-generation span; interaction-type span; human/nonhuman-other span; real/illusory-interaction span) that any future measure should satisfy without modification (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.474–476). The conclusion explicitly frames the contribution as foundation-laying — "some modest contribution toward building a foundation" — rather than a finished theory or validated instrument (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.477).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* Within Strand F, this article functions as the measurement-critique hub against which the strand's empirical instrument-building papers can be read: it cites Gunawardena & Zittle (1997/ped-sec-25) directly, twice — once in Table 2's "Immediacy of the medium" cell and once in the body text noting that Gunawardena & Zittle (1997) measured intimacy by adapting Short et al.'s "semantic differential scales" toward the intimacy construct (FAITHFUL-Biocca-Burgoon-Harms, p.465, 467–468) — treating SPRES as one instance of the broader self-report family it is critiquing rather than singling it out for special either endorsement or rebuttal. The article does **not** cite Tu (2002/ped-sec-27) or Garrison, Anderson & Archer (1999/ped-sec-26), despite sharing Tu's central target (Short et al.'s scale as inadequate for CMC) almost exactly; the two pieces arrived at parallel diagnoses independently and within months of each other, which this unit treats as a debate-axis/contest-reading-of relationship rather than a citation relationship (P/anticipatory-application). *(anticipatory-application)* For the VLE anchor, this article's five-limitation diagnosis is most useful as a caution rather than a confirmation: vle-01's F7 finding ("Presence & the WE," a per-100-respondent gap in presence-coded open-text language between app-users and video-only respondents) and vle-03's exemplar quotes on social co-watching are exactly the kind of unstructured, medium-embedded evidence this article's §5.4 warns against over-reading as a validated social-presence measurement — the VLE has no instrument built to this article's proposed multidimensional/cross-media standard, so any claim that F7 "measures" social presence in Biocca-Burgoon-Harms's sense should be flagged as suggestive, not confirmatory (P/anticipatory-application). *(anticipatory-application)* More generally, this article's core methodological caution — that a measure embedding assumptions about one medium (display type, input modality) cannot support valid comparisons across media — bears directly on Part III's own desktop-3D/2D-poles design: any survey item asking about "presence" or "connectedness" that was phrased with one modality's affordances in mind risks exactly the cross-media invalidity this article diagnoses in Short et al.'s instrument, a consideration Part III's instrumentation discussion should weigh explicitly rather than assume away (P/anticipatory-application).

Not treated: learning outcomes (retention/transfer or procedural/skills), cognitive load, affect/enjoyment/intrinsic motivation, situational interest, self-efficacy, engagement/flow, and cybersickness/physical discomfort — none of these §3 construct-axis rows receive any treatment in this article, which stays entirely within social-presence definition-and-measurement critique and never engages the pedagogy/learning-outcomes literature that anchors the rest of this cluster.
