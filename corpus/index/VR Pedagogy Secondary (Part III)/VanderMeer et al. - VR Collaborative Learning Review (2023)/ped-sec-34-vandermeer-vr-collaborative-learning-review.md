# ped-sec-34 — van der Meer, van der Werf, Brinkman & Specht, "Virtual Reality and Collaborative Learning: A Systematic Literature Review" (2023)

**Type:** PRISMA systematic review (empirical-literature review) · **Discipline:** educational technology / computer-supported collaborative learning (CSCL) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand F's second VR-specific bridge piece and, per the manifest's own Phase-1 correction, the *stronger* of the two candidates for genuinely bridging the cluster's CMC-tradition social-presence literature (ped-sec-25…32) to its VR/immersive-learning literature (Strands B–E) — though even this unit does not perform that bridge itself; it surveys the VRCL empirical base broadly and cites this cluster's own VR-pedagogy sources directly.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

The review's stated aim is to be the first systematic (PRISMA-compliant) literature review specific to Virtual Reality for Collaborative Learning (VRCL) — a subfield the authors position as an outgrowth of Computer-Supported Collaborative Learning (CSCL, since the 1980s), itself grounded in social-learning theory (FE, p.2). Three prior VRCL reviews (Muhammad Nur Affendy & Ajune Wanis 2019; Zheng et al. 2019; Scavarelli et al. 2021) are read as jointly establishing a "general optimism" toward VR for collaboration, five prior-identified affordances, and four gaps (accessibility, VE-real-world interplay, pedagogical strategy, and a real-vs-novel-situation balance) that this review inherits as its point of departure (FAITHFUL-vanderMeer, p.2). Against that backdrop, the authors pose four research questions — what skills/competences are trained, what domains/disciplines are addressed, what systems are used, and what empirical knowledge has been established — and pursue them via a formal PRISMA screening of 1,608 candidate records down to 139 included articles (FE, p.2–3). The review's own framing throughout privileges breadth and taxonomic classification over close reading of any single study: its unit of analysis is the field's empirical base as a whole, not individual constructs in the depth this cluster's Strand-C process-experiment units (e.g., ped-sec-10, ped-sec-13) achieve.

## 2. Structure

The article runs Introduction → Methods → Descriptive Results → Qualitative Results → Conclusion, a standard systematic-review skeleton rather than a chaptered argument.

1. **Introduction** (pp.1–3) — situates VRCL within CSCL and social-learning theory; surveys the three prior VRCL reviews and the gaps they leave; states the four research questions.
2. **Methods** (pp.3–4) — Scopus + Web of Science search string; five inclusion / seven exclusion criteria; PRISMA screening from 1,608 to 139 articles; a three-category taxonomy (Education / System / Evaluation) built partly on Bloom's taxonomy (1956), with inter-rater reliability reported for both the screening (Cohen's κ=0.77, five additional coders on a 50-study sample) and the taxonomy coding (κ=0.60, 85% agreement, one second coder on 30 studies).
3. **Descriptive Results** (pp.4–9, Tables 1–3) — quantitative distributions across all three taxonomy dimensions (detailed under §4 below).
4. **Qualitative Results** (pp.9–13) — five skill categories trained, nine required VRCL design features, an extended discussion of the HMD/non-HMD disparity and its likely causes, disciplinary motivations (education; computer science/robotics/informatics; social sciences), and the five identified affordances of VRCL with supporting citations.
5. **Conclusion and future research** (p.13) — restates the skill categories, design features, and affordances; proposes four future-research directions (systematic HMD-vs-non-HMD comparison, more affective/psychomotor attention, more true-experimental designs, adoption of physiological measures).

## 3. Constructs

This unit uses **"co-presence"/"social presence" only glancingly and never as a load-bearing organizing construct** — a datum worth stating plainly rather than inferring. Full-text search confirms exactly two mentions of "social presence" in the running text (p.9, p.11), both citing VRCL-specific sources (Edirisingha et al. 2009; Denoyelles & Kyeong-Ju Seo 2012) rather than the CMC-tradition canon this cluster's Strand F otherwise tracks (Short/Williams/Christie, Gunawardena & Zittle, Garrison/Anderson/Archer — all confirmed absent from the reference list) (FE, p.9, p.11). Where the review does have sustained vocabulary for the social dimension of VRCL, it prefers the broader, less theorized terms "collaboration," "socialization," and "co-presence"-adjacent phrasing like "awareness and belonging," borrowed at second hand from Molka-Danielsen & Brask (2014) and Edirisingha et al. (2009) rather than developed as the review's own construct (FAITHFUL-vanderMeer, p.11–12).

- **social presence / co-presence** — treated only as a cited factor influencing "perceived usefulness" and group cohesion (Denoyelles & Kyeong-Ju Seo 2012; Yeh et al. 2012), and as part of Molka-Danielsen & Brask's (2014) "presence, awareness, belonging" triad for remote collaboration; never defined or measured by the review itself (FAITHFUL-vanderMeer, p.9, p.11–12).
- **presence (spatial/physical)** — invoked generally as VR's capacity to make users "behave inside the VE as they would in the physical world" (citing Jensen & Konradsen 2018), and as one of VRCL's high-level affordances (immersion, presence) strengthening "social interaction" (FE, p.2, p.12).
- **immersion (technological)** — the review's central taxonomic axis is "degree of virtuality" (AR/MR vs 3D-simulation-non-HMD vs HMD VR), which operationalizes immersion as a hardware category rather than a phenomenological construct; 78.4% of studies used non-HMD 3D simulation, 16.5% AR/MR, and only 7.2% HMD VR (FE, Table 2, p.6–7).
- **embodiment / self-representation** — coded as "type of virtual embodiment" (none/tools/partial/full-body); full-body embodiment dominant at 67.6%, largely because platforms like Second Life and OpenSimulator default to customizable avatars (FE, Table 2, p.7).
- **engagement / flow** — treated as a learning-goal category ("student engagement," 31.7% of studies) and as one of the review's five VRCL affordances ("efficient tool to engage and motivate learners") (FE, Table 1 p.5, p.11–12).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — folded into the "cognitive domain" learning-goal category (50.4% of studies) and discussed narratively as VR-to-real-world knowledge transfer (Carron et al. 2013) (FE, p.5, p.10).
- **learning outcomes: procedural/skills** — the review's own primary organizing taxonomy for RQ1: five skill categories (cultural, domain-specific, learning, physical, social skills), each with worked examples (Table 4) (FE, p.9).

Not carried forward as its own construct row but worth flagging: "physiological synchrony" is discussed as an under-used but promising evaluation channel (only 1 of 139 studies used physiological measures), a methodological gap rather than a construct the review itself theorizes (FE, p.11).

## 4. Conditions & design

As a PRISMA systematic review, this unit's "design" is the review's own methodology rather than a single study's; its "conditions" are the aggregate distribution of conditions across the 139 included primary studies. Both `review / meta-analytic aggregate` and `IVR (HMD)` concordance-axis labels apply, the latter as one coded category among the reviewed studies rather than as this unit's own experimental arm.

- **Search & screening:** Scopus + Web of Science, combined search string (collaboration terms × VR/AR/MR/XR terms × empirical-data terms × education terms × system terms); 1,608 unique records after de-duplication; five inclusion criteria (collaborative interaction; VR/AR/MR/XR as 3D simulation; empirical data; educational objective; described system) and seven exclusion criteria (patents; conference summaries only; literature reviews only; inaccessible; non-English; duplicate/older version; N not stated) narrowed this to 139 articles (FE, p.3–4).
- **Reliability:** five additional coders screened a 50-study sample for inclusion/exclusion agreement (Cohen's κ=0.77, "moderate," McHugh 2012); a second coder applied the full taxonomy to 30 studies (κ=0.60, 85% agreement) (FE, p.3–4).
- **Sample-of-studies design distribution (N=139):** pre-experimental 79.9%, quasi-experimental 13.7%, true-experimental 5.8%, single-subject 1.4%; survey 84.9%, observational 56.1%, correlational 15.8%, comparative 7.9% (categories not mutually exclusive) (FE, Table 3, p.6).
- **Evaluation-method distribution:** self-report 85.6%, behavioral/tracking 59.0%, knowledge/performance assessment 20.9%, physiological 0.7% (one study, heart rate) (FE, Table 3, p.6).
- **Sample-size distribution:** 1–25 participants 53.2%, 26–50 participants 26.6%, 51–100 participants 13.7%, 100+ participants 6.5% (FE, Table 3, p.6).
- **Hardware/virtuality distribution:** monitor/display 89.2%, CAVE 3.6%, HMD/glasses 10.8%; 3D-simulation-non-HMD 78.4%, AR/MR 16.5%, HMD VR 7.2%; general-purpose (mouse/keyboard) controls 79.9% vs positional tracking 11.5% and gaze control 15.1% (FE, Table 2, p.6–7).
- **Collaboration-size distribution:** small groups (3–9) 37.4%, pairs 22.3%, large groups (10+) 15.1%, open/unspecified 25.2% (FE, Table 2, p.6–7).
- **Outcome distribution:** positive/promising 53.2%, significantly positive 17.3%, mixed 7.2%, inconclusive/no-effect 20.1%, negative 2.2%, significantly negative 0.0% (FE, Table 3, p.6).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Five skill/competence categories are trained across the VRCL literature — cultural, domain-specific, learning, physical, and social skills — each illustrated with examples (Table 4) (FE, p.9).
- Nine features/design parameters a VRCL environment needs to train these skills: virtual embodiment; efficient communication tools (verbal audio as essential, haptic as an enhancer); usability/accessibility; perceived usefulness (including presence and social presence as contributing factors); intuitive object-interaction; task design supporting equal input; educator support/moderation; learner autonomy (independence from both educators and peers); and support for socialization as the environment's primary function (FAITHFUL-vanderMeer, p.9–11).
- Systems overwhelmingly favor non-HMD, monitor-based VR with general-purpose (mouse/keyboard) controls over HMD VR and specialized/positional-tracking hardware — a finding the authors read as running counter to VR's popular association with HMDs and immersive hardware (FE, p.6–7, p.11).
- Four candidate explanations are offered for the low HMD representation: cost; the accessibility burden of applying HMD to *collaborative* (multi-person) settings specifically; cybersickness/motion-sickness risk; and — closest to this cluster's own concerns — a persistent lack of established pedagogy for HMD VR and weak empirical evidence of its educational value, citing Sousa Santos et al. (2009), Makransky et al. (2019), and Radianti et al. (2020) (FAITHFUL-vanderMeer, p.10–11).
- Following Girvan's (2018) definitions, the review argues that VRCL's affordances belong to the *virtual environment*, not the access tool (HMD vs non-HMD) — but immediately qualifies this by conceding that tool choice still moderates outcomes: HMD VR is associated with higher embodiment and (per Makransky & Lilleholt 2018) higher arousal/engagement/motivation, while non-HMD is associated with fewer of the affective/cognitive distractions that Makransky et al. (2019) and Parong & Mayer (2021) report for HMD conditions (FAITHFUL-vanderMeer, p.11–12).
- The review's own resolution of the HMD-vs-non-HMD tension is deliberately conditional rather than a verdict: which is "better" depends on the goals (skills targeted), the setting (discipline/field), and which of the five affordances matter most for those goals — HMD VR may be preferable for social-skill development via embodiment, non-HMD VR for cognitive-outcome-focused work given HMD's distraction risk (FAITHFUL-vanderMeer, p.13).
- Five affordances of VRCL for education are identified in the conclusion: (1) an efficient tool to engage/motivate learners; (2) support for distance learning and remote collaboration via a heightened sense of "immediacy" (Edirisingha et al. 2009); (3) multi-/interdisciplinary learning and collaboration spaces; (4) development of social skills, facilitated by social presence and socialization (Edirisingha et al. 2009; Ke & Lee 2016); (5) fit with CL-related pedagogical paradigms (constructivism, experiential learning, PBL) (FE, p.11–13).
- Overall the literature shows "general optimism" (70% positive/promising or better outcomes) but a corresponding lack of established, clearly structured pedagogy for applying VR to collaborative learning specifically (FE, p.13).
- Four future-research directions are proposed: systematic HMD-vs-non-HMD comparison; more attention to affective and psychomotor learning domains (currently 7.9% and 5.0% of studies respectively); more true-experimental designs (currently 5.8%); and adoption of physiological measures (currently 0.7%, one study) (FE, p.13).

## 6. Concordance feed

- **social presence / co-presence × review / meta-analytic aggregate** — the construct appears only as a secondary, cited factor (Denoyelles & Kyeong-Ju Seo 2012; Molka-Danielsen & Brask 2014; Edirisingha et al. 2009) in perceived-usefulness and remote-collaboration discussions, never operationalized by the review's own taxonomy (p.9, p.11–12).
- **immersion (technological) × review / meta-analytic aggregate** — coded via "degree of virtuality" across all 139 studies; 78.4% non-HMD, 16.5% AR/MR, 7.2% HMD (p.6–7, Table 2).
- **immersion (technological) × IVR (HMD)** — the HMD-vs-non-HMD affordance/distraction tension is discussed at length, citing Makransky & Lilleholt (2018) for HMD advantage on arousal/motivation and Makransky et al. (2019)/Parong & Mayer (2021) for HMD distraction/poorer-learning findings (p.11–12).
- **embodiment / self-representation × review / meta-analytic aggregate** — coded via "type of virtual embodiment"; 67.6% full-body, 18.0% tools-only, 3.6% partial, 10.8% none/unspecified (p.7, Table 2).
- **engagement / flow × review / meta-analytic aggregate** — coded as "student engagement" learning goal (31.7% of studies) and named as VRCL affordance #1 (p.5, p.11).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer × review / meta-analytic aggregate** — folded into "cognitive domain" learning goals (50.4%); knowledge-transfer discussed narratively via Carron et al. (2013) (p.5, p.10).
- **learning outcomes: procedural/skills × review / meta-analytic aggregate** — the review's own primary RQ1 taxonomy: five skill categories with worked examples (Table 4, p.9).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* Per the manifest's own Phase-1 correction (recorded against ped-sec-33), this unit is Strand F's stronger candidate for a genuine CMC-to-VR bridge role — not because it performs that bridge itself (it does not cite Garrison, Gunawardena, Short/Williams/Christie, or Walther, and its two "social presence" mentions cite VRCL-specific rather than CMC-tradition sources), but because it surveys the VR-collaborative-learning empirical base broadly and, unlike ped-sec-33, directly cites this cluster's own VR-pedagogy interlocutors: de Back, Tinga, Nguyen & Louwerse (2020) — almost certainly the companion paper behind ped-sec-33 — plus Makransky et al. (2019), Parong & Mayer (2021), Radianti et al. (2020), Makransky & Lilleholt (2018), and Dalgarno & Lee (cited here as 2010, this cluster's ped-sec-04) *(anticipatory-application)*. Both facts should be held together, not resolved into a single verdict: this unit does more of the cluster-internal citation work Strand F's original design hoped for, but it still does not itself ground the "social presence / co-presence" concordance row in the way the CMC-tradition sources (ped-sec-25…32) do; that grounding remains split across two vocabularies that this cluster, not the sources themselves, is choosing to bring into contact.

For Part III, this review's most directly usable finding is empirical rather than theoretical: its 139-study aggregate confirms that the field's actual practice skews heavily toward non-HMD, monitor-based collaboration (78.4%) over HMD VR (7.2%) — a distributional fact that bears on Part III's own desktop-3D-vs-video-only strata by showing that Part III's "lab=VR" pole is, in the wider VRCL literature, the *minority* condition rather than the default (*anticipatory-application*). The review's own candid concession that VRCL affordances are field-level generalizations, "moderated" but not determined by tool choice, offers Part III a caution against reading its own VR/2D comparison as revealing context-free properties of the medium rather than properties contingent on collaboration design, task, and goals (p.13) *(anticipatory-application)*. On social presence specifically, `vle-01`'s F7 finding ("Presence & the WE": F1-PRESENCE codes 4.3 vs 1.2 per-100, APP vs VID strata; optative "watch with others" language) is a stronger empirical hit for the general presence/co-viewing theme than for any construct this review develops in depth — this unit supplies field-level context (social presence is a cited-but-undeveloped factor even within the VR-collaboration literature itself) rather than a theoretical apparatus for reading vle-01's finding, and the two should be linked cautiously as parallel observations rather than as source-and-application *(anticipatory-application)*. Finally, this review's own future-research call for physiological measures (0.7% uptake) and true-experimental designs (5.8%) usefully contextualizes, without resolving, the methodological gap the Boredom Experiment dataset (unbuilt anchor) may eventually fill for this cluster *(anticipatory-application)*.

**Not treated:** Lombard/Ditton- or Witmer/Singer-style presence measurement instruments (PQ, IPQ, or any validated presence scale); cognitive-load theory or seductive-details mechanisms; CAMIL or any single dual-process model of immersive learning; cybersickness as a focal construct (mentioned only in passing as an HMD-adoption barrier); and any construct-level treatment of social presence as a defined, measured variable — the review cites it only as a factor named by other studies, never operationalizing or measuring it itself.
