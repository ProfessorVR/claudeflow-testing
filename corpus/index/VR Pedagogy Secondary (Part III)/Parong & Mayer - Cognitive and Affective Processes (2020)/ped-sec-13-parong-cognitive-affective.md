# ped-sec-13 — Parong & Mayer, "Cognitive and Affective Processes for Learning Science in Immersive Virtual Reality" (2021; online-first 2020)

**Type:** empirical media-comparison experiment (educational psychology / cognitive science) · **Discipline:** multimedia learning, cognitive load theory · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand C's primary source for the debate axis 4 "cognitive-load route" pole — the cluster's most instrumented single test of whether immersion's affective payoff (presence, enjoyment, arousal) helps or hurts learning, mediationally decomposed into self-report, EEG, EDA and ECG channels.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Parong and Mayer set out to adjudicate, with a fully instrumented single experiment, a two-sided debate about immersive virtual reality (IVR) in the classroom (FE, p.226–227). Proponents hold that IVR's perceptual richness increases presence and induces positive affect, which in turn increases motivation and improves learning outcomes (FE, p.227, citing Bailenson 2018; Kozhevnikov et al. 2013; Markowitz et al. 2018). Opponents, working from the cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML) and cognitive load theory, hold that the same perceptual richness and induced emotional arousal function as extraneous, seductive stimuli that consume limited working-memory capacity and displace essential and generative processing (FE, p.227, citing Mayer 2014; Sweller, Ayres, & Kalyuga 2011). The authors' own prior study (Parong & Mayer, 2018 — ped-sec-08 in this cluster) had already found an IVR cell-biology lesson underperformed an equivalent slideshow; this 2020 study replicates that finding and, as its central contribution, opens the "black box" between medium and outcome by measuring the cognitive and affective processes that mediate it (FE, p.227–229). A secondary aim tests whether adding a generative learning strategy — oral practice-testing questions during the lesson — can offset any IVR deficit (FE, p.229).

## 2. Structure

Seven numbered/titled sections. §1 Introduction, in four subsections, states the case for IVR (§1.2), the case against IVR (§1.3, grounded in CTML/coherence principle), the case for improving IVR via generative learning (§1.4), and the current study's three research questions, five directional "distraction hypothesis" predictions, and a 2×2 between-subjects design (§1.5, p.226–229). §2 Method covers participants/design (N=61), materials (the IVR and PPT lessons), apparatus (HTC Vive; B-Alert X10 EEG; Biopac EDA/ECG), and procedure (p.229–233). §3 Results is organized by research question: instructional-media effects on learning outcomes (§3.2.1), affective processes (§3.2.2), cognitive processes (§3.2.3), mediation (§3.2.4), practice-testing effects (§3.3), and presence/other self-report measures (§3.4) (p.233–237). §4 Discussion interprets the pattern as support for the distraction hypothesis, draws practical and theoretical implications, and catalogs limitations (p.237–239).

## 3. Constructs

- **presence (spatial/physical)** — measured via a 7-item scale adapted from Witmer & Singer (1998); IVR groups reported significantly higher presence than PPT groups, a large effect (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.235–236).
- **immersion (technological)** — treated definitionally, not as a measured self-report variable: immersion is glossed as an objective property of the technology (head-tracking, stereoscopy, field of view), distinct from the subjective feeling of presence it tends to elicit (FE, p.227–228, citing Nilsson, Nordhal, & Serafin 2016). The IVR-vs-PPT manipulation instantiates a high-vs-low immersion contrast.
- **cognitive load (extraneous vs germane)** — operationalized via CTML's three-way split (extraneous, essential, generative), each measured by a 2–3-item self-report subscale; extraneous load was significantly higher for IVR, while essential and generative load showed no significant media difference (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.234–235).
- **affect: enjoyment & intrinsic motivation** — enjoyment (2-item scale) was significantly higher for IVR (large effect); motivation, interest, effort, understanding, and difficulty ratings showed no significant media difference (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.235–236). Emotional *state* more generally is measured on Russell's (2003) two-dimensional core-affect grid (valence × arousal): IVR elicited more positive/high-arousal and less negative/low-arousal affect than PPT (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.233–234).
- **engagement / flow** — operationalized biometrically via EEG-derived cognitive-engagement probabilities (high engagement, low engagement, distraction, sleep onset) and a workload metric whose lowest band is labeled "boredom" by the instrument's own classification scheme; PPT groups showed a significantly higher probability of high engagement and a significantly higher proportion of "ideal workload" classifications, while IVR groups had a significantly higher proportion of participants classified as bored (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.234–236).
- **learning outcomes: retention & transfer** — a 16-item multiple-choice retention post-test and a 4-item open-ended transfer post-test; PPT groups scored significantly higher on transfer and marginally higher on retention (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.233).
- **cybersickness & physical discomfort** — not directly measured; the discussion instead names an unmeasured "novelty effect" (unfamiliarity with the HMD/controllers) as a candidate confound requiring future isolation (FE, p.239).
- **representational fidelity** — not operationalized as a separate variable; the study explicitly held instructional content, script, and graphics constant across media to isolate presentation format, though the authors flag this as an imperfect control because IVR's continuous animation/narration is inherently confounded with PPT's static-slide/text format (FE, p.238–239, citing Clark & Feldon 2014 on media-comparison critique).

## 4. Conditions & design

Between-subjects 2 (instructional medium: IVR vs. PPT) × 2 (practice testing: oral questions vs. none) design, N=61 (40 female, 20 male, 1 other; ages 18–38), power-analyzed from Parong & Mayer (2018) effect sizes (d=1.12, power=.80) to ~10/cell; actual cells ranged 15–16 (FE, p.229, 233). The IVR condition used an HTC Vive running *The Body VR: Journey into a Cell*, a ~12-minute narrated, interactive tour of the bloodstream and a cell; the PPT condition used a 24-slide, self-paced desktop slideshow built from IVR screenshots and the same narration transcribed as printed text (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.230–231). The practice-testing manipulation inserted six oral recall/transfer questions at matched points in both media, with no feedback given (FE, p.230–231). Instruments: self-report presence (Witmer & Singer 1998, adapted), affect (Russell 2003 core-affect grid, four quadrants), cognitive load (CTML three-way split); biometric EEG cognitive-workload/engagement classification (Advanced Brain Monitoring B-Alert X10); EDA (skin-conductance level, responses/sec, peak-to-peak amplitude); ECG-derived heart rate and heart-rate variability; a 16-item retention and 4-item transfer post-test (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.230–233). Effect directions: medium → transfer (PPT>IVR, d=−0.71, sig.); medium → retention (PPT>IVR, d=−0.52, marginal, p=.058); medium → presence (IVR>PPT, d=1.74, sig.); medium → enjoyment (IVR>PPT, d=1.20, sig.); medium → extraneous load (IVR>PPT, d=0.80, sig.); medium → EEG high-engagement probability (PPT>IVR, d=−0.99, sig.); medium → EDA/HR/HRV (no significant difference on any physiological-arousal channel); practice testing → retention/transfer (no significant main effect on either) (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.233–237).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Prediction 1 (PPT beats IVR on outcomes) was partially supported: significant for transfer, marginal for retention (FE, p.233).
- Prediction 2a/2b (IVR elicits higher self-reported and physiological emotional arousal) was partially supported: strongly supported on self-report (positive/high-arousal and negative/low-arousal states) but not on any physiological arousal channel (EDA, HR, HRV showed no significant media effects) (FE, p.233–234).
- Prediction 3a/3b (IVR causes more self-reported and EEG-measured distraction) was partially supported: strongly supported for self-reported extraneous load and for EEG engagement/workload classification, but IVR and PPT did not differ on EEG "distraction"-state probability specifically (FE, p.234–236).
- Prediction 4 (cognitive/affective processes mediate the medium→outcome relationship) was supported for retention only: parallel mediation (PROCESS macro, 5,000 bootstrap samples) found both self-reported extraneous load and self-reported positive/high-arousal emotion significantly mediated the medium→retention path (IVR → higher load/arousal → lower retention); for transfer, none of the four candidate mediators (extraneous load, high engagement, positive/high-arousal emotion, HRV) reached significance, and the direct effect of medium on transfer remained significant after including mediators (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.236–237).
- Prediction 5 (practice testing improves outcomes) was not supported; no significant main effect of practice testing on retention or transfer, though a significant medium × practice-testing interaction on positive/low-arousal emotion showed practice testing raised that affect state in IVR but lowered it in PPT (FE, p.236).
- The authors interpret the overall pattern as support for a "distraction hypothesis" consonant with CTML's coherence principle: IVR's perceptual richness and induced high-arousal affect function as seductive details that consume limited cognitive capacity even as they elevate presence and enjoyment — the very features that make IVR appealing are implicated in the mechanism that undercuts learning (FE, p.237–238).
- Practically, the authors caution against replacing conventional declarative-knowledge instruction with IVR of this type, while suggesting IVR may be better suited to spatial/3D-transformation content or as pre-training rather than primary instruction, and flag novelty effects, single-domain scope, and the media-comparison method-vs-medium confound as limitations warranting caution before generalizing (FE, p.238–239).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit is Part III's best-instrumented citable source for the claim that a desktop/2D comparator can outperform an HMD condition on declarative-knowledge outcomes even while trailing badly on presence and enjoyment — directly relevant whenever Part III must explain why its own lab=VR/deployment=2D-PC poles might diverge on affect versus achievement measures rather than moving together *(anticipatory-application)*. The EEG workload metric's own three-way classification — boredom (<.40), ideal (.40–.70), overload (>.70) — and its finding that IVR participants were disproportionately classified into the "boredom" band during a highly immersive lesson is a useful complication for `vle-04-mark-attention-span`'s Focus/Rote/Bored/Frustrated engagement-by-challenge quadrant: it shows a *technologically* high-immersion condition can still register as low-engagement by an independent physiological instrument, so "immersive" and "engaging" must not be treated as synonyms when Part III reads its own attention-span literature against its VR/2D contrast *(anticipatory-application)*. `vle-02-boredom-raw-dataset`'s per-video self-report boredom/engagement record is a plausible future companion dataset for this unit's EEG-workload/self-report-load pairing, but any direct cross-walk of instruments (Parong & Mayer's B-Alert workload band vs. the raw dataset's 1–9 self-report boredom item) is `****** UNVERIFIED: instrument equivalence not established; treat as parallel, not commensurable, until crosswalked`. Most consequentially for the cluster's debate map, this unit is the primary evidentiary anchor for the **cognitive-load-route pole** of debate axis 4 (affective route ↔ cognitive-load route): where `ped-sec-09` (Makransky & Lilleholt 2018) reports a structural-equation model in which immersion's affective path (immersion→presence→enjoyment/motivation→learning) is a *positive* contributor to perceived learning, this unit's mediation model treats the structurally similar variable — self-reported positive/high-arousal emotion tied to presence — as a *significant negative mediator* of retention, i.e., a distractor rather than a facilitator (FAITHFUL-Parong&Mayer, p.236–237, cf. ped-sec-09 abstract). The two studies are not strictly incommensurable (different lessons, media pairs, and outcome measures), but the contrast is sharp enough that Part III should present it as a live, unresolved methodological/theoretical disagreement about whether presence-linked positive arousal helps or hurts learning, rather than folding one study's reading into the other's *(anticipatory-application)*.

Not treated: cybersickness/physical discomfort as a measured construct (see `ped-sec-23-cossio-cybersickness` for that gap filled), social presence/co-presence (single-user apparatus, no social-VR element), self-efficacy, and any SEM/process-model treatment of the constructs (this unit runs mediation analysis on discrete self-report/biometric variables rather than a latent-variable structural model, unlike `ped-sec-11-makransky-petersen-desktop-sem`).
