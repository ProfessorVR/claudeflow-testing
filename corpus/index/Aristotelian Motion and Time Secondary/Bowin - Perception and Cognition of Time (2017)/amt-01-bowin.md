# Bowin, "Aristotle on the Perception and Cognition of Time" (2017) — Analytical Unit AMT-01-BOWIN

## Overview and Bowin's Circularity Thesis

John Bowin's contribution to Sisko's *Philosophy of Mind in Antiquity* (Routledge 2017, ch. 9, pp. 175-193) takes its bearings from a deceptively simple aggregation of Aristotelian claims. Aristotle says time can be perceived: in *Physics* 4.11 he says we perceive motion and time together (219a3-4) and that we perceive instants, or "nows" (219a30-b1). Aristotle also says time can be grasped intellectually: in *De Memoria* 1 the perception of time is involved in remembering (449b29, 450a19, 451a17), and at *De Memoria* 2.452b7 we apprehend time by a measure. Bowin's organizing question is how these two modes — *perceiving* time and *intellectually grasping* time — interact. The chapter's two-pronged thesis is announced at the outset: (i) for Aristotle, perceiving time does not presuppose grasping it intellectually; (ii) grasping time intellectually nonetheless has an effect on how rational beings perceive it. The chapter develops thesis (i) negatively in §§1-3 against ancient and modern over-intellectualizers, and thesis (ii) positively in §§4-5 via Pseudo-Philoponus's distinction between determinate and indeterminate time.

The chapter's deepest contribution is the resolution of an apparent circularity between memory and the perception of time. Aristotle says memory is possible only in beings who perceive time (*Mem*. 1.449b28-29). But to perceive motion is to perceive a plurality of motion stages, and since only one instantaneous motion stage can be present at a time, perceiving motion (hence time) seems to require remembering earlier motion stages. Thus memory presupposes the perception of time, and the perception of time (via motion) presupposes memory. Bowin's resolution is structural and elegant: both memory and the perception of time are mediated by *phantasmata*, and the activities of "perceiving time" and "remembering an event" are the same activity under different descriptions. The circle is interdefinitional, not vicious — and the non-viciousness is delivered by the *phantasma* of an event happened before functioning as itself a *phantasma* of a past event AS past, with no further regress required. This is the exact doctrinal site at which Bowin's chapter, despite being formally a time-perception study, threads into the Aristotelian Phantasia secondary literature. The cross-pipeline bridge is set out in a dedicated paragraph below.

## Section-by-Section Reconstruction

### Introduction (pp. 175-176)

Bowin aggregates the foundational Aristotelian claims and positions his two-pronged thesis. He notes immediately that the alternative reading — perception of time as already requiring intellect — would deprive non-rational animals of memory, contradicting *Metaph*. Α.1.980a28 ff. where Aristotle credits animals with memory and sagacity (*phronimos*). The introduction also flags the two interpretive obstacles that the chapter must defuse: the apparent counting-requirement in the definition of time at *Physics* 4.11.219b1-2, and the apparent mind-dependence of time at *Physics* 4.14.

### §1 Perceiving Time (pp. 176-181)

The longest and most exegetically intricate section. Bowin works through three commentators — Albert the Great (Alberti Magni 1890, p. 100), Themistius (1899, 120.12-17), and (later, in §4) Pseudo-Philoponus — whose readings over-mathematize *Physics* 4.11. Albert distinguishes time *in se* (perceived only by rational beings) from time perceived relationally (available to animals); Themistius requires *nous* for perceiving the before-and-after in motion. Bowin's reply has three moves. First, on the status of mathematical objects: although Aristotle elsewhere talks of geometers bringing geometric objects to actuality through proof-construction (*Metaph*. Θ.1.1051a21-33; cf. M.3.1078a28-31), the time case is special because instantaneous motion stages are perfectly and actually indivisible *qua* instantaneous, and no abstraction is required. Second, on *Physics* 4.11.219a22-29 (the "soul says the nows are two" passage): "number" here refers to a perceptible plurality of instantaneous motion stages bounded by a now-before and a now-after, with temporal duration lying between. The common sense, in animals and humans, perceives common sensibles including number directly (*DA* 3.1.425a16; cf. *DA* 2.6.418a7-11) — just as it perceives "two" or "three" of any sensible item without counting them. Aristotle credits this even in birds (the eagle laying three eggs and hatching two, *HA* 6.6.563a17-19). Third, on the verb *arithmein* (to count): it appears only after Aristotle's definition of time at 219b1-2, and a survey of its eight post-definitional occurrences in Book 4 shows that three are concerned only with counting, three repeat the count-vs-countable distinction, and two are idiosyncratic extended-sense uses (footnote 19). Counting plays a much smaller role than commentators have supposed (fn. 18 contests Coope 2005 and Sorabji 1983). The Coope-style move — that nows are brought into existence by the act of counting — is rebutted on three grounds: (a) the now divides *potentially* at *Phys*. 4.13.222a14 (contradicting the "nows are not already there to be counted" assumption); (b) *Phys*. 8.8.263a27-8, 263b3-6 shows time has infinitely many potential parts; (c) even if marking-off is necessary, it can be done by perceptual discrimination (*Post*. *An*. 2.19.99b35; *Top*. 2.4.111a14-20; *DA* 2.11.424a6; *DA* 3.2.426b8-21). Bowin closes §1 by noting that Aristotle's perception of individual *phantasmata* in the *De Memoria* (signet-ring metaphor at *DA* 2.12.424a19 + *Mem*. 1.450a27-32; *Mem*. 2.452b23 ff.) shows that animal perception delivers fully individuated particulars; the worry that perceiving an individual *qua* individual requires a sortal concept is contained by Cashdollar 1973 on incidental perception and Caston 1996 p. 42 (fn. 15, 16).

### §2 Measuring Time (pp. 181-182)

The pivot. Bowin defines *perceiving* time as either perceiving a number of nows (two or more), or perceiving how much time has passed in purely relative terms by perceiving the order as well as the number of nows. *Measuring* time, by contrast, requires counting and so intellect. The textual anchor is *Physics* 4.14.223b13-24, where the verb *arithmein* is used in the extended sense of measuring: time is measured by some determinate (*hōrismenon*) time, and uniform circular motion is "most of all a measure." The rationale, on Bowin's reading, is that counting is the most exact form of measurement because its unit is absolutely indivisible in relation to perception (*Metaph*. I.1.1053a1, 23). Continua such as durations have only "a number of a sort" (*tis arithmos*, *Phys*. 4.12.220a27-8) — they are divided into parts. Measuring time, accordingly, affords an intellectual grasp (*gnōrizein*) of time: *Mem*. 2.452b8-9 contrasts apprehending the time "either by a measure (*metrō*) or indeterminately (*aoristōs*)," and the broader contrast (*gnōrizein* vs *aisthanesthai*, *dianoeisthai* vs *aisthanesthai*, *cf*. *GA* 1.23.731a33-4) makes apprehension by measure an achievement of intellect, while apprehending time indeterminately is an achievement of perception. The genus-of-measure constraint (the measure must be of the same genus as what it measures) requires sortal concepts (*day*, *hour*) that animals lack.

### §3 Memory, Imagination, the Perception of Time, and the Perception of Motion (pp. 182-185)

The chapter's philosophical centerpiece. Bowin begins with a methodological distinction between *perceiving time as it is passing* and *perceiving that time has passed* (perceiving its passage). He argues that Aristotle in *Physics* 4.11 speaks throughout of perceiving time *having passed* (perfect tense *gegonenai chronos*), never of perceiving time *passing*. The puzzle of the ceasing instant (*Phys*. 4.10.218a8-21) and the always-other-and-other now (*Phys*. 4.11.219b9-10) are metaphysical references to temporal passage but not phenomenological references to perceiving time passing. Sorabji 2004 p. 70 thinks Aristotle must be using "perceive" in a broader sense at *Mem*. 1.450a19-22 (the *prosaisthanetai* passage); Bowin disagrees (fn. 25). His reading: Aristotle calls remembering-that-time-has-elapsed "perceiving time" because the two are the same activity under different descriptions.

The structural argument proceeds. The perception of motion appears to presuppose memory: only one instantaneous motion stage can be present at a time, so to perceive a plurality of motion stages one must remember earlier ones. The perception of time similarly: to perceive a number of motion stages *as* before and after, one must remember the earlier stages. But Aristotle says memory itself requires the perception of time (*Mem*. 1.449b28-29). So memory and the perception of time appear to presuppose each other.

The resolution is the signature contribution of the chapter. *Mem*. 1.450a19-22 reads (in Bowin's slightly modified translation): "When someone is actively engaged in memory, he perceives in addition (*prosaisthanetai*) that he saw this, or heard it, or learned it before; and before and after are in time." Chapter 2 of the *De Memoria* (452b30-453a4) tells us what this perception-in-addition consists in. Both memory and the perception of time involve *phantasmata* — two *phantasmata*, in fact, one representing the event remembered and one representing the time elapsed since the event. To remember, one must have a *phantasma* corresponding to a movement of the event AND a *phantasma* corresponding to a movement of the time, and they must be matched. If either is missing, one fails to remember. So memory presupposes the perception of time, and both presuppose having *phantasmata*; the perception of time depends on *phantasia*. But the perception of time does NOT presuppose memory: the *phantasma* of a past phase of motion *as past* is itself constitutive of perceiving time, with no further memory required.

A regress worry hovers: does perceiving the *phantasma* of the time itself require a further series of *phantasmata*? Bowin grants that the *phantasma* corresponding to the time is itself an *appearance* of a series of events, not an unconscious internal clock process. But, he replies, perceiving time and remembering an event are the same activity under different descriptions; one need not posit a further series. To perceive a *phantasma* of an event that happened before AS before must inevitably be a *phantasma* — and *Mem*. 1.450b17-18 makes clear that Aristotle thinks we can perceive *phantasmata*. The circularity between memory and time-perception is interdefinitional, not vicious. The structural parallel for motion is set out: a retained plurality of *phantasmata* representing motion stages may not be perceived as before, in which case the perception of a number of motion stages does not amount to perception of time — past motion phases must be perceived *as* past.

### §4 The Effect of Intellect on the Perception of Time (pp. 185-188)

The positive development of thesis (ii). Pseudo-Philoponus (Philoponus 1897, 580.27-37, in Charlton 2000 p. 31) is the anchoring commentator: "By 'time' Aristotle means determinate (*hōrismenon*) time, not indeterminate (*aoriston*) time. Non-rational beings, even if they have conscious perception (*synaisthēsis*) of time, do not have conscious perception of determinate time, but only of winter or summer. Human beings have conscious perception of determinate time because they count days and hours. Counting is proper only to the rational soul." The distinction is between *types* of perception (*aisthēsis*), not apprehension in general (*gnōsis*), and so is different from the perception/apprehension distinction in *Mem*. 2.

Bowin's positive reading routes this through *De Anima* 3.11.434a6-15: "Perceptual *phantasia*, as we have said, is found in the other animals also, but deliberative *phantasia* [is found] in those which are capable of calculating (for the decision whether to do this or that is already a task for calculating); and one must measure by a single standard; for one pursues what is greater; hence one has the ability to make one image greater out of many *phantasmata*." The "measuring by a single standard" is the *metrētikē technē* of Plato *Protagoras* 356d-e (Bowin reads it more generally than the value-of-actions case); the synthesis-of-images by deliberative *phantasia* is the mechanism that produces the unified *phantasma* of, say, a day — usable thereafter as a measure of time. The general background for synthesis-of-*phantasmata* is *Post*. *An*. 2.19.100a3-9 (memory–experience–universal) and *Metaph*. Α.1.980b26-981a12 ("many memories of the same thing produce finally the capacity for a single experience"). Bowin contests Polansky 2007 p. 530 and Moss 2012 p. 146 (fn. 37), who hold that deliberative *phantasia* is distinguished from perceptual *phantasia* only by what it is used for: Bowin insists on a phenomenal-content difference — the active manipulation of *phantasmata* by intellect yields a different kind of image, the normalized image of a day shorn of its rough seasonal edges (variations in length of daylight). The image, although guided by a sortal concept, can remain non-conceptual in its phenomenal content (fn. 39, citing Tye 1995 pp. 140-141 on faces-vase figures and ambiguous decompositions).

Bowin then triangulates: deliberative *phantasia* is like a special form of *empeiria*-formation but with active intellectual manipulation. *Posterior Analytics* 2.19.100a6-7 is read epexegetically (Sorabji 1993 p. 34, fn. 40; McKirahan 1992 p. 243 surveys the corrective reading): experience involves universals only on one reading, and *NE* 7.3.1147b5 makes animal "experience" non-universal (animals "have no universal beliefs but only imagination [*phantasia*] and memory of particulars"). So a non-conceptual *empeiria* and a non-conceptual normalized day-image are conjointly available.

### §5 *Physics* 4.14: If There Were No *Nous* Would There Be Time? (pp. 188-190)

The chapter's defensive section. *Physics* 4.14.223a21-29 reads (Hussey-Bowin): "One might find it a difficult question, whether if there were no soul there would be time or not. For if it is impossible that there should be something to do the counting, it is also impossible that anything should be countable… But if there is nothing that has it in its nature to count except soul, and of soul [the part which is] intellect, then it is impossible that there should be time if there is no soul, except that there could be what underlies time; as for example if it is possible for there to be motion without soul. The before and after are in motion, and time is these *qua* countable." Naively this seems to make the existence of time depend on counting and so on *nous*, threatening Bowin's main thesis. Many commentators treat it as a logical error (Mignucci 1987 pp. 184-186, fn. 43) or a modality confusion (Sorabji 1983 p. 89 ff., fn. 44).

Bowin's positive reading is structural. Since perceiving something presupposes its existence, if perceiving time required intellectual counting then no being without *nous* could perceive time. But Aristotle is explicit in the *De Memoria* that animals can perceive time. The Phys. 4.14 passage must therefore have a more modest claim: in the absence of beings with *nous*, time would not be MEASURABLE (or countable in the extended sense of being countable by means of some determinate time as discussed at *Phys*. 4.14.223b13). The metaphysical grammar is delivered by *Metaph*. Δ.15 (measurables, knowables, and visibles are relatives, called such not because they are of something else but because something else, the measure, IS of them) plus *Categories* 7.6b28-36 ("the measurable is measurable BY the measure," instrumental dative). The measure is only incidentally related to the measurable's measurability. For discrete pluralities, a measure is always automatically available (every horse in a plurality is itself a measure). For continua such as time and motion, no part is intrinsically indivisible; continua are indivisible only "in relation to perception" (*Metaph*. I.1.1053a1, 23) — they can be measured only by something that *appears* indivisible, that is, by an inner movement or *phantasma*, namely the normalized *phantasma* of a day synthesized by deliberative *phantasia*. Without *nous* and so without deliberative *phantasia*, no measure stands in measuring relation to time, and time is not measurable. But motion's before-and-after still obtains.

## *Physics* 4.11 as the Anchor on Time-Perception

The systematic role of *Physics* 4.11 in Bowin's argument deserves a moment of focus. *Physics* 4.10-14 contains Aristotle's most sustained account of time. Bowin treats 4.11 as the *perceptual* anchor (we perceive motion and time together; we perceive nows; the soul "says" the nows are two) and 4.14 as the *measurement* anchor (time is measured by determinate time; counting in the extended sense; the no-soul-no-time passage). The hinge is the definition at 219b1-2 ("a number of motion in respect of the before and after") and its repetition at 219b5-9 ("number is so-called in two ways: countable and that by which we count; time is that which is counted and not that by which we count"). On Bowin's reading, the definition uses "number" in the sense of countable plurality, not in the sense of an act of counting. Perception delivers the countable plurality directly via the common sense; counting is reserved for the rational soul in *Phys*. 4.14's extended-sense measurement. The 4.11 / 4.14 hinge therefore inherits the perception/measurement split.

## *De Memoria* 1, 450a19-22 as the Resolution Locus

Bowin's resolution of the memory–time-perception circularity is concentrated in this single Aristotelian sentence, with the structural detail filled in by *Mem*. 2.452b30-453a4. Three substantive claims are loaded onto the passage. First, *prosaisthanetai* (perceives in addition) is treated literally: the perception-in-addition is part of the act of remembering, not a logically prior intellectual dating. Second, "before and after are in time" is read as supplying the structural before/after of motion *via* the *phantasma* of the past phase *as past* — the *phantasma* carries temporal modality intrinsically. Third, the *Mem*. 2 passage (the two-phantasmata account) tells us how the structural before/after gets cashed: two *phantasmata*, one of event, one of elapsed time, matched. Both memory and time-perception need *phantasmata*; only memory additionally requires the matching. So the perception of time depends on *phantasia*, but does not depend on memory.

This is the precise point at which Bowin's chapter, despite its primary topic being time-perception, doubles as a contribution to the *phantasia* literature.

## Cross-Pipeline Bridge to Aristotelian Phantasia Secondary

The bridge to the *Aristotelian Phantasia Secondary (1985-2017)* pipeline is explicit, structural, and necessary. Three connections must be preserved.

First, Bowin's resolution of the time-memory circularity at *Mem*. 1.450a19-22 + *Mem*. 2.452b31-453a4 deploys the *phantasma*-as-image-of-past doctrine that Nussbaum 1985 ("The Role of *Phantasia* in Aristotle's Explanation of Action," in her *De Motu Animalium* commentary) and Frede 1992 ("The Cognitive Role of *Phantasia* in Aristotle," in Nussbaum and Rorty's *Essays on Aristotle's De Anima*) make load-bearing. Frede's reading of *phantasia* as the faculty that presents content under a modal description (as past, as future, as merely possible) is precisely what Bowin's reading of the *prosaisthanetai* passage requires — *phantasia* must be the faculty that presents the past motion-phase AS past. Caston 1996 ("Why Aristotle Needs Imagination," *Phronesis* 41.1) supplies the same doctrine from the *DA* 3.3 side: *phantasia* is required because basic sensation and conception cannot supply the modal/intentional dimension of mental content that admits of error. Bowin's chapter is silent on the error-thesis of *DA* 3.3, but his appeal to *phantasmata* as the mediators of time-perception is structurally the same move: *phantasia* delivers a content under a description that goes beyond what basic *aisthēsis* alone can deliver.

Second, the deliberative-*phantasia* doctrine at *DA* 3.11.434a6-15, central to Bowin's §4, is the same doctrine load-bearing in Moss 2012 (*Aristotle on the Apparent Good*) and Polansky 2007 (*Aristotle's* De Anima). Bowin's reading is in friendly contestation with both: he agrees with the deliberative/perceptual distinction but contests their use-individuation thesis with a content-individuation thesis. The bridge here is `draws-on` and `contests` simultaneously — the same primary Aristotelian text, alternative readings.

Third — and this is the bridge to Dalton's dissertation phantasia chapters — Bowin supplies the T0 motion/time pre-condition for any phantasia-chapter argument that wants to make *phantasia* the faculty individuating moments-as-past. The A3-phantasma chain (the "one-in-substrate-two-in-being" resonant-*kinēsis* thesis, currently paused at the GA 18 §15-§18 threshold and the Plato *Sophist* 248e-249d question) needs Bowin's circularity-resolution as its non-vicious-circle structure: *phantasma* and time-perception are co-grounded, neither prior, and the *phantasma* of a past motion-stage *as past* is structurally identical to the *phantasma* that constitutes the resonant *kinēsis* of A3. Bowin's chapter does not engage Heidegger's BCAP (GA 18) account of *chronos* and *kairos* in Aristotelian rhetoric — but that is exactly where the dissertation engagement must add value, by showing that Heidegger's *hexis*-disposition reading of *phronesis* (which folds time-perception into the comportment of *Dasein*) cuts against Bowin's more analytic Aristotelian time-as-countable, while still sharing the structural commitment to *phantasia* as the mediating faculty.

The bridge is concrete, not gestural. Any phantasia-cluster traversal that hits the *De Memoria* nodes of Nussbaum, Frede, Caston, or King 2009 should also surface Bowin via the `bridges-to-pipeline` edge in `amt-01-bowin-edges.csv`.

## Closing Note on Method

Bowin's chapter is methodologically distinctive in two respects. First, it engages ancient commentators (Albert, Themistius, Pseudo-Philoponus) not as obstacles to be cleared but as productive resources — Pseudo-Philoponus in particular is treated as supplying the right distinction (determinate/indeterminate time) with the wrong mechanism, which Bowin then re-mechanizes via deliberative *phantasia*. Second, it deploys a quantitative method on Aristotle's vocabulary (the inventory of *arithmein* occurrences in fn. 19) that is rare in the secondary literature and that delivers a load-bearing exegetical result: counting plays a much smaller role in *Phys*. 4.10-14 than commentators have supposed. Both methodological features make the chapter unusually well-positioned to bear cross-pipeline edges — its conclusions are exportable to neighboring debates (phantasia, common sense, animal cognition, mathematical-object metaphysics) without rewriting.
