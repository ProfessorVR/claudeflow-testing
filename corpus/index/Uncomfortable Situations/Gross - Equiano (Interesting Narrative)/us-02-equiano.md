# US-02-EQUIANO — "Bearing Up in *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano, or Gustavus Vassa, the African*"

**Author**: Daniel M. Gross
**Volume**: *Uncomfortable Situations* (Chicago 2017), Chapter 2, book pp. 52–76 / PDF 62–86
**Phase 2 deep-analysis narrative** — 2026-05-10

---

## 1. The chapter argument

Daniel Gross's Equiano chapter executes the volume's "uncomfortable situation" thesis on the hardest possible test case: a slave's first-person testimony of being "happily situated." The chapter's organizing strategy is to take Equiano's 1789 declaration — "I became inured to that service, and began to consider myself as happily situated; for my master treated me always extremely well; and my attachment and gratitude to him were very great" (PDF 62, epigraph from *Interesting Narrative* p. 77) — and submit it, in turn, to three analytical frames each of which would, at first inspection, vindicate the testimony's apparent sincerity: hedonic psychology's "adaptation" thesis, situated-cognition theory's "growing into one's environmental situation," and pragmatic phenomenology's habituated-comfort model. In each case Gross demonstrates that the frame absorbs Equiano's prose by suppressing the feature of Equiano's environment — namely, its constitutively race-based hostility — that the prose is in fact tracking. The chapter's positive contribution is the figure of "bearing up": a rhetorical-emotional disposition that carries (OED *bear* sense I), sustains (sense II), is patient (sense 17), and ultimately invokes "the prop / That bears the world and all things up" (Toplady, *Psalms and Hymns* 1776, No. 312), where the prop is Christ and the bearing is not the bearer's own. The chapter thus rewrites the spiritual-autobiography genre as a rhetorical-emotional analytic capable of registering exactly what pragmatic phenomenology cannot.

Two epigraphs anchor the chapter. The first is the Equiano "almost an Englishman" passage from *Narrative* 77, presented at PDF 62. The second is Merleau-Ponty's figure of the "intentional arc" from *Phenomenology of Perception* p. 136 (Colin Smith translation, Routledge 1989 [1945]), the arc that "projects roundabout as our past, our future, our human setting" and that "goes limp in illness." The pairing is programmatic. Equiano's three-or-four-year period in England is the would-be canonical case of the arc successfully reorganizing past, future, human setting, sense, and motility around a new normal. But Merleau-Ponty's arc has illness, breakdown, and going-limp written into it from the start; and as the chapter unfolds, the slave's "happy situation" is shown to be precisely the social-bodily going-limp that pragmatic readers of Merleau-Ponty miss.

The chapter has three internal sub-section titles: "Time Doesn't Add Up" (PDF 63–74), "Attachments / Feeling" (PDF 74–79), and "Bearing Up" (PDF 79–86). These are the only internal headings; the rest is continuous prose.

## 2. The "bearing up" construct

The chapter's titular construct is built on an OED-archaeological reading of the verb *bear*. Gross interrupts his exegesis at PDF 81 with an explicit OED "excursus" cataloguing the relevant senses: main verb senses I (carry), II (sustain), III (thrust, press), IV (bring forth), plus 2b (bear in mind), 2c (bear witness), 6a (bear arms against), 7b/7c (bear a name or office), 9 (entertain a feeling), 17 (bear with, be patient), 21 (bear up — keep up one's courage, with Burke's 1796 "Bearing up against those vicissitudes of fortune" as locus classicus), and the nautical senses at 37 (bear away, bear down, bear off, bear up). The noun *bearing* yields deportment (Shakespeare *Much Ado* 1600), supporting (Pope *Essay on Man* 1733 "Of this vast Frame the Bearings, and the Ties"), influence-tendency (Burke *Speech on Nabob Arcot's Debts* 1785), positional reckoning, and child-bearing.

This OED is not antiquarian decoration. It is the apparatus by which Gross reads Equiano's alliteration of *born* and *bore* as a deep morphological cluster rather than a stylistic flourish. When Equiano writes "I reproached my fate, and wished I had never been born. I was ready to curse the tide that bore us, the gale that wafted my prison, and even the ship that conducted us; and I called on death to relieve me from the horrors I felt and dreaded" (*Narrative* 97), the alliteration figures the entire problematic of the chapter: birth (sense IV) into a world that bears down on you, in which you cannot bear (carry, sustain) your own situation, and yet — through the retrospective qualification "in the first expressions of my grief" — you nonetheless did bear it, you did sustain, you were patient, you are even now bearing this story as you tell it.

Bearing up is therefore the rhetorical-emotional structure of carrying-what-one-was-ready-to-curse, narrated retrospectively from the position of having carried. It is irreducible to stoic endurance because it does not posit an internal resource of fortitude; it is irreducible to pragmatic-phenomenological adjustment because it does not posit an environment that has become friendly. The carrying is, at the limit, not the bearer's own: the Toplady hymn that arrives at the Cadiz-voyage shipwreck scene (PDF 84) — "How can I sink with such a prop, / That bears the world and all things up?" — names Christ as the bearer that the bearer cannot be for himself. This is the conversion-narrative's analytic, not its devotional, content. As Gross puts it at PDF 86: "As a slave, Equiano bears down on a world that is not his own; then Jesus bears everything." The slave bears down (OED sense III, *thrust, press* and nautical *bear down*); Christ bears up (sense 21).

The chapter's most diagnostic formulation of bearing up arrives a page earlier, after the long Mount Calvary set-piece: "Equiano's recovery and the unity of his senses cannot be borne by himself, since that option is available only to someone who is already in comfortable conversation with the world as such" (PDF 85–86). Bearing up names what carriage looks like for someone whose conversation with the world is "awkward at best and foreclosed in fact."

## 3. The Merleau-Ponty "Freedom" chapter reading

The chapter pivots on a single, carefully sited citation from *Phenomenology of Perception* p. 84 (Smith trans.), inside the "Freedom" chapter that closes the *Phenomenology*: "It may even happen when I am in danger that my human situation abolishes my biological one, that my body lends itself without reserve to action." Gross treats this sentence as Merleau-Ponty's clearest articulation of a Hegelian-Marxist line of thought running through the *Phenomenology* — a line he distinguishes from the pragmatic-phenomenological reading exemplified by Hubert Dreyfus.

For Gross, the sentence does two things. First, it locates the *Phenomenology*'s analytic of embodiment within a frame where the "human situation" — meaning, in Gross's gloss, the situation of mutually-acknowledging consciousnesses, the Hegelian *Anerkennung* situation — has priority over biology. Second, it identifies *danger* as the operative threshold at which that priority becomes visible. The slave's situation, on Gross's reading, is the continuous instantiation of danger; therefore the slave's "human situation" perpetually abolishes the biological one, and the body lends itself without reserve to the action that the situation demands — service, deference, bearing up.

Gross supplements this single citation with a second piece of Merleau-Pontian text: the three-page footnote at PDF 77 / *Phenomenology* 172n1, which compares revolutionary general strike to sickness. Both are situations in which factors normally taken for granted — production relations, vital rhythm — "come clearly to light, and are specifically seen as decisive." Gross reads this footnote as Merleau-Ponty's own demonstration that the social body can go limp the way the individual body can, and that the figure of going-limp from the intentional-arc passage at *Phenomenology* 136 (the chapter's second epigraph) generalizes to social pathology. The slave's situation, then, is the social body going limp. The "happy situation" is what going-limp can look like from inside.

A third Merleau-Pontian moment is Proust on Swann's love-jealousy for Odette (*Phenomenology* 425): "Proust shows how Swann's love for Odette causes the jealousy which, in turn, modifies his love, since Swann, always anxious to win her from any possible rival, has no time to really look at Odette." Gross uses this as Merleau-Ponty's own demonstration that "consciousness takes root in being and time by taking up a situation" — and that the situation is best analyzed through the long form of literary fiction. This licenses Gross's central methodological commitment: literature is not Merleau-Ponty's ornament but his analytic instrument.

The reading also incorporates Sara Ahmed's recovery of the queer-disorientation moment in *Phenomenology* (138–139), cited in Ahmed's *Queer Phenomenology* and in Gross's note 12. Merleau-Ponty's "intellectual experience of disorder, but the vital experience of giddiness and nausea, which is the awareness of our contingency, and the horror with which it fills us" registers, on Ahmed's reading, the moment of disorientation that pragmatic phenomenology routinely overcomes by re-orientation. Gross is "not trying to find joy and excitement in Equiano's horror" (n.12) but rather to demonstrate that the rhetorical-humanities reading must be brought to bear precisely on cases like Equiano's, where the disorientation has no socially-supplied re-orientation available.

## 4. The contest with Dreyfus

The chapter's most explicit polemic — though, characteristically for Gross, polemic delivered in note-paratext rather than running text — is the contest with Hubert Dreyfus's pragmatic-phenomenological reading of Merleau-Ponty. Note 28 names the target: Dreyfus, "Intelligence without Representation — Merleau-Ponty's Critique of Mental Representation," *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 1.4 (2002). Note 35 elaborates: Dreyfus reads Merleau-Ponty through the Heideggerian background-of-intentionality literature — practical, everyday orientation that makes action possible and Dasein possible — and in so doing absorbs Merleau-Ponty into a register where the unequal, contestatory, Hegelian-recognitional dimension of social life cannot be properly thematized.

Gross's positive claim is that Merleau-Ponty, "unlike Noe and his more pragmatic colleagues, retains some critical perspective from which normal involvements might appear 'queer'" (PDF 77). The pragmatic reader sees the everyday as the field of skilled coping; the Hegelian-Merleau-Pontian reader sees the everyday as a field that can suddenly go limp — under retinal inversion, under sickness, under general strike, under the recognition-foreclosure of master-slave. Pragmatic phenomenology, on Gross's reading, has no theoretical place for the second register; it can register skilled coping (Equiano "becomes inured") but not the rhetorical-irony of the coping (Equiano "considers himself" happily situated, writes retrospectively that he was, "almost" an Englishman with the qualification fully load-bearing).

This contest is conducted not as a refutation but as an extension. Gross does not deny that Dreyfus has correctly identified one strand in Merleau-Ponty. He denies that this strand exhausts Merleau-Ponty. The "Freedom" chapter and the 172n1 footnote are his textual evidence that the Hegelian-Marxist strand was always there, and that pragmatic readings have simply lost it.

The structural significance of this contest for the volume is considerable. Gross's allies in *Uncomfortable Situations* — Barsalou-Barrett's situated conceptualization, Berlant's cruel optimism, Gibson's affordances — all run the risk of being absorbed into Dreyfusian pragmatism. The Equiano chapter is therefore the volume's pre-emptive defense: situated cognition need not be pragmatic; situatedness has a Hegelian strand; affordance theory has a hostile-environment register (forecast here for Ch. 3 on Radcliffe). The chapter is structurally a hinge: it secures the volume's methodological apparatus against the friendly-environment default of its scientific allies.

## 5. Race-based slavery as environment

The chapter's most consequential conceptual coinage — and the one that bridges most strongly to *Uncomfortable Situations*' Chapter 3 (Radcliffe / hostile environment) — is the treatment of race-based slavery not as an institution acting on individuals but as the environment within which sense-perception, attachment, and bearing take their shape. The formulation at PDF 75–76 is decisive: "the slave [is] perpetually 'endangered' as he or she is subject to the master within the environment where even a free person is constantly threatened by the institutions and practices of race-based slavery. As Equiano testifies, in this environment the free black can be treated unfairly without much recourse, kidnapped, and resold or sold for the first time into slavery. In this environment interests are vested differently, and life remains unbearable even when a piece of paper attests to equality before the law. Otherwise put, black freedom in this legal sense dissipates into the white world."

This is a non-trivial extension of Gibson's affordance theory. Gibson's environments offer affordances; Gross's environments — read through Equiano — also withdraw the recognitional infrastructure within which affordances become operative. A piece of paper attesting equality before the law is, in Gibsonian terms, an affordance for being-treated-as-equal; in the race-based-slavery environment, this affordance dissipates because the recognitional infrastructure that would honor it is structurally absent. The free black is freer in name only.

Vincent Carretta's biographical formulation is the chapter's source for this analysis. Carretta (*Equiano, the African* 2005, p. 122, cited in n.26) gives Gross the "vested interest" vocabulary: the overseer lacks the owner's vested interest in slave well-being; once freed, Equiano finds "no one with a vested interest in his well-being." Carretta notes the terrible irony that, in some ways, Equiano is more secure as a slave than as a freed black in a race-based-slavery environment. Gross extracts the analytic core: "security and attachment, in other words, must be understood in terms of vested interests that might work at cross purposes with the feelings involved" (PDF 75). Equiano may feel attached to the very relationships that subject him — Judith Butler's "passionate attachments" (*Psychic Life of Power*, n.27) — but this felt attachment is precisely the vehicle through which subjection operates.

The black-testimony moment at PDF 86 / *Narrative* 161–162 is the chapter's most striking instance of environmental withdrawal: throughout the West Indies, no black man's testimony is admitted on any occasion against any white person. Equiano therefore has no recourse when his "kind master" Robert King shuffles instead of repaying a financial debt that Equiano desperately needs. The environment has withdrawn the affordance of legal redress.

## 6. Afro-Pessimism: engagement without endorsement

The chapter engages Afro-Pessimism — Jared Sexton, Frank Wilderson, Fred Moten — at three loci that the index points to (p. 19, pp. 72–73, n. 42 at p. 162). Note 42 is the doctrinal anchor, citing Sexton's "The Social Life of Social Death: On Afro-Pessimism and Black Optimism" (*InTensions Journal* 5, 2011) at p. 28: "Nothing in afro-pessimism suggests that there is no black (social) life, only that black life is not social life in the universe formed by the codes of state and civil society, of citizen and subject, of nation and culture, of people and place, of history and heritage, of all the things that colonial society has in common with the colonized, of all that capital has in common with labor — the modern world system. Black life is not lived in the world that the world lives in, but it is lived underground, in outer space." Wilderson and Moten are flagged as key figures.

Gross's positive use of this material is at PDF 82: Equiano "does not know who he is precisely insofar as he is excluded from the systems of knowledge that make one's identity intelligible. Not free, not equal, not quite an Englishman, indeed not human in the ways that count. And not ever, as Afro-Pessimism has recently argued in the face of an affirmation that would give black people a piece of the action." The "not ever" is the load-bearing word: Equiano is not merely currently-excluded but constitutively-excluded; the systems of knowledge in question do not admit him and structurally cannot.

But Gross's chapter is not an Afro-Pessimist chapter. The methodological commitment is rhetorical-humanities analysis that can register exclusion without dissolving into negation. The litany of the inoperable at PDF 86 — enslavement of one's own progeny, buried virtue, violated natural rights, no common currency for rational communication, the inadmissibility of black testimony — is presented as the material of rhetorical analysis, not as the end-point of an Afro-Pessimist demonstration. The chapter's final figure of Equiano "neither here nor there, neither secured in the practical world where he does his business, nor in the spiritual world where he tends" preserves the analytic distance from the Afro-Pessimist insistence on social-death-without-remainder. Equiano's narrative is "vivifying as only a narrative of such improbability can be" (PDF 86) — the vivifying being precisely the rhetorical-humanities work that the chapter performs.

This positioning is consistent with Gross's broader rhetorical-ontology project (HR-01-GROSS 2005): the analytic of *Uncomfortable Situations* is reformist-rhetorical, leaving space for pedagogical-rhetorical intervention. Afro-Pessimism's structural diagnosis is registered; its programmatic conclusion is held at distance.

## 7. The spiritual-autobiography genre

The chapter's third and final analytic move is the recovery of spiritual autobiography as a rhetorical-emotional analytic form. The Mount Calvary conversion set-piece — *Narrative* 189–190, reproduced by Gross at PDF 84–85 in full paragraph form with his own underlining and annotation — is its central instance. Equiano is reading the fourth chapter of the Acts, twelfth verse (Peter's miraculous escape from prison); the Lord breaks in upon his soul with "bright beams of heavenly light"; the veil is removed (a metaphor Gross flags as Du Boisian as well); the scriptures become an "unsealed book"; the crucified Saviour appears at Mount Calvary; Romans 7:9–25, John 3:5 ("born again"), Romans 8:14 ("the Spirit you received does not make you slaves"), and Acts 8:26 (the Ethiopian) are densely keyed. Gross underscores precisely those words that Merleau-Ponty would identify as the intentional arc rehabilitated after going limp: "the unity of the senses, of intelligence, of sensibility and motility."

Mary Wollstonecraft, reviewing the 1789 *Narrative* in the *Analytical Review* vol. 4 (pp. 27–29), found this latter material "rather tiresome" after praising the first volume's "blood-turning course" (n.44). Gross's chapter is, in part, a 25-page rebuttal of Wollstonecraft's verdict. The conversion narrative is conventional, yes — the four-step allegorical structure (godly sadness, rebirth, challenge, manifestation of the new creature) is admitted — but the conventionality is precisely the analytic. The form does work that the content cannot do alone. It "gives things" to Equiano: it gives him the unified senses he cannot generate from his own situation; it gives him a bearer (Christ) for the bearing he cannot perform himself; it gives him the rhetorical-emotional disposition (bearing up) that is otherwise structurally unavailable to him in his litany-of-inoperable environment.

The earlier Cadiz-voyage shipwreck scene (PDF 84 / *Narrative* 198–199), with its Toplady hymn ("Christ is my pilot wise / my compass is his word... How can I sink with such a prop, / That bears the world and all things up?"), is the chapter's most concentrated demonstration of this giving. Toplady's hymn (No. 312 in *Psalms and Hymns for Public and Private Worship*, London 1776, identified by Carretta in n.43) supplies a vocabulary of bearing — the prop that bears the world — that exactly fits Equiano's situation of needing-a-bearer. The hymn does for Equiano what no philosophical anthropology can do: it gives him the figure that makes his bearing intelligible.

The conversion narrative is also where the chapter's prosthetic-emotion thesis (from the Introduction) gets its strongest in-text demonstration. The Introduction develops "prosthetic emotion" with reference to the *Imitation of Christ* and Aquinas, treating religious-emotion forms as performative emotion-installations. The Equiano conversion is exactly such an installation: the spiritual-autobiography form installs in Equiano the emotional disposition (bearing-the-cross-for-Jesus, *Narrative* 189: "I saw the Lord Jesus Christ in his humiliation, loaded and bearing my reproach, sin, and shame") that the practical environment had denied him. This is prosthetic, in the Introduction's strict sense, and it is what makes the conversion narrative *not* tiresome — it is the prosthesis that completes a rhetorical-emotional disposition the slave could not muster on his own.

## 8. Hedonic psychology in detail: Kahneman, Schwarz, Diener

The longest stretch of the chapter's first sub-section is its engagement with hedonic psychology (PDF 67–71). Gross works through three layers. First, Kahneman & Miller's 1986 "Norm Theory" supplies emotional amplification and counterfactual availability: as years pass, Equiano's compelling counterfactual ("what might have been" — return to Africa) dissipates, and so does the amplitude of his terror. Second, the hedonic treadmill (Brickman, Janoff-Bulman & Coates 1978; Brickman & Campbell 1971) supplies the lottery-winner and paraplegic case studies, and Kahneman's *Well-Being* (1999) supplies the analytic refinement: *becoming*-paraplegic must be distinguished from *being*-paraplegic. Third — and this is where Gross's local critique lands — Schwarz & Strack's chapter in *Well-Being* supplies what Gross treats as the rhetorical-analytic core of hedonic psychology: a taxonomy of distortions in subjective-well-being reports including question-order effects, conversational norms, episodic chunking, counterfactual availability, direction of comparison, social-improvement expectations, peer-comparison, attention-manipulation, and self-censoring.

Schwarz and Strack themselves "punt" (Gross's word, PDF 71) — defer to future cognitive science for resolution. Gross refuses the deferral: "But instead of deferring to cognitive science as a magic bullet of some unknown futurity, why not further legitimate a methodology around these profound rhetorical insights they offer right now? No amount of cognitive science will ever adequately explain Equiano's happiness, or anything like it. However the hedonic psychologist's deep sensitivity to context, variables — many of them rhetorical — can help in ways heretofore lost on the humanities as well" (PDF 71).

The disciplinary triad that crystallizes the engagement — experimental sciences interested in the predictably irrational, social sciences in the practically irrational, humanities in the possibly irrational (PDF 70) — is the chapter's most portable formulation. The "possibly irrational" names how emotions figure the world around limit-cases that qualify common sense. Equiano is a possibly-irrational case: practical reasoning cannot adjudicate whether he was "really" happy, but the question itself is the analytic engine.

## 9. The Alva Noe interleaving

The chapter's most distinctive rhetorical move is the line-by-line interleaving of Alva Noe's *Out of Our Heads* p. 51 with Equiano's biography (PDF 72–74). Noe's claims appear in italics; Gross's Equiano glosses appear in roman immediately below. The interleaving runs through six Noe-claims:

1. "Maturation is not so much a process of self-individuation and detachment as it is one of growing comfortably into one's environmental situation" — gloss: Equiano considers himself happily situated.
2. "We grow apart, but we attach to the world without. We integrate" — gloss: Equiano is almost an Englishman.
3. "In learning to walk or mastering language, in developing friendships, in acquiring an occupation, in learning to navigate and use technology, we root ourselves in the practical environment" — gloss: Equiano "attaches" to his master, gets his sea legs, learns English, acquires an occupation, learns to navigate a ship.
4. "This is one reason, certainly, why radical changes to one's environment, especially occurring later in one's life — for example, in the course of migrating from one country to another — are enormous, maybe even devastating personal challenges" — gloss: Equiano experienced just such a migration as a child of 8–11.
5. "Another effect of this kind of disruption is that time, in an interesting, felt way, slows down" — gloss: Equiano's astonishment (Latin *attonare*, to strike with a thunderbolt; obsolete senses 'loss of sense, or insensibility') is precisely time standing still.
6. The implication that bearing a relation is what makes a thing within reach — gloss: Equiano cannot bear the relations that the situated-cognition theorist takes as given.

The performance is the demonstration. Pragmatic situated cognition can absorb Equiano's case *only* by suppressing the hostility coefficient of his environment. The interleaving renders this suppression visible, line by line, by making Noe's friendly-environment vocabulary speak Equiano's biography. The result is not refutation but exhibition: Noe's vocabulary is shown to fit, and to fit catastrophically, because the fit conceals what it should be revealing.

The same Noe is then recovered, at PDF 71–72, as having admitted (in *Varieties of Presence*) that perception has a "rhetorical structure": "aesthetic experience happens only where there is the possibility of substantive disagreement, and so also the need for justification, explanation and persuasion" (*Varieties* 126); "perception is not a matter of sensation; it is never a matter of mere feeling... an activity of securing access to the world by cultivating the right critical stance... human experience has a 'rhetorical structure'" (*Varieties* 128). Gross treats this as situated cognition's own spontaneous admission of the humanities' centrality.

## 10. Hegel: the master-slave dialectic via Findlay

The chapter's most sustained philosophical exegesis (PDF 75–77) is its reading of Hegel's *Phenomenology of Spirit* ¶¶186–193 in the J. N. Findlay translation, accessed via the Hegel-by-Hypertext at marxists.org (n.30). Gross moves through the dialectic stepwise. ¶186: self-consciousness first appears in its immediacy and is "not risen above the bare level of life" — a position Gross identifies (provocatively) with Dreyfus's and Noe's "deeply absorbed in the business of living" (n.31). ¶187: perspective is first achieved as the negation of all mere objectivity, particularly the negation by which "I am not a thing and I have things for myself." ¶188: complete destruction of the other would destroy the self, so the other must be preserved. ¶189: the master-slave relation appears as "a pure self-consciousness, and a consciousness which is not purely for itself, but for another, i.e., as an existent consciousness, consciousness in the form and shape of thinghood." ¶191: the relation is essentially unbalanced, with Findlay's gloss key: "the bondsman acquiesces in the situation, and becomes in fact part and parcel of the total objective situation." ¶193: "The truth of the independent consciousness is accordingly the consciousness of the bondsman."

The crucial Gross-extension is that this Hegelian relation does not tend toward homeostasis. There is no living-wage threshold or equality-before-the-law minimum that would right the relation. Neither stoicism (¶198) nor skepticism (¶206) nor the unhappy consciousness "devoted to the discordant clang of ringing bells, or a cloud of warm incense" (¶217) resolves it. Nor can the objective world become a sensuous certainty simply looked at, experienced in itself, without having arrived in some form (n.33 cites Marx-Engels critique of Feuerbach via Ahmed). The Hegelian mutation lives on around an incomplete negation that, on Gross's reading, is exactly what Equiano grapples with.

The bondsman becomes "part and parcel of the total objective situation" — and Equiano, in the chapter's reading, is part and parcel of an objective situation that is itself the social-bodily going-limp Merleau-Ponty's 172n1 footnote forecast. The Hegelian frame and the Merleau-Pontian frame are not alternatives. They are the same analysis from different angles. Master-slave is what the social body looks like when it goes limp.

## 11. Sara Ahmed: ally and amendment

Sara Ahmed's *Queer Phenomenology* (Duke 2006) is recruited as the chapter's methodological ally and then carefully amended. The ally-role is announced at n.12: "my approach is close to Sara Ahmed's 'queer phenomenology.'" Ahmed's account of comfort (n.29) — "to be comfortable is to be so at ease with one's environment that it is hard to distinguish where one's body ends and the world begins... whiteness may function as a form of public comfort by allowing bodies to extend into spaces that have already taken their shape" (*Queer Phenomenology* 134) — supplies the chapter's underlying figure of comfort-as-fit. The corresponding figure of discomfort — "discomfort... allows things to move" — supplies the analytic engine.

The amendment arrives at PDF 82–83. Ahmed's account of "following directions" assumes that direction-following is a "commitment, a social investment that promises return" (Ahmed 17). Gross's amendment: "what if we are pressed into service outside the domain of consent or even beyond the social contract per se? Here we must amend Ahmed. For in Equiano's case, the service into which he is pressed bears false witness to the promise that he would be freed after fulfilling some kind of social contract, whether that means becoming almost an Englishman, buying his freedom, or even, via baptism, cleansing his soul before the eyes of God and thus joining the Christian fellowship where everyone is equal." The social-investment frame presupposes a contract from which Equiano is constitutively excluded. Queer phenomenology's tools require structural revision to register this.

The relation here is exemplary of the chapter's method: an ally is not abandoned but extended. Ahmed has done the analytic work for orientations that are anomalous within a heteronormative social-investment frame; Gross extends the analytic to a situation where the frame itself is foreclosed.

## 12. Robert King, Pascal, and the certificate of behavior

Two named slave-masters appear in the chapter's text. Michael Henry Pascal (PDF 67, 75, 82) is the earlier master who forces the name "Gustavus Vassa" on Equiano in place of his preferred "Jacob," beating him until he submits (*Narrative* 64), and who then sells Equiano to Captain Doran just when freedom seems within reach (*Narrative* 93–94). Robert King (PDF 82–83, 86) is the later Quaker master at Montserrat who signs the certificate of behavior that Equiano reproduces in the *Narrative*:

> Montserrat, January 26, 1767.
> The bearer hereof, Gustavus Vassa, was my slave for upwards of three years, during which he has always behaved himself well, and discharged his duty with honesty and assiduity.
>
>                                                                  ROBERT KING.
>                                                 To all whom this may concern.

The certificate is, on Gross's reading, the chapter's most concentrated symbol of bearing's paradox. It bears (carries, certifies) Equiano's slave-name. It bears (sustains, makes legally available) Equiano's freedom. It is the flimsiest sort of attachment — "a written voucher referencing a certain Gustavus Vassa, who supposedly behaved himself well as a slave, whatever that means, for whomever it may concern" — and yet it is "worth its weight in gold." Equiano expresses the deepest gratitude. Yet the same King, on Gross's reading at PDF 86, defaults on a debt to Equiano (*Narrative* 161–162), and Equiano has no recourse because no black man's testimony is admissible in the West Indies.

The Pascal–King pair stages, at the level of named character, the chapter's "vested interest" thesis. Pascal demonstrates that felt attachment runs through betrayal; King demonstrates that legal attachment runs through default. Equiano feels grateful to both. The feeling is real; the structure of recognition that would honor the feeling is foreclosed.

## 13. Cross-pipeline bridges

The chapter's most explicit forward-reference is to *Uncomfortable Situations* Chapter 4 (Austen / mixed feelings): Bentham's *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* was first published in the same year (1789) as the first edition of Equiano's *Interesting Narrative*, and Gross cites this coincidence as a methodological anchor for the disciplinary triad (PDF 69). Chapter 4 will develop Kahneman on mixed feelings as ambivalencies referencing multiple points subject to negotiation or persuasion; the Equiano chapter forecasts this with its retrospective-layered "Was Equiano really happy?" decomposition.

The chapter also forecasts Chapter 3 (Radcliffe / hostile environment) through the figure of race-based slavery as environment. The link is explicit in Salamon n.10: "see also my following chapter 3 on hostile environments." The negative-affordance vocabulary that Chapter 3 will develop via Gibson is anticipated here by the slave's environment as one in which affordances of legal redress, social investment, and rational communication systematically withdraw.

The same-author antecedent is *The Secret History of Emotion* (Chicago 2006), particularly chapter 1's critique of Joseph LeDoux's brain-science of emotion (cited at n.21). The Equiano chapter is *Uncomfortable Situations*' clearest demonstration that rhetorical analysis is constitutive — not supplementary — for the analysis of emotion.

The rhetorical-ontology framework of HR-01-GROSS (2005) is operative throughout but only thematized at PDF 77–79, where "rhetorical" is glossed as (a) effective and (b) positing-a-social-relationship. The 2005 essay's thesis that emotions are constitutively rhetorical is the Equiano chapter's working premise.

The Rickert *Ambient Rhetoric* bridge runs through the "books are orientation devices" formulation (PDF 80). The rhetorical situation, on Gross's account, is the environment within which orientation is performed, not the Bitzer-style proximate occasion. This positions the chapter within the same theoretical neighborhood as Rickert's ambient-rhetoric project.

The Aristotelian-pathē bridge runs through the gloss at PDF 78–79: "the emotion — *pathos* in the classical lexicon — is not first and foremost something Equiano feels... instead, it posits a social relationship." This is the same gloss of pathos that *The Secret History of Emotion* derived from Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II.

## 14. The chapter's place in the volume

The Equiano chapter is the volume's hardest test case and its methodological hinge. The hardest test case because Equiano's first-person testimony of being "happily situated" should — on any version of the friendly-environment, adaptation, situated-cognition model — be taken at face value. The chapter demonstrates that taking it at face value requires suppressing precisely the rhetorical-environmental features that the testimony is itself tracking. The methodological hinge because the chapter's contest with Dreyfus, its recovery of the Hegelian strand in Merleau-Ponty, and its rhetorical-analytic recovery of the spiritual-autobiography form together secure the volume's broader apparatus (situated conceptualization, affect theory, affordance theory) against absorption into pragmatic-phenomenological orthodoxy.

The chapter ends in deliberate non-resolution. Equiano is "neither here nor there, neither secured in the practical world where he does his business, nor in the spiritual world where he tends" (PDF 86). The narrative is "vivifying as only a narrative of such improbability can be." The bearing-up that the chapter has been analyzing is not a resolution to slavery's analytic; it is the figure by which the analytic refuses resolution. The chapter does not give Equiano a comfortable place. It demonstrates that the comfortable place is precisely what cannot be given to him — and that demonstrating this is itself a piece of rhetorical-humanities work that no situated cognition or hedonic psychology, taken at its own self-description, can perform.

The figure of "bearing up" remains, on the chapter's last page, a paradox: the bearing is real, the carrying is sustained, the rhetorical-emotional disposition is registered — but the bearer is not the bearer. Christ bears, the world bears down, the slave bears up but only in the sense of carrying-what-was-ready-to-be-cursed and in the further sense of being borne by a prop that he did not place there. The figure does not resolve the analytic; it carries the analytic across the gap that the chapter has opened, and it leaves the gap open. This is the chapter's contribution to the volume's "uncomfortable situation" thesis: the most rigorous available figure for staying in the uncomfortable situation without dissolving it into either resignation or resolution.
