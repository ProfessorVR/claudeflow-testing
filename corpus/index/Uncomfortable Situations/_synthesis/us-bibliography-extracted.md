# Bibliography — Daniel M. Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations* (Chicago 2017)

Extracted from the volume's Notes (pp. 147–174) and organized into four working sections. 114 entries total (Section A: 16 / Section B: 30 / Section C: 33 / Section D: 35). Each entry is cross-referenced to the unit IDs in which it surfaces (US-00 = Introduction; US-01 = Darwin; US-02 = Equiano; US-03 = Radcliffe; US-04 = Austen; US-05 = Epilogue with Stephanie Preston). For full bibliographic detail see the companion JSON.

## Section A — Primary literary and scientific objects

The volume's primary archive is eighteenth- and early-nineteenth-century sentimental and gothic writing read alongside Darwin's *Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872). The four chapter-anchor texts are **Darwin's Expression** (Oxford 1998 [1872], third edition by Gross's preference), **Olaudah Equiano's Interesting Narrative** (Penguin / Carretta 2003), **Ann Radcliffe's Romance of the Forest** (Oxford / Chard 1986), and **Jane Austen's Sense and Sensibility** (Oxford 2008). Gross also brings in **Austen's Persuasion** (Oxford 2004) and **Emma** (Oxford 2003) for cross-novel comparison, **Sterne's A Sentimental Journey** (Oxford / Jack 1968) and **Tristram Shandy** (Oxford / Ross 2009) as recurrent counter-objects, **Sarah Fielding's Adventures of David Simple** (UPK 1998), **Henry Mackenzie's Man of Feeling**, and **Mary Wollstonecraft's Vindication** (Norton 1988) plus her *Analytical Review* (1789) commentary on Equiano. **Horace Walpole's Castle of Otranto** and **M. G. Lewis's The Monk** function as gothic backdrop. Literary references brought in *through* Darwin include **Shakespeare** (*Love's Labour's Lost*, *Much Ado*, *Comedy of Errors*) and **Spenser**; **Virgil's Aeneid** (Ahl trans., Oxford 2007) appears via the Laocoön excursus in Ch. 1.

## Section B — Affect theory and history of emotions

The volume's most-cited theorists in this register are **Lauren Berlant** (*Cruel Optimism* 2011; *Compassion* 2004; the "Nearly Utopian" essay 2007), **Sara Ahmed** (*Queer Phenomenology* 2006), **William Reddy** (*Making of Romantic Love* 2012; "Against Constructionism" 1997; Plamper interview 2010), **Brian Massumi** (*Parables for the Virtual* 2002; "The Autonomy of Affect" 1995), and **Ruth Leys** (the trio of "Turn to Affect" 2011, "How Did Fear Become a Scientific Object" 2010, "Both of Us Disgusted in My Insula" 2014). The history-of-sensibility scaffolding is provided by **Chandler** (*Archaeology of Sympathy* 2013), **Goldsmith** (*Blake's Agitation*), **Festa** (*Sentimental Figures of Empire*), **Markman Ellis** (*Politics of Sensibility*), and **Barker-Benfield** (*Culture of Sensibility*). **Franco Moretti's *The Bourgeois*** anchors the bourgeois-comfort thread; **Sianne Ngai** (*Ugly Feelings*; *Our Aesthetic Categories*) and **Philip Fisher** (*The Vehement Passions*) supply the affect-theory tonality. The Biess/Gross edited volume *Science and Emotion after 1945* (Chicago 2014) is the most heavily reused single source in the whole book, cited in every unit except US-03. Recent affective-turn anthologies (Clough & Halley 2007; Wetherell 2012) and Carolyn J. Dean's "Empathy, Pornography" (2003) and "Erasures" (2014) round out the section.

## Section C — Neuroscience and cognitive science

The pivotal neuroscience interlocutors are **Lisa Feldman Barrett** and **Lawrence Barsalou**, whose situated-conceptualization program (the "Grounding Emotion" Wilson-Mendenhall et al. 2011 paper) supplies the volume's epigraph and is reused in Ch. 4. Barrett also appears via "Language as Context for Emotional Perception" (2007) and *The Psychological Construction of Emotion* (Barrett & Russell eds. 2015). **Antonio Damasio** is the standing foil — *The Feeling of What Happens* (1999) cited five times, plus *Looking for Spinoza* (1999) and the Adolphs–Tranel–Damasio amygdala paper (1998). **Daniel Kahneman** is the heavyweight of the hedonic-psychology axis: *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), the *Well-Being* anthology with Diener and Schwarz (1999), prospect theory with Tversky (Econometrica 1979), and "Norm Theory" with Miller (1986) anchor Chs. 2 and 4. **Jerome Kagan's** *What Is Emotion?* (Yale 2007) is the volume's most frequently invoked critique of Ekman/Damasio. **Paul Ekman** himself is represented through three primary publications. The Epilogue's neuro-empathy archive is dense: **Phelps et al.** on amygdala and race (2000), **Stephanie Preston** with de Waal (2011) and with Hofelich (2012), **Tania Singer et al.** on empathy-for-pain (2004), **Hannah Faye Chua et al.** on smoking-cessation tailoring (2009), **Simon Baron-Cohen** (*Zero Degrees of Empathy* 2011; the 1985 ToM paper), **Chris Frith** on the social brain (2007), and **Russell Poldrack** on reverse inference (2006). **Marco Iacoboni's** *Mirroring People* (2008) supplies the mirror-neuron baseline. Earlier-twentieth-century sources include **Knight Dunlap** (1932) on emotions as teleological constructs, recovered by Gross as proto-situated, and **R. B. Zajonc's** "Feeling and Thinking" (1980).

## Section D — Rhetoric, classical, and philosophical

The classical-rhetorical spine is **Aristotle's Rhetoric** (cited in all chapters) plus Plato's *Philebus*, Cicero's *Verrines* (via Innocenti 1994), Galen, **Charles Le Brun's Method to Learn to Design the Passions** (1698 / 1980 reprint), and **Henry Peacham** on paradiastole (via Skinner in *Renaissance Figures of Speech*, Cambridge 2007). The modern rhetorical-theory backbone is **Lloyd F. Bitzer's "The Rhetorical Situation"** (1968), **Kenneth Burke** (*Philosophy of Literary Form* 1941/1973; *Permanence and Change* 1984; the Prelli–Anderson–Althouse essay on recalcitrance 2011), and **Thomas Rickert's Ambient Rhetoric** (2013). Gross's own *Secret History of Emotion* (Chicago 2006) is the load-bearing self-citation. The phenomenology / philosophy-of-mind cluster is dominated by **Hubert Dreyfus** (four separate items including *What Computers Still Can't Do* 1972/1992 and the 2007 "Why Heideggerian AI Failed" essay), **Maurice Merleau-Ponty's Phenomenology of Perception** (Smith trans. 1989), **Martin Heidegger's GA 18** (Klostermann 2002, cited in Intro n.37 as the philosophical hinge between Aristotelian rhetoric and Gross's project), and **Hegel** (the *Phenomenology of Spirit* via the Findlay translation and the *Encyclopedia / Philosophy of Mind* on extended mind). Cognitive-rhetorical and literary-Darwinist interlocutors include **Steven Pinker** ("Toward a Consilient Study of Literature" 2007; *How the Mind Works* 1997), **E. O. Wilson's Consilience**, **Robert Trivers**, **John Searle** (via Dreyfus), **Wittgenstein's Lectures on Aesthetics**, **Marx/Engels** (the Feuerbach critique), **Aquinas** and **Vygotsky** (Index references), **Alasdair MacIntyre's After Virtue** (2007), **Gilbert Ryle's "Jane Austen and the Moralists"** (1966), and **Jordynn Jack's Neurorhetorics** (2012). **Jami Bartlett's Object Lessons** (Chicago 2016) is the late-arriving theorist of non-referring terms in the negation analysis of Ch. 1.

## Top-of-page summary of citation density

- Most cited single source in the whole book: **Biess & Gross, *Science and Emotion after 1945*** (Chicago 2014), cited across US-00, US-01, US-02, US-04, US-05.
- Most cited theorist across chapters: **Daniel Kahneman** (≥7 distinct publications across US-00 / US-02 / US-04 / US-05).
- Most cited single article: **Wilson-Mendenhall, Barrett, Simmons, Barsalou (2011), "Grounding Emotion in Situated Conceptualization"** — supplies the epigraph and is the protocol-text for Ch. 4.
- Most cited primary literary work: **Darwin, Expression of the Emotions** (the anchor for US-01 throughout).
- Most cited rhetorical-theory source: **Bitzer (1968)** — the operative definition of "exigence" that Gross transposes into "basic discomfort."
