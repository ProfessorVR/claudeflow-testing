# US-01-DARWIN — Paraphrastic Narrative

**Chapter 1: "Defending the Humanities with Charles Darwin's *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*"**
Daniel M. Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations* (Chicago 2017), book pp. 28–51 / PDF pp. 38–61.
First published as Gross, "Defending the Humanities with Charles Darwin's *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872)," *Critical Inquiry* 37, no. 1 (Autumn 2010), in an issue edited by Lauren Berlant.

---

## 1. The polemical opening: Darwin's chimpanzee as rhetorical exhibit (PDF 38–39 / bk 28–29)

The chapter opens not with theoretical scaffolding but with a primary-source exhibit. Gross places the reader in front of Figure 1.1 — "Chimpanzee disappointed and sulky. Drawn from life by Mr. Wood," reproduced from Darwin's *Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (London: J. Murray, 1872), p. 139, via the Wellcome Library's CC BY 4.0 archive. The accompanying passage from Darwin (E 139–140) describes how the lips of young orangs and chimpanzees protrude under all manner of conditions — sulk, anger, alarm at the sight of a turtle, even pleasure — and how the woodcut depicts a chimpanzee made sulky by an orange offered and withdrawn, with the same pouting visible "in sulky children." Gross's claim about this passage is precise: it is *both* rhetorical and inseparable from its science. The rhetorical work is not ornamental husk surrounding a scientific kernel; it is constitutive. Darwin's purpose in publishing the *Expression* in 1872 was to demonstrate emotional continuity across species and thereby reinforce evolutionary theory, and the descriptions surrounding his illustrations are accordingly designed "to elicit our recognition as members of a universal audience." But Gross also flags an additional layer that the historical audience may miss: a "sly provocation of contemporaries" hidden in the passage's punctilious comparison of orang and child. The exhibit's purpose, in other words, is double — proof of continuity *and* satire of human exceptionalism.

This opening establishes the thesis of the chapter, which Gross states plainly: he will recall how Darwin's *Expression* "foregrounds the inherent rhetoricity of emotion, thereby outstripping Paul Ekman's science of emotion that claims to follow in its wake, and which has recently infiltrated the humanities like a Trojan horse settling in a new critical subfield of Cognitive Approaches to Literature (CAL)." Darwin's rhetoric of emotion is "remarkably skeptical and humanistic," which does not diminish its scientific piquancy but aligns it with the situated theories now operative in the cognitive sciences — Gross cites Alva Noé's *Out of Our Heads: Why You Are Not Your Brain* (Hill and Wang 2009) as a touchstone.

## 2. The reception arc: Ekman's 1998 third edition (PDF 40–41 / bk 30–31)

Gross sketches the *Expression*'s reception history with rhetorical economy. The book was initially a bestseller, then lapsed into relative obscurity through the twentieth century as Darwin's evolutionary theory consolidated itself on other grounds — the fossil record, homologies, geographic distribution, and artificial selection. Emotion meanwhile became a "difficult and even suspect science" because it lacked the kind of clean metric that rational-choice models could deliver. ("It's raining" might reliably link mind and world; "I am afraid" suffered semantic and practical ambiguities that frustrated experimental design.) Recent decades, however, have brought a dramatic comeback: emotion-study has mushroomed across academic disciplines and applied fields — homeland security (Ekman, *Washington Post* 2006: "How to Spot a Terrorist on the Fly") and the Fox cop drama *Lie to Me*, which built its plots around microexpression analysis à la Ekman.

The decisive 1998 event was Paul Ekman's editorial introduction to and running commentary on the third edition of Darwin's *Expression* (Oxford University Press, the edition Gross cites throughout). Gross quotes the key Darwin method passage (E 21): Dr. Duchenne galvanized facial muscles in an old man whose skin "was little sensitive," produced photographs of the resulting expressions, and Darwin "fortunately" decided to show several plates "without a word of explanation" to "above twenty educated persons of various ages and both sexes," recording their answers. Several expressions were "instantly recognized by almost every one." Ekman's annotation, inserted into the 1998 edition, reads: "Darwin describes here his use of a method to find out what information is conveyed by an expression: asking people to judge the emotion shown in a photograph without any information about the situation in which the expression occurred, and determining whether or not they agree. This has become the most commonly used method for studying facial expression, although Darwin is rarely cited as the first to use it." Ekman has applied "essentially the same methodology" for thirty-five years to develop his work on "basic pancultural emotions" — the image set reproduced as Figure 1.5 (anger, fear, disgust, surprise, joy, sadness, and Ekman himself as contempt; reproduced with permission from Paul Ekman Group, LLC). Gross teasingly asks the reader to name them.

From this methodological foundation Ekman built the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), now widely used in cognitive neuroscience to locate emotional centers in the brain by correlating activity with exposure to carefully selected photographs. Among the cognitive scientists who rely on FACS, Antonio Damasio is "the most influential in the humanities." Gross cites Damasio's 1998 *Nature* paper (Adolphs, Tranel, and Damasio, "The Human Amygdala in Social Judgment") in which amygdala-damaged patients judged as trustworthy and approachable faces that "you or I would consider suspicious and try to avoid"; the experiment then "located" the emotion in the brain "while bracketing some pressing questions about what counts as a trustworthy and approachable face, to whom, under what circumstances, and why."

## 3. The plates as arguments: Duchenne and the "horror" experiment (PDF 41–43 / bk 31–33)

The chapter's first figure cluster appears here. Figure 1.2 ("Horror and Agony, copied from a photograph by Dr. Duchenne," *Expression* p. 307) carries an unusually long caption reproducing Duchenne's original 1862 French — "expression d'effroi mêlé de douleur extrême … cette expression doit être celle du damné" — alongside Cuthbertson's 1990 translation ("the expression of the damned"). Figure 1.3 stacks Rejlander's "astonishment" (first-edition holdover) atop the Duchenne photograph Darwin showed to twenty-four persons (twenty: "intense fright"/"horror"; three: pain; one: extreme discomfort). Figure 1.4 reproduces the uncropped Duchenne photograph — restoring the staging context cropping concealed. These plates do double duty: Darwin's evidence and exhibits of the *mediation* Ekman's reception strips away. The horror is "obviously extorted by way of galvanism" and "thus the photograph draws attention to its own staging."

## 4. The methodological core: Noé on the "New Phrenology," Kagan's category mistake (PDF 43–44 / bk 33–34)

Gross's critique of Ekman-Damasio runs through Noé's *Out of Our Heads*, specifically the section provocatively titled "The New Phrenology?" Noé outlines three debilitating problems for researchers who try to locate cognition in the brain: (1) the impossibility of eliminating feedback produced by the two-directionality of the brain-senses loop; (2) normalizing imperatives that produce results against a stock brain; and (3) the impossibility of eliciting direct information about consciousness or cognition through PET and fMRI, which must correlate physical magnitudes to blood flow, blood flow to neural activity, and neural activity to mental activity (Noé, 19–24). "Brain scans are not pictures of cognitive processes in the brain in action" (Noé, 24).

But for Gross "the shortcoming that matters most is methodological." Emotions cannot be broken down into basic units — molecules (Candace Pert), brain images, or facial expressions — without losing the phenomenon. Jerome Kagan (*What Is Emotion?*, Yale 2007) sharpens the point with a category-mistake analogy: the neuroscientist who insists a particular brain state *represents* an emotion "resembles the physicist who denies the reality of my pen because the mathematical interpretation of quantum mechanics rejects the existence of stable objects." Complex phenomena, Kagan concludes, possess distinct properties and therefore require "distinct metrics and principles" (*What Is Emotion?* 214). That Kagan's "valedictorian address" to his students and colleagues requires this reminder is not surprising. *Surprising* is that the cautionary tale "speak[s] to a growing number of humanities scholars who overlook precisely the situational nuances that would seem most amenable to their indigenous methodologies."

## 5. The CAL Trojan horse: Howard, Crane, Thrailkill, Keen (PDF 44–46 / bk 34–36)

The chapter's polemical center now arrives. Ekman defines *basic* emotions as adaptive-value-evolved for fundamental life tasks ("Basic Emotions," *Handbook of Cognition and Emotion*, 1999, p. 46); his commentary on Darwin winds down with the disgust-as-model passage (E 392 afterword): "ingesting something repulsive … or ingesting something attractive … is universal. I think this is a good model for all the emotions." This, Gross argues, is where Ekman "and his legions leave Darwin behind, with a sleight of hand." Anything beyond the basic emotions simply is not an emotion in Ekman's program.

This impoverished model has been imported into literary studies. Gross runs through CAL exemplars: June Howard's "What Is Sentimentality?" (*American Literary History*, 1999) — sentimental reading as "bodily act" producing "pulse beats and sobs" (quoting Sánchez-Eppler); Mary Thomas Crane's *Shakespeare's Brain* (Princeton 2001) — mind grounded "in the brain and other bodily systems"; Jane F. Thrailkill's *Affecting Fictions* (Harvard 2007) — novel as "aesthetic technology of the human organism"; and Suzanne Keen's *Empathy and the Novel* (Oxford 2007), which records that Ekman, Damasio, and LeDoux have achieved "virtually canonical status" in CAL. Gross flags his explicit "literary Darwinism" critique (Pinker, Carroll) for Chapter 4. The alternative he proposes is a "rhetorical model of cognition where consciousness is situated in the brain-body-world nexus," with affinities to Terence Cave, Jonathan Kramnick, Elaine Scarry, and David Herman.

The Crane critique is sharp. Her lexical analysis of *home* in *The Comedy of Errors* mobilizes Lakoff and Turner's cognitive rhetorics while offering "a possible background" for Patricia Parker's cultural criticism (*SB*, 33). The argument fails "precisely at the point where Crane situates such familiar linguistic analyses against a cognitive science background." Crane treats the brain as "the material site where discourse meets the body" (*SB*, 7), but "everything humans do can be located in the brain at some point, so the observation does not help us read Shakespeare any more than it helps us drive to the grocery store." ## 6. The political stake: Berlant on compassionate conservatism (PDF 47–48 / bk 37–38)

Gross escalates the critique from methodology to politics. Even where humanities critics intend no compassionate-conservative agenda, the move that "squeeze[s] culture between the pincers of a brain-world divide" reproduces, structurally, the maneuver Lauren Berlant tracks in her introduction to *Compassion: The Culture and Politics of an Emotion* (Routledge 2004), p. 3. Compassionate conservatism — in Berlant's diagnosis — substitutes the sanctification of personal labor "at work, at home, and in communities" for the structural foundations of a living wage, public education, affordable housing, and universal access to economic resources; "income-producing labor is deemed valuable chiefly in the context of its part in making smaller-scale, face-to-face publics." Gross's punchline: face-to-face emotional encounters "must always be considered in a larger political context where the status of social institutions is at stake." The cautionary tale applies regardless of the literary critic's politics; the brain-world pincer "hampers our facility in cultural work and criticism."

## 7. Darwin's methodological self-awareness: the imagination caveat (PDF 48 / bk 38)

Gross now returns to Darwin's own text to make the positive case. "Early in the *Expression*, Darwin describes his methodology while introducing an important caveat now typically sidelined." Upon viewing the Duchenne photographs, several expressions were instantly recognized "by almost everyone," but others were subject to "widely different judgments." Rather than explaining the divergence away — via emotional hierarchy, "display rules" (E 383, Ekman's term), or stipulative definition, as Ekman does — Darwin introduces *imagination* as the relevant variable: "This exhibition was of use in another way, by convincing me how easily we may be misguided by our imagination; for when I first looked through Dr. Duchenne's photographs, reading at the same time the text, and thus learning what was intended, I was struck with admiration at the truthfulness of all, with only a few exceptions. Nevertheless, if I had examined them without any explanation, no doubt I should have been as much perplexed, in some cases, as other persons have been" (E 21, with Gross's emphasis added).

Darwin here draws *positive* attention to the interpretive relationship between word and image, "rather than justifying a reduction." The photographs are not unmediated emotion-events; they are reports of an experiment designed around a set of photographs.

## 8. The omitted emotions and the devotion passage (PDF 49 / bk 39)

This digression into imagination, Gross notes, "also allows Darwin to discuss emotions Ekman leaves off of his basic list, including love, sympathy, hatred, suspicion, envy, jealousy, avarice, revenge, deceit, devotion, slyness, guilt, vanity, conceit, ambition, pride, humility, and so on." The very "antiquation" of including *deceit* and *devotion* as emotions signals that human history — not just evolutionary history — is essential to Darwin's science.

The chapter's devotion passage (E 217–218) is decisive. The kneeling posture with joined upturned palms appears "so appropriate to devotion" that it might be thought innate, but Darwin reports no evidence for it across "extra-European races of mankind" and notes that during the classical Roman period "the hands were thus joined during prayer" was unknown. Hensleigh Wedgwood's etymological explanation is then quoted at length: "When the suppliant kneels and holds up his hands with the palms joined, he represents a captive who proves the completeness of his submission by offering up his hands to be bound by the victor. It is the pictorial representation of the Latin *dare manus*, to signify submission." The implication: the posture is not innate but historical, "and this could hardly have been expected, for it is very doubtful whether feelings, *such as we should now rank as devotional*, affected the hearts of men, whilst they remained during past ages in an uncivilized condition" (E 218; Gross's emphasis).

Civilization, Gross concludes, "entails, apparently, a distinct emotional regime that separates it from the uncivilized, thereby contradicting an evolutionary theory that sees our continuity with Pleistocene hunter-gatherers but doesn't see the distortions inevitably imposed by our interpretive lens." Darwin's historical work is "part and parcel of his careful scientific observation"; without it, the "we" of the universal audience would naturalize a parochial posture as innate.

## 9. The maternal-love crux (PDF 50 / bk 40)

The chapter's most often-cited Darwin passage now appears: "No emotion is stronger than maternal love," "qualifies Darwin in a Victorian mood, 'but a mother may feel deepest love for her helpless infant, and yet not show it any outward sign'" (E 82). The Victorian gendering is Gross's tactful acknowledgment, but the methodological point lands hard. Darwin "doubts whether a long list of what he calls *complex states of mind* are revealed by any fixed expression and are sufficiently distinct to be described or delineated in strictly scientific terms."

And this is precisely where Darwin "turns to the literary humanities" — the move Gross's chapter is keyed to recover. Quoting Darwin (E 260): "When 'Shakespeare speaks of envy as 'lean-faced in her loathsome ease'; or black, or pale, and jealousy as 'the green-eyed monster'; and when Spenser describes suspicion as 'foul ill-favoured, and grim,' they must have felt this difficulty. Nevertheless, the above feelings — at least many of them — can be detected by the eye; for instance, conceit; but we are often guided in a much greater degree than we suppose by our previous knowledge of the persons or circumstances."

Ekman's response to this complication is to relabel: emotions are "brief and episodic, lasting seconds or minutes," whereas "parental love, romantic love, hatred, envy or jealousy last for much longer periods — months, years, a lifetime for love and hatred, and at least hours or days for envy and jealousy. In each of these there is a strong commitment, an important attachment.… They also differ from emotions in specifying something about the cast of characters involved" (*Commentaries*, E 83). What Ekman calls these instead is "affective commitments."

The irony Gross extracts is precise: Ekman's *grounds* for excluding love/hatred/envy/jealousy from emotions proper — namely that they specify "something about the cast of characters involved" — are exactly the grounds Aristotle uses in *Rhetoric* Book II to constitute the pathē as emotions. Darwin, like Aristotle, writes "long and fascinating Aristotelian passages where the cast of characters that compose an emotion are specified in their complex sociopolitical relations." Take Darwin on hatred (E 234): "If we have suffered or expect to suffer some wilful injury from a man, or if he is in any way offensive to us, we dislike him; and dislike easily rises into hatred.… But if the offending person be quite insignificant, we experience merely disdain or contempt. If, on the other hand, he is all-powerful, then hatred passes into terror, as when a slave thinks about a cruel master, or a savage about a bloodthirsty malignant deity." The relative significance of characters is constitutive, and Gross adds that subjective perceptions of significance "can be systematically distorted, of course, by hegemonic constructs including racism and patriarchy." (Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* is footnoted here with the bitter footnote-30 example: a sudden fall into disease deserves sympathy, but being jilted by one's mistress or henpecked by one's wife does not.) There is "no way a facial expression or isolated brain scan can adequately represent an emotion like hatred."

## 10. The visual-arts case: Laocoön and Charles Bell (PDF 51–53 / bk 41–43)

For the so-called inactive emotions Darwin "finds recourse in the visual arts and literature, and not just as a repository for accidental wisdom in the manner of Damasio, who also likes to quote Shakespeare, but rather as a *necessary component of a larger theory*." Figure 1.6 is the famous first-century Laocoön sculpture, variously read as "bellowing" like a bull (*mugitus*) by Virgil (*Aeneid* 2.223), "noble" (*edle*) by the philologist Winckelmann (Fusseli trans. 1765, p. 30), "groaning" by Lessing (whose *Laokoön* Darwin footnotes at E 436n16), and "silently struggling" by Charles Bell, "whose book *The Anatomy and Philosophy of Expression as Connected with the Fine Arts* served as Darwin's polemical foil" (Bell, London 2006 [1844], pp. 174–175). In Bell's *Anatomy* Darwin found the sketch reproduced here as Figure 1.7, which he relies on to invoke Lessing's argument that the visual arts must disperse violent emotions: contracted facial muscles destroy beauty, so "visual arts provide an insufficient venue for the scientific study of emotion."

Insufficient, but necessary. As in literary narrative — where hatred emerges only through a cast of characters — the visual arts tell the story of the composition "with wonderful force and truth by skillfully given accessories" (E 22), both human and nonhuman, animate and inanimate, foregrounded and backgrounded. In the Laocoön the sea serpent is the indispensable accessory ("we are not talking about orgasmic ecstasy, after all"); the two perishing sons force the scene beyond resolute noble self-sacrifice; the receding background includes the vengeful Apollo (punishing his disobedient priest) and the Trojan people for whom Laocoön sacrificed himself in warning. (Footnote 19 connects this argument to Aby Warburg's *Pathosformel* and *bewegtes Beiwerk* — "the accessory in motion" — citing Didi-Huberman's 2003 *Journal of Visual Culture* discussion.)

## 11. The Happy Meal countermodel (PDF 52–54 / bk 42–44)

So where is the emotion (Figure 1.8)? Is it simply in that first face — Photograph 1 of the Pictures of Facial Affect (POFA) Collection smile sequence, reproduced under license from Paul Ekman Group, LLC? Gross's countermodel triptych places, next to that smile, a General Happy Meal Box (center) and a black-red joystick "petri" by Ansgar Hellwig (right) "in case you are thrown by the anthropomorphism of a smile on the middle image." His point is that the Happy Meal — "much more than a burger, fries, drink, and plastic toy, more than simply an emotionally competent object (like Ekman's delicacy) triggering happy faces" — is "an emotional artifact seemingly untethered to — Karl Marx would say *alienated* from — the complex social transactions that produce it." This is the chapter's heretical move: "I prefer to imagine Darwin turning toward his contemporary Marx as he says in this case an accessory that tells the emotional tale has apparently taken on a life of its own." Commodity-fetish reading of an Ekman-tradition emotional-trigger object. Revisiting Darwin's work on emotion, Gross suggests, makes this "turn to sociorhetorical analysis at least as plausible as the other."

A rapid synthesis of Extended Mind theory follows. Chalmers's Foreword to Andy Clark's *Supersizing the Mind* (Oxford 2008) proposes "extended desires, extended reasoning, extended perception, extended imagination, and 'extended emotions.'" Noé's dictum "In the Beginning Was the Situation" (*Out of Our Heads*, 101) replaces the Cartesian computing machine; the mother-and-infant example illustrates: "the baby's relation to the other unfolds in 'an emotional setting'" (Noé, 31). Dreyfus argues that AI must include "a model of a body very much like ours with our needs, desires, pleasures, pains, ways of moving, cultural background, etc." ("Why Heideggerian AI Failed," 1160). But, Gross objects, EM theorists "come up short when describing this worldly side of the brain-body-world nexus. To put it bluntly, they too need the arts and literature in the spirit of Darwin."

## 12. The Sterne case: peasants, negation, sentimental commerce (PDF 55–56 / bk 45–46)

The chapter's closing literary study is Laurence Sterne's *A Sentimental Journey through France and Italy* (Penguin, ed. Ian Jack), "which is a masterful study of emotional situations of a certain sort." Yorick travels through "the treacherous mountains between Turin and Savoy" and offers this passage on encountered peasants: "Poor, patient, quiet, honest people! fear not: your poverty, the treasury of your simple virtues, will not be envied you by the world, nor will your valleys be invaded by it. — Nature! in the midst of thy disorders, thou art still friendly to the scantiness thou hast created — with all thy great works about thee, little hast thou left to give, either to the scythe or to the sickle — but to that little thou grantest safety and protection; and sweet are the dwellings which stand so shelter'd" (Sterne 120).

"Nowhere in this scene do we find suggestive documentation of face-to-face encounters à la Paul Ekman." The pathos "thrives on a variety of distancing mechanisms familiar to the landscape artist and the critical theorist, respectively." Yorick's pity for the peasants must be understood "in contrast to the geographical horror it supersedes," escaping Steven Pinker's "literary Darwinism where the novel is 'cheesecake for the mind,' and novelistic fear provides an opportunity for virtual experience of a situation better avoided in real life" (Pinker, "Toward a Consilient Study of Literature," *Philosophy and Literature* 31, 2007, 162–178; cheesecake from *How the Mind Works*, 524–525). Yorick's pitiful perspective requires a romanticized landscape, the "feast of love" preceding the scene (Sterne 119), and the "sentimental commerce" Yorick had introduced earlier (Sterne 9) — being-out-of-place produces imbalanced sentimental exchange, where humiliation over a botched commercial transaction or awkwardness over a joke in a foreign tongue raises the price of conversation. (Lisa Zunshine's theory-of-mind Cartesian-computing-machine model of reading-mind, *Why We Read Fiction*, 47, 27, 47, 63, is named here as the inadequate alternative; against June Howard's quotation of Sánchez-Eppler, these words on the page do *not* produce "pulse beats and sobs" — except for "Sterne's racier moments admittedly do, for this reader at least.")

## 13. Negation as rhetorical operator: from Sterne to Austen (PDF 56–58 / bk 46–48)

"Fear not, envied not. What do negated emotions feel like after all? Look like? What bit of information about the real world do they represent? Where might they be located on the face, or mapped in the brain?" Gross's payoff turns on the observation that "the most emotionally pregnant words in the passage — *fear* and *envy* — appear only in negation," which "blocks the royal road to experience we might very well expect on a sentimental journey." The flat alternative — "Poor, patient, quiet, honest people! rejoice…" — would have been the more immediate stylistic solution; Sterne's chosen negation "invokes a particular 'world' at the same time that it forces readers to account for multiple perspectives, including our own." Gross then issues the chapter's explicit Ch.1→Ch.4 cross-reference: "That's one function of negation as we will see in Austen as well: the rhetorical situation including the reader is, in this case, implied."

The Austen bridge passes through Laurence Horn's *A Natural History of Negation* (Chicago 1989; cited Stanford 2001), a "neo-Gricean masterpiece" that pays close attention to the asymmetry between logical and rhetorical negation. The case: "What should we do without double negatives in Jane Austen's *Emma*, for instance, where Harriet can stand in 'no unhappy reverie' completely different from the logically equivalent 'happy reverie' that would lose the implication of surrounding characters, narrator, author, and reader?" The exact passage from *Emma* (Kinsley/Pinch Oxford 2003, p. 321): "Harriet, who had been standing in no unhappy reverie, was yet very glad to be called from it, by the now encouraging manner of such a judge, and such a friend as Miss Woodhouse." Horn would no doubt direct attention to "the traditionally suspect rhetorical figure litotes, which is a form of understatement where an affirmative is expressed by the negative of the contrary" (Horn 303). Pope (under a pseudonym) saw in litotes "the peculiar Talent … of Ladies, Whisperers, and Backbiters" (Horn 304). Just as hyperbole, per Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, "is a figure fit for young men of quality, and ellipsis the favored figure for politicians" — Gross's index entry on "Aristotle … on hyperbole, 47" anchors here — litotes invokes a Q-based implicature flagging stereotype-exceeding situations. The Maxim of Manner (Grice: be perspicuous, avoid obscurity and ambiguity, strive for brevity and order) is being flouted to signal "you are getting at something else altogether."

Austen's double negatives, accordingly, "render critical situations that refer to, but exceed a stereotype: in this case a happy reverie that would leave out the 'judge' and 'friend' Emma Woodhouse as well as the narrator who suggests indirectly the two descriptions are mutually exclusive." (Since when is a judge your friend or vice versa? Not recently, Gross drily observes.) The "cultural background etc.," to use Dreyfus's term, "needs to be rendered in detail that exceeds the capacity of philosophical pragmatics narrowly conceived."

## 14. Background contra Searle/Dreyfus, and "uncomfortable situatedness" (PDF 58–59 / bk 48–49)

Here the chapter delivers the volume's titular thesis in compressed form. "We are not talking about the unarticulated 'background of intentionality' à la John Searle, or differently Hubert Dreyfus, which set the stage inadequately if we use Horn's model to look at examples from literature." Dreyfus is right that the gestalt causing an action "is in no way represented when it comes to an expert tennis swing or a master chess move" (Dreyfus 29, 32). Likewise literature does not represent the gestalt causing an action. "But the quality of nonrepresentation across these examples differs significantly. In the examples from Dreyfus, nonrepresentation is an afterthought when we comport ourselves normally. In the example from Austen as interpretable through Horn, nonrepresentation is the result of *flouting a conversational norm*, which is something we do all the time." Literature is "especially suited for this kind of abnormality because it takes definitive shape in unnatural styles, unnecessary length, and undue complexity."

The titular concept now crystallizes: "Cognition in this scene is never comfortably embedded in the scenery (as some theorists might imagine) nor is it detached (as other theorists might imagine): it is *practically situated but uncomfortably so*, which points us to another order of the emotional phenomenon where the author and reader appear on the scene." The character Yorick's "terrible discomfort" is pseudo-resolved diegetically — told from a first-person perspective — which produces "second-order discomfort in a reader who alone can register the irony of this scene that beautifully 'models' a social world where class makes all the difference down to the most minute details of perception, action, emotion, and cognition." (Henry Mackenzie's *The Man of Feeling* and Fielding's *David Simple* are named as parallels, each implicating "a world turned upside down by men who feel.")

Gross restates the thesis at high abstraction: "The mind is never comfortably embedded in the scenery (*pace* EM theory) nor is it detached (*pace* certain forms of CAL): it is practically situated but uncomfortably so." Emotional phenomena "do not announce themselves unequivocally. Indeterminacy (but not radical indeterminacy) must be in-built for the phenomenon to emerge as emotional in the first place."

## 15. Roughgarden's biology of indeterminacy + Fig. 1.9 (PDF 59–61 / bk 49–51)

The chapter closes by deflecting the inevitable evolutionary-biology objection. Wouldn't unequivocal emotion-display be adaptive? Wouldn't Vulcan mind-meld eliminate the need for emotions altogether, "as the creators of *Star Trek* so cleverly realized"? Isn't emotional indeterminacy maladaptive? Gross turns to Stanford biologist Joan Roughgarden's *Evolution's Rainbow: Diversity, Gender, and Sexuality in Nature and People* (UC Press 2009 [2004]). Instead of selecting sexually for ideal types, a species needs "a balanced portfolio" of genes to survive over the long term (Roughgarden 5), and sex — entailing "a very wide (but not indefinite) range of behaviors" (177) — is the social activity that rebalances the portfolio under dynamic environmental pressure (311). "Antisocial eugenics or cloning are Roughgarden's counterexamples; just like the computer scientist knows that focusing only on the code while ignoring the execution environment is a mistake, cloning biologists who focus on the nucleus of the cell while ignoring the cytoplasm make the same mistake insofar as they have ceased to work ecologically." Semiotic indeterminacy of the sign "is compatible with biodiversity insofar as it constitutes the social."

Figure 1.9 is the chapter's closing exhibit: Darwin's "weeping children" plate — best known as Ginx's Baby. Phillip Prodger, in *Darwin's Camera: Art and Photography in the Theory of Evolution* (Oxford 2009), shows that this image, though considered a technical *tour de force*, "was not a true photograph but a drawing made to look like an original photograph" (Prodger 125); Rejlander added details like a large padded chair to convey "a tangible moment in everyday life readers could mentally verify, and with which they could sympathize." The infant might be hungry, sleepy, gassy. Why aren't we "permanently sequestered in utero so that all of these communication problems might be avoided in the first place?" Because, "in the spirit of Roughgarden we might observe the semiotic indeterminacy of emotional expression is a corollate of biodiversity precisely because it necessitates communication; the work of triangulating is never done nor should it be."

The chapter's last paragraph delivers the disciplinary thesis: "We need the arts and literature to study emotion because they transpire on the appropriate phenomenological level where experiences happen, on the one hand, and where experience is characterized, on the other. Just as we cannot understand bird flight by studying only feathers … we cannot understand emotional experience by studying only the face, the eyeball, the ear, the brain" (Noé, 157). Experience needs "people in situations," subject to "maximum manipulation and critical scrutiny" so that "complexities of experience that shimmer just beyond our immediate grasp" register through tropes like irony — tropes "that help define the humanities. As Darwin understood over a century ago we need the arts and humanities for many reasons, including for the sake of good science that can account for both the basic ways we are, and the ways we can be."
