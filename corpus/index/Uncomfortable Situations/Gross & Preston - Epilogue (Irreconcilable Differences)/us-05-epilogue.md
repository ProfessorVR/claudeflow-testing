# Phase 2 Narrative — US-05-EPILOGUE: "Irreconcilable Differences?"

**Volume**: Daniel M. Gross, *Uncomfortable Situations: Emotion between Science and the Humanities* (Chicago 2017), Epilogue (book pp. 129–146; PDF 139–156).
**Co-author**: Stephanie D. Preston (University of Michigan; behavioral neuroscience).
**Earlier publication**: an earlier version appeared in Frank Biess + Daniel M. Gross (eds.), *Science and Emotion after 1945: A Transatlantic Perspective* (Chicago 2014).

---

## 1. The Snow framing and the two dominant responses (PDF 139–140 / book 129–130)

The Epilogue opens at the threshold of the volume's titular cross-disciplinary brief by invoking C. P. Snow's 1959 Rede Lecture "The Two Cultures" as the operative framing. Gross and Preston jointly state the impasse: the sciences strive "to maintain clean, consistent, results by controlling the environments in which phenomena are studied," while the humanities embrace "natural complexities in the world, including culturally specific frames of reference that shape any particular study" (PDF 139). This co-authored headnote is important methodologically: by stating the divide in two parallel clauses that each name only one virtue per side, the authors already begin to expose Snow's dichotomy as an analytic fiction. Each side has its own intelligibility but neither is self-sufficient for the study of emotion — a phenomenon that, the authors emphasize *jointly*, is "a fundamental human phenomenon that should be available to laboratory study like anything else that transpires in our material lives; at the same time, it seems defined by complexities that disappear in the controlled environment of the laboratory."

The post-1980s "emotional turn" — Gross's framing, drawing on the affect-theory and history-of-emotions literatures he has worked in — has been shaped by this problem ever since the rise of neuroimaging. From this premise, two typical responses follow. The first, attributed primarily to Gross's diagnosis of the methodological landscape, is **incommensurability**: emotion-science in the Pleistocene-evolutionary register cannot be brought into comparison with empathy-history that runs from German *Einfühlung* (1873) to its post-Holocaust "exhaustion" (Carolyn J. Dean). Scarantino and Griffiths represent the most respectable incommensurability variant — their proposal that there is a "Folk Emotion Project" (how people actually categorize emotions) distinct from a "Scientific Emotion Project" (how emotions should be categorized for scientific purposes, with subscripted folk-categories like "anger_B"). The second response, **consilience** (E. O. Wilson's banner), rejects incommensurability and insists every human phenomenon must be explicable in natural-scientific terms. Steven Pinker exemplifies this move: novelistic emotion in Austen's *Sense and Sensibility* (the very text of Gross's Chapter 4) "should be read in terms of evolutionary psychology that includes mating and sex differences, parent-offspring conflict, sibling rivalry, self-deception, taboo, coalitional psychology, and what he calls, following Robert Trivers, the 'moral emotions,'" supposed to modulate reciprocity for reproductive success (PDF 140).

Crucially, **Gross & Preston jointly refuse the third-response slot**. They write: "Instead of proposing a third response to the two-culture problem, we offer in this essay a critique that points to its **dissolution** in the study of emotion." This is the volume's signature methodological move and it must be attributed to the *joint* authorship: it is a methodological wager that the rhetorician-and-neuroscientist team itself enacts.

## 2. The eight limits of laboratory science of emotion (PDF 141–147 / book 131–137)

Under the heading **"Limits of Laboratory Science of Emotion,"** the authors stage an eight-point critique of contemporary brain-imaging research. The technical depth and confidence of this section make Preston's primary authorship unmistakable, while the framing and Equiano callback are Gross's.

**(1) Technological limitations.** Preston catalogs the dominant tools — fMRI, PET, TMS, EEG/ERP/MEG — and stresses that these techniques "require subjects to sit or lie completely still during the recording, inside of or attached to machines that are cumbersome and sometimes also loud (especially fMRI, which is the most widely used)" (PDF 141). The few-second stimulus durations, the visual-slide / audio / finger-movement protocol restrictions, and the typical reduction of "value" to a money-based dependent measure all combine to make ecological designs nearly impossible.

**Gross's Equiano callback intervenes here** (PDF 142): even if surface-electrode or near-infrared technologies eventually loosen the immobility constraint, they will only "access better a momentary ecology involving the intentional subject, but not the more durable affordances like social stratification that render the environment more legible to history: an example from chapter 2 of this book is the 'happy' situation of the then-slave Equiano." This is the only place in the eight-point critique where Gross's volume-internal anchors appear, and it sets up the rhetorical-situational reframing that will follow.

**(2) Money and the limitation of a utilitarian assumption.** Preston elaborates the monetary-incentivization problem: people do not actually value money linearly or in the same way across individuals (citing Rick, Cryder, and Loewenstein 2008; Vohs, Mead, and Goode 2006). For altruism and empathy especially, "money is known to produce a mindset that specifically limits one's prosocial motives" (PDF 142). Lab-monetary decisions therefore do not generalize to real-world prosociality; Preston insists on supplementing scanner studies with "ecological tasks" like helping someone pick up dropped papers or responding to a baby's cry — citing her own (and Preston-de Waal) altruism research (n.16).

**(3) Professional journal impact factor.** A pressure-vector that distorts inferential management: an accurate-but-detailed title like "The temporal-parietal junction (TPJ) appears to be involved during theory of mind (ToM) processing, but maybe only because the task requires tracking movement in space over time" will not attract editorial interest. A simpler-and-less-accurate version — "Brain region found for the unique human ability to read minds" — wins citation contests but degrades the inferential basis. This is Preston's professional-cultural critique of her own field.

**(4) Mass media attention.** Compounds (3): supplementary personal income (speaking events, book contracts) tracks public attention, which rewards "simplicity and generalization that easily explains key problems of everyday experience such as how we feel, and how we act toward others" (PDF 143). Three recent data-fabrication scandals exemplify the resulting vulnerability — Preston cites Yong and Simonsohn's 2012 *Nature* "Data Detective" piece (n.17).

**(5) Localization.** The most consequential limit for the volume's argument. Preston critiques the oversimplified "social brain" (Frith 2007), "empathy circuit" (Baron-Cohen 2011), and "self-related regions" shorthands. Each brain region "is adapted for particular functions — such as tracking visual motion in space — which is presumably mobilized for any task that requires that function including motor acts, person perception, and imagining the deceptions of another" (PDF 143). The result: "it is all but irresponsible to ever infer that a cell or neural region is 'for' a specific psychological task." A Preston-internal critique of localization rhetoric.

Gross's voice surfaces briefly via a historico-political hypothesis (PDF 144): when empathy or racial hatred or self can be localized in a particular brain region, "prospects for intervention are in some ways easier to imagine whether that means a therapeutic cure or, alternatively, resignation in the face of biology. At the same time, it can become harder to imagine social explanations and institutional responses that seem more difficult to implement and attributable to social welfare frameworks that lost much of their appeal in the Anglo-American 1980s." This is plainly a humanities-side reflection on the political economy of brain-localization rhetoric and reads as Gross's contribution within the jointly authored section.

**(6) The limits of professional expertise.** Preston's most elegant illustration: the angular gyrus is variously construed as the arithmetic area (Menon 2000; Dehaene et al. 1999), the metaphor area (Ramachandran + Hubbard 2003), the out-of-body experience area (Blanke et al. 2002), the cross-modal-integration area (Bonda et al. 1995; Calvert et al. 2000), or the "self-relevant" area, "depending on the specialty and task of the researcher and the brain hemisphere being studied" (PDF 144). Single-discipline reading habits prevent triangulation.

**(7) Reverse inference limitations.** Preston deploys Russell Poldrack's canonical 2006 critique: inferring that a current task's activation of a brain area indexes the same cognitive process previously associated with that area is suspect. The DLPFC working-memory / executive-control example is followed by the Theory-of-Mind "Sally" task setup and its movement-tracking confound; the functional-localizer remedy (subtracting a ball-tracking task) is described and acknowledged as expensive and methodologically demanding. Preston then introduces a striking case from her own laboratory: a recent study found insula activity associated with the decision to *acquire* the most valuable goods (Wang et al. 2012), which was unexpected because the hypothesis predicted insula activation during the relinquishment of wanted goods. Confirmatory inference, drawing on extensive prior literature, would be productive but is foreclosed by the reverse-inference ban. This is a Preston self-reflexive example: her lab is implicated in the very methodological constraints it critiques.

**(8) Technocratic hierarchy.** Preston closes the section with the cost figures: ~$600 per subject, sample sizes of 20–30 per task, "a minimum of $12,000 per experiment and usually over $100,000 due to the ancillary costs of hiring a trained full-time staff that run and analyze imaging studies" (PDF 147). The high-funding labs that can afford this infrastructure are precisely the labs least likely to use the simpler techniques and logical inferences that nonetheless "can produce useable information that moves the field forward if done responsibly." This eighth point reads simultaneously as Preston's discipline-internal political economy critique and as joint setup for the alternative phenomenological-ecological tradition the next section will deploy.

## 3. Fear Situated: the Phelps amygdala study in detail (PDF 147–152 / book 137–142)

Under **"Fear Situated"** the authors pivot to a positive alternative: a phenomenological-ecological tradition (Wittgenstein, Vygotsky, Heidegger via Martin's phenomenological lineage, and J. J. Gibson's ecological perception) that can meet the professional expectations of both neuroscience and the interpretive humanities. Here the authors articulate their **central historical claim**, which must be attributed jointly: "the topics of laboratory work on the emotional brain coincide meaningfully with particular socio-historical concerns that show up at the margins of experiment design and interpretation" (PDF 147). Fear-research in the Phelps and Barsalou labs aligns with post-war anti-racism; empathy-research in the Preston lab aligns with post-war humanitarianism. The thesis is sharper than the "emotional topics happen to track political moments" cliché: the topics *coincide meaningfully* — Snow's controlled-laboratory tradition is already shaped by the same socio-historical pressures the humanities side studies explicitly.

The Phelps study (PDF 147–150) anchors the chapter's most detailed methodological case. Elizabeth A. Phelps and colleagues, "Performance on Indirect Measures of Race Evaluation Predicts Amygdala Activation," *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience* 12.5 (2000): 729–738, used fMRI to examine the neural response of "White American" subjects viewing images of African Americans and White Americans. The study's stated motivation, which Phelps's team identifies as the empirical puzzle, was the gap between a steady decline in *self-reported* racial prejudice and persistent *unconscious* racial prejudice directed at Black people (p. 729). The amygdala was selected as the region of interest because it is implicated in emotional learning especially in relation to fear, memory, and evaluation (p. 729).

Two experiments structure the study:

- **Experiment 1** used unfamiliar Black/White male faces with "neutral" expressions (PDF 148). Phelps's team measured (a) Modern Racism Scale (MRS) scores indexing conscious, self-reported beliefs and attitudes toward Black Americans (p. 736); (b) unconscious measures including startle response and bias; and (c) fMRI-derived amygdala activity. The headline finding: differential amygdala activation correlated significantly with the *unconscious* measures of race evaluation, but **not** with the conscious self-report (MRS) scores. The amygdala registers what the questionnaire cannot.

- **Experiment 2** substituted "famous and positively regarded" Black and White individuals (p. 730). Gross's narrative captures the full stimulus list, which functions almost as a roster of US public memory ca. 2000: **Arsenio Hall, Bill Cosby, Magic Johnson, Martin Luther King Jr., and Colin Powell** on the Black side; **Conan O'Brien, Tom Cruise, Larry Bird, John F. Kennedy, and Norman Schwarzkopf** on the White side (p. 730, 736). The Experiment 1 pattern — differential amygdala activity for Black vs. White faces — **was not reproduced** under the famous-and-positively-regarded condition (p. 734). Phelps's team interprets the contrast as evidence that "amygdala activation as well as behavioral responses of race bias are reflections of social learning within a specific culture at a particular moment in the *history of relations between social groups*" (p. 734, italics in original on the term *history of relations*, emphasis flagged by Gross + Preston).

It is here that Gross's rhetorical-situational reframing enters with full force, and the attribution to Gross individually is unmistakable even though the prose is in the first-person plural. The argument unfolds in three moves:

**First**, Gross points out that Phelps herself qualifies — "these data cannot speak to the issues of causality" (p. 734) — but simultaneously gestures toward "the history of relations between social groups." The disconnect is structural: "the protocol for connecting the two — let us call it a mediating field of socio-historical phenomena — is not sufficiently articulated to shape the laboratory work and its interpretation" (PDF 149). Phelps stops at the threshold of the two-cultures divide; the bridge is precisely what Phelps's methodology cannot supply.

**Second**, Gross supplies that bridge via critical race theory and rhetorical studies. The signal sentence (PDF 149) — and the Tier-1 attribution to Gross — is unequivocal: "**Racism is not located primarily in the brain. It is located primarily in the racist institutions — like US race-based slavery and subsequent Jim Crow laws — that exceed any particular subject and can persist without any particular kind of incorporation or brain state.**" Both halves of the equation are necessary: without physiology, declining-racism narratives might be overstated; without history, physiological correlates have no object. A "more robust brain science of emotion" — here, racialized fear — "would take seriously in design and interpretation the 'history of race relations' invoked incidentally by Phelps."

**Third**, Gross deconstructs the Experiment 2 stimulus design. "Familiarity" in the famous-and-positively-regarded condition equivocates between face-to-face acquaintance and **televisual / capital mediation**: an Arsenio Hall face seen by a White subject only "in the context of television and accompanying media" is not the familiarity of Equiano's Chapter-2 "familiarity" with his slavemaster Henry Pascal. In the slavery case, Gross reminds us, familiarity "did not mitigate racism." Phelps may, on Gross's diagnosis, be correlating **celebrity** with unconscious racial attitudes rather than face-to-face familiarity per se. Further: fame, achievement, and longevity are "unevenly distributed across Black and White populations," so a famous Black face carries markedness into the laboratory that a "supposedly equivalent" famous White face does not. The MLK Jr. / JFK pairing — "roughly equivalent" in fame and age — is itself a rhetorical-political claim about US public memory that the experimental design treats as a methodological given.

The Barsalou intervention follows (PDF 150–151). Wilson-Mendenhall, Barsalou, and colleagues' "Grounding Emotion in Situated Conceptualization" (2011) critiques the Ekman basic-emotion approach — emotions as inborn instincts triggered in stereotyped fashion (snake → "fear circuit," at 1105) — and replaces it with situated conceptualization. Emotions become concepts that "refer to entire situations" representing "settings, agents, objects, actions, events, interoceptions, and mentalizings" (1107). The runner-on-a-wooden-trail-at-dusk *fear* differs from the unprepared-presenter *fear*; the latter activates left dorsolateral prefrontal + inferior frontal gyrus consistent with executive demand (1124). This is a significant advance — but Gross diagnoses its limit (PDF 151) as the Barsalou-elliptical-figures problem: "Barsalou's modular, functionalist focus on situated conceptualization *is* forced to rely upon elliptical figures ('and so forth,' 'and many others') to articulate a background that recedes indefinitely into what we would consider *non*-conceptual affordances located in the social environment, including the social environment like Phelps's 'history of race relations.'" Racialized fear is *built into a social situation* that affects people directly without requiring a persistent conceptual representation. This is the more radical move within the volume's "uncomfortable situation" / "negative affordance" theoretical apparatus.

Griffiths and Scarantino's "emotions in the wild" project (PDF 151) is the closer-to-Gross alternative. Anger in a marital quarrel, embarrassment while delivering a song to an audience: environment "represented in terms of what it affords the emoter in the way of skillful engagement with it" (441). They consider synchronic scaffolding (Vygotskian: confessionals in churches enable a kind of emotional performance) and diachronic scaffolding (broader Catholic culture supports the *development* of the ability to engage in the emotions of confession, 443), plus "emotional capital" (the emotional resources associated with a given social status, gender, disability, 444). This is the model Gross + Preston offer as a way forward: what would a science of emotion look like if laboratory, ethnographic, and historical considerations were genuinely integrated? Their answer: the Preston Ecological Neuroscience Lab.

## 4. Empathy Situated: Preston's empathy/apathy/reticence triad (PDF 152–154 / book 142–144)

The **"Empathy Situated"** section is Preston's strongest signature contribution to the joint chapter, with Gross weaving the humanistic comparison through Carolyn J. Dean's reticence archive. Preston opens with the standard mirror-neuron account (Iacoboni 2008): empathy as non-cognitive, trans-primate, fired by simply observing an action. She then exposes its circularity — "empathy is everything that involves mirror-neuron activity, and all mirror-neuron activity is by definition empathic" — without any explanation of why empathy appears in some suffering circumstances but not others, even with the same objective conditions.

The Preston-Hofelich hospital-patient video study (PDF 152), drawing on Alicia J. Hofelich's 2012 University of Michigan dissertation, anchors the empirical case. Real hospital patients with serious or terminal illness discuss their experiences on video; observers (the experimental subjects) watch the videos and report empathic responses and desire-to-help. The patient sample varies widely along an emotional spectrum, from "distraught crying throughout" to "laconic resistance to talk" to "resilient sadness potentiated by affiliation." Observer reactions vary even more widely. Two findings break the mirror-neuron circle and ground Preston's signature **empathy / apathy / reticence triad**:

- Most observers empathize with the most distraught patients, but **observers experiencing their own intense negative affect due to depression report less sympathy** for distraught patients (PDF 152). The mirror-neuron account predicts pain-triggers-pain in a linear fashion; depression breaks this prediction. Empathy is context-sensitive — it is constrained by the observer's own affective economy.

- Observers who report regularly "taking another's perspective" in daily life report **greater sympathy for the reticent patient** — the patient who is less demonstrative but appears to have deep emotions and need (PDF 152). Disposition (perspective-taking habit) interacts with stimulus (reticence) in non-linear ways.

The third term — **reticence** — is the chapter's most consequential conceptual innovation. Preston's hospital-study reticence stands alongside Gross's Dean-derived reticence in a deliberate contrast that opens, rather than closes, methodological inquiry.

Gross's contribution within this section is the Carolyn J. Dean material (PDF 153–154). In "Erasures: Writing History about Holocaust Trauma" (in Biess + Gross, *Science and Emotion after 1945*, 2014), Dean tracks "reticence" as it became "an important quality in the recent production of empathy, or compassion, in one crucial post-war emotional domain: Holocaust victimization." As a result of false-victim controversies and the surrounding cultural melodrama, "reticence has become a key marker of authentic suffering and hence a minimum requirement for empathic or compassionate response in the case of survivor stories" (PDF 153). Dean's broader formulation, which Gross quotes (PDF 153): "The preference for minimalist style in victim testimonies… is but one component of a much broader discourse about the exemplary victim. Over and over, the presumptive cultural demands of false victims… trump any inquiry into how some victims are deemed more credible than others since we are presumed to know that 'real' survivors would by definition be reluctant to seek attention. Reticence thus distinguishes real and false victims" (396).

The disanalogy Gross + Preston then jointly draw out (PDF 153) is structurally important: in the Preston-lab hospital study, **the reticent patients receive on average the LEAST sympathy** — distraught patients receive the most help. In Dean's Holocaust-survivor archive, reticence is the marker of **MAXIMAL credibility** — the loud or attention-seeking are by definition suspect. The same surface term, "reticence," cannot do equivalent emotional work across these two cultural-historical situations. The contrast itself is the methodological point: empathy research must be located in particular historical-cultural situations where the meanings of its key terms cannot be presumed transparent.

Preston extends the analysis (PDF 154) with the **gender** axis: in the hospital study, "gender expectations play a key role in differentiating the extent to which people want to help the distraught versus the resilient patients," even though all observers agree the distraught need more help. A gender-neutral hospital study would have to distort the apathy-empathy phenomenon. Gross then introduces **"anxiety of transmission"** in the historians-and-audience reception of survivor's tales (Dean), and ties the section to a broader **"humanities construction of dignity"** — how victims of "systematic oppression or 'natural' circumstances beyond one's control are constituted, along with the agencies that are supposed to respond" (PDF 154). At the very least, Preston's empathy/apathy/reticence study "suggests how it is not necessarily impractical to consider history and situation when studying empathy scientifically, and the hope is that such considerations enhance the quality of the conclusions."

## 5. Summary and Conclusion: the joint methodological wager (PDF 154–155 / book 144–145)

The closing **"Summary and Conclusion"** is shared-voice but the conceptual armature is Gross's. The two responses to the two-cultures impasse — explained-by-incommensurability vs. *addressed*-by-consilience (in the "reductive tradition of E. O. Wilson") — are summarized one last time and refused as inadequate. The authors instead propose that ecological experiments within neuroscience can yield genuinely new "brain-body-world dynamics" insights, especially as those dynamics are implicated in socio-historical phenomena.

A methodological **treaty** follows. Scientists must construct experimental protocols that are simultaneously **ecological**, **situated**, and **statistically powerful** within the constraints of existing technology — a difficult triple that the eight-point Limits section has shown to be a stretch but not impossible. Humanists, in turn, must refuse the **all-or-none proposition**: humanities topics like racial hatred, empathy, or the self are not either *completely* available to experimental protocol or *completely* unavailable. Both sides must give up their respective methodological purisms.

The volume's titular question receives its answer (PDF 155): "We hope that this essay, written by a rhetorician and a neuroscientist, can serve as an example of two-cultures collaboration at its early stages. In situated cognition we identify an approach that invites such collaboration." And finally: "Ultimately the two-cultures divide dissolves when shared phenomena like human emotions are treated with the methodological diversity and cross-disciplinary conversation their complexity deserves." This is the qualified "No" answer to Snow — *no*, the differences are not irreconcilable, but reconciliation requires both the rhetorical-situational reframing of neuroscience objects (Gross's contribution) and the rigorous experimental enactment of situated empathy/apathy/reticence (Preston's contribution).

## 6. Joint methodological reflection on the volume

The Epilogue closes the volume by enacting the methodology the prior four chapters presupposed but did not name as such. Chapter 1's Darwin-as-rhetorician reframing, Chapter 2's "bearing-up" reading of Equiano against pragmatic phenomenology, Chapter 3's Gibsonian "hostile environment" theory of Radcliffe's Gothic affordances, and Chapter 4's "mixed feelings" reading of Austen against the Galenic disequilibrium model — all four have been rehearsals of rhetorical-situational analysis applied to canonical primary texts. The Epilogue extends the same operation to a contemporary fMRI laboratory finding. The continuity is methodological: the Phelps amygdala study is treated, by Gross, the way Equiano's "happy" situation is treated in Chapter 2 — as a phenomenon whose intelligibility requires both physiological description AND the historical-institutional analysis (here, US race-based slavery; there, transatlantic slave-trade affect) that exceeds any particular subject. Preston's side enacts the converse complement: she shows from inside the laboratory that empathy is irreducibly situational without ever abandoning fMRI / behavioral methodology.

The earlier publication of the Epilogue in Biess + Gross's *Science and Emotion after 1945* (2014) is a useful biographical-bibliographic anchor: this collaboration began before *Uncomfortable Situations* was conceived as a unified monograph, and Preston is named in the Acknowledgments as the colleague who met Gross at the 2003 University of Iowa "Promise of Empathy" conference. The volume is in this sense a single-author monograph framed by a joint-authored capstone — the same methodological-collaborative gesture executed inside the book that the book theorizes about emotion.

## 7. Cross-pipeline bridges

**To HR-01-GROSS (2005).** The Epilogue's "rhetorical-situational reframing of neuroscience objects" presupposes the "rhetorical ontology" thesis Gross worked out in 2005. The Phelps-study analysis is a direct application of rhetorical-ontology to a neuroscientific object: amygdala activation correlates with race-evaluation but the *referent* of "racism" is institutional, not cerebral. Lightweight bridge edges only, per user directive.

**To Rickert *Ambient Rhetoric*.** Rickert is acknowledged elsewhere in the volume, and the situated-cognition framework here is methodologically isomorphic to Rickert's ambient-rhetoric extension of kairos and rhetorical situation beyond Bitzer-style proximate-situation models. Griffiths + Scarantino's "synchronic scaffolding" (confessional in church) and "diachronic scaffolding" (broader Catholic culture) are explicit cousins of Rickert's ambient-attunement.

**To Aristotelian rhetoric-of-emotion (Rhetoric II / BCAP heritage).** Less directly anchored in the Epilogue body than elsewhere in the volume; the bridge is through Gross's underlying claim that emotion is always already rhetorical-situational — a thesis Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II first systematizes.

**Bridges NOT pursued.** No Heidegger BCAP citation in the Epilogue body (Heidegger is named once via the phenomenological-tradition lineage at PDF 147, but only as a name in a list). No Uexküll *Foray*. No BT.
