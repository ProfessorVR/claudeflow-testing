# US-00-INTRO — Daniel M. Gross, "Uncomfortable Situations" (Introduction, *Uncomfortable Situations*, Chicago 2017, pp. 1-27 / PDF 11-37)

## § Overview

Gross's Introduction to *Uncomfortable Situations* operates as a hinge between his 2006 *Secret History of Emotion: From Aristotle's Rhetoric to Modern Brain Science* and the four chapter-and-epilogue case studies that follow. Where the 2006 book was polemical — defending a rhetorical genealogy of emotion against the imperial reach of "brain science of emotion" — the 2017 Introduction explicitly recants its earlier refusal of cross-disciplinary common ground. The intervening decade, Gross argues, has produced situated alternatives within cognitive science itself (notably the Barsalou-lab "situated conceptualization" program associated with Wilson-Mendenhall, Barrett, Simmons, and Barsalou, *Neuropsychologia* 49.5 [2011]: 1105-1127, at p. 1110 — the book's very first footnote, PDF p. 11) that now permit a more productive humanities-science conversation. The price of entry, Gross contends, is methodological: a "rhetoric of emotion" that treats emotional phenomena as transactional and audience-oriented; that replaces the tired binary of "hardwired versus socially constructed" with a phenomenology of emotional types; and that recovers eighteenth-century sentimental literature as the optimal research domain for studying the *uncomfortable situation* — the load-bearing category that gives the book its title.

The Introduction proceeds through four inline sub-headed movements (the book's only such structuring device — chapters proper run as continuous prose): an opening on the dinner-party scenario from cognitive science (pp. 1-7); a Sterne set-piece (pp. 7-9); a seven-mode typology of the rhetoric of emotion (pp. 9-14); a critique of consoling situated-cognition metaphors (pp. 14-17); and a long chapter-and-epilogue forecast (pp. 17-27) that previews every body unit of the book.

## § Argument arc

The Introduction opens, characteristically, with a passage from "the cognitive science literature" rather than a literary primary text (PDF p. 11, book p. 1): a dinner-party scenario in which the reader defends an unpopular view, is met with embarrassed silence, and feels her chest tighten. The passage is taken from Wilson-Mendenhall et al. 2011, the Barsalou lab's defense of "situated conceptualization" against the basic-emotions program of Paul Ekman. Gross's first move is to confess that even on rereading the scenario discomforts him — and then to crowd it with multiplying possibilities (anger at oneself, isolation fear, pride, indignation, sadness) so dense that "basic emotions slip away as we stumble over voice: appearing to whom?" (PDF p. 11, book p. 1). The Austen subtext is already in play: Lady Russell in *Persuasion* (Oxford ed. p. 198, note 2) "must learn to feel that she had been mistaken" about Anne's suitors — emotions retroactively revised under audience pressure.

Lauren Berlant's *Cruel Optimism* (Duke 2011, p. 5, note 3) supplies the foil against which Gross stakes his alternative definition of *situation*. Where Berlant points to "We have a situation here" — the situation comedy, the police procedural — as the lived form of the "state of things in which something that will perhaps matter is unfolding amidst the usual activity of life," Gross's emphasis falls instead on "emotional instability defined rhetorically" (PDF p. 12, book p. 2). Sentimental literature, not television, is announced as the optimal research domain. The Introduction then engages James Chandler's *An Archaeology of Sympathy: The Sentimental Mode in Literature and Cinema* (Chicago 2013, note 5; with Steven Goldsmith's *Blake's Agitation* cited in extended quotation at note 6) — only to redirect Chandler's spectator-focused archaeology toward a "critical history of well-being" pivoted on the eighteenth-century sentimental novel (PDF p. 13, book p. 3). The argument here is that Chandler's Smith-to-Sterne-to-Capra arc, which culminates in "Adam Smith Goes to Hollywood," looks too readily through the spectator and away from the situation as such.

The third movement of the opening (PDF pp. 13-17, book pp. 3-7) narrates the methodological journey from *The Secret History of Emotion*. Gross retrospectively names that book's three objectives — to show that new brain science does not account for situated phenomena like the public mourning of Princess Diana, to recover the post-Aristotelian humanistic tradition that humanists such as Martha Nussbaum and Richard Sorabji had inadvertently neglected, and to genealogize early-modern brain science as the displacement of that tradition. Challenged with "Are you anti-science?" and "Can't there be a worthwhile science of emotion?" Gross now answers yes — and proceeds to identify Paul Griffiths and Andrea Scarantino's "Emotions in the Wild" (in *The Cambridge Handbook of Situated Cognition*, 2009, note 7) as the bridge text: wedding ceremonies, emotional capital, emotional affordances, transactional sulking. To this transactional cognitive science Gross adds two literary primaries: Sarah Fielding's *The Adventures of David Simple* (1744, ed. Sabor 1998, note 9), where French romances furnish the "cultural scaffolding" against which Fielding writes; and Lawrence Stone's *Family, Sex and Marriage in England 1500-1800* (1977, note 11), supplemented by William Reddy's *The Making of Romantic Love* (Chicago 2012, note 13). Reddy is load-bearing: his account of "I love you" as a performative emotive — an utterance that instantiates rather than describes — supplies one of the typology-modes to come, and his blunt summary that "there is, therefore, nothing in the latest neuroscience research on sexual desire, sexual arousal, or romantic love that permits one to conclude these states are caused or orchestrated by hard-wired brain systems" (Reddy p. 15, quoted PDF p. 16) consolidates the convergence between humanities-side history of emotion and the situated wing of cognitive science.

The Sterne set-piece (PDF pp. 17-19, book pp. 7-9), under the inline sub-heading **A Sentimental Journey**, is the Introduction's pivot. Gross reads at length the Yorick-and-the-lady hand-grasping scene from Sterne's *A Sentimental Journey through France and Italy* (1768; Ian Jack ed. 1968, pp. 18-19, note 14) and decomposes it into four numbered beats: Yorick grasps the lady's hand and remarks on the fortunate situation; the lady replies that his very reflection has ruined it and disengages her hand; Yorick records his shame at the loss; the lady touches his coat-cuff and the situation is regained, "God knows how." A footnote-supplement from *Tristram Shandy* (Ross ed. 2009, p. 475, note 14) — "love, you see, is not so much a SENTIMENT as a SITUATION" — donates the coinage. The lesson Gross extracts is that sentiment appears only through its negation: in Sterne, semantic negation and theological negation work together as the rhetorical engine of the sentimental, a maneuver Gross signals will return as a chapter-1 anchor for the Darwin reading.

Under the sub-heading **The Rhetoric of Emotion** (PDF pp. 19-23, book pp. 9-13) Gross announces the methodological replacement of "Emotions: hardwired or socially constructed?" with a "phenomenology of emotion … which takes shape around certain types of emotional transactions" — and then, against the false binary, supplies a seven-mode typology that runs as the Introduction's most-cited apparatus:

1. **Mimetic** — Ngai's "literary strategy of sympathy" (*Ugly Feelings* pp. 81-82, note 16): tears producing tears, terror producing terror, with mirror-neuron and emotion-recognition analogs in the natural sciences, biologically adaptive for kin alignment with one's infant.
2. **Cathartic** — Aristotle's *Poetics*: Oedipus's suffering producing pleasant relief in the spectator.
3. **Projective** — Ngai again: the subject's appraisal-emotion treated as if a property of the work itself; in aesthetic history Lipps's *Einfühlung* (feeling-into). *Mysteries of Udolpho* is not boring; I am bored.
4. **Prosthetic** — the object or scene extends our emotional range. The sublime, the Passion of Christ via 1 Peter 3:14 ("But and if ye suffer for righteousness' sake, happy are ye"). Note 17 credits Susan Miller's *Trust in Texts* (p. 14) and Cynthia Haynes's "pathos@play.prosthetic.emotion" (*Works and Days* 13.1-2 [1995]: 261-276) for the term, along with Libby Catchings's dissertation.
5. **Performative** — Reddy's "I love you" instantiates emotion unpredictably.
6. **Voluntary** — Philip Fisher's *The Vehement Passions* (Princeton 2002, pp. 142, 144, note 18): a "blank spot" where reader or spectator "volunteers passion" to supply what the character does not feel; Darcy's shamelessness as solicitation of reader-shame.
7. **Systematic** — the most important mode for Gross's book. Emotions appear only as parts of systems — Hobbes's political economy of fear and vainglory unevenly distributed across the corporate body; or Sterne's *Sentimental Journey* where object-and-theory are self-co-constitutive. Systematic considerations license the analysis of emotions that are *excluded* or rendered unavailable.

The remainder of the sub-section parses the affect/emotion controversy. Historical usage permits "affect" as a synonym for emotion, but a post-Massumi consolidation treats affect as a "general physiological disposition" prior to emotion (Robert Zajonc *American Psychologist* 1980, note 19) — a re-staging that Ruth Leys ("The Turn to Affect: A Critique," *Critical Inquiry* 37.3 [2011], note 20) reads as the inadvertent return of Cartesian dualism. Gross sides with Leys's caution while refusing to dismiss affect-theory, and recovers Raymond Williams's *The Long Revolution* (Broadview 2001 [1961], p. 321, note 23) as the better historicist tool: a "creditable emotion" — Williams's example is the "feeling of freedom carefully controlled" in late liberal democracies — that operates simultaneously as ideology, as lived experience, and as a phenomenon that the post-Massumi affect-theorist cannot describe without "sounding mystical because they are not left with much to talk about" (PDF p. 23, book p. 13). Jordanna Bailkin's chapter in the Biess/Gross 2014 volume *Science and Emotion after 1945* (note 24) extends the Williams diagnosis to postwar British decolonization and the welfare-state production of "givers and receivers of aid."

The four-mode reading of *Imitation of Christ* at the end of the sub-section (PDF p. 24, book p. 14) is the worked example to which the user-prompt rightly directs attention. God's love is parsed across the typology: **mimetic** at Ephesians 5:2 ("walk in love, as Christ also hath loved us"); **prosthetic** at John 4:19 ("we love Him, because He first loved us"); **performative** in the everyday utterance "Jesus loves you," which "instantiat[es] the emotion unpredictably because the outcome is not certain"; and **systematic** in Aquinas's "systematic theology of the passions" or, "very differently, in Equiano's spiritual autobiography, where we will read how the crucified Savior bleeding on the cross on Mount Calvary 'unseals' the book and makes us read everything differently again, from the beginning (chapter 2)." This sentence does two pieces of structural work simultaneously: it consolidates the seven-mode typology into a four-mode example, and it forecasts the central conversion-scene anchor for chapter 2.

Under the sub-heading **Discomfort with the Humanities** (PDF pp. 24-27, book pp. 14-17) Gross turns to the regnant metaphors of situated-cognition philosophy. Kent Puckett's *Bad Form* (Oxford 2008, note 26) supplies the literary-critical counterpart: Charles Bovary's opening hat-mishap as embarrassment-as-narrative-form. But the polemical target is the consoling rhetoric of philosophical situated cognition itself. Gross reads Griffiths-Scarantino's "cultural scaffolding of emotional performances" alongside Andy Clark's "continuous reciprocal causation" (*Supersizing the Mind*, with Chalmers's foreword, note 27), Dreyfus's "fluid" coping, John Haugeland's mind "intimately embedded in its world," and Evan Thompson's "Culture is no mere external addition or support to cognition; it is woven into the very fabric of each human mind from the beginning" (*Mind in Life*, p. 403). Read polemically, this metaphor-system "appears on the edge of melancholia as it recuperates a world that would be much more comfortable — Freud might call this a canny return home — its rhetoric reassuring, as it allays our worst fears about being-out-of-place and feeling *unheimlich* as we are exiled from the familiar" (PDF p. 25, book p. 15). Footnote 30 (Freud, *Das Unbehagen in der Kultur*, Strachey trans. *SE* 21) and note 29 (Uexküll *Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans* p. 172; Michel Serres *Natural Contract* p. 33; Thomas Rickert *Ambient Rhetoric* 2013) underwrite the genealogy.

The counter-tradition is queer-Gestalt-Wittgensteinian. Sara Ahmed's *Queer Phenomenology* (note 33) supplies the analysis of comfort as concealment of domestic labor (Husserl's wife in the kitchen); Kurt Lewin's 1930s Gestalt psychology of the "hopeless situation" and Wittgenstein's 1938 *Lectures on Aesthetics* on "discomfort" (note 34) supply the deeper philosophical roots; and Jonathan Kramnick on Locke's "fantastical uneasiness" (same note) shows how social-institutional distribution of uneasiness along lines of power has its early-modern philosophical archive. Franco Moretti's *The Bourgeois* (Verso 2013, pp. 45, 46, 50, 51, note 32) is the load-bearing literary-critical companion: Moretti's third keyword *comfort* — alongside *seriousness* and *gentleman* — names the historical sea change in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth century in which comfort ceased to be the return-to-normality-after-adversity and became "well-being as an end in itself" (with *Robinson Crusoe* 1719 as transitional case). Comfort and *Genussmittel* (means of pleasure: coffee, tobacco, chocolate, spirits) "managed to forge a compromise" between ascetic production and bourgeois enjoyment. Saidiya Hartman's *Scenes of Subjection* (paraphrased, PDF p. 27) supplies the negative counter-archive of post-abolition man and pedestrian practices below formal equality, anticipating chapter 2.

Under the final sub-heading **Chapter Outlines (with a Definition of Rhetoric and a Defense of Fiction)** (PDF pp. 27-37, book pp. 17-27) Gross forecasts every body unit of the book. The forecast for **Chapter 1** (Darwin) names the methodological gesture as "ground clearing, with Darwin left standing": *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872) is to be disentangled from Paul Ekman's heavy-handed editorial mediation, whose basic-emotions program has done Darwin "a profound disservice." Restored, Darwin's "photograph, illustration, and story-filled" *Expression* foregrounds the inherent rhetoricity of emotion and aligns with Alva Noë's *Out of Our Heads: Why You Are Not Your Brain* (2009). The chapter, Gross says, will schematically contrast a workable situated-emotion model with computational and evolutionary-psychology rivals.

The forecast for **Chapter 2** (Equiano) — "Bearing up in The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano, or Gustavus Vassa, the African (1789)" — pivots on a basic question: "What on earth might it mean when Equiano writes he is 'happily situated' as a slave?" The chapter draws on critical race theory from W. E. B. Du Bois on double consciousness to recent Afro-pessimism (Fred Moten, Jared Sexton), and engages Hartman's worry about the "barbarous archive." On first impression, Gross notes, Equiano slips conveniently into the sciences of happiness (Kahneman) and situated cognition (Noë's Merleau-Ponty), but the demonstration will work the local problematic at the intersection of science and humanities through time, attachment/feeling, and bearing up — eventually dissipating any easy sense of well-being.

Embedded in the Equiano forecast is the **three-dimensional definition of rhetorical theory** (PDF pp. 30-32, book pp. 20-22) that the book operates on:

1. Rhetoric **alerts us to forms of address** wherever they may appear (effectiveness; intentional-fallacy bracketing; Equiano's happy-situation as the test case).
2. Rhetoric **invites critical distance from those forms** (the pathos transacts rather than describes: Equiano's happy situation posits a triangulated attitude toward subjection-terror, master-slave gratitude, and English social identity).
3. Rhetoric provides a **field guide to threatening and promising environments** (deliberative / forensic / epideictic; "rhetoric, as a humanistic form of affordance theory, posits a situation that is not neutral but is rather 'persuasive.'").

Burke's *Philosophy of Literary Form* (LSU 1973 [1941], pp. 60-61, note 38) supplies the Introduction's longest non-Sterne block quotation — on the eighteenth-century poetry of the sublime as *consolatio philosophiae*, "equipment for living, as a ritualistic way of arming us to confront perplexities and risks." Burke's dialectical pairing of comfort and discomfort (medicine and the threatened distress it answers) is read as the methodological model. Note 36 cites Lloyd Bitzer's 1968 "The Rhetorical Situation" (*Philosophy and Rhetoric* 1.1: 1-14, at p. 6), recasting exigence as "a basic discomfort." Note 37 (which the Phase 0 overview's "no direct Heidegger citation" assertion does not anticipate) cites Heidegger's 1924 Aristotle lectures — *Grundbegriffe der aristotelischen Philosophie*, *Gesamtausgabe* 18, ed. Mark Michalski, Klostermann 2002, p. 261 — on "lack of composure [*Aus-der-Fassung*] brought on by terror" and on the cleavage of friend and enemy as the ground of *Mitsein*. This is a direct BCAP-heritage bridge for the present project and should be marked as a Phase 0 correction.

The forecast for **Chapter 3** (Radcliffe) — "Hostile Environments in Ann Radcliffe's *The Romance of the Forest* (1791)" — turns to J. J. Gibson's perceptual psychology as the load-bearing apparatus, with the proviso that Gross will *adjust Gibson's definition* by abandoning the invariance criterion, since Antonia's (in Lewis's *The Monk*) and Adeline's (in Radcliffe) hostile environments are not invariant with respect to human beings in general. Walpole's *Castle of Otranto* Preface to the 2nd edition (1765) — with its Voltaire-via-melange description of the domestic scene of father-scolding-daughter-and-son-mocking — is cited as the genre's foundational vexed-emotional-scene statement. The chapter also recovers some Cognitive Approaches work (Elaine Scarry; George Lakoff cognitive rhetoric; David Herman's Umwelt-research narratology) that already benefits from a Gibsonian environmental approach. The chapter's deep claim — embedded as a generic-aesthetic argument (PDF p. 34, book p. 24) — is that "real terror, the novel teaches us in this instance, is fictional. Terror, unlike a dangerous situation, is the stuff of uncertainties as it takes provisional shape and is characterized with respect to our fragile sense of well-being." The "affectability of nonreferring terms" — Adeline as fictional heroine threatened virtually by a hollow sigh; Antonia's rape as a grammatically possible sentence about a person who never existed — is the chapter's signature device, attributed in note 39 to Jami Bartlett's *Object Lessons: The Novel as a Theory of Reference* (Chicago 2016).

The forecast for **Chapter 4** (Austen) — "Mixed Feelings in Jane Austen's *Sense and Sensibility* (1811)" — refuses the disequilibrium model of mixed feelings (Galen's humours, "combine two parts a feeling like gratitude, one part happiness, a dash of resentment and you get something like Elinor"). Mixed feelings are instead "a matter of negotiated circumstances where feelings may be at odds as they converge on character" (PDF p. 35, book p. 25). The novel's opening Dashwood scene — Fanny telling Mr. Dashwood "how excessively comfortable your mother-in-law and her daughters may live on the interest of seven thousand pounds" — is read as the rhetorical establishment of norms, with Mary Wollstonecraft's "equivocal beings" the structural diagnosis. Daniel Kahneman and Dale Miller's "Norm Theory: Comparing Reality to Its Alternatives" (*Psychological Review* 93.2 [1986]: 136-153, note 41) supplies the cognitive-science counterpart: sympathy as a function of perceived victim-fate abnormality, counterfactual availability, focal-individual mutability, and the affective intensity correlating with perceived abnormality. The chapter's polemical target is Lisa Zunshine's theory-of-mind / mind-reading approach to Austen, which Gross will treat as unecological because it brackets the real-world anchors of resource distribution.

The forecast for the **Epilogue** with Stephanie Preston — "Irreconcilable Differences?" (note the question-mark) — is brief but consequential. Classic laboratory studies of fear "document the persistence of unconscious white vs. black bias while, at the same time, neglecting circumstances like the history of racial slavery that could provide causal explanations of just the sort that these same scientists first raise, then anxiously defer" (PDF p. 37, book p. 27). The epilogue, Gross announces, will be "more promising" insofar as it draws on experiments that incorporate a critically informed, situated emotions approach. Note 42 records that the chapter expands work the two collaborators contributed to the Frank Biess and Daniel M. Gross-edited *Science and Emotion after 1945: A Transatlantic Perspective* (Chicago 2014).

## § Concept introductions

The Introduction installs every volume-pillar concept named in the Phase 0 overview, with the following inflections worth flagging:

- **Uncomfortable situation** (titular) — defined as rhetorical emotional instability against Berlant's situation-as-state-of-things-unfolding (PDF p. 12). The book's "basically uncomfortable" claim (p. 2) is grounded historically: sentimental literature is the empirical site, the European bourgeoisie the polemical historical referent.
- **Situated conceptualization** — installed via the opening footnote (Wilson-Mendenhall et al. 2011), with a four-axis diagnostic (exigencies / key terms / mediation / interpretation) for what laboratory studies systematically fail to capture (PDF p. 19).
- **Rhetoric of emotion** — the book's organizing methodological coinage; the seven-mode typology is its operationalization (PDF pp. 20-21). Gross is careful that "asserting relationships" is co-equal with describing transactions: rhetoric is constitutive, not merely descriptive.
- **Prosthetic emotion** — note 17 furnishes the disciplinary genealogy (Miller; Haynes; Catchings) that the Phase 0 overview did not specify.
- **Performative emotion** — Reddy is the explicit anchor (PDF pp. 16, 20-21), and the cross-reference to the speech-act lineage is implicit (the term is not formally cited to Austin or Searle).
- **Voluntary emotion** — Fisher's *Vehement Passions* (pp. 142, 144, note 18) is the source; Austen's Darcy-as-shamelessness is the worked example, forecasting ch. 4.
- **Systematic emotion** — by Gross's own admission "most importantly for my study" (PDF p. 21). Hobbes's political economy of fear and vainglory is the historical anchor; Sterne's *Sentimental Journey* the literary case.
- **Comfort (as bourgeois keyword)** — derived from Moretti via Weber (PDF pp. 25-26): comfort shifts in the late 17th / early 18th century from a return-to-normality category to a take-normality-as-starting-point pursuit of well-being as an end in itself.
- **Affordance theory (rhetorical extension)** — Gibson's invariance criterion is to be relaxed for hostile-environment analysis (PDF p. 32, book p. 22; ch. 3 forecast at PDF p. 33, book p. 23).
- **Bearing up** — explicitly forecast for chapter 2 (PDF p. 28); Gross announces the conceptual triangulation: time / attachment-feeling / bearing up.
- **Mixed feelings (anti-Galenic)** — explicitly contrasted with the "disequilibrium model recalling the long tradition back to Galen" (PDF p. 35).
- **Real terror is fictional** — the methodological mantra for ch. 3 (PDF p. 34): terror as form of address, not a property of dangerous situations. The companion concept *affectability of nonreferring terms* (after Bartlett 2016, note 39) is the technical apparatus.
- **Creditable emotion** — Williams's term (Long Revolution p. 321, note 23) at PDF pp. 22-23, central to Gross's defense of "emotion" as historicist-rhetorical analytic.
- **Loss of composure** — chapter-1 quality of the uncomfortable situation (PDF p. 27); via note 37 explicitly aligned with Heidegger's *Aus-der-Fassung*.
- **Aristotelian heritage** — present in the Poetics-cathartic citation (PDF p. 20), the rhetoric-tripartite (PDF p. 32), and the GA 18 Aristotle-lectures citation in note 37.

## § Tensions

The Introduction stages at least nine load-bearing tensions, each of which the book will work through in detail:

1. **Hardwired versus socially constructed emotion** (PDF p. 19) — the false binary Gross will replace.
2. **Berlant's situation versus Gross's rhetorical situation** (PDF p. 12) — supplementation rather than refutation.
3. **Chandler's archaeology of sympathy versus Gross's critical history of well-being** (PDF p. 13) — spectator-focus replaced by situation-focus.
4. **Affect (Massumi) versus emotion (Williams/Berlant)** (PDF pp. 22-23) — refusal to valorize either.
5. **Consoling situated-cognition metaphors versus uncomfortable Gestalt-queer-Wittgenstein counter-tradition** (PDF pp. 25-27) — bourgeois return-home versus *unheimlich* exile.
6. **Darwin-as-proto-Ekman versus Darwin-as-rhetorician** (PDF p. 28) — chapter-1 thesis.
7. **Equiano-fits-sciences-of-happiness versus Equiano-exceeds-them** (PDF p. 29) — chapter-2 thesis.
8. **Galenic disequilibrium versus socially-negotiated mixed feelings** (PDF p. 35) — chapter-4 thesis.
9. **Cognitive-Approaches mind-reading (Zunshine) versus Kahneman-Miller norm-theory** (PDF p. 37) — chapter-4 thesis.

## § Phase 0 corrections

Two Phase 0 claims warrant correction:

1. **"Continuous prose — NO internal section headings"** (Phase 0 §9.1) is over-stated for the Introduction. The Introduction does in fact carry four inline sub-headings, presented in italics-and-small-caps and not numbered: *A Sentimental Journey*, *The Rhetoric of Emotion*, *Discomfort with the Humanities*, and *Chapter Outlines (with a Definition of Rhetoric and a Defense of Fiction)*. Body chapters appear to remain unbroken; Phase 0's claim is correct *for chapters proper* but not for the Intro.
2. **"(excluded) Heidegger BCAP — No direct citation"** (Phase 0 §7) is also too strong: note 37 of the Introduction cites Heidegger's 1924 Aristotle-lectures (*Gesamtausgabe* 18, Michalski ed. 2002, p. 261) directly. The citation occurs in the context of Burke's three-dimensional rhetorical theory, on *Aus-der-Fassung* and *Mitsein*. This is a load-bearing BCAP bridge for downstream synthesis.
