# US-04-AUSTEN — Daniel M. Gross, "Mixed Feelings in Jane Austen's *Sense and Sensibility*"

*Uncomfortable Situations* (Chicago 2017), Chapter 4 (book pp. 111–128 / PDF pp. 121–138)

Phase 2 narrative analysis. 2026-05-10.

---

## 1. The titular thesis: mixed feelings against the disequilibrium model

The chapter's opening sentence stakes the volume's most direct anti-cognitivist claim: "Mixed feelings are at the heart of Jane Austen's novel *Sense and Sensibility*, and we think we know what that means, like a recipe: combine two parts a feeling like gratitude, one part happiness, a dash of resentment and you get something like Elinor" (book p.111). The recipe metaphor is not a strawman that Gross invents for convenience; it names a long historical tradition. Note 1 anchors that tradition to Gail Kern Paster's *Humoring the Body: Emotions and the Shakespearean Stage* (2004) — Renaissance medical humorism — which itself stretches back to Galen's four-humour psychophysiology. The model Gross names "disequilibrium" assumes that an emotion-state is a *proportional internal mixing* of more elementary affective ingredients, that mixing tends toward homeostatic equilibrium, and that the analyst's task is to specify the recipe.

Against this entire tradition, Gross sets a one-sentence thesis that the rest of the chapter unfolds: "In fact, mixed feelings are a matter of negotiated circumstances where feelings may be at odds as they converge on character" (book p.111). Four moves are compressed into the sentence. (1) Mixed feelings are *negotiated*, not mixed. (2) The negotiation happens in *circumstances*, not in interior chemistry. (3) Feelings can be *at odds* with each other — incommensurable — rather than mutually decomposable into common units. (4) They *converge on character* — they precipitate as character-formation rather than being products of pre-formed characters. The Aristotelian-rhetorical inheritance is audible in (4), although Gross is careful in note 16 to qualify the Aristotelian reading he inherits from Ryle and MacIntyre.

The methodological complement to this thesis is articulated immediately: "Hence the significance of literature and particularly the sentimental novel as a cross-disciplinary research domain, where this kind of rhetorical situation is exquisitely detailed" (book p.111). Referencing "the science of situated emotion (Barsalou, Barrett) and behavioral economics (Kahneman on bias and persuasion)," Gross promises a reading of mixed feelings "as rhetorically situated." The chapter is thereby positioned as the volume's most explicit *bridge* between scientific situated-emotion research and humanistic rhetorical analysis — a bridge built without subordinating either to the other, and without falling into theory-of-mind reading practiced "unecologically" by what Gross calls Cognitive Approaches to Literature (book p.111).

## 2. The anti-Galen polemic and the homeostatic formula

The Galen-disequilibrium model has remarkable cultural durability, and Gross is interested in tracing its present-day manifestations. On book p.112, he reads *Sense and Sensibility* from the surface as exemplifying the folk-psychological formula EMOTIONAL STABILITY → MIXED FEELINGS → EMOTIONAL STABILITY: the Dashwood women begin "happily settled" in Sussex (S&S p.3) and end "happily resettled in Barton and Delaford where 'strong family affection' is easily communicated" (S&S p.289). Between these endpoints lies the trial-filled journey of mixed feeling. Surface idiomatic English supports the formula: "'I FEEL NOT HALF BAD.' 'I HAVE HALF A MIND TO . . .'" where, as Gross notes, "the dot dot dot of aposiopesis leads by nothing good." The German parallel "Ich hätte fast Lust zu . . ." confirms the structure: "the half mind we are talking about in this case is indeed the emotional half." Half-mind English idioms ("I FEEL TORN") would be, in Fauconnier and Turner's terms, conceptual blending (book p.113, n.6).

The homeostatic formula has a more sophisticated history than folk language: it has been technically codified by Galenic medicine; it shadows Freud's ambivalence-following-Bleuler (n.5); and Dorothea Frede has tried to read Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (against the texts' own usage) as if it described a Phileban-remedial homeostasis (also n.5). Gross's polemical point is that the model is wrong as descriptive psychology but more importantly wrong as *the* implicit framework within which much modern emotion science still operates — including Paul Ekman's evolutionary-psychological basic-emotions program. Ekman's seven basic emotions function in the science the way Galen's four humours function in folk-language: non-basic emotions are "combinations of the basic emotions, which may be called blends or mixed emotional states" (Ekman 1992, quoted book p.113); "smug = enjoyment + contempt." The single qualification Ekman adds is that "what we perceive as mixed emotions on the face are likely basic emotions passing in microseconds" — a technical move that preserves the recipe-logic while denying that the recipe is ever visible.

Figure 4.1, reproduced from a 2005 *Virtual Reality* article on muscle-based talking heads, gives the visual analog of the recipe model: a sadness face plus a fear face (or remorse, or joy + gratification) yields a mathematically interpolated "blended" middle face. Gross's reading is precise: "blending algorithms no doubt have their application. Art is transformed by the aesthetics of a mathematical procedure that gives us Pixar." But the middle face is uncanny — "they depict emotions mathematically unhinged even from implied humanity" (book p.112). The uncanny valley (n.3: Masahiro Mori, *Wired*) provides the diagnosis: stimuli with human and nonhuman traits "undermine our sense of human identity by linking qualitatively different categories . . . by a quantitative metric, degree of human likeness."

The deepest target of Gross's polemic is Bentham. "We still live in the wake of a general utilitarianism famously articulated by Jeremy Bentham just before Austen drafted in the 1790s a precursor epistolary novel 'Elinor and Marianne'" (book p.113). For "the crass utilitarian, emotion, and most importantly happiness, can be quantified and therefore halved." The temporal anchor is crucial: Austen drafted what would become *Sense and Sensibility* in the immediate wake of Bentham's *Fragment on Government* (1776) and *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1780). The chapter's deep claim is that Austen wrote her novel *against* the crass-utilitarian hypothesis already in operation. "But who wants to be a crass utilitarian? Certainly not Jeremy Bentham" — a striking concession that Bentham himself qualified the model (book p.113, returned to at book p.121 + n.25, n.26). Coleridge is mentioned in passing through Mill's famous synthesis ("the two men are each other's 'completing counterpart'," n.8), but the alternative Gross proposes lies elsewhere: "I will pose in this chapter an alternative where the rhetorical situation is central — more precisely, I will explore how mixed feelings materialize in reference values that are negotiated only by way of rhetoric" (book p.113).

## 3. The Elinor–Marianne–Edward–Lucy quadrangle and the £200 scene (Austen pp. 217–218)

The chapter's hinge is the climactic scene in the final Volume III of *Sense and Sensibility*: Elinor, reciprocally in love with the unavailable Edward Ferrars, is obliged by Colonel Brandon's offer of a Delaford rectory to inform Edward that his stalled engagement to Lucy Steele might now be realized on a modest £200 per annum. As Elinor prepares to write Edward, he "appears suddenly on the scene." Note 10 specifies that Austen p.213 already prepared the ground: "Elinor's astonishment at this commission" from Colonel Brandon. Now "for the second time in so many chapters Elinor is astonished and feels 'particularly uncomfortable for some minutes' (217) before she delivers the news with congratulations, reporting with some irony the £200 per annum might just establish all his 'views of happiness' (218), including, it is presumed, his marriage to Lucy" (book p.114). The "views of happiness" phrase is operative: it is precisely the unit on which a crass-utilitarian calculation would have to be performed. The narrator's response to this calculation refuses to perform it. "What Edward felt, as he could not say it himself, it cannot be expected that anyone else should say for him. He *looked* all the astonishment which such unexpected, such unthought-of information could not fail of exciting; but he said only these two words: 'Colonel Brandon!'" (Austen p.218, quoted book p.114).

Gross extracts three levels of utilitarian frustration from this passage. First, "contra Lisa Zunshine, Blakey Vermeule, and like-minded literary critics (see previous chapter), mind reading per se is frustrated insofar as Edward's legibility is denied not only in terms of external expression, but also in terms of feeling that the narrator tells us Edward could not say to himself" — internal opacity, not just external. Second, "contra calculating externalists, including the character Marianne, who would look to the face for physiognomic truths, Edward's emotional expression refers only to absence at the heart, but not apathy." Third — and this is where Figure 4.2 enters — "a physiognomist sees on Edward's face only 'astonishment'" (Fig. 4.2, the 1833 Greatbatch engraving from *Pride and Prejudice* where Mr. Bennet hears the news of Darcy's part in saving Lydia), "that registers, in its unadulterated form, nothing will change per se, or what the narrator calls 'excitement.'" Astonishment is reflexive, predictable, "all too predictable," a "reaction 'such unthought-of information *could not fail of exciting*'" (book p.116, citing Austen). Crucially, it is also scalar — "canonic representations of astonishment incorporate the scalar inflection admiration and fear" — a fact Charles Le Brun's classical-French expression studies make visible (Figure 4.3 — Le Brun's *Astonishment with Fear* and *Admiration with Astonishment*, n.11).

Gross adds a further temporal twist: the Mr. Bennet astonishment scene "destabilizes the emotional arc in both temporal directions as the focal characters, and we as readers, must revise feelings already felt — since prejudice against Darcy's 'pride' turns out to be unjustified" (book p.114). Astonishment is double-direction destabilizing. "Astonishment — or what we now call surprise — renders any calculation of happiness or another basic emotion difficult if not impossible since it is only a common denominator that registers: fear over surprise, admiration over surprise, anger over surprise, and so on" (book pp.114–115). The OED etymology (n.12) reinforces the point: *astonare*, to strike with a thunderbolt, "stun, stupefy"; "active meaning includes astonishment as 'mental disturbance or excitement due to the sudden presentation of anything unlooked for or unaccountable' — which speaks directly to our utilitarian concerns." Beyond the seven brief and episodic emotions Ekman demarcates, Ekman categorizes "love or boredom" under "affective commitments" (n.13). Astonishment thus marks the failure-point of basic-emotions decomposition.

Jerome Kagan (book p.115, n.14) provides scientific corroboration of astonishment's diagnostic role. Citing one gender study where subjects took longer to decide whether a man's face was fearful (because they did not expect fearful men), Kagan urges relations between emotional incentive and brain profile to be conceived "in terms of response uncertainty, not just in terms of incentive-response matching." "Likewise in our Austen passage, astonishment obscures precisely because it is reflexive and all too predictable." It is also, importantly, what scientists since the nineteenth century have called "excitement" (n.14: Daniel Dror, "What Is an Excitement?" in Biess and Gross 2014).

## 4. The Austen-as-Aristotelian thesis (Index 167n16) — and Gross's qualification

A pivotal cross-pipeline anchor surfaces in note 16. The "Austen-as-Aristotelian" thesis is Gilbert Ryle's "Jane Austen and the Moralists" (*Oxford Review* 1, 1966): Ryle reads *Sense and Sensibility* as modeling Aristotelian "equilibrium" and Shaftesbury-derived virtue ethics. Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (3rd ed., 2007, p.240) amplifies the reading: "When Jane Austen speaks of 'happiness', she does so as an Aristotelian. . . . It is a reuniting of Christian and Aristotelian themes in a determinate social context that makes Jane Austen the last great effective imaginative voice of the tradition of thought about, and practice of, the virtues which I have tried to identify. . . . The restricted households of Highbury and Mansfield Park have to serve as surrogates for the Greek city-state and the medieval kingdom." Deirdre McCloskey's *The Bourgeois Virtues* (2007) extends the reading: Austen as exemplar of the seven cardinal virtues narrated for an implied audience.

Here Gross departs decisively. His note 16 reads: "I would add by way of this chapter that Austen is a thorough critic of certain kinds of systematic thinking, in this case utilitarian. This essay also suggests that Austenian happiness is *not* Aristotelian + Christian, as MacIntyre suggests." The departure is theoretically rich. Gross's Aristotle is not the virtue-ethical Aristotle of *Nicomachean Ethics* with eudaimonia as telos of habituated practice (Ryle/MacIntyre); it is the *Rhetoric* Aristotle of situated pathē, the Aristotle whose treatment of mixed feelings, as Frede admits, never uses the vocabulary of "mixture" at all (n.5). The Aristotelian inheritance Gross retains from his own 2006 *Secret History of Emotion* is rhetorical-situational, not telic-virtue-ethical. *Sense and Sensibility* is, on Gross's qualified reading, a critique of systematic-utilitarian calculation *and* of any equivalent systematic-Aristotelian virtue-equilibrium — both of which presuppose precisely the proportional-internal-mixing model that Galen instantiates and that Austen's novel demolishes.

The argument-by-procedure that Gross substitutes for omniscient narration is itself revealing. "The first anti-skeptical clue should come from the simple fact that Austen's story does proceed, and quite successfully" (book p.116) — without delivering up the God's-eye perspective on mixed feelings that an Aristotelian or evolutionary-psychological reading would require. The novel's continued reception history is itself the warrant for non-omniscient reading.

## 5. Barrett and Barsalou: interested onlookers as the chapter's argumentative crux

Mid-chapter (PDF p.130 / book p.119–120), Gross arrives at his pivotal formulation. The Barsalou–Barrett collaboration in *Neuropsychologia* 2011 ("Grounding Emotion in Situated Conceptualization," Wilson-Mendenhall et al.) renders Kagan's uncertainty problem productive by separating, in catch trials, the response to an emotional incentive from "what they call its *conceptualization*" (n.17). Their methodological showpiece is the Jim Webb photograph series (Figure 4.4): in (a) Webb's face looks "agitated and aggressive" out of context; in (b) the same face appears "happy and excited" surrounded by firefighter campaign signs. "When situated, and with only the structural information from the face as a guide, it is easy to mistake the emotion that you see in another person. A similar error in perception was said to have cost Howard Dean the opportunity to run for President of the United States in 2004." This is Barrett, Lindquist, and Gendron's "emotion paradox" (n.18: "Language as Context for Emotional Perception," *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 11.8, 2007) — automatically perceiving someone as either angry or elated "depending upon the image, even though sufficient information for this judgment is not unambiguously displayed on his face or body" (book p.117).

Barrett and Barsalou anchor their account in Knight Dunlap's 1932 article (n.19): "I may apprehend my situation primarily as one in which my welfare is threatened. I call my inner responses *fear*. *You* may apprehend my situation as one in which I fight against the threat; you say I am *angry*. Which is correct? The answer may be: Both!" (book p.118). The brain physiology corresponds: social fear mobilizes left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and inferior frontal gyrus (executive control); physical fear mobilizes motor regions. "Social fear gets our wheels spinning, physical fear gets our legs moving" (book p.118, citing *Grounding Emotion* p.1124). The experimental protocol Gross block-quotes from the supplementary materials is, he notes, "a manner that speaks to the literary humanities and especially the art of rhetoric": participants immerse in second-person narrative scenarios (Gross corrects the article's "first person" to "second person") designed to induce particular conceptualizations of ambiguous situations.

To make the rhetorical-experimental analogy concrete Gross invents a scenario reminiscent of "Creative Writing 101": "You're at a dinner party with friends. A debate about a contentious issue arises that gets everyone at the table talking. You alone bravely defend the unpopular view. Your comments are met with sudden uncomfortable silence. Your friends are looking down at their plates, avoiding eye contact with you. You feel your chest tighten" (book p.119). Anger-at-self or fear-of-distance depends on the situation, "which would need more details including my relationship to interested onlookers and the view I defended" — and *here* is the chapter's argumentative crux, the bridge sentence that fuses Austen with the neuroscience: "Now returning to Austen's critique of utilitarianism, my mixed feelings would be determined not by any predictable calculation but rather in dynamic relationship with onlookers who might in fact be extrinsic." Two sentences later (book p.120): "In light of Barrett and Barsalou, we can say that Edward's mixed feelings are defined by interested onlookers who, in this case, include us."

The definition is structurally remarkable. Mixed feelings are not internal events with social witnesses; they are *constituted by* the onlookers. Elinor is implicated by the "look" only she could grant at that moment; "anyone" — including the reader — "is denied the insight that would come with an emotional label." Reader, Elinor, Marianne, Lucy, John Dashwood, Mrs. Jennings, and even the absent Mrs. Ferrars are interested onlookers, and Edward's mixed feelings are what they collectively produce. This is what the volume's rhetorical-ontology-of-emotion looks like in operation.

## 6. The £200 + Brandon-living calculation and Lucy's secret happiness-calculation (Austen pp. 220–221)

The chapter's economic anchor sharpens this point. "The entire scene is framed by a mockery of utilitarian calculation that would have all Edward's views of happiness realized by a sum lying somewhere between £200 and x = the availability of Colonel Brandon's servants, carriage, cows, and poultry, as 'secretly' calculated by Lucy in the following scene (221)" (book p.120). The Austen text Gross is reading places Edward's "views of happiness" (Edward's own phrase, S&S p.218) inside an implicit range whose upper bound is what Lucy will compute privately in S&S p.221 — a calculation Austen presents in scare-quote ironic mode ("secretly"). The range £200 ↔ Brandon-establishment is what crass utility would have to fix to deliver Edward's happiness as a quantity. Austen's narrative frustrates this exactly insofar as the calculation is performed only by Lucy, "the following scene," and only "secretly," that is, in a mode of bad faith. The novel demonstrates utility's bad-faith requirement: the calculation can be performed only by characters from whom the reader is supposed to recoil.

Gross then walks back one episode to the Elinor–Marianne dialogue at S&S p.198 — the **disingenuous expected-utility framing** that bookends the £200 calculation: "'Edward will marry Lucy; he will marry a woman superior in person and understanding to half her sex; and time and habit will teach him to forget that he ever thought another superior to *her*'" (Elinor, S&S p.198, quoted book p.120). Gross's gloss is precise: this is "expected utility that rationally calculates absolute value." Marianne's retort, also S&S p.198, is the **Kahneman-Tversky prefiguration in miniature**: "'If such is your way of thinking,' replies Marianne, 'if the loss of what is most valued is so easily to be made up by something else, your resolution, your self-command, are, perhaps, a little less to be wondered at.'" Marianne addresses Elinor "as a non-abstract and unaveraged person who should be interested not in absolute value, but in her own *relative* superiority to Lucy." Marianne is — in the technical sense — a relative-value theorist of feeling. Where Elinor's frame averages and substitutes, Marianne insists that what is most valued cannot be made up by something else: loss aversion before its name.

## 7. The Kahneman–Tversky prefiguration reading

The reading turns, here, on a specific bibliographic claim. Austen drafted "Elinor and Marianne" — the epistolary precursor to *Sense and Sensibility* — in the 1790s, "just before Austen" finished *Sense and Sensibility* and "just after Bentham" had articulated the canonical utilitarian formulation. That dating positions Austen as a *contemporary* critic of crass-utility-of-happiness, not as a retrospective one. The chapter's deepest historical claim is that Marianne's relative-value retort to Elinor *prefigures* what would, almost two centuries later, become Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk" (*Econometrica* 47, March 1979, 263–291), which "demonstrates how relative values in any given situation matters more to an individual than absolute value calculated rationally" (n.24). What Marianne names — that "the loss of what is most valued" cannot "be made up by something else" — Kahneman and Tversky will quantify as loss aversion.

The chapter then pivots to Kahneman's 1992 article whose title is the chapter's structural mirror: "Reference Points, Anchors, Norms, and Mixed Feelings" (*Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 51.2, 296–312). "Explicitly for Kahneman, mixed feelings are not calculable intermediates but rather ambivalencies that reference multiple points subject to what he calls, depending upon the example, negotiation or persuasion" (book p.120). The $3000-vs-$5000-expected raise example follows: "A simple model is that the raise of $3000 is compared to a single reference point, which is a weighted composite of the two salient reference points. . . . A formulation that may be more satisfying is that any raise of less than $5000 is experienced *both* as a gain and as a loss, at different times" (Kahneman p.306, book p.120–121).

The technical takeaway is decisive: "Practically, mixed feelings in judgment tasks are irresolvable with reference to single weighted composites or an averaging formula, or a fixed temporality; Kahneman would not be impressed by our blending algorithm, for instance. Nor would be Austen, who might poke fun at a crass utilitarian telling the $3000 raise recipient the following: 'Why don't you just add up your gratitude and resentment, elation and disappointment, happiness and unhappiness, then divide by two . . . now feel that way'" (book p.121). The mathematically interpolated middle face of Figure 4.1 has been re-described as a fiscal-affective absurdity.

Kahneman's "rhetoric of possibilities" follows. Negotiators, politicians, and advertisers communicate reference points that serve their purposes — labor-union negotiators lowball expectations; politicians announce small initiatives with great fanfare; advertisers exploit Kahneman's "focusing illusion" (Schkade and Kahneman 1998, n.27) so consumers believe weight, scent, or whatever change "will produce marked improvements in his or her happiness" (book p.121–122). "In a nutshell, Kahneman ties value to a rhetoric of possibilities."

Gross handles the obvious objection by introducing the Renaissance figure of *paradiastole* (book p.122). "Persuasion is *inillimitable* because reference points are not fixed absolutely and they are subject to revision" — Kahneman's "I am not losing a vehicle, I am gaining freedom from debt"; "Remember you are not losing a daughter — you are gaining a son." But Gross is at pains to distinguish his reading from Quentin Skinner's generic account of paradiastole (n.28: "it will always be possible to argue *in utramque partem*, on either side of the case"). "Kahneman's example shows how reference values are lodged in a recalcitrant world subject, however, to negotiation." The world is not infinitely revisable; rhetoric is not all-powerful. Reference points are negotiable but not invented from nothing — there really *is* a $3000 raise; the stingy boss's balance sheet really *is what it is*.

## 8. The double-negation passage: rhetorical-emotional device

In one of the chapter's most quoted formulations Gross treats double negation as a load-bearing rhetorical-emotional device, not as stylistic ornament. "*Not no* feelings, to use the double negative Austen found so stylistically congenial. Mixed feelings that tempt us to read along with astonishment, excitement, gratitude, and happiness, perhaps also love, regret, shame, unhappiness, disgust, terror, melancholy, and we could go on like Elinor who finally sits down to 'reconsider the past, recall the words and endeavour to comprehend all the feelings of Edward; and, of course, to reflect on her own with discontent' (219)" (book p.120). The double negative is the *grammatical* analog of the rhetorical situation Gross has been describing: it refuses to convert ambivalence into a single positive (or negative) descriptor, and it forces the reader to do the work of triangulation that — under Galenic or Ekman-style assumptions — should not be necessary. Note that Austen herself stages such reconsideration narratively: Elinor sitting down to "comprehend all the feelings of Edward" is doing Gross's chapter from the inside, working through an enumerated but unresolvable list of feelings. The very impossibility of the calculation is what produces the analytical proceeding. "Thus our analysis proceeds not despite, but precisely because of the mixed feelings that Austen treats masterfully when considered from this perspective of recent cognitive science and post-utilitarian psychology."

Double negation belongs to a wider class of negation-effects in Austen that Gross's *Uncomfortable Situations* indexes at p.46, p.47–48, and p.120. Aposiopesis (the dot-dot-dot of "I HAVE HALF A MIND TO . . ."), the narrator's flat refusal of omniscience ("What Edward felt, as he could not say it himself . . ."), and the half-mind idiom together compose what could be called Austen's negative-emotional grammar. The point is not stylistic but conceptual: these are the *forms* in which a non-Galenic, non-Ekman, situated theory of mixed feelings registers in prose.

## 9. Edward's six reference points and Elinor as skilled rhetorician

After the Kahneman exegesis Gross returns to Edward with surgical precision (book p.123). "Following Kahneman, let's call him the subject faced with multiple and conflicting reference points, including at least 1) his gratitude toward Colonel Brandon and Elinor, who have cooperatively enabled his minimal happiness; 2) his resentment toward Colonel Brandon and Elinor, who have at the same time *disabled* his optimal happiness; 3) his sense of minimal obligation toward Lucy, whom he had engaged; 4) his sense of maximal obligation toward Elinor, whom he had more recently wooed; 5) his disgust with whomever, including himself, had put him in this awkward situation; and 6) his specific astonishment that Elinor must also populate this last category because she had thrown their awkward situation directly in his face." Edward's "Colonel Brandon!" — his only two-word utterance — is the linguistic compression of six conflicting reference points. The Galenic recipe model has no resources to register six. The Ekman basic-emotions model would have to either split astonishment microsecond-by-microsecond into the six (an unfalsifiable claim) or relegate them to "affective commitments" outside the basic-emotions taxonomy. Kahneman's reference-points model handles the six naturally because it gives up the requirement of mutual decomposability.

The corresponding move on Elinor reframes the character with similar precision. "In turn, Elinor might be called by Kahneman an interested negotiator, and we might call her a skilled rhetorician, even a very, very subtle seducer" (book p.123). Elinor's six-fold rhetorical operation: "she has extracted Edward's gratitude without allowing that he would owe her anything, she has confronted Edward with an obvious choice between lowered and raised expectations, she has exploited what Kahneman and Tversky famously call *loss aversion* by presenting herself as the more immediate love object at the same time that she articulates the very alienation that would force Edward's hand. No wonder Edward is not just specifically but also generally astonished." Elinor's *sense* — her sensible self-command — is here re-described as a skilled rhetorical performance. She is the very type of the Kahneman negotiator, smoothing loss-aversion's sharp edges. The reading does not impugn her; it elevates the rhetorical structure of her practical wisdom.

## 10. Scenario as literary fiction; Goring on sentimental-fiction scene mechanics

Gross presses one further claim. Kahneman's own example "is also a work of literary fiction . . . an imaginative mini-narrative populated not with real human beings but implied human beings for whom context never disappears completely into the depths of psyche. There is a way in which scenario building — even for oneself — always relies upon narrative fictionality" (book p.123). The Barsalou-Barrett scan protocols are explicit in their narrative dependence: the experiment uses "training phases," "Creative Writing 101"–style mini-narratives, and (Gross corrects) second-person immersion templates. The boundary between Austen's fiction and Kahneman's experimental "mini-narrative" is not a categorical boundary but a difference of embedding genres.

The witnessed-eloquence sentence (book p.124) generalizes the point. Paul Goring's *The Rhetoric of Sensibility in Eighteenth-Century Culture* (Cambridge 2005, p.153) extends Gross's case beyond Austen to Richardson's *Pamela*, Fielding's *David Simple*, McKenzie's *The Man of Feeling*, and Sterne's *Sentimental Journey*. Sentimental fiction "insists upon an association between visible emotionalism and modern virtue, but the actual *eloquence* of bodies represented in fiction — the basis of their emotive hold over readers, and thus the basis of their social potential — hangs not only upon eighteenth-century thinking about the passions' visibility, but also upon the mechanics of the scenes in which they are depicted." Gross's gloss draws the full bridge to Kahneman: "With Kahneman we conclude that the emotional 'mechanics of the scene' in sentimental fiction materializes in reference values that are negotiated only by way of rhetoric. And this should be no great surprise considering that sentimental fiction, including its *Sense and Sensibility* transformation, was deeply concerned with the interference between emotional economies and economies that would be exclusively financial."

## 11. John Dashwood, Mrs. Jennings, and the Wollstonecraft frame: secondary characters as norm-setters

The chapter's concluding movement (book pp. 124–128) generalizes its theory by foregrounding what Kahneman + Miller's "Norm Theory" (1986) calls a "phenomenology of surprise": perceived abnormality drives sympathy, as does the availability of counterfactuals. Figure 4.5 (Barrett's recent article on top-down gossip influencing vision) provides the experimental analog: what we *know* about someone influences not only what we feel and think about them but whether we *see* them in the first place. In Austen's economy, this work is done by John Dashwood and Mrs. Jennings. John frames Edward's engagement to Lucy as a violation of family loyalty: "'Duty, affection, and everything was disregarded. I never thought Edward so stubborn, so unfeeling before'" (S&S p.200). Mrs. Jennings re-situates: "'he has acted like an honest man!'" (S&S p.201). John, astonished, will not offend "anybody of good fortune," so he reframes again in light of Mrs. Ferrars's preference for Robert — "every conscientious, good mother, in like circumstances, would adopt" (S&S p.201). These are *competing reference points*, sequentially deployed by the same character to manage his own affective positioning relative to his stronger interests. They are the Kahnemanite norms operative around Edward.

The point generalizes to tertiary characters and even to the legal-economic structuring of the novel. "Willoughby's otherwise obscure servant who appears out of nowhere to hold his horse's reins"; "the places like Barton Cottage that may or may not afford happiness"; "the legal arrangements, in the inheritance guidelines that structure a novel around abnormalities and their local negotiation" (book p.126). These tertiary backgrounded elements are what Alex Woloch in *The One vs. the Many* (2003) and "Character Insecurity in Austen's *Sense and Sensibility*" (2011) treats in Hegelian relative-value terms (n.35): an object's defining characteristics "emerge only through juxtaposition. . . . An object does not possess intrinsic qualities. . . . Like Hegel's *Phenomenology of Mind*, a text written almost contemporaneously with Austen's work, [S&S] bears witness to a world in which absolute value no longer inheres within objects themselves but only emerges as the individual object or individual person is placed in radically contingent relationship to other, equally unstable, objects or persons" (Woloch p.29, quoted n.35).

The Mary Wollstonecraft block-quote (book pp. 126–127, from *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* 1792, n.36) gives the social-scientific analog. Wollstonecraft identifies the "equivocal humiliating situation" of girls "weakly educated, often cruelly left by their parents without any provision; and, of course, dependent on, not only the reason, but the bounty of their brothers." Once the brother marries, "the wife . . . is jealous of the little kindness which her husband shows to his relations; and her sensibility not rising to humanity, she is displeased at seeing the property of *her* children lavished on an helpless sister." "It is as if Wollstonecraft is describing the Dashwood situation exactly," Gross writes — but with the difference that Wollstonecraft's gratuitous reference to "poor education" turns out to be inconsequential, since the Dashwoods are "relatively well-off in terms of such cultural capital." The "tolerable degree of comfort" both authors attribute to women "depends upon the favor of men who might be quite good enough, that is until they marry."

Gross's deepest synthesis is bibliographic. Both Wollstonecraft and Austen, he writes, are "skilled negotiators as Kahneman might describe them, which is to say that they both show how reality is established with respect to its alternatives. Each author increases sympathy by showing how the victim's fate should not be considered normal, each makes it easy to imagine alternatives to the unhappy situation, and each carefully orchestrates the story so that blame is removed from the suffering sisters and redistributed not to the would-be benevolent brothers, and not to the petty wives who are just looking after their offspring, but rather to the occasions that offer themselves all-too regularly" (book p.127). Wollstonecraft's analytic-disciplinary work can "simply foreground institutional culprits like law and education. But this is only half the story as any good novelist knows. How does Austen pull it off?"

The answer comes in Austen's reading of Mrs. John Dashwood (S&S p.5, quoted book p.128): "'Mrs. John Dashwood had never been a favourite with any of her husband's family; but she had had no opportunity, till the present, of shewing them with how little attention to the comfort of other people she could act when occasion required it.'" The offending actions "are quite squarely the result of predictable opportunities, whereas character is beside the point." Austen redistributes responsibility so that character behavior "appears circumstantial, but not inevitable." Circumstances can be overcome only by extraordinary sensibility for humanity — what Wollstonecraft calls "uncommon feeling" and Austen, in the same scene (S&S p.5), calls "only common feelings" producing the unpleasantness "to be expected." "In the hands of Wollstonecraft and Austen, virtuous sentiments appear regrettably abnormal; the norm is vicious." Mrs. John Dashwood's circumstantial vice is the structural condition of the novel's moral economy; Austen's reader's task is to see the *occasion-character distinction* clearly enough to refuse the lazy moral-character reading the genre invites.

## 12. The chapter's closing claim: rhetoric rediscovered at the heart of cross-disciplinary analysis

"In her *Vindication*, Wollstonecraft is writing critically in a disciplinary form we would now consider social science. But our social science is not solely responsible for the analysis and criticism of social norms. . . . Such a situation — and the mixed feelings it engenders — deserve a certain kind of rhetorical analysis that can, indeed must, cross disciplines to do its work adequately. No single discipline has a monopoly on how norms are established referentially, and measured" (book p.128). The closing sentence states the volume's broadest methodological commitment: "At the same time we should see how rhetoric cannot be ancillary to knowledge in the disciplines, the mere linguistic form into which content is poured. In an age where the limits of rationality are newly interesting across disciplines, rhetoric is rediscovered at their very heart."

The chapter has done what its opening sentence promised: it has built a bridge between situated-emotion neuroscience (Barsalou + Barrett), behavioral economics (Kahneman + Tversky), eighteenth-century sentimental-novel rhetoric (Goring on Richardson, Fielding, McKenzie, Sterne, Austen), classical-rhetorical figures (paradiastole via Peacham, contested in Skinner), and feminist analytic philosophy (Wollstonecraft) — all without subordinating any of them to any other, and all anchored in the specific page-anchored moments of *Sense and Sensibility* (S&S pp. 5, 198, 200–201, 213, 217–219, 221, 289). The chapter's titular concept, *mixed feelings*, has been redescribed step-by-step: not as Galenic disequilibrium, not as Ekman's microsecond-passing blends, not as Aristotelian + Christian virtue-equilibrium (Ryle/MacIntyre), but as the rhetorically-negotiated outcome of multiple, irreducible reference points configured by interested onlookers — among whom the reader is unavoidably one.

What remains for the rest of the volume is the political extension of this thesis. If mixed feelings really are rhetorically-situated, then the politics of emotion is the politics of the staging of scenes — Goring's *witnessed eloquence*, Kahneman's *negotiator's framing*, Wollstonecraft's *equivocal humiliating situation*, Austen's *opportunity-not-character* distribution of responsibility. The Epilogue with Stephanie Preston picks up exactly here.
