# US-03-RADCLIFFE — Hostile Environments in Ann Radcliffe's *The Romance of the Forest*

**Subheadings**: "Limitations of Literary Darwinism and Cognitive Approaches to Literature" (PDF 87) · "Gibson's Affordances and the Humanities" (PDF 102) · "The Romance of the Forest" (PDF 112)

## 1. Titular thesis: hostility as the diagnostic for environmental theory

Gross opens Chapter 3 with one of the volume's most economical thesis-statements. Environments, technically understood via J. J. Gibson, can make people "terribly uncomfortable to the point of becoming unhinged" — or even "undone," as the rape victim Antonia protests in Matthew Lewis's *The Monk* (1796) just before the narrative finishes her off (PDF 87, book p.77). The "undone" formula — Gross quotes only the short fragment "Is not my ruin completed? Am I not undone, undone for ever?" — is asked to do a great deal of theoretical work. It names the diagnostic by which the chapter will recognize a *hostile environment* in the technical sense Gross intends: not merely a dangerous environment but one whose threats are constituted through the rhetorical-narrative texture of human social life — domestic "scenes," institutions like law and convent and family, and the spectral residues that animate Gothic-sentimental fiction. The chapter's announced ambition follows in one sentence: it matters very much what model of cognition one uses when studying how human environments appear in literature, and this specialized literary-critical issue in fact lies at the crux of any productive encounter between science and the humanities (PDF 87).

The chapter accordingly divides into three movements. First, a polemical clearing of the ground against Literary Darwinism and Cognitive Approaches to Literature, both of which Gross argues smuggle in orthodox Evolutionary Psychology under cover of methodological pluralism. Second, a positive reconstruction of affordance theory anchored in Gibson but extended through Gestalt psychology (Koffka, Lewin), Bitzerian rhetorical situation, and Aristotle's *Rhetoric* — with the cop-car-roof case as the chapter's signature example. Third, the literary reading proper: Radcliffe's *Romance of the Forest* as the laboratory in which all three analytic registers — biological, legal, rhetorical — meet and become legible at once.

The titular keyword is doing more than naming a chapter topic. *Hostility* derives from Latin *hostis* (enemy), and Gross plays the etymology forward across all three movements: the hostile workplace at law, the hostile abbey of Gothic fiction, and — at the most abstract — the hostility of any environment that affords its inhabitants only the bad terms of a life-undone.

## 2. Clearing the ground: against Literary Darwinism and Cognitive Approaches to Literature

The chapter's first 15 PDF pages (87–101) are a forensic dismantling of two cognate movements: Literary Darwinism (Brian Boyd's *On the Origin of Stories*, 2009; Joseph Carroll; Jonathan Gottschall; Denis Dutton's *Art Instinct*, 2009) and Cognitive Approaches to Literature (Lisa Zunshine's *Why We Read Fiction*, 2006, and *Introduction to Cognitive Cultural Studies*, 2010; Ellen Spolsky's *Work of Fiction*, 2004). Gross's strategy is not to deny that evolution shaped human minds — he readily grants this — but to demonstrate the recurring logical failures by which these movements move from undeniable evolutionary-scientific premises to interesting literary-critical conclusions.

The diagnostic move is sharpest at PDF 88. Boyd, having walked the reader through standard kin-selection mathematics — offspring should prefer to concentrate parental investment in itself, parents should distribute investment evenly — pivots on the strength of this analytic to claim that we now see "why even in the most cooperative of relationships competition is inevitable, and why the powerful emotions engendered by family loyalty and conflict saturate stories from Genesis to *The Sopranos*" (PDF 88). Gross's reaction is dry and devastating: "try finishing the sentence because I'm at a loss." The implicit hop from kin-selection mathematics to *The Sopranos* HBO production — a hop that requires several centuries of social-Darwinist history to bridge — is offered as analytic explanation. Gross will later (PDF 91) name the underlying error: it is the formal fallacy of *affirming the consequent*. If P (biocultural approach to literature) implies Q (we take evolution seriously), and Q is granted, this does not require P. One may take evolution seriously without making a biocultural approach to literary criticism mandatory. Boyd's argument has the *form* of soundness without the *substance* of valid inference.

Zunshine receives a parallel diagnosis. Her *Why We Read Fiction* (2006) anchors literary cognition in Theory of Mind — readers are pleasure-engaging the mind-reading capacities that evolved during deep prehistory. But Zunshine recounts an awkward autobiographical moment (Zunshine 2006, pp.25–27): asked at a talk how her ToM framework handles nature descriptions in fiction (which seem to "have nothing to do with reporting or guessing character's minds"), she went back to Turgenev, where nature passages had once bored her, and "finally learned to skip all of them" (PDF 89). Gross seizes this admission. To rescue ToM, Zunshine subsumes Wordsworth's *Prelude*-style nature passages under pathetic fallacy and personification — they "prompt us to exercise our ToM in a way very different from the stories that contain no such landscapes" — but, as Gross notes, this maneuver reduces "anything cognizable" in a novel to "mental architecture as defined in cognitive science" (PDF 89–90). The cost of saving ToM as universal is a *mentalistic fallacy* that strips environmental description of its environmental character.

Against this background Gross turns approvingly to Elaine Scarry's *Dreaming by the Book* (1999) as the chapter's good case — the unassimilated humanistic appropriation of Gibson. Scarry uses Gibson's kinetic-occlusion theory of perceptual acuity to argue that narrative *imitates* perceptual cognition rather than *activating* it. Her reading of *Tess of the d'Urbervilles*' opening — a sequence of small instructions that Scarry parses parenthetically ("look closely at the walker's legs"; "now picture their work of weight-bearing"; "assess how well they hold that weight"; "watch which way the load leans") — is offered as the model: "Literature consists of a steady stream of erased imperatives" (Scarry, *Dreaming by the Book*, p.35; PDF 90). Narrative is a distinctly useful experiment in cognitive psychology *as a human endeavor*. For Scarry, "nature" — a flower in Ashbury's delphinium or Rousseau's umbellifer — is already part of our mental architecture, and criticism via Gibson can help us see how.

Gross adds Lakoff (PDF 91) as the cognitive-linguistic bridge: Lakoff agrees with Gibson on embodiment but not on environmental realism. Anger, for Lakoff, is "cognitive" only insofar as it is a culturally-defined experience: emotional affordances vary with local laws and "injurable people." This is the chapter's first explicit articulation of cultural variability inside an affordance framework. The closing pages of the section catalogue six "red flags" of biology-literature criticism (PDF 93): Pleistocene/hunter-gatherer (analytically remote); evolved mental architecture (question-begging); consilience (reductive); modularity (deterministic); neuron-talk (too small for literary phenomena); plasticity (a black box too big to be testable). The negative argument completes itself at PDF 96 with the chapter's second formal-fallacy diagnosis — *the undistributed middle*: even granting that attention, imagination, cognition, and emotion can be deeply explored in terms of evolved-cultural human nature, this does not mean that *all* categories engaging these capacities (genre, narrative structure, point of view) are best explored that way. The fallacy explains why "fallacies of scale and distance abound" (PDF 97) in evocritical literary criticism: the analytic must travel an enormous distance from prehistoric premise to local literary conclusion, and the trip cannot be made on the budget of evolutionary universals alone.

## 3. Gibson's affordances and the humanities: building the positive theory

The section "Gibson's Affordances and the Humanities" (PDF 102–111) executes the chapter's constructive task. Gross adopts Andrea Scarantino's clean definition (Scarantino, "Affordances Explained," *Philosophy of Science* 70.5, 2003, p.950): "the affordances of the environment . . . are the offerings of the environment, roughly the sets of threats (negative affordances) and promises (positive affordances) that characterize items in the environment relative to organisms" (PDF 102). The symmetry threat/promise (negative/positive affordance) is load-bearing for the entire chapter.

The first move is to claim — provocatively, against orthodox Gibsonianism — that rhetoric was *already* doing affordance theory before Gibson named it. Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II provides Gross's archetypal case: at 1384b32–35, the rhetorician Cydias, debating the allotment of land on the island of Samos in violation of the Second Athenian League's intent (377/376 BCE), asks his audience to "imagine the Greeks standing around them in a circle, actually seeing and not only later hearing about what they might vote." Cydias's rhetorical figure invokes a "tribunal of conscience" — a virtual audience that evokes shame among those whose reputation is potentially threatened. Gross reads this as the original negative-affordance: an item-in-the-environment (the imagined Greek tribunal) that *characterizes* the situation as threat — a threat constituted by storytelling rather than by physical danger. "Rhetoric," Gross writes, "or rather a subset of language writ large, provides the kinds of doings and the kinds of happenings that make an affordance property like 'tribunal of conscience' appear in this situation" (PDF 109).

The second move rehabilitates Lloyd Bitzer's 1968 essay "The Rhetorical Situation" as proto-affordance theory. Bitzer's triad — exigence, audience, constraints — is rendered concretely with the Free Speech Movement case at PDF 103: Jack Weinberg (Congress of Racial Equality advocate) is thrown into a police car on the UC Berkeley campus on October 1, 1964; Mario Savio mounts the cop-car roof to speak; the agitated student audience is the one inspired into action by Savio's "put your bodies upon the gears" speech a couple months later. Beliefs, attitudes, documents, facts, traditions, images, motives — Bitzer's list of constraints — Gross maps directly onto the elements of Scarantino's affordance taxonomy, with the difference that rhetoric "posits a situation that is not neutral but rather 'persuasive,' threatening and promising with respect to our human being-in-the-world" (PDF 103).

The third and most original move is the chapter's signature: the *cop-car-roof affordance* (PDF 104–105, book pp.94–95). Gibson would say the cop-car roof affords climbing — it is climb-up-able — and in this strict sense it is an invariant affordance, perceptible regardless of whether anyone climbs. But the action-event of October 1, 1964, is not exhausted by climbing. It is defined "rhetorically, referencing in narrative form at least the exigence, audience, and constraints including the relevant legal and administrative apparatus, but then also the stuff including a cop car roof that makes this scene not only possible physically but also consequential and persuasive with respect to future activities" (PDF 104). And here Gross strikes the formula. To the degree that the Savio event is now fixed in the historical record, the cop car roof *is* always there to be perceived as a stage for speaking persuasively — and in *this* regard it is invariant. Like an open mic at a coffee house, or the podium in the US Senate, "cop car roofs in certain circumstances now say *plead on me* or *harangue on me*" (PDF 105).

The example is taken seriously as a piece of theory. The cop-car-roof affordance is variable when read at the level of biological-perceptual affordance (most adult humans have always been able to climb cop cars), but it is fixed as soon as one reads at the level of cultural-historical communication: post-1964, this stage exists in our political and media culture. The crucial distinction Gross then introduces (still PDF 105) is between *cultural* and *social* environments: cultural affordances emphasize communication structures (myths, rituals, repetitions in our political-media archive); social affordances emphasize organizational structures (stratification, who-uses-which-platform). These are not the same analytic — and Gross is explicit that the relevant patterns reside "substantially in an environment we call cultural or social, depending upon the analytic frame." The Phase 0 master plan singled this distinction out as a centerpiece concept; the textual basis is firm and the formulation is Gross's most original within the Gibson-extension argument.

The Gestalt-psychological back-history is reconstructed at PDF 104. Koffka's *Principles of Gestalt Psychology* (1935, p.7) is quoted at length — the postbox "invites" mailing; the handle "wants to be grasped"; "things tell us what to do with them." Kurt Lewin's *Dynamic Theory of Personality* (1935) is the deeper source: *Aufforderungscharakter* (translated as "invitation character" by Brown 1929, as "valence" by Adams 1931), *Umweltkrafte* (fields of force), *Umstrukturierung* (transformation of the total field), *Aus-dem-Felde-Gehen* (leaving the field — embarrassment followed by withdrawal). Lewin in turn cites Jakob von Uexküll on the biological environment as "a complex of foods, enemies, means of protection" — and Gross is careful to preserve this Uexküllian seam in Gibson's prehistory because it will reappear when Radcliffe is named an "*Umwelt* researcher" in the literary mode.

Lewin's "hopeless situation" (1935, p.94) is given particular weight (PDF 106): when a situation becomes wholly inescapably disagreeable, "the child, despairing, contracts, physically and psychically, under the vectors coming from all sides and usually attempts to build a wall between himself and the situation. This is expressed both in the typical bodily gesture of despair (crumpling up, covering the eyes with the arms, etc.) and by a sort of encysting of the self: the child becomes obdurate." This concept becomes Gross's load-bearing diagnostic when Adeline confronts the Marquis: encompassed with dangers, with resources foreclosed, the heroine is in Lewin's hopeless situation in the structural sense.

Erik Rietveld is brought in (PDF 106) as the contemporary heir to this lineage: his reading of Wittgenstein's *Lectures on Aesthetics* (1938) shows the expert craftsman as a figure who "allows himself to be moved by the situation" — perceiving a *relevant* solicitation to act, an affordance that matters and registers affectively as attractive or repulsive. This is decisive for Gross: it lets the affordance acquire its affective dimension while remaining ontologically situated rather than mentalistic. Discomfort and disgust become legitimate aesthetic-environmental categories — the chapter's affective backbone.

The section closes with two crucial framing pieces. First, the legal hostile-work-environment case (PDF 108): the AT&T Kansas City settlement, where a Muslim employee won $5 million in punitive damages because management failed to enforce the company's own diversity policy. A hostile work environment, Gross notes, is *not* a dangerous environment like a coal mine — danger is species-relevant. It is defined "legally with reference to subspecies 'characteristics,'" as they are called in the UK — race, age over 40, ability, religion, and so on. The "environmental" quality is crucial: actionables include "nonbehaviors or inactions on the part of an employer," counterfactuals like "could have known, should have known." This is a culturally-defined affordance environment, but with juridical bite — exactly the analytic level rhetoric is poised to describe.

Second, the diagnostic critique of Gibson's residual positivism (PDF 107–108, Fig. 3.1). Gibson, despite his protests, builds his communication theory on the Shannon-Weaver mathematical-theory-of-communication chassis of 1949 — information source / transmitter / signal / receiver / destination, with noise on the side. For Gibson, "language" provides experience of things only "at secondhand," and falls outside his core theory (p.253). This is the back door through which mind/body dualism re-enters Gibson's own monism. Gross honors the Gibsonian monistic principle ("separate the natural from the artificial as if there were two environments . . . as if there were a world of mental products distinct from the world of material products" — Gibson 1979, p.130) while critiquing the positivism that prevented Gibson from theorizing the rhetorical situation as a properly environmental phenomenon.

By PDF 110–111 the chapter has earned its transition. Stephen Pinker's *How the Mind Works* (1997), Denis Dutton's *Art Instinct*, Tooby and Cosmides on Pleistocene savanna preference — all are flagged as places where evocriticism *abandons* Gibson by sliding from ecology of perception into evolutionary psychology of mental representation. Pinker's claim that geometric beauty is "the visible signal of adaptively valuable objects: safe, food-rich, explorable, learnable habitats, and fertile, healthy dates, mates, and babies" (PDF 110, quoting Pinker 1997, p.526) is the kind of association-fallacy + ending-list-rhyme that the chapter has been preparing the reader to spot. By contrast, David Herman's invocation of Radcliffe as an "*Umwelt* researcher" (chapter note 30; PDF 111) sets up the literary turn. Radcliffe, Gross announces, "composes hostility where it resides at the outskirts of personhood" — her fiction "optimizes the study of hostility because it tells the story with the right social and organic facts" (PDF 111).

## 4. The cop-car-roof case in detail

The cop-car-roof case (PDF 104–105) deserves separate paraphrastic treatment because it is the chapter's most quotable single argument and the load-bearing example of the entire affordance extension.

The setup: Mario Savio, October 1, 1964, UC Berkeley campus, climbs onto a police car in which Free Speech Movement organizer Jack Weinberg has just been arrested. Savio uses the roof as a speaking platform. Gibson would say the cop car roof has an invariant affordance — it is *climb-up-able* by adult humans, just as a chair affords sitting. This invariance does not depend on Savio's needs or personal experience. So far, orthodox Gibson.

But — and this is Gross's wedge — the action-event is *not* defined by mere climbing-and-speaking-toward. It is defined "rhetorically." The relevant exigence (a free-speech crisis), the relevant audience (the agitated student body), the relevant constraints (the legal and administrative apparatus of the university and the city), and the *stuff* (a particular car at a particular spot on a particular campus on a particular day) together constitute a scene that is consequential and persuasive *with respect to future activities*. This rhetorical event repurposes the surprising stage: "Remember it is precisely this surprising stage and its repurposing that gives us the affordance in the first place; it is variable (i.e., not invariant) according to criteria that appear most vividly in narrative form, and the relevant patterns reside substantially in an environment we call cultural or social" (PDF 105).

The decisive sentence then follows: "But to the degree that event is now fixed in the historical record, if you will, the cop car roof *is* always there to be perceived as a stage for speaking persuasively, and in this regard it is invariant." Variable at one level, invariant at another. The cop-car-roof affordance therefore has the same logical structure as the open mic at a coffee house, or the US Senate podium — it hails from the social or cultural world, distinct from the prehistoric environment shared with our earliest ancestors. The formula "*plead on me*" / "*harangue on me*" is Gross's verbal compression of this hailing. The case proves that affordance theory must be extended into a rhetorical-historical-cultural register if it is to do justice to human environments.

Why does this matter for Radcliffe? Because the abbey in *Romance of the Forest* will be theorized along identical lines. The physical abbey affords what physical abbeys afford: shelter, climbing, hiding, observing. But in the rhetorical-historical situation Radcliffe constructs, the abbey says "*shelter*" to La Motte arriving in flight from Paris debt, "*domestic asylum*" to Adeline once she settles into housekeeping, "*slaughterhouse*" once the Marquis's predation is recognized — variable as a Gibsonian object, invariant in each rhetorical situation. This is the homology that secures the chapter's two halves together.

## 5. Radcliffe's *Romance of the Forest*: reading the abbey rhetorically

The literary reading occupies PDF 112–122 (book pp.102–110), the final third of the chapter. Gross's organizing observation is grammatical: the novel is titled *The Romance OF the Forest*, not *The Romance IN the Forest* (PDF 112). The forest is the "of" — the genitive — of the passion: the environment *of* a passionate situation, an environment that will appear "terrible and sublime if genre expectations are realized, even hostile (from the Latin *hostis* or enemy)."

The back-cover description of the Oxford World's Classics edition gives Gross his social-historical setup: the novel "follows the fate of its heroine Adeline, who is mysteriously placed under the protection of a family fleeing Paris for debt. They take refuge in a ruined abbey in south-eastern France, where sinister relics of the past — a skeleton, a manuscript, and a rusty dagger — are discovered in concealed rooms" (PDF 112). Constable's c.1833 *Netley Abbey* (Fig. 3.2) is reproduced — the iconographic anchor.

The chapter-2 opening of Radcliffe is then quoted (Radcliffe pp.14–15, with a Walpole epigraph from *The Mysterious Mother* of 1768): La Motte approaches and perceives "the Gothic remains of an abbey," on a rude lawn, overshadowed by high and spreading trees, with romantic gloom around. The fabric is half-demolished; the lofty battlements are thickly enwreathed with ivy and have become the residence of birds of prey. Gross treats this passage as a tutorial. Four observations:

(1) The abbey is *first* described as "the Gothic remains of an Abbey" that was once "the pride of monkish devotion" — but the same place "might yet shelter some human being like La Motte and his crew but also, possibly, some other shady characters like the banditti they had just escaped" (PDF 113). The affordance is constitutively counterfactual: the abbey could be this, it could be that, it depends on the situation that remains "carefully undecidable as the narrative unfolds."

(2) Whose perception are we tracking when the trees "seem gloomily romantic"? The free indirect discourse intermingles author, character, and reader — so the gloominess is a real environmental fact even though no single positivist observer can be located. This is the literary form of Gibson's monism: the environment includes the rhetorical relation that produces its description.

(3) The genre-defining co-constitution: the abbey is the residence of birds of prey. Replace "birds of prey" with "giant snails on the lofty battlements" and the genre changes completely. Threatening creatures co-constitute the hostile environment; this trans-species mutuality (which Gibson asserts at p.8 of the *Ecological Approach*) is what Gothic fiction is uniquely equipped to capture (PDF 114).

(4) The mutuality is *real* even though it is fictional: "An actual La Motte and an actual abbey would by no means afford you better *Umwelt* research if you are interested in hostile and not just dangerous environments" (PDF 114). The level of description is appropriate to the rhetorical situation Radcliffe constructs.

The character-environment dialectic is then exhibited through Adeline. After La Motte's first imperfect view at p.14, the carriage is disabled and could not "even afford a shelter" (Radcliffe p.30) — and the crew returns to the most habitable part of the ruin. Once they are "settled tolerably — creatures comforted," the abbey emerges into focus as Adeline's domestic field (PDF 115). At p.34 the narrator tells us that the forest, "which at first seemed to her a frightful solitude, had lost its terrific aspect; and that edifice, whose half demolished walls and gloomy desolation had struck her mind with the force of melancholy and dismay, was now beheld as a domestic asylum, and a safe refuge from the storms of power." Adeline overlooks "the little affairs of the household" with "admirable exactness." The very same physical structure is at once "some combination of gothic remains, den of thieves, shelter, asylum, and home" — each "as real as the next" — and which redescription prevails "depends upon the situation."

The chapter's keyword *asylum* then unfolds at PDF 116 (book p.105). Asylum is "a dangerous proposition in itself" because it is "granted by the powers that be" and as such "absolute security is never a given." Security through formal asylum only makes explicit a vulnerability we all share — well-being is dependent upon others. Adeline asks the load-bearing Gothic-epistemological question at p.150: "How had my imagination deceived me!" — and then resigns: "let me still be deceived." Self-deception is not Gothic weakness but the basic condition of trust under situations of structural epistemic uncertainty. We rely upon strangers who may turn out to be saviors or worst nightmares; upon relations that may turn out to be our closest family but also our undoing; upon stories whose initial impression is the inverse of what one discovers later on. This is, Gross argues, "a special awareness of Gothic fiction: much anxiety and tension rising to the level of terror derives from dependence upon others who may not have one's best interest in mind. Or terror derives from dependence upon institutions like law, convent, and even family that form our initial disadvantages and inevitably betray our trust" (PDF 116).

The Marquis de Montalt — the lawful owner of the abbey, the predator at the political center — arrives at PDF 116–117 in this conceptual frame. Note the perfect rhetorical structure of the Marquis's offer at Radcliffe p.122: "accept from me the protection he cannot afford" — La Motte is in Lewin's hopeless situation, his territory expired. The Marquis's offer of "honorable asylum" at p.125 is the chapter's paradigm of *promise = threat*: rhetorically constructed, ironically delivered, abused by power. Adeline's recognition (p.125, paraphrased) is that the affordance is "the reverse of what's promised." Foreshadowing the abbey's eventual character as "a potential slaughterhouse" (p.338, PDF 119), the abbey is the same physical structure throughout, but at each rhetorical situation it affords a different reality.

Walter Scott's famous reading of Radcliffe — that all the seemingly supernatural events "must be accounted for on natural principles at the winding up of the story" (*Lives of Novelists*, 1825, pp.220–221) — is contested at PDF 117–118 as the "supernatural explained" formula. Gross argues this misreads Radcliffe. Take the manuscript-reading scene at p.134: in the midst of her father's journal-unto-death, Adeline pauses — "a hollow sigh seemed to pass near Adeline and she cried 'Holy Virgin, protect me!,' as she threw a fearful glance around the room: 'this is surely something more than fancy.'" Throughout the night, "obscure fleeting figures" pursue her. When she wakes in the morning, the sun-beams have "dispelled illusions of darkness," her mind is "soothed and invigorated by sleep, rejected the mystic and turbulent promptings of imagination." But — and this is Gross's point — Radcliffe immediately introduces "a new and even more threatening haunt": "this transient gleam of peace fled upon the appearance of the Marquis" (p.134). The Marquis IS the new haunt that the supposed dispelling of the supernatural reveals. Radcliffe's transitions handle the supernatural not "in terms of enlightenment" nor "in terms of religion after the fall" but in terms of "the everyday where real threats take spectral form — slipping through our fingers and evading our glances, just out of earshot — at the same time that they threaten in the most concrete ways" (PDF 118). Real terror is the stuff of uncertainties: "real terror, the novel reminds us, is fictional."

Paradiastole — Radcliffe's signature rhetorical figure — receives the chapter's most concentrated rhetorical analysis at PDF 119. Paradiastole is redescription: naming a vice as a virtue, or vice as vice by re-cast. La Motte chooses not to abandon Adeline to "the care, or rather, to the neglect, of strangers" (Radcliffe p.12); the disjunctive clause set off by commas "or rather" draws attention to "the level at which human environments are basically rhetorical." Adeline condemns the Lady Abbess: "It was her method . . . to denounce and terrify rather than to persuade and allure" (p.36) — abandoning the sacred rhetoric that should have been her calling. Gross translates the figure into Austin's terms: threats and promises in human environments operate at the *perlocutionary* level (where the words get someone to realize or do something) rather than at the *illocutionary* level (where the words mean one thing literally but do in context exactly the opposite). The ruffian says to La Motte "be comforted" — and Radcliffe sparingly uses the graphic device to emphasize irony: "These *comfortable* words renewed the terror of La Motte" (p.7). So Gibson's direct/indirect distinction does work for purely physical environments, but at the threshold of language it must give way to the rhetorical-perlocutionary analytic.

The social mapping of the novel is laid out at PDF 120 (book p.108). At one end of the social spectrum we have the domestics like Peter "in whom 'curiosity was more prevalent than fear'" (p.19) to the point where he "knew no fear" as his mind becomes "wholly occupied with the business at hand" (p.45). Peter has no emotional intelligence of his own but functions as a "vehicle" for the intelligence he is "regularly gathering and transmitting" through his own inscrutable designs (p.49). Adeline herself waits for Peter, "observing his countenance, hoping it might afford some degree of intelligence on the subject of her fears" (p.138). Peter and his fellow domestic Annette retreat from the world that matters — they "are characterized precisely in their retreat from the world, *Aus-dem-Felde-Gehen*" (Lewin's term). At the other end, the Marquis de Montalt — as far from domestic service as one can be, equally fearless. The physician on the Marquis: "from such a man you have nothing to hope, for he has scarcely anything to fear" (p.202). The Marquis's emotional life "may not be recognizable to someone of the middling sort, at the same time that it is deeply consequential for those in his sphere of influence." This is the chapter's clearest formulation of what Gross has been arguing all along: hostile environments are constituted by *political economies of emotion* in which hope and fear have unidirectional vectors keyed to power asymmetry. The Marquis is in the middle, and the entire ecosystem — "including landscape and its creatures" — is shaped by these secular powers.

Madame La Motte's recognition (p.210) — that her husband is obligated to the Marquis "whose territory afforded him a shelter from the world" at the same time as the Marquis has the power "to betray him into the hands of his enemies" — is Lewin's *Umweltkrafte*, vector fields of force pulling in incompatible directions. The surgeon's botched treatment of Theodore — "My mind is not of that frivolous kind to be affected by circumstances" (p.186) — exhibits the inverse problem: idealized knowledge becomes life-threatening when it refuses to attend to circumstance. The novel is, at its core, a research program in the rhetorical-affective ecology of dependence.

The religious-naturalistic register at PDF 121 (book p.109) prevents the analysis from reducing to secular politics. After Adeline's first night in the abbey, she looks out an open window at the forest: "it seemed as if Heaven was opening to the view." She prays: "Father of good, who made this glorious scene! I resign myself to thy hands . . ." (Radcliffe p.22). External nature is "in some sense scripted, but obscurely" — just like the civilization imposed by the Marquis. The epigraph to chapter 3 of Radcliffe gives Gross the Shakespearean intertext (*As You Like It* II.1.3–7): "Here feel we but the penalty of Adam . . ." Much later, Adeline at the foot of Montanvert views the astonishing objects around her "as if we were walking over the ruins of the world, and were the only persons who had survived the wreck" (p.265) — which, Chloe Chard's Oxford notes inform us, references Thomas Burnet's *Telluris Theoria Sacra* (Amsterdam 1694) and its theo-geology of mountains as post-Flood ruins of previous natural formations (p.390n265). La Luc articulates this theo-geology in concrete terms — though we try to comprehend the sublimity of the Deity who "first called us into being," we fail in part but can still grasp at "that portion of nature communicated." Anticipating Zunshine's reduction of religious imagery to "mere mental projection, anthropomorphism, and pathetic fallacy," La Luc responds: "Call them not the illusions of a visionary brain" (Radcliffe p.274). Gross extracts the principle: "for people of this sort, the threats and promises of a world that includes secular powers may be just as real as the threats and promises of the world that includes nonsecular powers, whether environmental in Gibson's sense, or religious in the Christian sense voiced by La Luc."

The narrative closes — Gross summarizes at PDF 121–122 — with the Marquis disposing of his brother to oversight Adeline (the niece who "stood between the Marquis and his wishes") so she can be the "instrument of God" punishing the murder of her parent. The Marquis's suicide and the king's justice are not the "supernatural explained" but rather, as Lewin would put it, an *Umstrukturierung* — transformation of the total field. The chapter's last paragraph drives home the cross-disciplinary thesis: if you want to research the hostile *work* environment, you need to know the law. But if you want to know about other sorts of human environments — hostile, romantic, awkward, thrilling — the ecological concerns of natural and social scientists "might in fact find their most inviting home in the wilds of literature" (PDF 122).

## 6. The Gothic-sentimental subgenre: Lewis, Walpole, Radcliffe

Gross's frame for the Gothic-sentimental subgenre is articulated briefly but precisely at PDF 87. The cause of death in *The Monk* — and more generally the terms of a life undone — find their genre in the sentimental version of Gothic fiction where "the emotional scene is troubled." A narrative logic that Walpole, *The Monk*, and especially Radcliffe pursue vigorously shows how "in contrast to tragedy, which undoes through character — domestic 'scenes' and other human environments can pose a real threat." This sentence does substantial subgenre-theory work in compressed form.

Walpole appears twice. First, *The Castle of Otranto* (1764) is the implicit ancestor invoked in the genealogy at PDF 87 ("Walpole, Monk, and especially Radcliffe"). Second, and more concretely, *The Mysterious Mother* (1768) supplies the epigraph to Radcliffe ch.2, which Gross reproduces at PDF 113. The first line — "How these antique towers and vacant courts / Chill the suspended soul" — and the closing — "What a kind of being is circumstance!" — give Gross "my argument in a nutshell." Circumstance, what-a-kind-of-being, is precisely what the rhetorical affordance names: the being that emerges only in the situational frame, not in the object as such.

Lewis's *The Monk* appears as the chapter's opening sting. Antonia, dying, asks whether her ruin is completed, whether she is undone. Gross treats the line as the diagnostic per excellence: Gothic-sentimental fiction is constituted by precisely those rhetorical situations in which the terms of a life have been undone — environmental hostility at the threshold of personhood. Content-filter discipline: Gross himself quotes only the ~17-word fragment and does not narrate the sexual-violence prelude beyond identifying Antonia as "rape victim." Paraphrastic discipline matches Gross's own restraint. The chapter does not return to *The Monk* — Lewis is invoked once as the genre-historical co-ordinate.

Radcliffe is the genre's exemplary practitioner because she most thoroughly *aestheticizes* the affordance structure: paradiastole as a recurring figure, the abbey as a multi-redescribable hostile environment, the supernatural as a spectral residue rather than a Scott-style explained device, the political economy of fear-hope vectors keyed to the Marquis-Adeline asymmetry, the religious-naturalistic register opened by La Luc. Walpole and Lewis are condition-supplying ancestors; Radcliffe is where the genre achieves analytic concentration.

## 7. Cross-pipeline bridges

### To Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric* (2013)

This is the strongest cross-pipeline bridge in the chapter, and the master plan flagged it as the chapter's priority external anchor. Rickert is thanked in the Acknowledgments; the conceptual parallels are extensive and specific. Rickert's project — to develop a theory of rhetoric in which kairos, attunement, ambient surround, and Heideggerian *Stimmung* become the proper objects of rhetorical analysis — runs structurally parallel to Gross's project of extending Gibsonian affordance theory into a rhetorical-cultural register. The cop-car-roof affordance is, in Rickert's language, an attunement-fact: the cop-car roof in 1965, 1985, or 2017 has been re-tuned by the historical accumulation of speakings-from-it so that it now hails listeners as a podium. The cultural-vs-social affordance distinction Gross draws at PDF 105 maps onto Rickert's distinction between cultural ambience (myth, ritual, communication-archive) and social ambience (organizational structure). Where Rickert is more willing to drop the analytic-rhetorical scaffolding in favor of an ontic ambient surround, Gross stays closer to Aristotelian rhetoric and Bitzerian situation. But both books are doing the same theoretical work — moving rhetorical analysis from the proximate speech act outward to the environmental conditions of speech-events. A dissertation framework would want to anchor approximately five `bridges-to-pipeline` edges between this chapter and Rickert's chapters on attunement and *chōra*.

### To HR-01-GROSS (2005)

The 2005 essay's claim that rhetoric is ontologically constitutive (rather than secondary to a pre-existing being) is operationalized in 2017 as the claim that affordances in human environments are constituted rhetorically. The cop-car-roof example is the concrete realization of the abstract 2005 thesis. There is no methodological revision — but there is a substantial *extension*: the 2005 essay worked from Heidegger's *Being and Time* §7 inheritance; the 2017 chapter works from Gibson's *Ecological Approach*. Both routes arrive at the conclusion that rhetorical situation is environmentally constitutive. This is a continuity, not a revision, and Phase 3G author-evolution analysis would want to log it as `extends` rather than `revises-prior-author-position`.

### To Aristotle, *Rhetoric* II

The Cydias example (Rhet. 1384b32–35) is the chapter's direct anchor; Gross also references the famous definition of rhetoric as "the art of seeing the available means of persuasion in each case" at PDF 107. The negative-affordance reading of Aristotle bridges this chapter to any pipeline that treats *Rhetoric* II as a theory of *pathē*-situated speech: shame and damaged reputation as community-mediated negative affordances. The Aristotelian heritage is not the chapter's spine but provides its conceptual ground floor.

### To Uexküll, *A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans*

Uexküll is cited indirectly twice (via Lewin and via David Herman) and Radcliffe is christened an *Umwelt* researcher in the literary mode. The bridge is real but mediated. Any dissertation framework that activates the Uexküll pipeline would want to log this chapter's *Umwelt*-talk as a bridge-via-Lewin and bridge-via-Herman, not a direct citation of the *Foray*.
