# svw-01 — Boellstorff, "For Whom the Ontology Turns: Theorizing the Digital Real" (2016)

## 1. Thesis / Aim

Boellstorff's target is a false opposition that "haunt[s] contemporary theories of technology: the opposition of the 'digital' to the 'real'" (FE, abstract, p.387). He argues this opposition "fundamentally misrepresents the relationship between the online and offline, in both directions" (FE, p.387): it obscures the many ways the online *is* real, and it wrongly implies that everything physical *is* real (ignoring physical-world play and fantasy). Drawing on the anthropological "ontological turn," his own Second Life ethnography, and a range of thinkers including Tarde, he builds "an ontological approach that problematizes both similitude and difference" as a resource "for understanding digital culture as well as for culture theory more generally" (FE, abstract, p.387). The aim is definitional/theoretical, not empirical: to license treating the virtual as real.

## 2. Structure

Introduction (pp.387–389): catalogues the false opposition across otherwise-incompatible frameworks (Hine's "embedded, embodied, everyday" internet; Turkle's "let down when they move from the virtual to the real world"; Rogers's "real and virtual divide"), and states the two-directional misrepresentation. Middle sections work through the ontological-turn literature (recursivity, alterity, the "reviews of reviews of reviews" self-description of the turn) and Boellstorff's critique that the turn shares with its opponents an over-investment in *difference*. Closing sections develop an ontology of the "real" (etymologically routed through *res* and the Latin for "have") on which the digital comes out as real. Runs pp.387–407 (body + references).

## 3. Key constructs

- **The digital is real / the online is real** (core, FE) — the article's central thesis; the virtual is a genuine modality of the real, not its negation.
- **False opposition of digital vs. real** (core, FE) — the misframing the article dismantles, shown to operate even in sophisticated frameworks.
- **Ontology of the virtual** (core) — an account of reality that "problematizes both similitude and difference," so the virtual need be neither a copy of the physical nor its Other.
- **Virtual world as ethnographic field site** (important, FAITHFUL-B) — Second Life invoked as a real site of "digital culture," continuous with his monograph *Coming of Age in Second Life*.

## 4. Findings / Claims

- The online is real in ordinary, consequential ways: "if you learn German online, you can speak it in Germany; if you lose money gambling online, you have fewer dollars" (FE, p.387).
- Opposing "virtual world" to "real world" silently equates real with physical and thereby predefines the virtual as unreal (FAITHFUL-B, p.387; on Hine).
- Correcting this requires an ontological move, not merely an empirical one: the reality of the digital is a question about *being*, which is why the ontological turn is the right resource — once it is freed from its own fixation on difference.

## 6. Relevance to the Part III social-interior passage

The drafting passage hedges that "few deployed pedagogical virtual environments had a social interior," and that where educators taught inside "socially capable virtual worlds," the world was "often a general-purpose social platform adapted to teaching rather than an environment authored for a curriculum." Boellstorff supplies the **ontological warrant** that makes the phrase "social interior" defensible in the first place: if the online is real (L1), then a virtual world genuinely *is* a place where people are together, not a metaphor for one — so a pedagogical virtual world can have a real (not merely simulated) social interior. **(P)** He does not himself study pedagogy or credit-bearing deployments, so he neither confirms nor falsifies the empirical rarity claim; his contribution is to keep the passage from sliding into the very "false opposition" (L2) it risks — treating the Second Life classroom as less-than-real space. Pairs directly with svw-02 (his Second Life co-authored fieldwork) and with the Part II ontology-of-the-virtual material.
