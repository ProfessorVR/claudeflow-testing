# svw-02 — Davis & Boellstorff, "Compulsive Creativity: Virtual Worlds, Disability, and Digital Capital" (2016)

**MLA:** Davis, Donna Z., and Tom Boellstorff. "Compulsive Creativity: Virtual Worlds, Disability, and Digital Capital." *International Journal of Communication*, vol. 10, 2016, pp. 2096–2118.
**Type:** empirical article (digital anthropology / ethnography of virtual worlds) · **Corpus role:** the cluster's *empirical anchor* — a four-year ethnography of an inhabited Second Life community, documenting a virtual world as a durable social place where people are together.
**Provenance tags:** **FE** = the source's own published claim · **FAITHFUL-DB** = close paraphrase of a specific passage · **P** = *our* interpretive link to the Part III drafting purpose.

## 1. Thesis / Aim

Davis and Boellstorff "analyze the intersection of creativity and agency by examining… disability and the digital" (FE, p.2096), through "compulsive creativity" as experienced by people living with Parkinson's disease "who are active in the virtual world Second Life" (FE, p.2096). Extending Bourdieu, they introduce **"digital embodied states"** and **"digital objectified states"** to move the analysis of capital "beyond density and quality of social networks" (FAITHFUL-DB, p.2096) and to show that "online technologies do more than 'compensate' for disability: New possibilities for selfhood and community can emerge" (FE, p.2096).

## 2. Structure

Introduction + methods/ethics (pp.2096–2098): frames compulsive creativity; notes the four-year ethnographic study of an online Parkinson's community ("Creations for Parkinson's"), including in-world dances, exploration, and 150+ hours of weekly participant observation. Literature Review — "Virtual Worlds, Disability, and Affordances" (from p.2099): defines virtual worlds and situates disability/affordance literatures. Analysis (through ~p.2114): the two focal artists (David Denton / "DB Bailey," Deborah Foster-Salsa / "Solas Nagealai"), digital embodied vs. objectified states, cultural capital. Conclusion — "What Compulsive Creativity Reveals About Digital Cultural Capital." Runs pp. 2096–2118.

## 3. Key constructs

- **Virtual world as place / social interior** (core, FE) — "Virtual worlds are places of human culture online" (p.2099); Second Life as inhabited social world, not interface.
- **Second Life (as inhabited virtual world)** (core, FAITHFUL-DB) — the specific general-purpose platform (gaming/nongaming, "such as Minecraft and Second Life") in which the community lives.
- **Being-together / community online** (core, FE) — the claim that "community can emerge," evidenced ethnographically.
- **Digital embodied states / digital objectified states** (core, FE) — the article's coined extension of Bourdieu's cultural capital into virtual-world embodiment and artifacts.
- **Virtual world as ethnographic field site** (important, FAITHFUL-DB) — four-year participant-observation methodology.

## 4. Findings / Claims

- Virtual worlds are genuine cultural places: "Virtual worlds are places of human culture online" (FE, p.2099).
- Second Life supported a real support-and-creative *community* for people with Parkinson's, not merely a compensatory tool — new forms of selfhood and community emerged (FE, p.2096).
- Cultural capital in virtual worlds is carried by embodiment and artifacts (digital embodied/objectified states), not just social-network density (FAITHFUL-DB, p.2096, ~p.2109).

## 6. Relevance to the Part III social-interior passage

This is the passage's clearest **confirming instance of the phrase it hedges around**: it is precisely a "Second Life [community] documented by anthropologists," and the world here is exactly "a general-purpose social platform adapted" to a purpose (a Parkinson's support/creative community) "rather than an environment authored for a curriculum." **(P)** So it *confirms and sharpens* the drafting claim in two ways: (a) it demonstrates a real social interior — people genuinely together, forming community (L1, L2) — establishing that socially-capable virtual worlds existed and were inhabited; and (b) it is not a credit-bearing pedagogical deployment and not curriculum-authored — it is a general-purpose platform (Second Life) appropriated by a community, which is exactly the character the passage attributes to the recent-history exceptions. The one caution for drafting: this is a *support/creative* community, not a "classroom," so it should be cited as evidence that socially-capable virtual worlds were inhabited and anthropologically documented, not as a pedagogical deployment per se.
