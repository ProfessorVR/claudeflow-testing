## 1. Thesis / Aim

Champion asks "Can a single player computer game evoke the sense of a social or cultural world?" (FE, PDF p.1). He argues that even a single-player CRPG (*Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion*) has "several key features [that] allow Oblivion to be considered as a social world," while it "fails as a rich cultural world" (FE, abstract, PDF p.1). He builds a framework distinguishing *world*, *social presence*, and *cultural presence* (hermeneutic richness), then proposes design improvements (enhanced embodiment, dynamic cognitive artefacts, social role mimicry, staggered multiplayer questing, use as a cultural-learning tool).

## 2. Structure

Abstract + Introduction (PDF p.1): the single-player-as-social-world question. §2 "Defining Virtual Worlds" (PDF pp.1–4): what a "virtual world" is; the "cyberspace vs. place" problem; social presence vs. mere co-location. §2.4.1 "A Social World Is Not Always a Cultural One" (PDF pp. ~7–8): separates social presence from cultural presence (shared values, beliefs, identity). Application to Oblivion (PDF pp.8–9): why its NPC interaction and role affordances make it a social world, and why it falls short culturally. Conclusion (PDF p.10): design directions. Unpaginated; 10 PDF pages.

## 3. Key constructs

- **Social presence / social agency** (core, FE) — the impression of shared worlds and of other agents meaningfully present, even without live human co-players.
- **Cultural presence / hermeneutic richness** (core, FE) — presence of shared values, beliefs, and identity; distinct from and not entailed by social presence.
- **Virtual world as place (not just cyberspace)** (important, FAITHFUL-C) — the "place" character a world must have; many environments are "cyberspace but not place."
- **Single-player vs. multiplayer social world** (core, FE) — a single-player world can convey social presence; multiplayer is one route to it, not a precondition.
- **Being-together / co-presence** (important) — the felt sense that another agent is there.

## 4. Findings / Claims

- "Single player games are now powerful enough to convey the impression of shared worlds with social presence and social agency" (FE, abstract, PDF p.1).
- Social presence is not simply a matter of a group of live people being co-located: "It is not clear that we can say social presence is a group of people" (FE, §2, PDF pp.2–3) — the felt presence of another can be produced by a well-designed single-player world.
- A social world is not automatically a cultural one (§2.4.1): cultural presence requires shared values/beliefs/identity, which Oblivion largely lacks.
- Oblivion qualifies as a social world on specific features but "fails as a rich cultural world"; it "could be considered as a social world" through those features (FE, PDF pp.8–9).

## 6. Relevance to the Part III social-interior passage

The drafting passage locates the "social interior" of a virtual world in "Multiplayer capability and spatial proximity-voice — the features that make a virtual world a place where students can be together." Champion is the cluster's **complicating / sharpening** source on exactly this equation. **(P)** He confirms the *place-and-being-together* intuition behind "social interior" (L1, L3) but *problematizes the sufficiency and necessity of multiplayer*: social presence can be authored into a single-player navigable world, and — via his social-vs-cultural distinction (L2) — a world can have social presence without the richer *cultural* presence that a curriculum-authored environment would aim for. For drafting, Champion warrants a more precise formulation: what a "social interior" needs is authored social presence and place-character, of which multiplayer + proximity-voice are the most common but not the only route.
