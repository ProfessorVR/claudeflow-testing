# RM-07 Deep Analysis: The Kill, Order & Pure Persuasion
## Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives* (1950), Book pp. 252–333 (PDF pp. 267–348)

---

## Overview

RM-07 is the culmination of *A Rhetoric of Motives*. Burke moves through three interlocking arguments that complete the book's architectonic. First, the "Kill" — the thesis that hierarchy necessarily generates guilt, and guilt must be discharged through victimage or scapegoating; this is the dark underside of Order, not an aberration but its structural consequence. Second, "pure persuasion" — the limit-concept of rhetoric, an appeal pursued for its own sake, autotelic in character, grounded not in the quest for advantage but in the formal demands of the three-term structure of communication (speaker-speech-spoken-to); Burke traces pure persuasion from its social instantiations (Alice in Wonderland as class-courtship, the actor-audience relation) through its metaphysical ground in the nature of symbol-using as such. Third, "ultimate identification" — the apex of the identification arc that RM-01 opened: as identification intensifies toward the absolute, it approaches mystical union with the hierarchic principle itself, the "god-term" as title-of-titles. The unit systematically surveys the "rhetorical radiance of the divine" through readings of Henry James, Hopkins, Yeats, and Eliot, culminating in the oxymoron as the literary figure of the mystical, and closes with a composite portrait of ultimate identification drawn from William James's testimony of mystic states.

## Argument (page-anchored walkthrough)

### The Kill and the Absurd (pp. 252–260)

Burke opens by revisiting Kierkegaard's reading of the Abraham-Isaac narrative to argue that Kierkegaard's "Absurd" and its cult of "the Kill" are products of a distorted emphasis: the story, read carefully, is about Abraham's *willingness* to sacrifice, not the act of killing — God stayed the hand and accepted the substitutive victim (the ram), which is the story's explicit theological point (pp. 252–253). Kierkegaard's over-particularization of the image (infanticide) at the expense of the idea (supreme sacrifice) produces a theology of the Kill that Burke regards as both intellectually distortive and historically dangerous — yielding, in the most extreme form, the Holocaust (p. 254). Burke's counter-position is that what Kierkegaard calls the "Absurd" is actually the rationally derivable behavior of dialectic itself; the operations of verbal composition and division are "quite rational" when approached methodically, and calling them "Absurd" installs a mystifying label over a transparent process (pp. 254–255).

The relation between Order, the Secret, and the Kill is announced as a structural triad: starting from any one, one encounters the others; the Kierkegaardian "Absurd" is his word for the Secret, but it implicates Order and the Kill (pp. 260, 265). Burke argues that hierarchy necessarily produces guilt — because hierarchy means invidious differentiation, and invidious differentiation means that some are above and some below, generating envy, resentment, and a sense of unworthiness in both directions — and guilt seeks its discharge in victimage. The Rattlesnake Club anecdote (p. 266) is Burke's paradigm: boys made consubstantial through the communal sacrifice of the snake, bonded by shared danger-and-triumph — a society in miniature, quickly splitting into cliques (the "rhetoric of the Scramble") once offices and dues were introduced. Consubstantiality here is established by common participation in a killing; but Burke insists the killing is not the essence, participation in a "solemn or notable experience" is (pp. 265–266).

The grammar of sacrifice is then articulated around the "paradox of substance" (p. 261): insofar as father and son are consubstantial, a father's fantasy of killing his son simultaneously destroys and saves the father — he is vicariously slain in the son while spared himself. Christian sacrifice exhibits the same logic: the ultimate father sacrifices the ultimate son; the two persons are consubstantial; the sacrifice achieves "simultaneous losing-and-having" (p. 266). This is why the cult of the Kill, wherever it appears — in Hemingway's devotional killing, in militarism, in fascist genocide — has a "pious" aura: it is an unconscious caricature of the sacrificial transcendence that belongs properly to the realm of dialectic (pp. 263–264).

### Pure Persuasion (pp. 267–293)

Pure persuasion is defined at multiple levels of specificity throughout this section. At the most formal level: the three-term structure of any communicative act (speaker, speech, spoken-to) dictates that the formal ideal of persuasion — as a persisting act — would require its own self-interference, because when the plea is answered persuasion ends (pp. 274, 267). Pure persuasion is thus the limit-concept arrived at when one asks: what is persuasion in its absolute, paradigmatic, archetype form? The answer: the saying of something for a satisfaction intrinsic to the saying, not for any extraverbal advantage. It "summons because it likes the feel of a summons" and "would be nonplused if the summons were answered" (p. 269).

Burke introduces the concept through Alice in Wonderland as a worked example of "meta-rhetoric": Dodgson's communication is not primarily sexual courtship but class courtship — appeal between youth and age seen in terms of "quality" vs. the vulgar — and its "standoffish" element is the hallmark of pure persuasion in the formal sense (pp. 267–268). Pure persuasion as an absolute exists nowhere in practice; but it can be present as a "motivational ingredient in any rhetoric, no matter how intensely advantage-seeking such rhetoric may be" (p. 269). The indication of its presence is "standoffishness" or "self-interference" as judged by the tests of acquisition.

Burke distinguishes pure persuasion from Malinowski's "phatic communion" (talk for sociable bonding): pure persuasion is far more intensely purposive — it has a "pure" purpose that, judged by the rhetoric of advantage, looks like no purpose, or like deliberate frustration of purpose (p. 270). The comparison to puzzle-solving is introduced: the puzzle-solver takes on a burden to throw it off but gains nothing material thereby; he has advanced "in the absolute" (p. 270).

The formal ground of pure persuasion is then located in the nature of symbol-using itself: language is the use of verbal symbols for purposes of appeal, formally constituted as speaker-speech-spoken-to; "standoffishness" is necessary to this form because without it the appeal could not be maintained — "if union is complete, what incentive can there be for appeal?" (p. 271). Rhetorically, courtship is possible only insofar as there is division. The paradox: "for a persuasion that succeeds, dies. To go on eternally (as a form does) it could not be directed merely toward attainable advantages" (p. 274).

Burke then conducts a sustained analysis of how pure persuasion operates in the capitalist economy of "new needs" — each attained object creates the need for the next, preserving the formal structure of perpetual self-courting without the self-interference of deliberate standoffishness: "the form of persuasion is permanently maintained" by the shifting of objectives (pp. 274–275). The Keats's Grecian Urn figure (p. 275): single need forever courted via self-interference vs. the rat race of new needs — formally equivalent, each perpetuating the persuasive act.

Pure persuasion is traced dialectically to its culmination in the "god-term": as one progresses from words for positive things, to titles, to title-of-titles, one arrives at the ultimate generalization of persuasion — prayer as pure beseeching, addressed not to an object that might answer it but to the hierarchic principle itself "where the answer is implicit in the address" (p. 276). The "god" of pure persuasion is the "dialectic god," equivalent to the Greek Peitho (Goddess of Persuasion) where Greek religion and science overlapped (pp. 276–277). Burke closes this section by cautioning that pure persuasion must not be confused with "religious" persuasion: the latter is often tremendously sanguine, even bloody (the Apocalypse) (pp. 291–292).

A sustained section on the hierarchic psychosis (pp. 278–290) diagnoses how psychoanalytic and institutional explanations — by deriving social motives from exclusively sexual or individual sources — conceal the "mystery" of class. The proper ground is formal/dialectical: man as a "classifying animal" (homo dialecticus) has a generic aptitude for classification that is the prior condition; social hierarchy is a special case thereof (p. 283). The "inferiority complex" is really a judgment one passes on oneself from the standpoint of "some real or imagined court of conscience" — another variant of the "courtship" theme (p. 282).

### Rhetorical Radiance of the "Divine" (pp. 294–333)

This long subsection subdivides into nine sections: Henry James on the deity of "Things"; "social ratings" of images in James; rhetorical names for God; the "range of mountings"; elation and accidie in Hopkins; Yeats's "Byzantium" poems; Eliot's early poems and *Quartets*; principle of the oxymoron; and ultimate identification.

**Henry James (pp. 294–298).** Burke reads James's preface to *The Spoils of Poynton* as key to James's entire motivation: the "Things" — household objects — acquire a hieratic radiance that James frames in religious vocabulary (mysteries, household gods, place of worship). Burke's "socioanagogic" method reads this radiance as the signature of the hierarchic motive: objects infused with the spirit of social privilege have an "enigmatic" glow that exceeds their empirical reality (p. 296).

**Social ratings of images (pp. 296–298).** Burke develops from Austin Warren's essay on James a method for disclosing hierarchic content in images: where James has used an image to build up a character of known social/moral status, reversing the direction allows the critic to interpret the image's hierarchical placement. This is the "pontification" device — bridging social and natural hierarchies through identified objects (p. 229 [sic, read p. 298]).

**Rhetorical names for God (pp. 298–301).** The single most concentrated conceptual passage in the unit: a catalog of "god-terms" — surrogate names for the dialectical completion of the linguistic process. The list includes: substance, nature, history, society, necessity, truth, logos, hierarchy, authority, consubstantiality and communication, nation, personality, final cause, soul, freedom, good, "pure persuasion," and many others (pp. 299–300). The list functions as an inventory of terministic screens through which the ultimate rhetorical motive is refracted.

**Range of mountings (pp. 301–313).** The symbol of "mounting" is analyzed into its motivational strands: kinesthetic (height as effort-and-exhilaration); erotic (sexual mounting from Prufrock's stairs to the Venusberg); maternal (the mountain as parental source); social (the "climber" in career-terms); ethical (the *Excelsior!* motive); fecal-scatological (Yeats's golden bird as esthetic output, Hopkins's hourglass image); and ultimately anagogic (the Upward Way / via negativa reaching the title-of-titles, then the Downward Way returning to particulars now infused with universal substance) (pp. 301–313).

**Hopkins (pp. 313–316).** Burke analyzes the arc of Hopkins's career — from lush sensory verse, through priestly renunciation, to the "redemption" of the sensory motive in a third movement where nature becomes Christ's "pontification" to God — as the paradigm of transcendence-not-by-subtraction-but-by-inclusion. "The Windhover" exemplifies elation in its purest form; the "terrible sonnets" record accidie — what happens when the identification of self-with-nature-with-God is disrupted (pp. 314–316).

**Yeats (pp. 316–318).** The two Byzantium poems are read against the "Last Poems" degradation: the golden bird as transcended fecal ambiguity vs. "the foul rag-and-bone shop of the heart" — the loss of the range of mountings leaving only unredeemed refuse (pp. 316–318).

**Eliot (pp. 318–324).** The early poems (Prufrock, Waste Land) are characterized as down-turning moods, fragile lamentation, unfulfilled courtship — proximity to accidie. The *Quartets* represent the full dialectical ascent: the resources of Heraclitean paradox, the intersection of temporal and eternal, the fire-and-rose oxymoron of *Little Gidding* (pp. 318–324).

**Principle of the oxymoron (pp. 324–328).** Burke argues that the mystic communicates ultimately in terms of the oxymoron — the figure that combines contradictory elements within a single expression — because mystical experience reports a condition of radical passivity in which all nervous impulses "attitudinally glow" simultaneously, without the repression required for ordinary purposive action; this is "noetic," felt as revelation (pp. 331–332). The oxymoron is the literary analogue of this neurological-cum-mystical state. The analysis of the *katharma* (pp. 328–329) — the ritually unclean prisoner who is also sacred, the scapegoat who is both loathsome and holy — exemplifies the oxymoron at its structural center.

**Ultimate identification (pp. 328–333).** Burke compiles, from William James's *Varieties of Religious Experience*, a composite account of mystic testimony: dissolution of individuality into "boundless being," union with the "infinite ocean of God," the sense of an "open secret of Being," the suspension of the ordinary filter of sensory categories (pp. 329–331). This state is characterized as "ultimate identification" — identification pushed to its absolute limit: the infinitesimally frail identifying with the infinitely powerful (p. 326). Burke frames it as the rhetorical-dialectical completion: "the motive that attains its ultimate identification in the thought, not of the universal holocaust, but of the universal order — as with the rhetorical and dialectical symmetry of the Aristotelian metaphysics, whereby all classes of beings are hierarchally arranged in a chain or ladder or pyramid of mounting worth, each kind striving towards the perfection of its kind ... while the strivings of the entire series head in God as the beloved cynosure and sinecure, the end of all desire" (p. 333).

## Key Concepts

**the Kill / victimage** [spine: hierarchy, the negative] — The structural mechanism by which hierarchy discharges the guilt it generates: once social ordering has produced invidious distinctions, the accumulated moral pressure finds an outlet in the scapegoating of a sacrificial victim. The Kill is not the essence of the sacrificial motion; the essence is *participation in a solemn shared experience*, of which the killing is the occasion (pp. 252–266). [provenance: burke-direct]

**consubstantiality through shared sacrifice** [spine: consubstantiality] — The Rattlesnake Club paradigm: participants made consubstantial by common involvement in a kill. Burke insists this is not the kill per se but the shared solemnity; consubstantiality arises from "common participation in a notable or solemn experience" (p. 266). Extends RM-01's definition of consubstantiality into the social-bonding dimension. [provenance: burke-direct]

**the Absurd (Kierkegaard's)** [spine: hierarchy, mystery] — Burke's critique: Kierkegaard labels the rational operations of dialectic (verbal composition and division) with a mystifying term that makes apparent irrationality seem foundational. The Absurd is dialectic approached as a curse rather than an instrument; it produces a "cult of the Kill" that mistakes byproduct for essence (pp. 254–256). [provenance: burke-direct]

**pure persuasion** [SPINE CONCEPT] — The limit-concept of rhetoric. In its absolute form: the saying of something for a satisfaction intrinsic to the saying, not for extraverbal advantage; an appeal for its own sake. Exists nowhere as a pure phenomenon but is present as a motivational ingredient in any rhetoric. Formally grounded in the three-term structure of communication: speaker-speech-spoken-to. The technical hallmark is self-interference or "standoffishness" — the supplying of obstacles to one's own successful appeal in order to perpetuate the formal act of persuasion (pp. 267–274). [provenance: burke-direct]

**self-interference / standoffishness** [spine: pure persuasion] — The structural requirement of perpetuating the persuasive act: if union is complete there is no incentive for appeal; if the plea is answered, persuasion becomes something else. Pure persuasion requires its own self-interference to go on eternally as a form (pp. 271, 274). The basis of courtship-tabus, the "athleticism of self-denial," priestly celibacy, and the neurotic artist's hesitation to be cured (pp. 271–272). [provenance: burke-direct]

**god-term / title-of-titles** [spine: hierarchy, pure persuasion, terministic screen] — The ultimate terminus of the dialectical process: starting from words for positive things, one ascends through titles, then titles-of-titles, arriving at the maximum generalization that functions as "God" in the linguistic system. Pure persuasion culminates in the god-term; prayer as pure beseeching is addressed to the hierarchic principle itself (pp. 275–277). A "dialectic god" is the god arrived at through the nature of the linguistic medium itself (p. 277). [provenance: burke-direct]

**rhetorical names for God / god-term catalog** [spine: hierarchy, terministic screen] — Burke's inventory of surrogate god-terms: substance, nature, history, society, necessity, logos, hierarchy, authority, consubstantiality, nation, freedom, pure persuasion, and many others (pp. 299–300). Each is a terministic screen that refracts the same ultimate dialectical motive. [provenance: burke-direct]

**socioanagogic criticism** [spine: hierarchy] — Burke's method for reading the hierarchic content of images, objects, and social configurations: by treating a text's moral/social judgments about characters as transparent, one can "reverse the direction" and read the image as a hierarchical placement, disclosing the enigmatic signature of the hierarchic motive in things that present themselves as aesthetically or naturalistically neutral (pp. 296–298). [provenance: burke-direct]

**range of mountings** [spine: hierarchy] — A hypothetical "ideal paradigm" for the symbol of mounting or ascending: kinesthetic, erotic, maternal, social-careerism, ethical, fecal-scatological, anagogic, and mystical-absolute strands. A symbol achieves maximum "vibrancy" if all are simultaneously operative (pp. 301–313). [provenance: burke-direct]

**elation and accidie** [spine: hierarchy, mystery] — Hopkins's arc: the state of successful identification (self with nature with God) produces elation; when the identification fails, what remains is "the self alone" — accidie, mystic drought, the sour dough of selfhood. The same structure applies to Yeats's Byzantium poems and Eliot's trajectory from the *Waste Land* to the *Quartets* (pp. 313–318). [provenance: burke-direct]

**principle of the oxymoron** [spine: pure persuasion, hierarchy] — The mystic communicates in terms of the oxymoron because mystical experience reports the simultaneous "glow" of contradictory impulses; the oxymoron is the literary analogue of this non-repressive condition. Identification is itself a kind of transcendence; identification pushed to its absolute is mystical identification of the infinitely frail with the infinitely powerful (pp. 324–328). [provenance: burke-direct]

**ultimate identification** [spine: identification, consubstantiality, hierarchy] — The apex of the identification arc: identification carried to its dialectical limit becomes mystical union — dissolution of individual boundaries into "boundless being," "immersion in the infinite ocean of God," consubstantiality with the universal order. Burke frames this as the rhetorical culmination: the motive that heads in "God as the beloved cynosure and sinecure, the end of all desire" (p. 333). [provenance: burke-direct]

**katharma / sacred-loathsome scapegoat** [spine: hierarchy, the negative] — The ambiguous figure who is simultaneously sacred and unclean — the criminal-prisoner held for a public sacrifice in Athens; Luther's Christ as the greatest criminal; the sacrificial king who is both bleeding victim and victorious lord (pp. 328–329). The oxymoron is here not merely a figure of speech but a figure of the social logic of victimage. [provenance: burke-direct]

**homo dialecticus** [spine: pure persuasion, terministic screen] — Burke's anthropological formula: man as the "classifying animal" whose formal aptitude for classification (in the linguistic/dialectical sense) is prior to social classification; social hierarchy is a special case of the general formal ground (p. 283). Contrasted with homo faber (tool-using) as the ground of distinctly human motivation (pp. 289–290). [provenance: burke-direct]

**Ersatzmystik (substitute mysticisms)** [spine: hierarchy, mystery] — Secular analogues of mystical identification in which means become ends: speed, sex, money, crime, war — each a vocation wherein some instrumentality of living is transformed into a "demonic purpose," producing the absorption and entrancement characteristic of genuine mysticism (pp. 331–332). [provenance: burke-direct]

**hierarchic psychosis** [spine: hierarchy, mystery] — Burke's diagnosis of the social pathology in which the mystery of class generates neurosis: the "inferiority complex" is a social judgment one passes on oneself, a variant of courtship; psychoanalysis, by re-describing class motives in sexual or familial terms, perpetuates mystification (pp. 278–284). [provenance: burke-direct]

**pontification / bridging device** [spine: hierarchy, identification] — The critical method of treating a known moral/social hierarchy as a vehicle for identifying it with images or objects, so that natural, artificial, and social orders are "bridged" through a common medium. Analogous to the priest's function of bridging divine and secular orders (pp. 298, 301). [provenance: burke-direct]

## Identification & Consubstantiation Development

RM-07 advances the identification arc of the entire book to its culmination in three distinct moves. First, the Rattlesnake Club passage (p. 266) provides the clearest paradigm of consubstantiality *as social bond*: members are made consubstantial not by propositional agreement but by joint participation in a solemn shared experience. This extends RM-01's substance-theoretic consubstantiality (shared sensations, concepts, images, attitudes making men "substantially one") into the register of communal ritual and enacted sacrifice — the dimension of collective action rather than merely shared standing.

Second, the "socioanagogic" catalog of god-terms (pp. 299–300) explicitly names "consubstantiality" and "communication" as items in the list of rhetorical names for God — confirming that consubstantiality is not merely a psychological or rhetorical mechanism but the linguistic-dialectical ground of the social as such. Any moment of genuine communion is already, in Burke's framework, a trace of the ultimate identification.

Third, ultimate identification (pp. 328–333) is the limit-case toward which all identification in the Rhetoric has been moving: as identification intensifies — from property-sharing (RM-01), through courtship (RM-06), to the mystic's union with the divine — it becomes indistinguishable from the identification of the individual with a universal order, the hierarchic principle itself. This is the endpoint of the "imaging of transformation" that RM-00 announced as the master motif of the book's opening.

## Attitude (Incipient-Act) Signals

Burke's Attitude as the 6th dramatistic term surfaces in several important passages in RM-07:

1. **Mountain-climbing as "attitude" literally enacted** (p. 302): Burke notes that the Alpinist, "while carrying out an 'attitude' in the most literal way possible (as were you to 'do a poem' on murder by actually murdering someone), its motives are wholly 'esthetic'." Here Attitude is explicitly named as an incipient-act whose full enactment is the climbing.

2. **Mystic "attitudinal glow"** (pp. 331–332): In the neurological account of mystical experience, Burke describes the state as one in which all nervous impulses "attitudinally glowed at once" — remaining in a "half-way status nascendi," the condition of pure incipience. This is Attitude at its most concentrated: the suspended-act character of the mystic state is precisely the condition of every impulse held as incipient simultaneously.

3. **Standoffishness as attitude** (pp. 269–271): The "standoffish element" of pure persuasion — the deliberate maintenance of a non-consummated appeal — is an attitudinal stance, a systematic incipience held against the drive toward full acquisition. The actor-audience relation (p. 270) is the clearest pure instantiation.

4. **Dante and Eliot's transitional "new motive"** (pp. 320–321): Burke reads the extra presence in the *Waste Land*'s "What the Thunder Said" as the poet's divided identity — an earlier and later "attitude" cohabiting in one legal person; the transition between stances is the incipient character of the new motive emerging.

## Game-Behavior Application (Anticipatory)

**The Kill / victimage → Competitive play, toxic-community scapegoating** [anticipatory-projection]
The mechanism of hierarchy→guilt→victimage that Burke diagnoses in Order maps directly onto competitive and PvP game contexts. In ranked multiplayer environments, hierarchical ordering (leaderboards, skill tiers, win/loss records) generates invidious distinction; that distinction generates accumulated resentment and shame; the community's need to discharge accumulated guilt tends to produce scapegoating — blaming a particular player for collective failure, "carrying" discourse, toxicity directed at identifiable low-performers or out-groups. The Rattlesnake Club model (consubstantiality through shared kill, quickly followed by cliques and quarrels over offices) is a precise miniature of how competitive game communities form and fracture. Maps to Calleja's **Shared** involvement dimension (collective identity formation through competitive ritual) and **Affective** dimension (emotional valence of hierarchy-induced guilt and its discharge). Pentad: **Purpose** (the community's need to discharge guilt) + **Act** (scapegoating as sacrificial act) + **Scene** (the competitive game environment as hierarchically ordered scene). [anticipatory-projection]

**Pure persuasion → Autotelic play / flow / play-for-its-own-sake** [anticipatory-projection]
Burke's pure persuasion is the formal archetype of any activity pursued for a satisfaction intrinsic to the activity itself, not for extraverbal advantage. This is structurally identical to Csikszentmihalyi's "flow" and to what game scholars call autotelic play — play engaged for its own sake rather than for extrinsic rewards. The formal hallmark Burke identifies (self-interference; "standoffishness"; the deliberate maintenance of challenge to preserve the form of the game-act) maps onto the design principle of difficulty calibration: a game that is too easy "kills" the appeal (the plea is answered); a game maintaining challenge perpetuates the "form of persuasion." The Keats's Urn image (p. 275) — "single need, forever courted" — is formally equivalent to the player in flow, perpetually approaching mastery without exhausting it. Maps to Calleja's **Ludic** dimension (rule-governed engagement for its own sake) and **Performative** dimension (skill expression as intrinsically satisfying). Pentad: **Act** (the autotelic play-act) + **Attitude** (the incipient-act of sustained engagement, never fully consummated). [anticipatory-projection]

**Ultimate identification → Incorporation (Calleja) at its fullest** [anticipatory-projection]
This is the keystone mapping. Burke's "ultimate identification" — the mystic union of the infinitely frail individual with the infinitely powerful universal — is the rhetorical limit-case that the integration with Calleja's *incorporation* maps onto. Calleja defines incorporation as the state in which the player is wholly consubstantiated with the game-world: not merely using an avatar but constituting a second locus of subjectivity, present *in* the game-world such that its demands are felt as one's own. Burke's composite portrait of mystic experience — "individuality seems to dissolve and fade away into boundless being"; "immersed in the infinite ocean of God"; "a soul-sight of that divine clue and unseen thread which holds the whole congeries of things" — is the rhetorical grammar of what deep incorporation feels like from the inside: the felt merger of individual and universal purpose, the dissolution of the boundary between player-self and game-world-self. The formal condition Burke identifies — pure persuasion's self-interference as the ground of sustained appeal — also maps onto the design condition of incorporation: the game-world must maintain its "otherness," its resistance, its "standoffishness" (difficulty, mystery, unfamiliarity) to sustain the player's reaching-toward rather than full consummation. Maps to Calleja's **Incorporation** (peak state) as well as **Shared** (collective mystical bond via communal play ritual) and **Affective** (the felt quality of the incorporated state). Pentad: **Purpose** (the identification-drive; the aim toward consubstantiality with the game-world) + **Act** (the act of full incorporation) + **Attitude** (the incipient state of impending total absorption). [anticipatory-projection]

**Oxymoron + elation/accidie → Flow break and player disengagement** [anticipatory-projection]
Hopkins's arc — elation when the three-way identification (self-nature-God) holds; accidie when it fails — provides a model for understanding "flow break" in games: when the conditions sustaining the player's identification with the game-world (challenge calibration, narrative coherence, community belonging) are disrupted, the player is left with "the self alone" — the exposed, self-conscious, no-longer-immersed state that game designers associate with disengagement and dropout. Maps to Calleja's **Affective** dimension (emotional arc of engagement and dropout). [anticipatory-projection]

**Socioanagogic method → Game-world's hidden hierarchy as rhetorical screen** [anticipatory-projection]
Burke's method of reading objects and settings for their "hierarchic content" — the enigmatic signature of social privilege in what presents itself as merely decorative — applies to game-world design: the choice of setting, character assets, upgrade aesthetics, and achievement iconography all carry hierarchic content that shapes player identification with social positions within the game-world's order. Maps to Calleja's **Spatial** and **Narrative** dimensions (the game-world as hierarchically inflected environment). [anticipatory-projection]

## Cross-Pipeline Bridges

**To Grammar of Motives (GM pipeline):**
- The pentad ratios **Purpose-Act** are primary here: the sacrificial act is motivated by the teleological Purpose of discharging hierarchy-guilt; pure persuasion as Act is grounded in the Purpose of sustaining the form of communication itself.
- The **paradox of substance** (consubstantiality through killing) is RM-07's most direct carry-over from the Grammar's discussion of the scene-act ratio and the substance paradox; the father-son consubstantiality passage (p. 261) is explicitly labeled "grammatically."
- The Attitude (6th term) surfaces in the mountain-climbing passage, the mystic "attitudinal glow," and standoffishness — all confirming the Grammar addendum's seeding of Attitude as incipient act.

**To Calleja *In-Game* (PIM pipeline):**
- **Incorporation** ↔ **Ultimate identification**: This is the book's culminating statement of the consubstantiation↔incorporation keystone. The mystic composite portrait in the final pages directly translates into the phenomenology of deep incorporation.
- **Shared involvement** ↔ **Kill/victimage + consubstantiality through sacrifice**: The Rattlesnake Club paradigm grounds the social-bonding dimension of Shared involvement in a mechanism of collective sacrifice; competitive scapegoating is its toxic variant.
- **Ludic involvement** ↔ **Pure persuasion**: Autotelic formal engagement, self-interference as design principle.
- **Affective involvement** ↔ **Elation/accidie arc**: The felt quality of identification-sustained vs. identification-disrupted.

**To RM-01 (Identification and Consubstantiality):**
- RM-07 completes the arc RM-01 opened. RM-01 introduced identification as compensatory to division; RM-07 shows identification pushed to its absolute limit becoming mystical union. The god-terms catalog's inclusion of "consubstantiality and communication" closes the loop.

**To RM-06 (Courtship):**
- Pure persuasion is courtship at its most refined: "only through interference could one court continually." Alice in Wonderland is placed explicitly in the same bin as *The Book of the Courtier* (p. 268). The formal ground of courtship (hierarchy-entails-division-entails-appeal) is the formal ground of pure persuasion.
