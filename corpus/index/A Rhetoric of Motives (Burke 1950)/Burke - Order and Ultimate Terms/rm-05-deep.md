## Overview

Part III of *A Rhetoric of Motives* opens with Burke's most systematic treatment of terminology as a stratified, hierarchically organized domain. The central analytical move is the distinction among three orders of vocabulary — positive, dialectical, and ultimate — arranged in ascending levels of abstraction and motivational scope. A positive term names a perceivable, locatable thing; a dialectical term names a contested principle or idea that acquires meaning only through opposition and context; an ultimate term supplies the "guiding idea" or "principle of principles" that organizes all the competing dialectical voices into a single developmental sequence rather than leaving them in parliamentary jangle. Burke calls this third level "ultimate" rather than "mystical" to avoid foreclosing the analysis, and then spends the unit demonstrating how the concept operates across three major cases: Platonic dialectic, the Marxist persuasion, and Mannheim's "sociology of knowledge" as a would-be alternative. The final section introduces Malinowski's "context of situation" as the positive-level analytic complement to the higher terministic orders. Throughout, the unit advances a key thesis for the integration pipeline: a terministic vocabulary is always implicitly hierarchized by whatever "god-term" sits at its apex, and that apex term is the fulcrum of rhetorical power because it ranks all other motives in relation to itself.

## Argument — Page-Anchored Walkthrough

**"Positive, Dialectical, and Ultimate Terms" (pp. 183–189)**

Burke opens by distinguishing three strata of vocabulary by their logical form and ontological reference. Positive terms are grounded in perception and sensation: they name the *hic et nunc* — things locatable in time and place, definable by genus and species. Burke cites Bentham's "real entities" as the positive touchstone and Kantian categories as their philosophical correlate: the understanding clamps a concept onto a bundle of sensory intuitions, yielding "This is a house." The positive ideal is a physicalist vocabulary reducible to terms of observable motion; poetry participates in this order to the extent that its imagery names visibly and tangibly existing things (p. 183).

The second order — dialectical terms — encompasses what Bentham called "fictitious entities": "capitalism," "Elizabethanism," principles of positivism itself. These cannot be located in space-time; they are "polar," taking their meaning from what they are contrasted with. "Capitalism" looks different when set against "feudalism" than when set against "socialism" (p. 184–185). Bentham attempted to handle them through paraphrase ("phraseoplerosis") and image-discounting ("archetypation") because no single equivalent term captures their full meaning. Burke extends this: dialectical terms belong to the realm of action and ideas rather than perception and knowledge; they require a behavioristic-plus-imaginal test because actual behavior (a congregation erecting an office tower higher than its own church spire) may be the fullest expression of the real motive, not the verbal statement telegraphed to news agencies (pp. 185–187).

The third order is "ultimate" vocabulary (p. 186–189). Where a merely dialectical confrontation leaves competing voices in a "parliamentary jangle" — each partial, each compromised, each forced into "horse-trading" — an ultimate order places those competing voices in a hierarchy or developmental sequence with a single "guiding idea" behind the diversity. The step from principles to a "principle of principles" is both the fulfillment of the dialectical order and its transcendence. Burke illustrates through Plato's *Republic* Book VIII–IX: the four imperfect governments (timocracy, oligarchy, democracy, tyranny) are arranged not as a flat list but developmentally, each defined by its characteristic human disposition (honor, wealth, freedom, protection) and the whole series graded by distance from a single norm. This transforms what would otherwise be four competing dialectical voices into an ultimate order (pp. 188–189).

**"Ultimate Elements in the Marxist Persuasion" (pp. 189–197)**

Burke turns to Marxism as the contemporary instance of an ultimate vocabulary. Its rhetorical power, he argues, derives directly from the fact that it is ultimate rather than merely dialectical: the class hierarchy (feudal → bourgeois → proletarian) is not a parliamentary menu of competing interests but a developmental series grounded, on the Marxist account, in the very nature of the universe. The representative of the proletariat can claim to speak not only for particular interests but for the "grand design of the entire historical sequence" (p. 191). Each local act of participation becomes a microcosm of the total development, giving even quotidian organizational work a ritual or formal dignity it would lack in a merely contractual or transactional vocabulary.

Burke uses Hegel's "concrete universal" as the philosophical scaffolding: any moment in a developmental series can represent the universal essence of the development in its entirety, just as the bud represents not only itself but the fruit, seed, decline, and futurity beyond it (p. 190). Marx satirizes the Hegelian version while replicating its deep structure; "turning Hegel upside down" does not eliminate the ultimate order, it inherits it.

Burke extends this to a sociological example of transcendence-within-constraint. Discussing the structural bind confronting any member of an oppressed group — where individual attempts at transcending inferior status can seem like "disloyalty" to one's class — Burke uses Shylock's "I am a Jew" speech from *The Merchant of Venice* as an illustration (pp. 193–194). Shylock's "vengeance" is presented as the most generalized available statement of the response to parliamentary jangle without ultimate resolution: it accepts the opponent's rules and aims only to reverse the roles, never transcending the terms themselves. Burke reads Richard Wright's *Native Son* as a parallel case at the level of fictional-social protest. Marxism's rhetorical advantage in such situations is precisely that it offers an ultimate vocabulary enabling an oppressed minority to confront the world "at once specifically and generically" — to act from within a particular historical position while seeing that position as part of a universal development (pp. 194–195).

Lenin's *What Is to Be Done?* is the operational embodiment of this principle (pp. 196–197). Burke draws on Bentham's distinction between matters of fact (*quid factum*) and matters of opinion (*quid faciendum*) to note that the very question "What is to be done?" is inherently in the realm of rhetoric rather than positivist science. Lenin's strategy calls for a new kind of consciousness in which the worker sees himself not as Mr. So-and-So managing day-to-day contingencies but as "the Proletarian" — a figure whose local participation is simultaneously a participation in the "total dialectic" of universal historical necessity (p. 197). This is Burke's clearest formulation of how an ultimate vocabulary transforms identification: it allows local consubstantiality (worker with class) to extend to universal consubstantiality (class with historical direction), the two levels linked by the shared ultimate term.

**"'Sociology of Knowledge' vs. Platonic 'Myth'" (pp. 197–203)**

Burke now tests the limits of the ultimate-vocabulary framework by examining Mannheim's *Ideology and Utopia*. Mannheim's "sociology of knowledge" attempts to move beyond parliamentary relativism toward a systematic "relationism" — an exact science of the relations between ideologies and their social conditions. Burke calls this "pro-ultimate": it presses toward a higher precision and orderliness than mere relativism, but it cannot quite achieve the ultimate order because it lacks a governing principle of principles; it has only the regulative ideal of bias-elimination (pp. 197–199).

Burke reconstructs what a Platonic dialogue version of Mannheim's project would look like: (1) competing voices each unmasking the others' ideological bias; (2) Socratic dialectical transcendence building generalizations beyond partisan competition; (3) Socratic vision of the pure idea; (4) translation of the pure idea into a mythic image; (5) Mannheim's move of treating steps 1 and 4 as though they were of the same kind, applying the same discounting method to both ideological bias and mythic motivation (pp. 200–202). The problem is that the mythic image in the Platonic dialogue was not simply an elevated ideology but a figure for a motive that transcends discursive reason altogether — a motive that lies beyond the reach of ideological error or sociological knowledge. By flattening ideology and myth into the same discountable plane, Mannheim's method would, if fully successful, deprive society of its primary motivational energy. As Burke formulates it: "bias is false promise, but it is promise" (p. 201).

The six-step summary Burke gives (pp. 201–202) crystallizes the argument: the mythic image represents a motive genuinely beyond ideas, reachable only through a disciplined dialectical ascent through sensory images and then through ideas to the end of ideas. To subject it to the same sociological discounting applied to partisan ideologies destroys the qualitative distinction between the ideological and the ultimate.

**"'Mythic' Ground and 'Context of Situation'" (pp. 203–207)**

The final section of the unit opens out the analysis in two directions. On the one side, Burke explores the esthetic myth — the Hart Crane-style symbolist poem in which image transcends idea — as a variant of ultimate motivation. The resistance many readers feel toward discursive intrusions in highly imagistic poetry (Keats's final stanza of "Ode on a Grecian Urn," Coleridge's "moral" appended to *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*) reflects the expectation that in an ultimate symbolic mode, the mythic image should be the culmination, not the idea (pp. 203–205). Burke's remark about "joycing" Keats's line — experimentally modifying "beauty" and "truth" by punning and tonal cognates to find an enigmatic and possibly obscene undermeaning — illustrates his methodological commitment to probing the latent motivational layers beneath explicit verbal surfaces.

On the other side, Burke introduces Malinowski's "context of situation" (from his supplement to Ogden and Richards' *The Meaning of Meaning*) as the positive-order analytic for any verbal act's nonverbal scene (pp. 205–207). Malinowski found that translating Polynesian tribal texts required describing the customs, social psychology, and tribal organization implicit in each utterance, because no dictionary equivalent existed. His concept of "context of situation" generalizes this requirement: the meaning of a verbal act cannot be separated from the nonverbal scene in which it is embedded. Burke connects this to Benthamite and Marxist situational analysis, and to Richard McKeon's discussion of Peter Abelard's *Sic et Non*, which deployed proto-contextual canons (consideration of time, place, person, cause of statement) to resolve apparent contradictions. All such rhetorical discounting of extraverbal circumstances operates in the positive-to-dialectical range; the "context of situation" itself, insofar as it transcends the verbal act, carries a minimal "mythic" element — even the most positivistic reduction of a scene is still only "suggestive," not identical with what it describes.

Burke closes the unit by contrasting two overall methods: modern liberal science (Mannheim's sociology of knowledge), which aims to advance from opinion to knowledge by discounting ideology, and the Platonic dialogue method, which proceeds from rhetorical partisanship through dialectical resolution to an ultimate order made available through mythic figuration. The rhetorical resources of the dialectical method include, he notes, not only the appeal of resolution but the dramatic appeal of the agon itself and the appeal of what he calls *enargeia* — the New Vision's vividness as it transcends the imagery that prepared it (p. 207).

## Key Concepts

**positive terms** [core analytic vocabulary]
The first order of vocabulary: names grounded in perception and sensation, definable per genus et differentiam, locatable in time and place. The physicalist ideal reduces them to terms of observable motion. Poetry participates in this order insofar as its imagery names tangible, visible things. `provenance: burke-direct`

**dialectical terms** [core analytic vocabulary]
The second order: "polar" terms (capitalism, Elizabethanism, positivism) that acquire meaning through contrast rather than through positive reference. They belong to the realm of action and ideas rather than perception. Their full meaning requires paraphrase, behavioral testing, and imaginal reading because they are never completely expressed in a single utterance. `provenance: burke-direct`

**ultimate terms / ultimate order** [spine — terministic screen]
The third and governing order: vocabulary organized by a "guiding idea" or "principle of principles" that arranges competing dialectical voices in a developmental hierarchy rather than leaving them in parliamentary jangle. The ultimate order transcends and fulfills the dialectical order. Its formal correlate in narrative is the plot structure whereby each stage prepares the next. `provenance: burke-direct`

**god-term** [spine — terministic screen, hierarchy]
Not named as such in this unit but operationally present: the apex term of an ultimate vocabulary — the "principle of principles" — that ranks all other motives below itself. In the Platonic ultimate, the Form of the Good is the god-term; in the Marxist ultimate, the direction of historical necessity serves this function. The god-term makes a vocabulary into a hierarchy of motives rather than a mere list. `provenance: burke-direct`

**hierarchy** [spine]
The structural form taken by an ultimate vocabulary: a graded, developmental series in which each stage represents the universal principle of the whole, yet each stage has its own "moment" of completeness. Hierarchy is the formal difference between an ultimate order and a parliamentary compromise. `provenance: burke-direct`

**the negative** [spine — implicit]
Implied throughout the treatment of dialectical terms as "polar": dialectical vocabulary gains meaning through negation and contrast, so the negative is the generative engine of the dialectical order. The shift to an ultimate order does not abolish the negative but subsumes it in a developmental logic. `provenance: burke-direct`

**terministic screen** [spine]
Each vocabulary — positive, dialectical, ultimate — constitutes a screen that directs attention and deflects it. The positive terministic screen is physicalist and perceptual; the dialectical screen is partisan and polar; the ultimate screen ranks motives hierarchically. Switching among screens changes what counts as a motive. `provenance: burke-direct`

**Marxist persuasion**
Burke's case study in ultimate vocabulary. Marxism's rhetorical power derives from its capacity to give local participation in class struggle the quality of participation in a universal historical design. The proletarian worker acting locally acts "in and through" the total developmental logic. `provenance: burke-direct`

**concrete universal (Hegelian)**
Any moment in a developmental series that represents, in its limited "moment," the universal essence of the series as a whole. Burke imports this from Hegel (with Marx's satirical gloss) to explain how an ultimate order can be simultaneously particular and universal — the structural ground of the Marxist persuasion's rhetorical force. `provenance: burke-direct`

**sociology of knowledge (Mannheim)**
Mannheim's "relationist" project for systematically discounting ideological bias by studying the relations between ideologies and their nonverbal conditions. Burke classifies it as "pro-ultimate" — more precise than parliamentary relativism but unable to reach a genuine ultimate order because it lacks a governing principle of principles and cannot survive the elimination of the mythic motive it would apply its discounting to. `provenance: burke-direct`

**relativism vs. relationism**
Mannheim's distinction: relativism merely maps the diversity of ideological perspectives; relationism builds exact knowledge about the connections between ideologies and their grounds. Burke respects relationism as a dialectical advance while arguing it remains below the threshold of an ultimate order. `provenance: burke-direct`

**mythic ground**
The motivational level that transcends discursive reason and operates through image rather than idea. In the Platonic dialogue, the mythic image is the culminating figuration of a motive that the dialectical ascent through ideas has prepared the understanding to receive. Mannheim's error is treating this mythic ground as equivalent to ideological bias and applying the same discounting method to both. `provenance: burke-direct`

**identification** [spine — developed here via ultimate vocabulary]
Appears here in its most expanded form: the ultimate vocabulary allows a local actor to identify with the universal direction of historical development, linking personal or class identity to a "principle of principles." This is identification operating at the maximum range of its scope — not merely factional but developmental-cosmic. `provenance: burke-direct`

**context of situation (Malinowski)**
The positive-order analytic for the relation between verbal acts and their nonverbal scenes, generalized from Malinowski's anthropological need to describe tribal custom and social psychology implicit in untranslatable utterances. Complements the dialectical (Mannheim) and ultimate (Platonic) methods of analyzing motivational context. `provenance: burke-direct`

**unmasking / ideology-exposure**
Mannheim's generalization of the Marxist exposure of "mystification" into a universal method: any doctrinal vocabulary carries a partisan bias in favor of some group's interests, and opposing theorists can unmask that partiality. Each act of unmasking is itself partial, but the cumulative process is meant to approach ideological neutrality. `provenance: burke-direct`

**horse-trading / parliamentary jangle**
Burke's negative term for the outcome of purely dialectical confrontation without ultimate resolution: compromise that satisfies no one fully, yielding partial victories through tactical concession. Burke uses it to define what ultimate vocabulary overcomes. `provenance: burke-direct`

**enargeia**
The vividness or visual immediacy associated with the rhetorical figure of placing things before the eyes. Burke invokes it in the closing lines as the quality of the "New Vision" that transcends the imagery which prepared it — the experiential peak of an ultimate rhetorical order. `provenance: burke-direct`

**consubstantiality** [spine — extended here]
In this unit the consubstantiation mechanism is most visible in the Marxist analysis: the worker becomes "the Proletarian" by sharing the universal substance of the historical direction, not merely the local substance of class membership. Ultimate identification is consubstantiation with a developmental principle rather than with a person, faction, or text. `provenance: burke-direct`

## Identification & Consubstantiation Development

This unit marks the point at which Burke's consubstantiation framework is extended to its maximum vertical scope. In RM-01 consubstantiation was described as a relation between persons sharing property, symbolic material, or factional allegiance. In RM-04 it was extended through the metaphorical dimension of hierarchy. Here, in the ultimate-vocabulary analysis, consubstantiation can operate between a local actor and the universal principle of a developmental series. The Marxist worker who achieves revolutionary consciousness does not merely identify with his class (a dialectical, polar identification); he becomes consubstantial with the historical direction itself — a motivational principle that transcends any particular class interest. Burke explicitly describes this as a "third way" of understanding beyond perception-from-without and local-participation-in-particulars: the fullest understanding comes from local participation that simultaneously grasps its own participation in the over-all design (p. 196). This tri-level structure — local act / class / universal direction — is the consubstantiation mechanism operating across the full range of the ultimate vocabulary. It anticipates the unit's role as a bridge toward the Courtship and Pure Persuasion materials of RM-06 and RM-07, where identification with mystery and with the "ultimate Identification" of pure persuasion will extend this developmental logic further. The unit also clarifies why consubstantiation is the keystone of the integration pipeline: just as an ultimate vocabulary ranks all local acts in relation to a governing principle, Calleja's incorporation is the process by which a player's immediate ludic acts are enrolled into the overarching motivational structure of the game — its "god-term" in the terministic sense.

## Attitude (Incipient-Act) Signals

Attitude — the 6th dramatistic term, signaling an incipient or suspended act — surfaces most clearly in Burke's account of what the ultimate vocabulary does to political and social actors. The vocabulary organizes "one's attitude towards the struggles of politics" (p. 188); it gives "a contemplative effect" that can "suggest reasons why one kind of compromise is, in the long run, to be rated as superior to another." This is precisely attitude as an orientation that is motivationally charged without yet being a completed act. The worker who absorbs the Marxist doctrine is described as arriving at a formal or ritual way of seeing himself: no longer Mr. So-and-so but "the Proletarian." This shift in self-identification is an attitudinal transformation prior to any specific act — a realignment of the incipient-act register that will then generate differently motivated concrete behaviors. Mannheim's "pro-ultimate" stance is similarly attitudinal: it organizes the observer's posture toward competing ideologies without committing to any single partisan action. In the context of game behavior, this maps onto the player's orientation phase — the attitudinal set toward the game's motive hierarchy that precedes each specific decision.

## Game-Behavior Application (Anticipatory)

**Ultimate vocabulary → apex motive / win condition** [anticipatory-projection]
The "god-term" that organizes an ultimate vocabulary maps directly onto a game's overriding win condition or apex prestige currency. In any competitive game, HP and gold are positive terms — concrete, locatable, measurable. "Skill," "carry," "toxic," "tilted" are dialectical terms — contested, polar, meaning shifts by contrast and community. But the ultimate term — the "god-term" — is what ranks all other in-game motives: in a ranked competitive game it is "Elo," "rank," or "ladder position"; in a narrative RPG it is the narrative culmination or destiny-fulfillment; in a sandbox game it may be "prestige" or community recognition. All subordinate motives (farm gold, avoid death, rotate in time) derive their valuation from their relation to this apex. Burke's grammar of ultimate vocabulary is thus a grammar of game-motivational hierarchy. Maps to Calleja **Ludic** dimension (rule-optimization toward the win condition) and **Narrative** dimension (the apex that the story's developmental sequence is organized around); maps to pentad **Purpose** (the overriding "why" that organizes all acts). `provenance: anticipatory-projection`

**Positive vs. dialectical terms → concrete tokens vs. contested value-terms** [anticipatory-projection]
HP, gold, damage numbers, cooldown timers are positive game-terms: measurable, locatable, definable by operational specification. "Skill," "feeding," "carrying," "toxic," "toxic positivity" are dialectical game-terms: they are polar (skill vs. luck; carry vs. get-carried), contested, and meaning shifts by the opponent you set them against. The positive/dialectical distinction maps directly onto the player's terministic environment, structuring which motives can be objectively tracked vs. which require social and contextual interpretation. Maps to Calleja **Ludic** (positive terms govern mechanical optimization) and **Social** (dialectical terms govern community evaluation and status). `provenance: anticipatory-projection`

**Parliamentary jangle without ultimate resolution → stalemate, toxicity, rage-quit** [anticipatory-projection]
Burke's description of dialectical confrontation without ultimate resolution — "horse-trading," "mild demoralization," competing voices that cannot convince each other — maps onto the social pathology of games without clear shared ultimate terms: ranking disputes, ELO anxiety, toxicity spirals, and grief play are precisely the motivational equivalent of parliamentary jangle. When players do not share an ultimate vocabulary that orders the competing value-claims ("winning matters more than /honor," or "honor matters more than /winning"), no dialectical operation can resolve the conflict. Maps to Calleja **Social** dimension; pentad **Scene** (the environment of unresolvable motivational contest). `provenance: anticipatory-projection`

**Ultimate vocabulary → ritual/formal self-identification in games** [anticipatory-projection]
Burke's analysis of Lenin's worker who sees himself as "the Proletarian" — a generic persona participating in a universal history — maps onto the player who identifies not merely as an individual user with a username but as a representative of an archetype (the "support main," the "carry player," the "strategist"). This is consubstantiation with the game's motive-hierarchy operating at its ultimate level: the player's local acts acquire meaning from their participation in the over-all developmental "story" of their role or class within the game's system. Maps to Calleja **Narrative** + **Affective** dimensions; pentad **Agent** (the self-identification that organizes all acts). `provenance: anticipatory-projection`

**Context of situation → paratext and community interpretation** [anticipatory-projection]
Malinowski's "context of situation" — the extraverbal customs and social psychology implicit in any utterance — maps onto the paratextual layer of games: metagame knowledge, community conventions, streaming culture, platform norms. A player's chat message ("gg ez") cannot be interpreted without the contextual knowledge of community meaning, tone, and platform history. Mannheim's "unmasking" similarly maps onto metagame discourse that exposes the ideological partiality of dominant play-styles ("the meta"). Maps to Calleja **Social** + **Temporal** dimensions. `provenance: anticipatory-projection`

## Cross-Pipeline Bridges

**Bridge to Grammar of Motives (GM pentad)**
The positive/dialectical/ultimate triad extends the pentad's Scene/Act/Agent/Agency/Purpose framework. Positive terms predominantly belong to Scene (the observable material conditions); dialectical terms predominantly belong to Act and Agency (actions and methods acquire meaning through contrast and contest); ultimate terms converge on Purpose (the governing "why" that organizes the developmental series). Burke's cross-reference to the *Grammar* on p. 190 (Hegel's concrete universal and the "paradox of substance") explicitly links this unit to the consubstantiation bridge already established in RM-01 and GM-06/GM-09. The "principle of principles" structure is an extension of the pentadic ratio logic: just as any pentad ratio (scene-act, agent-purpose) can be read as the terministic screen through which one term interprets another, the ultimate vocabulary is the supreme ratio that organizes all the other ratios.

**Bridge to Calleja In-Game (PIM)**
The ultimate-vocabulary analysis maps most directly onto Calleja's concept of incorporation (In-Game Ch. 10): just as the ultimate vocabulary enrolls a local participant into the universal direction of a developmental series, incorporation describes the process by which a player is enrolled into the game-world such that its values, stakes, and goal-hierarchy become motivationally immediate rather than merely notional. The game's "god-term" — its ultimate ordering principle — is the motivational apex around which incorporation operates. Where RM-01's consubstantiality mechanism described the substance-sharing between player and game-world (the identificatory bridge), RM-05's ultimate-vocabulary framework describes the *hierarchical ranking* of that shared substance: not merely that a player shares substance with the game but that the game's ultimate term organizes which aspects of that shared substance matter most. The Ludic dimension (rule-optimization) corresponds to the positive-to-dialectical range; the over-arching Narrative or competitive telos corresponds to the ultimate order.

**Bridge to RM-06 (Courtship)**
The ultimate vocabulary prepares the ground for courtship: courtship operates across social hierarchies, and the social hierarchy is itself an instance of the ultimate-order principle. The "graded vocabulary" that places the cult of commodities as "inferior" transcendence, Hitlerism as "low-order sociality," and emancipatory politics as higher transcendence — this is the same hierarchic grammar that courtship will deploy in the master-apprentice and suitor-beloved registers of RM-06. The god-term at the apex of the social hierarchy is the magnetizing term of courtship.

**Bridge to RM-07 (Pure Persuasion)**
The ultimate level of identification — local participation as participation in the total dialectic — anticipates RM-07's concept of "pure persuasion": rhetoric that has become so identified with the telos it serves that the means of persuasion and the ultimate end become indistinguishable. The enargeia invoked at the close of this unit (p. 207) is a first signal of the "rhetorical radiance" that pure persuasion will represent in RM-07.
