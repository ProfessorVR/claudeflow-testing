## Overview

RM-02 surveys the classical heritage of persuasion — from Cicero, Aristotle, Quintilian, and Augustine — and then reframes the entire tradition through the lens of identification. Burke's central move in this unit is to show that "persuasion" was always already grounded in something deeper: the appeal to shared substance, the working of formal devices that produce collaborative assent, and the image-building that aligns audiences with the speaker's cause. The section on *formal appeal* and *rhetorical form in the large* is decisive: Burke argues that purely formal structures (climax, antithesis, gradatio, amplification) arouse audience participation *independently of propositional content*, thereby disclosing form itself as a rhetorical motive. The unit closes with a treatment of imagination and of the image–idea dialectic, showing how images act as condensed sites of multiple identification-principles — "unearned increments" of meaning beyond any explicit ideological argument.

## Argument (page-anchored walkthrough)

### Persuasion (pp. 49–55)

Burke opens by cataloguing the classical definitions of rhetoric: Cicero's *dicere ad persuadendum accommodate*, Aristotle's faculty of finding persuasive means, Quintilian's *bene dicendi scientia*, Augustine's induction to action (*ad agendum*). He notes that these definitions, stretched across 750 years, converge on persuasion but diverge in emphasis (p.49–50). Quintilian's stress on the *good man speaking well* already introduces a moral dimension that collapses persuasion's pragmatic neutrality. Augustine's addition is more radical: he distinguishes persuasion to *act* from persuasion to *attitude*, the latter being what matters when outright action is unavailable or constrained. Burke quotes Augustine's catalogue of responsive mental states (liking, fearing, hating, embracing, etc.) to show that persuasion operates on the whole emotional-dispositional register, not merely the volitional (p.50).

Burke then introduces a distinction he will use throughout the *Rhetoric*: "persuasion to attitude" (p.50) is the more general and foundational form, because attitude is an *incipient act* — a leaning or inclination that is not yet overt conduct. This allows rhetorical analysis to extend to purely poetic and lyrical structures, since even non-practical appeals produce attitudinal states in readers (p.50–51). The classical range runs from an *art of cheating* (Sophists) to Quintilian's view of eloquence as virtue (p.51). Isocrates links right speech to moral improvement; the Greek *peitho* (persuasion) shares a root with Latin *fides* (faith), while its middle/passive forms mean "to obey" — hinting at rhetoric's complicity with social compulsion (p.52).

Burke's key intervention at pp.54–55: the kind of *opinion* with which rhetoric operates is not falsity as opposed to truth, but rather moral-evaluative opinion that sits "on the bias" across the true/false axis. Rhetoric works primarily on the ethical register of belief and admiration, not primarily on the epistemic register of factual claims (p.54–55).

### Identification (redux) (pp. 55–58)

Burke revisits identification through Aristotle's remark (from *Rhetoric*) that it is "not hard to praise Athenians among Athenians" (p.55). The orator persuades only insofar as he speaks the audience's language — through speech, gesture, image, attitude, and idea. Flattery is but a special case: its generalized form is *consubstantiality*, the display of shared substance through "signs" aligned with the audience's settled opinions (p.55–56). Burke notes that even the "commonplaces" or *topoi* — Aristotle's catalogue of what people find admirable, threatening, advantageous, or blameworthy — are fundamentally a *survey of identification possibilities*: the things with which an audience is already consubstantial, and which the speaker can exploit as fulcra (p.56–57).

Longinus supplies Burke with the crucial example of formal assent: audiences feel not merely receptive but *creatively participating* when a speaker's form invites them in. Purely formal patterns — antithesis structures, climactic series — awaken "an attitude of collaborative expectancy" regardless of propositional content (p.58). This collaborative yielding to form *is* a species of identification: the audience identifies with the speaker's movement, not just the speaker's cause.

### Other Variants of the Rhetorical Motive (pp. 59–65)

Burke reviews additional classical variants: rhetoric as *proving opposites* (Aristotle), as *agonistic* contest, as *eloquence for its own sake*, as *advantage-seeking* (Isocrates). He introduces *advantage* as a master-term capable of subsuming all psychological "drives" and "urges" deployed by rival social-scientific vocabularies (pp.60–62). The treatment of "timely topics" (Aristotle's topoi adapted to modern journalism and postal canvassing) shows that situational factors — the transitory salience of a commonplace — are intrinsic to rhetoric's operation (pp.62–65). Malinowski's "context of situation" is cited as the principle undergirding a "rhetoric of bureaucracy" (p.65).

### Formal Appeal (pp. 65–69)

Burke surveys the classical taxonomy of figures (polysyndeton, asyndeton, amplification, gradatio, epanaphora, homoioteleuton, bathos, etc.) — a terminology so exhaustive that every utterance falls under some formal category (pp.65–67). He insists these devices are not mere decoration but arose from "great functional urgency": when pagan rhetoric decayed into the "second sophistic," verbal ornamentation became an end in itself, losing the urgency that had generated it (pp.67–68). Augustine reinfused formal devices with the zeal of Christian persuasion. The discussion pivots to *amplification* (auxesis) as the most thoroughgoing formal device, capable of covering extension, intensification, and dignification — and therefore capable of making even a purely poetic lyric "rhetorical" insofar as it systematically exploits a theme (pp.68–69).

### Rhetorical Form in the Large (pp. 69–78)

Burke introduces the classical six-part structure of the full oration: exordium → narration → partition → proof → refutation → peroration. This large-scale form is described as "almost as formal as the movements of a symphony" (p.70), though literary theory has tended to neglect structural analysis in favor of isolated stylistic effects. The three Aristotelian *kinds* of rhetoric — deliberative (future/policy), forensic/judicial (past/guilt), epideictic/demonstrative (praise/blame, present display) — are reviewed (pp.70–74). Epideictic is most important for Burke's purposes: its emphasis on *present appeal*, *delight in eloquence for its own sake*, and affinity with the written word make it the hinge between rhetoric and poetic.

Cicero's three *officia oratoris* — to teach (*docere*), to please (*delectare*), to move/bend (*movere/flectere*) — correspond to three styles (plain, tempered, grandiloquent) and three purposes. Burke observes that each "office" eventually transcends persuasion by becoming an end in itself: pure teaching becomes semantics/exposition; pure delight becomes esthetics; pure emotional movement approaches pure religious fervency (pp.74–78). Longinus (on the sublime) and Augustine (on Christian fervor) represent the outer limits of each trajectory.

### Imagination (pp. 78–84)

Burke's account of imagination is framed as a genealogy of the term in Western thought: beginning in Aristotle's faculty psychology (imagination as inferior to intellect but superior to brute sensation, requiring no actual presence of the object imagined), moving through Pico Della Mirandola (imagination as the motor of action, since *desire depends on perception*), then Francis Bacon (*rhetoric applies reason to imagination for the better moving of the will*), and arriving at Hazlitt's emancipation of imagination as "the immediate spring and guide of action" (pp.78–84). The key Hazlitt passage is decisive for Burke's integration: Hazlitt explains that we *identify* more readily with our own future interests than with others' not because of abstract self-love, but because we anticipate our future with "greater warmth of present imagination" — which "in a manner identifies" future states with the present self (p.84). Identification, then, is partly a function of imaginative proximity and vivacity.

### Image and Idea (pp. 84–89)

Burke argues that the image–idea distinction maps onto the old rhetorical distinction between *infinite (general) questions* (theses: "whether it is best to marry") and *definite (particular) cases* (causae: whether this king should marry this woman) (pp.85–86). A poetic image is "built of identifications" (p.85): it condenses multiple organizing principles simultaneously, so that translating an ideological argument into an image yields an "unearned increment" from the cluster of associated meanings not explicitly claimed in the argument (pp.87–88). Images can also anticipate ideas — functioning, as Leibniz's *petites perceptions*, before their conceptual articulation (p.88). The section closes with a critique of the esthetic tendency to treat image and idea as antithetical — Burke holds this as a distortion. Both serve as "entitlings" or "identifications," aligning audiences with causes through the double register of the visible-tangible and the invisible-ideational. Ideology is but a species of rhetoric; the difference between imagery and ideology is one of degree, not kind (pp.88–89).

## Key Concepts

**identification** [SPINE]  
The master mechanism behind persuasion: you persuade only insofar as you speak the audience's language, identifying your ways with theirs. Flattery is the paradigm case, but identification generalizes to all signs of consubstantiality through which speaker and audience share substance — attitude, image, topic, style, and formal appeal.

**consubstantiality** [SPINE]  
The substance-theoretic ground of identification. At pp.55–56 Burke explicitly links "persuasion as identification" to "conditions of consubstantiality in general." Displaying signs of consubstantiality (shared opinions, attitudes, values) is the fulcrum of rhetorical appeal.

**Attitude (6th term)** [SPINE]  
Introduced at pp.50–51 as an incipient act, a leaning or inclination that is not yet overt conduct. Burke distinguishes "persuasion to attitude" from "persuasion to action," with attitude being the more general and foundational target. Formal devices and poetic structures operate primarily on attitude rather than on explicit volition.

**formal appeal**  
The persuasive power of rhetorical form *as such*, independent of propositional content. Purely formal structures (antithesis, climax, gradatio, amplification) arouse "collaborative expectancy" in audiences, who yield to the symmetry of the form and thereby become predisposed to assent to whatever content is carried by that form. Form itself is a rhetorical motive.

**formal assent / collaborative expectancy**  
The state an audience enters when it begins to "swing along" with a formal pattern. Once the trend of a form is grasped, the audience participates in its completion regardless of whether it endorses the proposition. A species of identification: the audience identifies with the speaker's movement.

**amplification (auxesis)**  
The most thoroughgoing formal device: extension, intensification, and dignification of a subject, capable of accumulating persuasiveness through sheer systematic exploitation. In its purest form it names a process indistinguishable from poetic development (as in a lyric built around a refrain).

**epideictic (demonstrative) rhetoric**  
Aristotle's third kind: rhetoric of praise/blame aimed at the *present*, at the audience's delight in eloquence for its own sake. Burke identifies it as the hinge between rhetoric and poetic, and as the form in which the formal motive becomes most fully explicit.

**the three *officia oratoris***  
Cicero's three duties of the orator: to teach (*docere*), to please (*delectare*), to move/bend (*movere/flectere*). Each corresponds to a style and a purpose, and each eventually transcends persuasion by becoming an end in itself.

**advantage** (*pleonexia*, interest)  
Burke's proposed master-term for subsuming all classical variants of rhetorical purpose (honor, wealth, pleasure, glory) and all "scientific" psychological terminologies of drives and urges. Advantage can be individual, partisan, or universal, and its rhetorical valence is determined by the terministic frame in which it is deployed.

**opinion** (moral order)  
The kind of opinion rhetoric operates on is not factual opinion (true/false) but moral-evaluative opinion — what the audience finds admirable, shameful, advantageous, just. This sits "on the bias" across the epistemic true/false axis. The *topoi* are a catalogue of available moral-evaluative opinions, i.e., available points of identification.

**imagination**  
A genealogical treatment: from Aristotle's faculty-psychology account (imagination between sensation and intellect, not requiring presence of its object) through Bacon's formula (rhetoric applies reason to imagination to move the will) to Hazlitt's emancipation of imagination as the immediate spring of action. Critically, Hazlitt's account grounds self-interested identification in imaginative proximity: we identify with our future selves because we anticipate them with greater imaginative warmth.

**image and idea**  
Image and idea are not antithetical but continuous: the image is "built of identifications" (p.85), condensing multiple organizing principles simultaneously. An ideological argument translated into an image yields an "unearned increment" of associated meaning. Images can anticipate ideas (as *petites perceptions*); both serve as "entitlings" or identifying appellations.

**timely topic**  
Aristotle's *topoi* adapted to contemporary conditions (journalism, postal canvassing): a commonplace that is especially persuasive with audiences living under particular circumstances. Topical shifts alter which images and appeals are maximally effective, without altering the underlying catalogue of universal commonplaces.

**context of situation** (Malinowski)  
The nonverbal situational factors that participate in a rhetorical act's effectiveness; Burke cites Malinowski's concept as the principle undergirding a "rhetoric of bureaucracy" — the semiverbal, semi-organizational tactics of institutional life.

**pure form / formal motive**  
The rhetorical motive lodged in form itself, prior to content. Distinct from formal *devices* (figures of speech) — this is the large-scale structural form (exordium→peroration; gradatio; antithesis series) that creates appetite and fulfills it independently of what content fills the formal slots.

**the *sublime* (Longinus)**  
The state of transport produced in audiences by elevated style. Burke aligns Longinus with the esthetic trajectory of epideictic: the sublime aims at transport as an *end*, not as a means to further persuasion. It marks a moment where the formal/esthetic motive achieves autonomy from ulterior rhetorical purpose.

**identification through titles / entitlings**  
Burke's synthesis: the rhetorician uses "titles" (either imaginal or ideological) to identify a person or cause with whatever will call forth the desired response. This is equivalent to Aristotle's *topoi* — which shift so easily between images and ideas that the two realms can hardly be held apart. Both serve identification.

## Identification & Consubstantiation Development

RM-02's development of identification operates on two registers simultaneously. First, it extends the *social-behavioral* register (how speakers align with audiences through shared opinions and topoi) from RM-01's more philosophical ground. The classical notion of *pistis* (rhetorical proof, sharing a root with "faith") is traced back to *peitho*'s middle/passive "to obey," showing that identification is always shadowed by power: to be persuaded is to be incorporated into the speaker's cause, to yield one's substance to a larger consubstantial whole (p.52).

Second, and more importantly for the integration project, RM-02 establishes **form itself as a mechanism of consubstantiation**. When an audience yields to the formal pattern of a gradatio or antithesis series, it enacts identification not with the speaker's ideology but with the speaker's *movement* — a kind of incipient or preliminary consubstantiality that primes the audience for subsequent ideological alignment (pp.57–59). This is the structural homology that will matter for game behavior: the formal structure arouses appetite and then fulfills it, producing a brief episode of consubstantiation-through-form.

The imagination section consolidates this by grounding identification's psychological mechanism in imaginative proximity (Hazlitt, p.84): we identify with what we can imaginatively inhabit. The image–idea section then shows that images act as condensed consubstantiation-nodes: the "unearned increment" of meaning that accrues when an idea is translated into an image is precisely the increment of *uncontracted consubstantiality* — the audience is incorporated into multiple overlapping substance-communities at once, without being explicitly asked to endorse each one.

## Attitude (Incipient-Act) Signals

The 6th-term material surfaces most explicitly at pp.50–51, where Burke distinguishes "persuasion to attitude" from "persuasion to action." The distinction is the hinge of the entire unit. Attitude is defined as an *incipient act, a leaning or inclination*: it is not yet committed to a course, but it is already oriented — already a beginning of movement. This allows Burke to extend rhetorical analysis to lyrical and poetic structures (which produce attitudes without demanding actions) and to purely formal devices (which produce collaborative orientation without requiring propositional assent).

The attitude-concept resurfaces at pp.57–58 in the discussion of formal assent: the audience's "attitude of collaborative expectancy" is precisely an *incipient act* toward completing the formal pattern. It also surfaces in the treatment of imagination (pp.80–84): imagination fills the will with pictures of virtue, moving it toward action that has not yet been taken. Hazlitt's account is perhaps the most precise: imagination *is* the incipient act that constitutes the self's anticipatory identification with its future states. The attitude, for Hazlitt, *is* the imaginative rehearsal of the not-yet-done.

## Game-Behavior Application (Anticipatory)

> All mappings in this section carry `[anticipatory-projection]` per provenance discipline.

### Form as the creation and satisfaction of appetite → Calleja's Ludic involvement

Burke's account of formal appeal is the most directly applicable material in RM-02 for game-behavior analysis. When a gradatio (climactic series) or antithesis structure arouses "collaborative expectancy," it creates an *appetite* in the audience for the formal completion — and then satisfies that appetite. This is precisely the structure of Calleja's **Ludic involvement**: the player forms a plan (appetitive orientation toward a goal), executes it (traverses the intermediate steps), and receives feedback (the formal completion that satisfies or frustrates the appetite). [anticipatory-projection]

The gradatio example from Demetrius is particularly vivid: "Who controls Berlin, controls Germany; who controls Germany, controls Europe; who controls Europe, controls the world." By the second stage, the audience "feels how it is destined to develop" — an anticipatory projection of the pattern's completion. In game terms: by mid-execution of a plan, the player projects the outcome and is already emotionally invested in it. The formal structure of the game level, combat system, or puzzle design functions exactly as rhetorical form: it creates appetite at the outset, and the player's continued engagement is a species of "collaborative expectancy" sustained through the execution arc. [anticipatory-projection]

Burke's formal devices also map onto **Affective PIM**: the emotional coloring of each stage of a formal progression (the "swelling effect" at the third member of a series) is not incidental but constitutive of the appeal. In games, the pacing of difficulty escalation and reward delivery is a formal rhetorical device that produces affective involvement through the creation and satisfaction of anticipatory tension. [anticipatory-projection]

### Attitude as incipient act → player as perpetually poised

The Burkean distinction between "persuasion to attitude" and "persuasion to act" maps onto Calleja's distinction between the player's **Kinesthetic involvement** (the readiness-to-move, the bodily orientation toward the game's affordances) and the fully committed act (button press, decision made). The player in a game is always in the attitude-state: perpetually poised, oriented, incipient — which is why game design's primary task is attitude management, not action-specification. [anticipatory-projection]

### Imagination and anticipatory identification → Narrative PIM

Hazlitt's account of identification through imaginative proximity maps directly onto Calleja's **Narrative PIM** (player's identification with the game's characters and story): the player identifies with game protagonists because she anticipates their situations with "greater warmth of present imagination" — she inhabits their perspective imaginatively, which "in a manner identifies" their fictional future states with her own present engagement. [anticipatory-projection]

### Unearned increment of images → game worlds as consubstantiation-nodes

Burke's account of the image's "unearned increment" — the multiple identification-principles that activate simultaneously when an idea is translated into a sensory image — maps onto game environments as *condensed identification-sites*. A game's visual world (its art direction, sound design, spatial layout) is not merely illustrative of a propositional game-concept but a *node of multiple consubstantiations*: it activates affective, kinesthetic, social, and narrative involvements simultaneously and "without earning" each one through explicit propositional argument. [anticipatory-projection]

### Advantage and agonistic rhetoric → competitive game design

Burke's treatment of *advantage* (*pleonexia*) as the rhetorical motive in agonistic contexts maps onto competitive multiplayer game design, where the rhetorical motive is precisely the pursuit of advantage through display, skill, and social positioning. The agonistic register — "counter the opponent's jest with earnest, his earnest with jest" (Gorgias via Aristotle, p.54) — is structurally present in adaptive game AI and player counter-strategy. [anticipatory-projection]
