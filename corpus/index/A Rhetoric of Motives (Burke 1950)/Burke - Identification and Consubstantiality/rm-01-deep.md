## Overview

This unit is the substantive opening of *A Rhetoric of Motives* and the conceptual keystone of the whole Burke × Calleja integration. Across these thirty pages Burke performs his single most consequential move: he redefines rhetoric's master term from **persuasion** to **identification**, and he grounds identification in the substance-theoretic notion of **consubstantiality** carried over from *A Grammar of Motives*. To identify A with B, he argues, is to make them "consubstantial" — substantially one while each remains a distinct locus of motives. Because identification presupposes that people are divided, it is "compensatory to division," and rhetoric is therefore the study of how individuals, separate by nature, are symbolically made one. Burke then traces identification through a series of concrete fields — the identifying nature of **property**, the deceptive autonomy of specialized activities (the **"autonomous"** and the "autonomy" of **science**), the post-Christian relocation of redemption onto the **scapegoat**, the difference between **ingenuous and cunning** identifications, the **rhetoric of "address"** (including self-address), and the reframing of **primitive magic** as "primitive rhetoric." He closes by reasserting the **realistic function** of rhetoric — the use of language to induce cooperation in beings that respond to symbols — and by binding persuasion, identification, and communication into a single inseparable motive. For the integration, the unit's payoff is exact: consubstantiality is the rhetorical/symbolic homologue of Calleja's *incorporation*, and "acting-together" is the mechanism beneath player identification with avatar, guild, faction, and playerbase. It also supplies, in "attitude being an incipient act" (p.42), the clearest *Rhetoric*-side seed of Burke's later sixth dramatistic term.

## Argument (page-anchored walkthrough)

**The benign/malign range and the dialectical turn to identification (pp.19–20).** Burke resumes from the opening *Samson* anecdote (RM-00) by noting that rhetoric spans a malign register — invective, polemic, eristic, logomachy, "the Scramble" of insult, injury and the lie — and a benign one, ranging from sacrificial, evangelical love, through the persuasions of sexual love, to "neutral" communication (which he glosses as love so generalized and "technologized" that only close scrutiny finds the original motive) (p.19). Rather than treat killing/strife as primary, he uses "a dialectical device (the shift to a higher level of generalization)": an imagery of slaying is "a special case of transformation, and transformation involves the ideas and imagery of identification" — because to change a thing's nature, and to state that nature before and after, is to *identify* it (p.20). He therefore proposes that rhetoric be "reduced" to the wider-scope term **identification**, with strife/killing treated as a species. The strategic payoff is announced frankly: by this choice one can treat "war" as a "special case of peace" — not a primary motive but "a derivative condition, a perversion" of communion (p.20).

**Identification and "Consubstantiality" (pp.20–23).** Here is the unit's theoretical core. "A is not identical with his colleague, B. But insofar as their interests are joined, A is identified with B" — and A may be so identified even when their interests are not joined, provided he assumes or is persuaded that they are (p.20). These are "ambiguities of substance": once identified with B, A is "substantially one" with another person while still, in Burke's phrase, an "individual locus of motives." A is therefore "both joined and separate" — at once "a distinct substance and consubstantial with another" (p.21). Burke illustrates with parent and offspring (consubstantial yet apart). The decisive formula follows: "To identify A with B is to make A 'consubstantial' with B" (p.21). He then situates this in his trilogy: the *Grammar* was built about **substance** as key term; the related *Rhetoric* selects identification for the areas of persuasion/dissuasion; the projected *Symbolic* would be built about **identity** as titular term (p.21). The substance-theoretic ground is then stated with the precision the integration depends on: although "substance" is an abstruse term that many modern thinkers tried to abolish, they cannot abolish its *function* — "For substance, in the old philosophies, was an act; and a way of life is an acting-together; and in acting together, men have common sensations, concepts, images, ideas, attitudes that make them consubstantial" (p.21). A doctrine of consubstantiality, explicit or implicit, "may be necessary to any way of life." Burke distinguishes the three volumes by their relation to conflict (Grammar and Symbolic "at peace"; the Rhetoric leading "through the Scramble," pp.22–23) and then asks why the titular term should be "identification" when individuals are "at odds." The answer makes the master claim: "Identification is affirmed with earnestness precisely because there is division. Identification is compensatory to division" (p.22). If men were truly of one substance, "absolute communication would be of man's very essence"; identification is needed only because they are apart. The concept of identification, even lightly examined, reveals at every turn its "ironic counterpart: division," so that rhetoric, for Burke, is "concerned with the state of Babel after the Fall" (pp.22–23), and war is named "the ultimate disease of cooperation" (p.22).

**The Identifying Nature of Property (pp.23–27).** "Metaphysically, a thing is identified by its properties"; rhetorically, identification is frequently by economic property (p.23). In surrounding himself with properties that "name his number" or establish his identity, "man is ethical" — and Burke inserts a pivotal parenthesis: "Avarice is but the scenic word 'property' translated into terms of an agent's attitude, or incipient act" (p.24). Moral growth is organized through properties (goods, services, status, citizenship, reputation, love), yet identity-by-property set against other identity-by-property "can lead to turmoil and discord." Hence Marxism (capitalistic rhetoric) and Veblen are read as theorists of rhetoric (p.24); Bentham's "eulogistic coverings" for "material interests" are "essentially rhetorical" (p.24). Persuasion is identifying your cause with another's interests; in "pure identification there would be no strife," and none in "absolute separateness" either, since opponents need a "mediatory ground" even to join battle — strife arises when identification and division are "ambiguously" joined, which is "the characteristic invitation to rhetoric" and why, "according to Aristotle," rhetoric "proves opposites" over the unfixable line between "cooperation" and "exploitation" (p.25). Burke's mid-century examples — food-as-policy and the "Sign of the Scraped Plate," press "screening" of representatives, the marginalizing of Henry Wallace (pp.24–26), and praising God or science in terms that sanction a particular ownership-structure or imperialist-militarist expansion (the Preen/Prone dialogue and "John Q. Militarist-Imperialist," pp.26–27) — all dramatize identification operating through property and by default.

**Identification and the "Autonomous" (pp.27–29).** The principle: that an activity is "capable of reduction to intrinsic, autonomous principles does not argue that it is free from identification with other orders of motivation extrinsic to it" (p.27). The shepherd qua shepherd acts for the sheep, "but he may be 'identified' with a project that is raising the sheep for market." "Identification" names the autonomous activity's place in a wider context "with which the agent may be unconcerned." Concentrating on one integrated motive-structure is Symbolic; considering "the identifications whereby a specialized activity makes one a participant in some social or economic class" is Rhetoric — "'Belonging' in this sense is rhetorical" (p.28). Stress on the "pure autonomy" of literature/fine arts can be "a roundabout way of identification with a privileged class" (Veblenian), and affirmations of art's autonomy can become, "by antithesis," identification with political conservatism — hence "whenever you find a doctrine of 'nonpolitical' esthetics affirmed with fervor, look for its politics" (p.28). Autonomy nonetheless allows historical shifts: Plato's thought (Winspear) and the Sophists were identified with different (and shifting) class-interests (pp.28–29).

**The "Autonomy" of Science; "Redemption" in Post-Christian Science; Dual Possibilities (pp.29–35).** Science "as mere instrument (agency)" takes on "the nature of the scenes, acts, agents, and purposes with which it is identified"; a faulty political structure can produce "a correspondingly perverted science" (the concentration-camp specialists of National Socialist Germany, p.29). The liberal apologist treats applied science not as instruments but as "a good and absolute," circuitously endowing it with "the philosophic function of God as the grounding of values," and so vacillates between science-as-method and science-as-intrinsically-good-substance (p.30). Such an "unavowed identification" smuggles "a theological function … into a term on its face wholly secular," reinforcing occupational autonomy and the pragmatist notion that specialized work suffices as a grounding of morality (pp.30–31); the conclusion is that "one's morality as a specialist cannot be allowed to do duty for one's morality as a citizen" (p.31). The "Redemption" section relocates the Christian redemptive structure onto a secular **scapegoat**: with socialization secularized, "some other person or persons" may "take over the redemptive role," and "anti-Semitism provided the secularized replica of the Divine Scapegoat" under Hitlerism, minorities "magically identified" with collective guilt (pp.31–32). A clinic or laboratory becomes "a kind of secular temple," its "impersonal" terminology sliding "from treating inanimate nature as mere 'things' to treating animals, and then enemy peoples, as mere things" — "but they are not mere things, they are persons" (p.32). "Dual Possibilities of Science" carefully qualifies that Burke is "most decidedly not saying that science must take on such malign identifications": the Federation of American Scientists tried to dissociate atomic war-power from national security; the Gromyko/ownership exchange shows the rhetorical paradoxes of "ownership"; the parable of the father who tells his son to trust no one teaches that good will alone is "not enough" once development is placed "primarily under the heading of 'war potential'" (pp.32–35).

**Ingenuous and Cunning Identifications (pp.35–37).** The rhetorical motive "can operate without conscious direction by any particular agent": we impose false or inadequate terms on ourselves "in varying degrees of deliberateness and unawareness," through motives "indeterminately self-protective and/or suicidal" (p.35). The analogy is the psychologist's "malingering"; a class "not too exacting in the scrutiny of identifications that flatter its interests" practices "a profitable malingering" (p.36). Rhetoric comprises both *rhetorica utens* (the use of persuasive resources — Demosthenes) and *rhetorica docens* (their study — Aristotle's treatise) (p.36). Protecting an interest "merely by using terms not incisive enough to criticize it properly … brings rhetoric to the edge of cunning"; Iago and Tartuffe are "demons of Rhetoric," and Stendhal's Julien Sorel internalizes the rhetorical motive as a "hypocrisy-to-out-hypocritize-the-hypocrites" (pp.36–37).

**Rhetoric of "Address" (to the Individual Soul) (pp.37–40).** Even individual psychology has "a strongly rhetorical ingredient": the Freudian dream attains expression by "stylistic subterfuges designed to evade the inhibitions of a moralistic censor" (the analogue of literature under censorship); the ego-with-its-id confronts the super-ego "much as an orator would confront a somewhat alien audience"; the psyche is "quite a parliament" (pp.37–38). The clearest case is Freud's *Wit and Its Relation to the Unconscious* — speaker and hearer as "partners in partisan jokes made at the expense of another," "the purest rhetorical pattern" (p.38). Hence rhetoric's "nature as addressed, since persuasion implies an audience," and a man "can be his own audience … what Mead would call 'an I addressing its me'" (p.38). Traditional rhetoric (Aristotle) stresses the external audience, listing beliefs and character-traits with which a speaker favorably/unfavorably identifies cause and character (p.38); a "post-Christian" rhetoric must add ideas "privately addressed to the individual self for moralistic or incantatory purposes" (pp.38–39). Socialization-as-moralizing is thus rhetorical: education "exerts such pressure upon him from without; he completes the process from within," and "only those voices from without are effective which can speak in the language of a voice within" (p.39). The Tanala *tromba*-seizure that "requires an audience" (Kardiner) extends the topic even to a "rhetoric of hysteria," since even non-linguistic appeal is "in its origins communicative, addressed" (pp.39–40).

**Rhetoric and Primitive Magic (pp.40–43).** Witchcraft (Kluckhohn's "Navaho Witchcraft"; Cassirer's *Myth of the State*) is brought within rhetoric's range. Against "the typically scientist view" that magic is merely "an early form of bad science," Burke insists rhetoric "has no systematic location" in a bad-science/good-science antithesis. The basic function of rhetoric is "the use of words by human agents to form attitudes or to induce actions in other human agents," and a "call for help" is neither magic nor science but "thoroughly realistic" and hortatory — "it is trying to move people" (p.41). The unit's pivotal formula classifies three uses of language: "poetic language is a kind of symbolic action, for itself and in itself," "scientific action is a preparation for action," and "rhetorical language is inducement to action (or to attitude, attitude being an incipient act)" (p.42). Word-magic gets "the whole subject turned backwards": magic was "a faulty derivation" from a proper linguistic function — the realistic use of addressed language to induce *action in people* was mis-transferred into a magical attempt to induce *motion in things*, things Burke calls "by nature alien to purely linguistic orders of motivation" (pp.42–43). The rhetorician's tricks are "an 'art,'" not "bad science" (p.42).

**Realistic Function of Rhetoric + closing recapitulation (pp.43–48).** Burke proposes that anthropologists "recognize the factor of rhetoric in their own field": magic read as "primitive rhetoric" — "a pragmatic device that greatly assisted the survival of cultures by promoting social cohesion" (Malinowski; Kluckhohn) — is truer than modern rhetoric read as "a survival of primitive magic" (p.43). Rhetoric "as such is not rooted in any past condition of human society"; it is "rooted in an essential function of language itself … the use of language as a symbolic means of inducing cooperation in beings that by nature respond to symbols" (p.43). This is "a realism of the act: moral, persuasive," whose claims are not "true" or "false" in the propositional sense (the "rhetoric of witchcraft" that, as an ideology, discourages rapid capital-accumulation among the Navaho, pp.44–45). The recapitulation (pp.45–48) names "two main aspects of rhetoric: its use of identification and its nature as addressed"; since identification implies division, rhetoric draws us into socialization, faction, and the wavering line between peace and conflict; persuasion re-enters explicitly — "Rhetoric is the art of persuasion, or a study of the means of persuasion available for any given situation" — bringing us "to the point at which Aristotle begins" (p.46). On the relation of identification and persuasion: a speaker persuades "by the use of stylistic identifications," so "there is no chance of our keeping apart the meanings of persuasion, identification ('consubstantiality') and communication (the nature of rhetoric as 'addressed')" (p.46). A final forward-seed: where a sense of consubstantiality is symbolically established "between beings of unequal status," the identification may, Burke notes, "extend far into the realm of the idealistic," out of which (later, on Order) "there may arise a kind of magic or mystery that sets its mark upon all human relations" (p.48) — pointing ahead to RM-03 (mystery) and RM-06 (courtship/hierarchy).

## Identification & consubstantiation development

This is the unit that *constitutes* the master term, so the development is foundational rather than incremental. Three moves are decisive. (1) **The reduction**: rhetoric's genus is shifted from persuasion to identification by a dialectical raising of the level of generalization (pp.19–20), with persuasion, strife, and communication re-described as species or facets (confirmed in the closing inseparability claim, p.46). (2) **The substance-theoretic ground**: identification is defined *as* making-consubstantial, and consubstantiality is given its precise both-joined-and-separate structure on p.21, with the act-basis ("substance … was an act; a way of life is an acting-together") supplying the mechanism. This is exactly the apparatus the *Grammar* pipeline could only seed (GM-00 p.xix "A may become non-A … consubstantial with non-A"; GM-02 type-16 "consubstantial-substance"; the *Grammar*'s paradox of substance) and flagged as "pending-future-rhetoric-of-motives-pipeline." RM-01 consolidates it with `burke-direct` text. (3) **Compensation to division** (p.22): identification is not a static likeness but a *response* to a prior separateness, which makes it inherently dynamic, partisan, and capable of operating by default, cunning, or self-address (pp.35–39). Property (pp.23–27), the autonomous specialty (pp.27–31), the scapegoat (pp.31–32), and magic/socialization (pp.40–43) are then read as the fields through which the single mechanism of consubstantial identification works.

## Attitude (incipient-act) signals

The *Rhetoric*-side seed of Burke's sixth dramatistic term surfaces unusually clearly in this unit, in three places. First, the substance-as-act passage lists, among what acting-together makes men share, "attitudes that make them consubstantial" (p.21) — attitude is thus internal to consubstantiality itself. Second, the property section gives the scene→attitude translation directly: "Avarice is but the scenic word 'property' translated into terms of an agent's attitude, or incipient act" (p.24). Third, and most explicitly, the magic/realism formula defines the rhetorical use of language as "inducement to action (or to attitude, attitude being an incipient act)" (p.42), distinguishing it from poetic symbolic-action and scientific preparation-for-action. Together these make RM-01 the principal *Rhetoric*-side anchor for operationalizing Attitude as the sixth term (seeded in *Grammar* GM-06/GM-09 per the 1962 addendum and phase0). For the integration this matters because Attitude is the dispositional/incipient-act hinge that connects identification to a player's *stance* toward avatar, guild, and gameworld.

## The consubstantiation↔incorporation bridge

*This subsection is the deliverable the entire integration depends on. The mapping to Calleja, and all pentad/PIM claims, are `[anticipatory-projection]`; the Burke exposition is `burke-direct`. RM-01 consolidates the bridge the Grammar pipeline explicitly deferred to "the future Rhetoric-of-Motives pipeline" (gm-pentad-operationalization-manual, Section E, `incorporation_consubstantiation_bridge`, `consubstantiation_pipeline_status: pending`).*

**(a) What consubstantiation means for Burke.** To identify A with B is "to make A 'consubstantial' with B" (p.21). Consubstantiality is not identity and not mere similarity; it is a precise double structure: once identified with B, A is "substantially one" with another person while still remaining, in Burke's phrase, a unique "individual locus of motives" — A is thus "both joined and separate," at once "a distinct substance and consubstantial with another" (p.21). Its ground is *action*: substance, on the old philosophies, "was an act," a way of life is "an acting-together," and in acting together men come to share the sensations, concepts, images, ideas, and attitudes "that make them consubstantial" (p.21). Consubstantiality is therefore (i) substance-theoretic — carried over from the *Grammar*'s paradox of substance; (ii) act-based — a co-*action*, not a static likeness; (iii) compensatory to division — affirmed precisely because people are apart (p.22); and (iv) the very accomplishment of identification — to identify is to make-consubstantial. Crucially, Burke insists the *function* of "substance" survives even where the *term* has been abolished (p.21), so consubstantiality can be present implicitly in vocabularies that never name it.

**(b) How it maps to Calleja's *incorporation*.** `[anticipatory-projection]` Calleja's *incorporation* (IG-10, canonical definition p.169) is "the absorption of a virtual environment into consciousness, yielding a sense of habitation, which is supported by the systemically upheld embodiment of the player in a single location, as represented by the avatar." It is a **dual-axis** construct: (Axis 1) the gameworld is absorbed *into* the player's consciousness; (Axis 2) the player is absorbed *into* the gameworld via the avatar — with a **simultaneity condition** (both axes must obtain concurrently, or the state reduces to mere involvement). The mapping to consubstantiality is structurally exact:

- **Axis 1 (environment into consciousness)** ↔ Burke's "A is 'substantially one' with" the other: the gameworld becomes part of the player's own structure of motives, as the other becomes substantially one with A.
- **Axis 2 (player into environment via avatar)** ↔ Burke's "yet he remains unique, an individual locus of motives … both joined and separate": the **avatar is precisely the single systemically-upheld locus** through which the player is joined to the world while remaining a distinct substance.
- **Simultaneity condition** ↔ Burke's both-joined-AND-separate simultaneity: consubstantial union does not dissolve distinctness; the two are *one in act yet two in being* — the very paradox-of-substance simultaneity that Calleja's "double axis" independently reconstructs.

The homology is **functional, not lexical**: Calleja never uses "substance," Burke never uses "embodiment," and the two arrive at the identical dynamic — a being made substantially one with something other than itself while remaining a distinct locus — from incommensurable starting points (Burke: substance-metaphysics + symbol-action; Calleja: experientialist embodied cognition).

**(c) The divergences.** `[anticipatory-projection]` The bridge is a homology, not an equation, and the integration must hold five differences in view. (1) **Medium of union**: Burke = rhetorical/symbolic identification through shared *substance* — property, interests, ideology, terministic "Belonging" (p.28); Calleja = phenomenological *absorption* through six involvement dimensions (kinesthetic, spatial, shared, narrative, affective, ludic), with the spatial+kinesthetic cornerstone. (2) **Ontological status**: Burke's consubstantiality is ambivalently real-or-persuaded ("or is persuaded to believe" interests are joined, p.20) and can be strategic or cunning (identification "by default," "profitable malingering"); Calleja's incorporation is a sustained-but-fleeting *experiential state* that either obtains phenomenologically or slips back to involvement — not a persuaded belief. (3) **Grounding theory**: Burke grounds union in act/substance-metaphysics and a "realism of the act"; Calleja grounds it in experientialist gestalts and consciousness-as-internally-generated-construct (Lakoff & Johnson 2003, Damasio 2000, Dennett 1991) — and, notably, *not* in Merleau-Ponty or Heidegger, which are absent from IG-10. (4) **Directionality**: Calleja insists on bidirectionality as a corrective to the "unidirectional plunge"; Burke's consubstantiality is symmetrical-in-structure but asymmetrically-deployed — the rhetorician engineers A's identification with B, often across *unequal* status (p.48), closer to a persuasion-directed vector than to a mutual double-axis. (5) **Scope**: Burke's identification is society-wide, includes self-address (p.38), and operates unconsciously/by default; Calleja's incorporation is bounded to ergodic media that acknowledge the player's presence and agency.

**(d) How identification underlies player identification with avatar / character / guild / faction / playerbase, and grounds Attitude.** `[anticipatory-projection]` The same mechanism — "substantially one yet a distinct locus," accomplished through "acting-together" — is the symbolic ground beneath the player's identifications. With the **avatar/character**, the avatar is the systemically-upheld locus through which the player is substantially one with the gameworld while remaining a distinct locus of motives (Calleja's embodiment-axis; PIM Shared involvement). With the **guild/faction/playerbase**, Burke's "acting-together" and "'Belonging' is rhetorical" (pp.21, 28) supply the substance-theoretic *why* beneath Calleja's descriptive *what* of co-presence and group belonging (IG-06 Shared involvement: cohabitation/cooperation/competition; guild/society sociality). And because consubstantiality is itself partly constituted by shared *attitudes* (p.21), and because "attitude is an incipient act" (p.42), this unit makes identification the ground of **Attitude**: a player's disposition/stance toward avatar, guild, and gameworld is an incipient act of identification — simultaneously grounding the Affective involvement dimension and the pentad's sixth (Attitude) term. The bridge therefore does double duty: it links consubstantiation to incorporation at the *substance/embodiment* level and to Shared involvement at the *social-belonging* level, while seeding Attitude as the dispositional hinge between them.
