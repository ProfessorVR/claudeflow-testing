# RM-03 Deep Unit — Mystification & Mystery (Bentham, Marx, Carlyle)
**Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives* (1950), book pp. 90–122 / PDF pp. 105–137**
Unit: `rm-03` | Folder: `Burke - Mystification and Mystery`

---

## Overview

This unit extends Burke's redefinition of rhetoric from persuasion toward identification by demonstrating that rhetoric is inescapably at work wherever language encodes — and conceals — the social distribution of power. Moving from Bentham's linguistic archaeology through Marx's theory of ideological inversion to Carlyle's affirmative mysticism, Burke shows that the vocabulary of motives is never neutral: it always embeds the charged relationships of social hierarchy. The unit's culminating formulation — "mystery is equated with class distinctions" (p.122) — establishes **mystery** as a spine concept, the rhetorical phenomenon that arises whenever beings of structurally different kinds communicate across a social divide. **Mystification** names the dyslogistic version of the same dynamic: the concealment of specific (class-determined) interests behind apparently universal or "spiritual" terms. Together they supply the social-motive apparatus through which the Grammar's pentad is extended into the sociology of language.

## Argument

### Rhetorical Analysis in Bentham (pp. 90–101)

Burke opens by situating Bentham within the image/idea antithesis already traced through Richards and Coleridge. Bentham's "theory of fictions" — a methodical search for the "archetypes" or images lurking behind abstract legal and moral vocabulary — is presented as an accidental rhetoric: a project aimed at exposing linguistic bias that simultaneously serves as a catalogue of persuasive technique (p.90–91).

The analytical core of the Bentham section is his triplicate vocabulary: every domain of motive produces a **neutral** term flanked by **eulogistic** and **dyslogistic** counterparts. The neutral designates a behavior without moral loading; the eulogistic and dyslogistic weight it positively or negatively, thereby smuggling a conclusion into the very name of the act. Burke traces Bentham's fourteen "interests" — pecuniary desire, honor, curiosity, sexuality, piety, and so on — each capable of being named either as virtue or as vice depending on the speaker's rhetorical stake (pp. 92–94).

From the triplicate vocabulary Burke extracts the doctrine of the **censorial appellative** and the **covering motive** (pp. 99–101). When the actual motive behind an act cannot bear eulogistic presentation, a speaker selects the most favorable co-operating motive and presents it as the whole. This "covering" procedure maps directly onto Burke's earlier identification of how identification is managed rhetorically: one substance is presented under the guise of another. Bentham calls the covering devices *tegumen* and *res tegenda* — the linguistic veil and the interest it veils — establishing the groundwork for what Burke calls the rhetoric of "unmasking" (p.100). Burke reads Bentham's own critique of parliamentary rhetoric (especially the attack on "Vague Generalities" like "rally round the Constitution") as an instance of rhetoric attacking rhetoric — a "rhetoric-in-spite-of-itself" (p.99).

Burke also notes that the Benthamite project of radical neutralization of vocabulary approaches, in oblique form, the via negativa of mysticism: the withdrawal from all weighted opinion toward a neutral ground, followed by a return to the world with a fresh evaluative framework (pp. 95–97). This quasi-mystical structure anticipates the explicit treatment of mystery in the Carlyle section.

### Marx on "Mystification" (pp. 101–110)

Burke reads Marx's theory of ideology primarily as a contribution to rhetorical analysis, not as a settled political doctrine. He begins by noting the paradox that Marxism, though deeply rhetorical in practice, refused the name of rhetoric — applying it only to non-Marxist persuasion (pp. 101–102). This refusal is itself an instance of the covering-motive device Bentham analyzed.

Burke then surveys seven meanings of "ideology" (pp. 103–105), converging on the seventh — the Marxist technical usage from *The German Ideology* — which he treats as the most analytically significant. In this sense, ideology is an **inverted genealogy of culture**: the error of treating ideas as causally primary when they are, in the materialist account, derivative of material (property, labor) conditions. This inversion generates "mystification" — the rhetorical effect whereby specific class interests are presented in the guise of universal human interests, making local advantage appear as universal truth.

Burke reconstructs the three "tricks of theodicy" Marx identifies (pp. 107–108): first, separating ruling ideas from the ruling class and treating them in "pure" form; second, arranging ideas in a dialectical-mystical developmental series; third, reintroducing apparent materialism through a series of representative historical persons who embody the "concept." In each case, the rhetorical mechanism is the substitution of a general, spiritualized term for the specific, material one — exactly the covering-motive structure Bentham had charted.

The rhetorical core of Marx's analysis, Burke argues, is the distinction between **specific motives** (class-determined, material, divisive) and **generic motives** (apparently universal, spiritual, unifying) (p.110). Mystification occurs precisely when the specific is presented as the generic — when national unity is invoked to conceal class conflict, or when "liberty" and "civilization" appear as the true motives of institutions driven by property. Burke endorses this as a productive diagnostic tool while questioning whether the Marxist remedy (abolition of the division of labor) is compatible with modern industrial society (pp. 109–110).

### Terministic Reservations in View of Cromwell's Motives (pp. 110–114)

Burke introduces an important qualification of the Marxist diagnostic through the figure of Cromwell. The question is whether a god-term like "honor" or "Providence" was as spiritually mystifying to its original users as it appears to later materialist critics. Burke's terministic reservation is that **context of situation** participates in meaning: a term that survives in written documents without its situational matrix may appear far more idealized — and thus far more "mystifying" — than it was to those who wielded it in practice (pp. 111–112).

Using Cromwell's speech to the House of Commons (January 22, 1655), Burke shows that Cromwell himself acknowledges that the Revolution did not begin for religious motives, thereby supplying the material qualification the Marxist critique would supply from outside. Burke uses this to illustrate that terms carry implicit material meanings that their users recognize but do not always verbalize, because those meanings were too obvious to require statement (p.112). La Rochefoucauld reinforces this: the eulogistic covering of an individual's motives is less "illusion" than conspiracy — a knowing performance — suggesting that self-serving presentations are not always self-deceived (pp. 113–114).

The Cromwell episode also demonstrates that even "materialist" terminologies are not immune: the Marxist appeal to "historical necessity" spiritualizes the same pragmatic details of governance that Cromwell's "Providence" spiritualized, omitting administrative minutiae from view in both cases (pp. 114–115).

### Carlyle on "Mystery" (pp. 114–122)

The Carlyle section marks the conceptual climax of the unit. Burke introduces the key structural definition: **mystery arises at that point where different kinds of beings are in communication** — where strangeness and potential communion coexist (p.115). This definition is irreducibly social: the conditions for mystery are any pronounced distinctions of rank, class, or kind. Burke lists the examples — nobility and commoner, employer and employee, judge and prisoner — arguing that the clerk and the manager are subtly mysterious to each other not as individuals but as representatives of different social principles (p.115).

The rhetoric of mystery is, Burke argues, formally analogous to the rhetoric of sexual courtship — the range of approaches between classes mirrors the range of erotic tactics: courtship, seduction, coercion, submission (p.115). Burke reads Shakespeare's sonnets, Wilde, James's *The Turn of the Screw*, and Dostoevski as literary instances where class distinction and sexual or quasi-sexual fascination are fused, with the erotic charge deriving substantially from hierarchic difference (pp. 116–118). The mystique of social rank is not merely a metaphor for erotic relation; the two orders are structurally homologous because both involve the crossing of a categorical boundary.

Burke introduces **hierarchy** as the governing concept through the reference to Dionysius the Areopagite: "hierarchy" is the eulogistic word for "bureaucracy," in which each level employs a rhetoric of obeisance upward and condescension downward — a rhetoric of courtship throughout (p.118). Kafka's bureaucratic fiction is matched with Carlyle's mystical-social theory as two treatments of the same phenomenon: the mystery of impenetrable rank, the ever-present-but-inaccessible Authority.

Carlyle's "philosophy of clothes" in *Sartor Resartus* provides the eulogistic counterpart to Marx's dyslogistic analysis. Both, Burke notes, are addressing the same social fact — the hierarchy that arose with the division of labor — but Carlyle endorses the mystery where Marx would expose and dissolve it (p.118–119). Carlyle's three stages are reconstructed (pp. 121–122): first, clothes symbolize a social order that commands reverence but does not represent man's true nature; second, behind the pageantry lies a profounder reality — the divine Mystery — to which reverence is owed; third, because social Clothes symbolize that divine order, they too deserve qualified reverence, including reverence for the ruler-ruled distinction. The syllogistic result: **mystery is equated with class distinctions** (p.122). Burke neither debunks nor endorses; he simply names the equivalence.

## Key Concepts

**mystery** [spine]
The rhetorical phenomenon that arises between beings of different social kinds when they communicate across a structural divide. Mystery requires both strangeness and the possibility of communion; it is the constitutive medium of social hierarchy. Burke's definition (p.115) grounds every subsequent treatment of courtship and rank in the *Rhetoric*. Not a subjective feeling but an objective structural condition generated by difference-in-relation.

**mystification** [spine]
The (primarily dyslogistic, Marxist) name for the rhetorical operation by which specific class interests are concealed beneath universal or spiritual terms — "ideology" in the technical Marxist sense. Where mystery is the condition, mystification is the concealing deployment of that condition. The two terms are evaluative variants of the same social-rhetorical structure; Burke's point is that Carlyle's affirmative mystery and Marx's critical mystification describe the same phenomenon from opposite moral stances (p.114, p.122).

**hierarchy** [spine]
The eulogistic term for bureaucracy: the graduated social structure in which each rank employs a double rhetoric — deference upward, condescension downward — permeated by the spirit of an Ultimate Stage (p.118). Hierarchy is the social scene within which mystery operates and from which mystification proceeds. Connected to the pentad's Scene term: hierarchy as the scenic condition of motivated social action.

**triplicate vocabulary** (Bentham)
The system of neutral, eulogistic, and dyslogistic terms for every domain of motive. The eulogistic and dyslogistic variants do not describe different acts but frame the same act differently for rhetorical advantage. The neutral term is a regulatory ideal, never a rhetorical reality, since language absent evaluative charge cannot induce action (pp. 92–97).

**censorial appellative**
Bentham's term for a word that carries evaluative weight as though it were descriptive — "having, without the form, the force of an assumption." The tonality of the term preloads the conclusion the argument is supposed to establish. Burke relates this to the broader rhetoric of petitio principii, arguing it is not an aberration but the normal condition of motivated discourse (pp. 94–95).

**covering motive** (tegumen / res tegenda)
When the actual motive behind an act cannot be presented eulogistically, the speaker selects the most favorable co-operative motive and presents it as the whole or essence of the act. The covering motive is the linguistic veil; the interest it veils is the res tegenda (p.99–100). The device links Bentham's vocabulary analysis directly to Burke's theory of identification as the management of how one substance presents itself in terms of another.

**ideology** (Marxist technical sense)
The inverted genealogy of culture that treats ideas as causally primary when they are derivative of material (property and labor) conditions. In the seventh of seven senses Burke distinguishes (pp. 103–105), ideology generates mystification by presenting the historically specific interests of a ruling class as the universal interests of humanity.

**generic vs. specific motives**
Burke's reconstruction of the key Marxist distinction (p.110): generic motives are apparently universal ("the essence of mankind," "liberty," "civilization"); specific motives are class-determined and material. Mystification names the rhetorical substitution of generic for specific — the presentation of the particular as the universal.

**terministic reservation**
Burke's qualification of the Marxist diagnostic (pp. 110–114): terms carry implicit situational meanings that their original users knew but did not verbalize, because those meanings were too obvious to need statement. A term may appear far more idealized to later critics than it was to contemporaries who used it against a lived material context. The warning is against reading mystification back into documents without recovering the context of situation.

**via negativa (secularized)**
Burke detects behind Bentham's project of vocabulary neutralization a secularized version of the mystical via negativa: the withdrawal from weighted partisan language toward a neutral ground, and the return with a new evaluative order (pp. 95–97). This quasi-mystical structure anticipates the rhetoric of mystery as traversal across a categorical boundary.

**eulogistic / dyslogistic**
Bentham's paired terms for the two poles of the censorial spectrum. Burke uses them throughout to track how the same reality is named for rhetorical advantage. Carlyle and Marx differ primarily in treating mystery eulogistically vs. dyslogistically (p.114); Burke refuses to adjudicate, instead identifying the equivalence beneath both evaluations.

**god-term**
A high-abstraction summarizing word — "honor," "liberty," "Providence," "Absolute Idea" — that gathers a whole complex of material conditions and social relations under a single spiritualized label. The Cromwell example (pp. 111–114) shows that god-terms are not necessarily mystifying to those who use them, because the material conditions they summarize may be understood by the user even when not stated. The concept anticipates the fuller treatment of Ultimate Terms in RM-05.

**identification (via mystery)**
In this unit, identification is developed along the social axis: the clerk identifies with and by a social principle that makes him subtly a different kind of being from the manager. Mystery is the medium of this differential identification — the felt awareness that communication is crossing a categorical boundary. This extends the RM-01 treatment of identification by specifying the social structure that makes identification across difference both possible and charged (p.115).

**rhetoric of courtship (class analogue)**
The formal analogy between erotic and class rhetoric: relations between ranks reproduce the full spectrum of courtship dynamics — appeal, seduction, coercion, submission, fascination. The class rhetorical form is not a metaphor of the erotic but a structural homologue: both are rhetorics of crossing categorical boundaries (pp. 115–118). This prepares the ground for RM-06's full treatment of courtship.

**symbol / clothes (Carlyle)**
Carlyle's "clothes" are not literal garments but symbols in general: the visible emblems of social distinctions that simultaneously conceal the naked common humanity beneath and reveal the hierarchic order as the expression of a divine principle. The symbol is always both revelation and concealment — a doubled significance that Burke will later connect to the secret and the mystery of pure persuasion (RM-07) (pp. 119–121).

**ultimate identification (via hero-worship)**
Carlyle's doctrine of hero-worship is identified (explicitly by Burke, p.121) as the "identification" principle at the summit of the hierarchy: the king summarizes all figures of heroism, and the community identifies with the divine through identification with him. This anticipates RM-07's treatment of ultimate identification and connects hierarchy to the grammar of the pentad's Agent term.

## Identification & Consubstantiation Development

This unit advances identification primarily along the social-hierarchic axis rather than the substance-theoretic axis of RM-01. The key move is Burke's demonstration that **mystery is the medium of identification across difference**: the clerk and the manager are identified with and by different social principles, and the charge of their interaction — the fascination and unease of class encounter — is precisely the rhetorical energy that mystery names (p.115).

The covering-motive analysis (Bentham) and the ideological analysis (Marx) both show that identification is not merely a positive merger of substance but can be managed asymmetrically: one party presents itself in the guise of the other's interests, achieving a rhetorical union that conceals a substantial division. This is identification's shadow — the production of false consubstantiality through terminological manipulation. The eulogistic covering of an unseemly motive with a respectable one is a micro-instance of how identification is performed and imposed.

Carlyle's three-stage argument culminates in the restoration of hierarchic reverence on a stronger ground: because the divine is expressed through the social order's "clothes," identifying with a genuine hero or ruler is identification with the divine principle itself. Here consubstantiation (the sharing of substance) operates through the symbol: the ruler is the symbol of the divine, and reverence for the ruler is participation in the substance the symbol conveys. The connection to the RM-01 keystone (consubstantiation as the mechanism beneath Calleja's incorporation) is oblique in this unit — the substance-theoretic language is muted — but the social-symbolic structure is being built out.

## Attitude (Incipient-Act) Signals

Attitude surfaces directly and explicitly in the Bentham analysis. Burke notes (p.91) that Richards' treatment of poetry as "imaginal action" — the sensitive suspension of overt action — redescribes Bentham's essentially rhetorical analysis. The attitude of sympathy may either lead into an overt act of kindness or serve as its substitute: this is precisely the definition of attitude as **incipient act** that Burke seeds throughout the *Rhetoric* as the 6th dramatistic term.

The discussion of tonality as gesture or posture (p.98) — Burke's gloss on the censorial appellative as "attitude" — reinforces this: the tonal weight of a censorial term is not an argument but an incipient orientation, a stance toward the subject that shapes subsequent judgment without demanding explicit commitment. The headline as "attitude" (p.98–99) is an especially clear example: it produces a disposition without completing an argument.

The Cromwell section contains an implicit attitude-signal: Cromwell's invocation of Providence is not merely descriptive but expresses an incipient orientation toward the scene — a posture of receptivity to divine necessity that licenses action without fully determining its form. The terministic reservation (that Providence subsumes material meanings the contemporary user held tacitly) suggests that even ultimate terms function as attitudes — orientations that gather and suspend a complex of motivational strands without resolving them into a single overt act.

## Game-Behavior Application (Anticipatory)

**[anticipatory-projection] mystery → Calleja Shared involvement (guild rank, prestige hierarchies)**
Burke's structural definition of mystery — arising wherever different kinds of beings communicate across a social divide — maps directly onto the phenomenon of **guild rank, prestige hierarchies, and elite status** in multiplayer games. The "mystery" that surrounds a high-rank raider or a prestigious guild from the perspective of a lower-rank player is not merely informational (what do they know?); it is structural: the two parties represent different social principles within the game's occupational hierarchy. The fascination with elite raiders, the aspiration to enter a mythic guild, the awe at encountering a world-first achiever — these are instances of mystery as Burke defines it, arising from pronounced social distinctions within a shared social world. Calleja's Shared involvement dimension, which covers social roles and status relations among players, is the PIM dimension where this mapping lands. The rhetoric of mystery — deference upward, condescension downward, fascination across the boundary — is the affective texture of Shared involvement at hierarchic distance.

**[anticipatory-projection] mystification → Ludic involvement (obscured drop rates, opaque matchmaking)**
Marx's mystification — the concealment of specific (material) conditions beneath apparently universal or neutral terms — maps onto the design practice of **occluding game systems**: hidden drop rates, opaque matchmaking algorithms, undisclosed probability weights, or unexplained reward structures. These are instances of ideological motive-concealment in the Marxist technical sense: the specific material logic of the system (probabilistic reward-scheduling designed to maximize engagement and monetization) is presented under neutral or positive terms ("fair matchmaking," "balanced loot"). Players engage with a scenic presentation that conceals its own structural determinism. This maps to Calleja's **Ludic involvement** dimension — the direct engagement with game mechanics and rules — as the dimension within which mystification operates. The player's relationship to hidden game logic is structurally homologous to the citizen's relationship to ideological veiling of class interest.

**[anticipatory-projection] triplicate vocabulary → narrative framing of in-game moral choices**
Bentham's triplicate vocabulary maps onto the way games name morally loaded player choices: the same act (e.g., betraying an ally, looting a village, choosing a faction) is named neutrally, eulogistically, or dyslogistically depending on the game's ideological orientation. "Tactical efficiency" vs. "ruthlessness" vs. "pragmatism" are triplicate framings of the same in-game decision. This maps to Calleja's **Narrative involvement** dimension, where the player's experience of story and moral weight is shaped by terminological framing. Pentad term: Agency (the vocabulary of naming is the instrument through which motivation is constructed).

**[anticipatory-projection] covering motive → player motivation self-presentation**
The covering-motive analysis applies to how players narrate their own in-game behavior: a player driven primarily by competitive ranking (specific motive) presents their engagement as "enjoying the social experience" or "mastering the craft" (eulogistic cover). This is not unique to games but is heightened there because of the public performance of motivation in social play contexts. Calleja's Shared involvement dimension captures this: how players present themselves to the social group requires the management of motivational presentation. Pentad term: Agent (the performing self who selects which of their motives to foreground).

**[anticipatory-projection] hierarchy as scenic condition → Scene term**
Burke's equation of mystery with class distinction, and his identification of hierarchy as the scenic background of all social rhetoric, maps to the pentad's **Scene** term as applied to game environments. The game world's social hierarchy — guild structures, server reputation, platform prestige — is part of the scene that conditions all motivated action within it. A player's acts are shaped by the scenic fact of where they stand in the hierarchy: the Scene constrains and inflects every Agent's available Agency. Calleja's Shared involvement dimension (social roles and status) is again the relevant PIM anchor, but the pentadic formulation emphasizes the scenic nature of hierarchy as the background condition of all in-game motivation.

**[anticipatory-projection] Carlyle's eulogistic mystery → player "mystique" of rare gear and elite status**
Carlyle's affirmative mystery — the reverence due to "Clothes" as symbols of a profounder, divine order — finds its game analog in the **mystique of rare items, legendary gear, and elite titles**. The player who possesses a world-first achievement title or a discontinued cosmetic item from a game's earliest era is not merely distinguished by functionality; the item or title is a symbol that simultaneously conceals its material origin (luck, time investment, early access) and reveals a presumed underlying excellence. The reverence other players show is formally Carlylean: it is directed at the "clothes" (the cosmetic symbol) while being understood as directed at something beyond them. Calleja's Shared involvement is the landing dimension; the pentad maps this as Agent + Scene (the distinguished agent within a hierarchically structured scene).

## Cross-Pipeline Bridges

**Bridge to Grammar pentad (GM-01/GM-02): Scene and Agency**
This unit enriches the Scene term (from the Grammar's pentad) by specifying hierarchy as the primary scenic condition of social motivation. Mystery arises from the Scene — the structure of social distinctions — not from any individual Agent's intention. Agency (the covering motive, the triplicate vocabulary) is the instrument through which hierarchic Scene conditions are managed rhetorically. The Grammar's scene-agency ratio is here cashed out in social-rhetorical terms: the scene of class hierarchy determines what rhetorical agencies are available and necessary.

**Bridge to Grammar pentad (GM-05/GM-09 Attitude addendum): Attitude as incipient act**
The Bentham analysis's explicit reference to attitude as incipient act (p.91) — and Burke's gloss of tonal gestures as "attitudes" (p.98) — connects directly to the 6th-term addendum seeded in the Grammar. The censorial appellative operates precisely as an attitude: it orients without completing, it disposes without determining. This unit provides some of the clearest RM-side evidence for Attitude as a distinct dramatistic term.

**Bridge to Calleja Shared involvement (In-Game Ch.6)**
Mystery as the rhetoric of social hierarchy maps to Shared involvement across multiple sub-dimensions: prestige hierarchies produce the "different kinds of beings" whose communication generates mystery; guild structures and rank systems are the institutional forms of hierarchy; the fascination with elite players reproduces the clerk-manager dynamic Burke anatomizes. The covering-motive analysis extends into player motivation self-presentation within shared social environments.

**Bridge to Calleja Ludic involvement (In-Game Ch.4/5)**
Mystification (concealed game-system logic) maps to Ludic involvement: the player's engagement with the mechanic layer is structured by the opacity of the underlying rule-system. Where the Ludic dimension is the direct interface with game rules, mystification is the rhetorical condition that keeps the deep structure of those rules from view, shaping Ludic engagement without the player's full awareness of how it is shaped.

**Bridge to RM-01 (identification, consubstantiality)**
The social-hierarchic treatment of identification in this unit (mystery as the medium of identification across social difference) is the complement of RM-01's substance-theoretic treatment. Both are needed for the full integration: identification as shared substance (RM-01) and identification as the charged crossing of categorical difference (RM-03). The consubstantiation bridge to Calleja's incorporation (RM-01 keystone) is extended here into the Shared involvement dimension: players are incorporated into a social world partly by learning to navigate its hierarchic mysteries.

**Bridge to RM-04 (hierarchy, metaphorical view)**
The hierarchy concept is introduced schematically here (the Dionysius the Areopagite reference, pp. 118–119) and will receive full elaboration in RM-04. The progression is: mystery defines the rhetorical texture of hierarchic difference; RM-04 develops hierarchy as an explicit structural concept and applies it through Veblen, Diderot, Machiavelli, and Dante.

**Bridge to RM-06 (courtship)**
The sexual-class analogy and the formal description of inter-class rhetoric as "courtship, rape, seduction, jilting, prostitution" (p.115) explicitly anticipate the full courtship analysis of RM-06. The formal homology between erotic and class rhetoric is stated here; RM-06 develops it through Castiglione, Venus and Adonis, and Kierkegaard.

**Bridge to RM-07 (pure persuasion, secret)**
Carlyle's symbol doctrine — "in a symbol there is concealment yet revelation" — anticipates RM-07's treatment of the secret and pure persuasion as limit-cases of rhetorical movement. The doubled significance of the symbol (speech and silence together) is the prototype of the pure-persuasion structure where rhetoric exceeds any particular addressee or occasion.
