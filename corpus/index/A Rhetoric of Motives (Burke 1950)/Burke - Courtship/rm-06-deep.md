# RM-06 Deep Unit — Courtship & Social Hierarchy
**Burke, *A Rhetoric of Motives* (1950), Part III: Order**
Book pp. 208–251 | PDF pp. 223–266

---

## Overview

This unit develops courtship as the rhetorical principle operative wherever beings of different social kinds communicate across the mystery of their estrangement. Burke introduces "courtship" not as sexual but as a structural phenomenon: the suasive address produced by hierarchic difference. Working through four extended case studies — Shakespeare's *Venus and Adonis* (read "socioanagogically"), Castiglione's *Book of the Courtier* (as paradigm), Kafka's *The Castle* (as grotesque caricature), and Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* (as "dialectical lyric") — Burke shows that identification across ranks is both the motor of rhetoric and its chronic tension. The unit crowns the unit-table's treatment of hierarchy by folding mystery, embarrassment, estrangement, and transcendence into a single rhetoric of social order; it simultaneously seeds the concept of pure persuasion by showing how courtship's dialectic tends toward an ultimate of address from which all practical advantage has been sublimated away.

## Argument (page-anchored walkthrough)

**Courtship defined (p. 208).** Burke opens by defining "the principle of courtship" as the use of suasive devices for transcending social estrangement. Wherever different kinds of beings communicate, there is "mystery," and wherever there is mystery, there is the raw material of rhetoric. Embarrassment — the involuntary self-constraint that arises in the presence of hierarchic difference — is the sign of this mystery. The observation about Sappho's poem (love's physical symptoms as the "magic" of a god-like beloved) anchors the claim: courtship motive pervades social intercourse at every level, however attenuated.

**Hierarchy and erotic imagery; censorship and class (pp. 208–212).** The interchangeability of sexual and social vocabularies rests not on substitution but on intrinsic fusion: sexual courtship is already saturated with hierarchic motive. Burke notes that apparently erotic infractions (Dostoevsky's mysticism of the people, Restoration drama, Wilhelm Reich's "sexual revolution") encode social-hierarchic tensions; censorship of sexual expression may actually dam political expression, and vice versa. "Stage fright," "glamour" (traced etymologically to "gramarye," necromancy — itself a mark of the literate class), and military caste discipline are all read as variants of the same hierarchic mystery. An army's esprit de corps is frankly identified as a "post-hypnotic spell" sustained by the "glamour" of caste; democratizing the army would dissolve the very motive that makes obedience possible (pp. 210–212).

**Mannheim and the persistence of rhetoric (p. 212).** Burke argues against Mannheim's hope that a perfected sociology of knowledge would eliminate social mystery. Rhetoric remains the mode of appeal essential for bridging estrangement "natural" to any society — primitive, feudal, bourgeois, or socialist — for so long as any division of labor produces occupational classes, it simultaneously produces the conditions for courtly intercourse between them.

**"Socioanagogic" reading of Venus and Adonis (pp. 212–221).** Burke offers the term "socioanagogic" for interpretation that reads social hierarchy where ostensibly divine or erotic imagery appears. In Shakespeare's narrative poem, three figures — goddess, mortal youth, boar — represent three motivational classes. Venus is not a "goddess" in any devout sense but a distinguished person forced to demean herself by soliciting an inferior; the underlying proportion is noblewoman-to-commoner. Burke's full (if provisional) class allegory maps Venus = upper class, Adonis = middle, boar = lower. More important than the particular allegory is the structural point: the poem's "erotic enigma" conceals a variant of revolutionary challenge — in sexual imagery it says what no courtly poet could have stated in social or political terms. Burke borrows the four-fold medieval senses (Aquinas's Summa: literal, allegorical, tropological, anagogical) to define the socioanagogic as the secular equivalent of the anagogical sense — reading the social order as the sign of a hidden worldly glory rather than celestial glory (pp. 220–221). The poet's symbols "glow" through a veil of Maya woven of the strands of hierarchy.

**Castiglione as paradigm (pp. 221–233).** The *Book of the Courtier* is treated not as a handbook of etiquette but as a systematic dialectical purification of rhetorical motive through four ascending stages: (1) qualifications and endowments for appeal at court (Book I); (2) tactics of address, the art of self-display, competitive laughter, and the "merry prank" (Book II); (3) the code of courtly intercourse between men and women as classes (Book III); (4) the transcendence of worldly advantage in Cardinal Bembo's Neoplatonic rapture on ideal Beauty (Book IV).

Book I establishes the endowments of appeal: noble birth, arms, horsemanship, wit, music, painting, writing. Burke notes that painting's inclusion recalls that in Greece it was "received in the first degree of liberal arts" and "not taught to servants and bondmen" — the very curriculum of the courtier is a mark of class distinction. The courtier's prime motive is "glory," an essentially addressed motive that lives in the good opinion of others; every endowment is simultaneously an endowment of appeal.

Book II turns to tactics of address — what Burke calls the "art of appearing to best advantage." The courtier should perform his notable feats in the sight of noble men, especially in the presence of the king: the relation to the social superior is "as martyr to God, as writer to public, as actor to audience." Martyrdom is so essentially rhetorical that it borrows its name from the law courts (witness); its difference from mere vanity is that it addresses an absolute rather than a human witness. Burke extracts La Rochefoucauld's maxim on mortifications that are "not known" as evidence of pure rhetorical address: mortifications must be witnessed to serve their purpose. Book II also analyzes the courtier's use of laughter — valuable precisely because it is "liberal" (befitting freemen), "humane" (distinctively rational), and capable of reaffirming the very propriety it violates. The merry jest and merry prank create fraternal solidarity among courtiers as a professional class, deflecting competition for the sovereign's favor into less dangerous channels. When the pranks are directed against the sovereign himself, the mystery becomes so subtilized that the sovereign exercises munificence in accepting them — the "sacrificial king" need not grow wrathful to restore dignity.

Book III introduces men and women confronting one another as classes. The device Burke extracts: the woman invited to dance or play, "like the courtier receiving favors, ought to be brought to it with suffering herself somewhat to be prayed." Sexual courtship and courtly courtship are here in explicit alignment: both operate through the calculated staging of reluctance, the invitation to press further.

The great change occurs in Book IV, signaled by the author's introduction of three courtiers who have died. Burke notes the pun of "end" — purpose vs. demise — as justification for a deathly opening to the section on the ultimate purpose of courtship. Two themes stand out: the courtier as educator of the prince (imparting even unpalatable truths, in contrast to flatterers); and Cardinal Bembo's Platonic oration on beauty as "influence of the heavenly bountifulness." Burke's key insight is that "glory" — simultaneously the courtly and heavenly motive — enables the rhetorical convertibility between social and theological hierarchy. The courtier's relation to the sovereign is structurally identical to the martyr's relation to God. When Castiglione equates the sun and stars as mirrors of God's goodness with good princes as reflections of divine justice, he explicitly performs the fusion that courtship always implicitly enacts. This work thus discloses in overt form the elements that elsewhere exist only in fragments — it makes precise the purely dialectical motives underlying the rhetorical convertibility between secular and sacred hierarchic vocabularies (pp. 232–233). Bembo's oration — ascending from sensory beauty through imagination to ideal beauty, thence through reason to the soul's angelic nature, ending in prayer and ravishment — is identified as the nearest available example of pure persuasion, the rhetorical motive dialectically made ultimate (p. 230). Burke also observes that the courtier-as-educator of the prince finds its model in Socratic erotic: Aristotle with Alexander, Plato with the kings of Sicily — "practiced the deeds of Courtiership" toward the end of philosophic truth.

**Kafka's The Castle as grotesque caricature (pp. 233–244).** Where Castiglione offers a dialectically symmetrical paradigm, Kafka offers its Expressionist caricature: the principle of courtship manifested in grotesque fragments. Burke follows Thomas Mann's formula ("religious humorist") but adds the class-motive Mann suppresses: the religious mystery in *The Castle* is inseparable from the social mystery of bureaucratic hierarchy. K. the land-surveyor is an outsider at multiple removes from the source of favor; the castle, whose German name *Schloss* connotes enclosure and locking, represents both social caste and the divine. Kafka "sees through" social mystery to divine mystery, but never lets the reader forget the disproportion between them, hence the grotesque humor. Burke maps three elements of the Castiglione paradigm onto Kafka: the problem of qualification/favor (K.'s nightmarish attempts to gain access correspond to the courtier's protocols of advancement); laughter (the grotesque "humor" of reverence); and education (K.'s role as land-surveyor, one who would specify positions and elevations, figuring the Jewish intellectual's ambiguous position — "in" by intellectual status, "out" by unnamed curse). The Barnabas episode (A mysterious to B, B mysterious to A, both because of their different relations to C) shows how the very absence of detectable motive intensifies the sense of mystery. The imagery of childhood throughout (the castle as narrative "first" paralleling logical essence, peasants and assistants described as childlike, the schoolroom as living quarters) follows a grammatical convertibility whereby essence stated logically becomes childhood stated narratively (pp. 241–242).

**Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling as "dialectical lyric" (pp. 244–251).** The transition from Kafka to Kierkegaard is bridged by Max Brod's observation that the Sortini episode in *The Castle* (where an official demands something obscene of a girl) is a parallel to the Abraham/Isaac story in *Fear and Trembling* — in both cases, what the higher authority demands is, by ordinary morality, a crime. This "incommensurability" of earthly and religious aims takes one to the heart of Kafka's novel. Burke uses this as the bridge into an extended analysis of Kierkegaard's "dialectical lyric."

Burke reads Kierkegaard's treatment of Abraham's sacrifice as a metarhetoric of perpetual courtship. Kierkegaard had jilted Regina; *Fear and Trembling* is the dialectical operation that redeems the jilting by transforming "scoundrel" into "knight of infinite resignation" and then "knight of faith." Burke's structural analysis distinguishes amplification (making explicit what is already in a text) from psychologizing (adding a new principle to the text); Kierkegaard adds the "scoundrelly" variant of Abraham's psychology to Genesis 22, thus inserting the very principle he needs. A subsidiary weaning-parable (mother blackening her breast) is identified as closer to Kierkegaard's actual motives than the featured Biblical anecdote, pointing toward a complex of maternal/erotic dissociation. The dialectical upshot: because Kierkegaard would court eternally (as a verbal/dialectical motive rather than a finite practical one), he required an objective impediment — once Regina married another man, the practical impossibility of their union became the "objective correlative" that restored the necessary courtship-distance. The unit closes identifying this as a dialectical resolution: renunciation and advance fused in a single attitude, the infinite present in the finite, perpetual courtship as metarhetoric (p. 251).

## Key Concepts

**Courtship** [SPINE]
The use of suasive devices for transcending social estrangement; the rhetoric generated whenever beings of different social kinds address one another across the "mystery" of their hierarchic difference. Not primarily sexual but structural: embarrassment, deference, display, and self-constraint are its symptoms. Burke's master term for the social-motive apparatus.

**Mystery (social)** [SPINE]
The quasi-magical opacity that attaches to hierarchic difference and makes it suasively potent. Wherever different kinds of beings communicate, mystery is present. Related: "glamour" (mystery in the idiom of publicity), military caste, the sacred aura of office that exceeds the intrinsic qualities of its holder.

**Hierarchy** [SPINE]
The ordered gradation of social kinds that generates the conditions for courtship. Burke argues that any society with a division of labor produces the conditions for courtly intercourse between classes; hierarchy is not an aberration but the structural ground of social rhetoric. The castle, the court, and the corporate org-chart are all instances.

**Identification** [SPINE]
Identification across hierarchic difference is the aim and product of courtship; the suasive address that closes (however provisionally) the distance between unequal social kinds. Socioanagogic reading detects implicit identifications between social orders and cosmic/divine orders.

**Consubstantiality** [SPINE — implicit]
The unit does not use the term directly but instantiates its logic throughout: courtship works because estranged parties are already substantially bound by common participation in the social order (consubstantial in hierarchy itself), even as they remain separated in rank. The convertibility of social and religious reverence rests on this shared substance.

**Socioanagogic interpretation**
Burke's secular adaptation of the medieval fourfold sense, specifically the anagogical: reading social hierarchy where divine or erotic imagery appears on the surface. The poet's symbols "glow" with hierarchic meaning. Named midway between Marx's rage at "mystification" and Carlyle's adulation of "mystery."

**Embarrassment (social)**
The involuntary self-constraint triggered by hierarchic difference; evidence of mystery in social intercourse. A non-deliberate rhetorical effect: the person of lower rank becomes rhetorical by virtue of their discomfort, the higher-ranked person by their studied reserve.

**Glamour**
Etymology: corruption of "gramarye" (necromancy, magic). Popular recognition that certain persons or objects possess a hierarchic radiance — "any artificial interest in, or association with, an object, through which it appears elusively magnified or glorified." The mark of status operating at the level of perception itself.

**Pure persuasion** [SPINE — seeded]
Glimpsed but not yet fully developed: education dialectically approached has a "mythic glow" as a form of pure persuasion, the rhetorical motive made ultimate. Castiglione's Book IV, with its transcendence of all practical advantage in Platonic ecstasy, is the nearest available paradigm. The concept will be foregrounded in RM-07.

**Attitude (incipient act)** [SPINE — implicit]
Kierkegaard's perpetual courtship — "renunciation and advance fused in one attitude" — is the clearest instance: it is not a completed act but a sustained orientation, a stance that holds the tension without resolving it. The "attitude" of the courtier toward the sovereign, perpetually in process, similarly qualifies.

**The "dialectical lyric"**
Burke's characterization of *Fear and Trembling*: a work that performs its argument through feeling-toned dialectical operations rather than through discursive demonstration; a lyric whose movement is dialectical (the Kierkegaardian "leap").

**Caricature of courtship**
The grotesque or fragmentary instantiation of the courtship principle, as in Kafka, where the formal elements of Castiglione's paradigm are present but distorted: the access-seeker never gains access, the sovereign's representatives are nobodies, the mystery is both intensified and made absurd by its own opacity.

**Hazing and initiation**
The informal, unacknowledged ritual through which outsiders are tested before admission to the inner sanctum of a hierarchic group. Burke's example of the 1929 radical literary organizations (where newcomers were subjected to informal freezing-out by old-timers) illustrates how mystery requires initiation rites, and how their absence creates uncanny suffering without resolution.

**Martyrdom as rhetoric**
The bearing of witness before a supermundane audience. The courtier's feats performed in the sight of the sovereign, the martyr's suffering before God — both are instances of address to an absolute witness. The word "martyrdom" itself derives from the law courts (witness). Epideictic oratory in extremis.

**Reversibility of social and religious reverence**
The structural interchangeability of deference to secular and sacred authority; Burke's recurring claim that wherever genuine religious reverence is present, social hierarchy subtends it, and vice versa. Castiglione makes it explicit; Kafka makes it grotesque; Kierkegaard makes it dialectical.

**Socioanagogic reading of Venus and Adonis**
The specific demonstration: goddess-to-mortal as noblewoman-to-commoner; the poem's "erotic enigma" as a concealed revolutionary challenge against the established social order, its ending on Venus's prophecy of a topsy-turvy world as the poem's true hierarchic argument.

**Paradigm vs. caricature**
The methodological pair: Castiglione as the symmetrical, complete, classically resolved instance; Kafka as the fragmentary, grotesque, unresolved instance. Together they mark the range within which the courtship principle actually operates.

**The courtier's addressed quality**
Every endowment of the courtier is an endowment of appeal; his prime motive is "glory," which is an essentially addressed motive — it lives in the good opinion of others. The courtier's entire existence is rhetorical in essence.

**Kierkegaardian leap**
The dialectical transformation that converts finite contingencies (the jilting) into a vocabulary adequate to the infinite. Burke reads it as a metarhetorical operation: by psychologizing the Biblical text (adding the "scoundrelly" variant of Abraham) Kierkegaard inserts the very principle he needs, rather than drawing it out of a text already there.

## Identification & Consubstantiation Development

This unit advances the master term by showing how identification operates across the most intractable of social distances — hierarchic rank — and how it does so through the irreducible mechanism of mystery. Courtship is the mode of identification appropriate to estrangement: it does not pretend the estrangement away but exploits it suasively. Every act of courtly address is an act of identification-seeking across a gulf that remains structurally in place.

The socioanagogic reading fuses identification's vertical and horizontal dimensions: the symbolic identifications in Venus and Adonis run simultaneously across sexual, social, and cosmic registers. This is not metaphor but the actual grammar of identification as Burke understands it — the poet's topics "glow" with hierarchic meaning because identification between the social and the cosmic is structural, not decorative.

The Castiglione paradigm shows identification's escalating trajectory: from identification between courtier and peer (laughter as fraternal solidarity among professionals), to identification between courtier and sovereign, to identification between the human soul and ideal Beauty/God. The convertibility of social and religious reverence is the demonstration that identification's logic, once set in motion, knows no terminal rank — it reaches toward an ultimate ground of shared substance. Consubstantiality is latent throughout: even as ranks are distinguished (and must be, to sustain the mystery that makes rhetoric possible), the parties to courtship are bound by participation in the same hierarchic order, the same system of glory-seeking, the same ultimate orientation toward a transcendent ground.

## Attitude (Incipient-Act) Signals

The unit contains several strong anticipations of the sixth dramatistic term:

1. **Castiglione's courtier's stance toward the sovereign** — perpetually in a posture of reverent appeal, neither too forward nor too retiring, always holding the tension between approach and deference. This is not a completed act but a sustained orientation — the paradigm of Attitude as the rhetorical-social stance.

2. **Kierkegaard's perpetual courtship** — the most explicit instance. "Renunciation and advance fused in one attitude" (p. 251) is Burke's own formulation. The "knight of faith" courts his "princess" in perpetual repetition; the act is never completed because completion would dissolve the motivating distance. The Attitude holds the incipient act perpetually in suspension.

3. **K.'s exhausting approach-behavior in The Castle** — K. never achieves the interview; his posture is one of unresolved, perpetually attempted approach. The grotesque caricature of the courtly attitude: all incipience, no completion.

4. **Embarrassment as pre-rhetorical Attitude** — the involuntary constraint of social estrangement is a bodily Attitude before it becomes a deliberate rhetorical act. Burke links it structurally to "stage fright" and to the audience/performer relation in Mann.

## Game-Behavior Application (Anticipatory)

All mappings in this section carry provenance `[anticipatory-projection]` — these are not Burke's claims.

**Calleja Shared involvement dimension** [anticipatory-projection]
Courtship as the rhetoric of social estrangement maps with high density onto Calleja's Shared involvement dimension — the mode of player engagement that arises from co-presence with and social orientation toward other players. Specifically:

- *Guild recruitment and initiation rituals* — the hazing dynamic Burke describes (informal rites that test outsiders before admission to the inner sanctum) is structurally identical to guild initiation: the prospective member endures a period of ambiguous standing, neither fully in nor out, before earning the mystery of full membership. The "mystery trampled" by newcomers who bypass initiation (Burke's 1929 literary-organization anecdote) maps directly onto the casual-player phenomenon in MMOs, where veteran players resist the "loss of mystery" when mass influx occurs. [anticipatory-projection]

- *Mentor-mentee and cross-rank dynamics* — the courtier's deference posture toward the sovereign, and the simultaneous need to display competence before an audience of peers, maps onto the social dynamics of ranked play: the apprentice performing for a master, the newer player absorbing the mystery of elite technique. The courtier's "addressed quality" — endowments designed as appeal — corresponds to the social performance of skill in visible, witnessed contexts. [anticipatory-projection]

- *Elite player allure / "glamour" of high rank* — Burke's gloss of "glamour" as "any artificial interest in, or association with, an object, through which it appears elusively magnified or glorified" anticipates the aura of high-rank players, legendary gear tiers, or exclusive titles. The mystery of elite status operates at the level of perception: ordinary players feel a hierarchic radiance when they encounter raided-out or prestige-ranked opponents that exceeds anything attributable to those players' intrinsic skill. [anticipatory-projection]

- *Cross-faction or cross-server social drama* — the mystery generated between beings of "different kinds" in Burke maps onto the social charge of cross-faction encounters, server-transfer arrivals, or PvP rivalries where the parties carry the mark of their different communities. These encounters have a courtship quality: neither pure identification nor pure antagonism, but a suasively loaded contact across hierarchic estrangement. [anticipatory-projection]

**Kafka's Castle as opaque progression system** [anticipatory-projection]
The grotesque caricature of courtship anticipates the phenomenology of alienated game bureaucracy: opaque progression systems, gatekeeping mechanics, and player experiences of permanent provisional standing. K.'s experience — approaching an access point that perpetually recedes, receiving contradictory signals from representatives of an authority that neither fully acknowledges nor definitively rejects him — describes the affective structure of:

- RNG-gated content and loot systems where "the sources of favor are at several removes"
- Matchmaking and rank placement systems whose criteria remain opaque ("the charge against him is never specified")
- Community gatekeeping in competitive games, where informal hazing processes operate without formal acknowledgment

The *Castle*'s mystery — intensified rather than dispelled by the absence of discoverable motives — matches the documented player frustration with progression systems that are felt as arbitrary rather than earned. Burke's insight that "once you learn the rules, the very lack of motives for the mystery adds to the sense of mystery" has direct application: engagement with opaque progression systems can itself become part of their appeal once players are socialized into them. [anticipatory-projection]

**Pentad mappings** [anticipatory-projection]
- *Agent* — the unit is overwhelmingly about Agent: the courtier as the agent-type defined by his social position and his orientation toward co-agents above and below him in rank. Co-agent relations (courtier/sovereign, K./castle, Kierkegaard/Regina) are the primary dramatic units.
- *Scene* — hierarchic order is the Scene within which courtship acts occur; the Scene constrains, enables, and motivates the Agent's rhetorical posture. The castle, the court of Urbino, the social milieu that makes Kierkegaard's jilting socially consequential — these are Scenes that generate rhetorical necessity.
- *Attitude* — courtship is pre-eminently an Attitude: the incipient-act orientation of deference, approach, display, and calculated withdrawal that constitutes the rhetorical stance. Kierkegaard's perpetual courtship makes this explicit.
- *Act* — individual acts of courtly address (the performed feat, the merry prank, the favor-seeking approach) are Acts, but the unit is more interested in the Attitude that surrounds and contextualizes them.

## Cross-Pipeline Bridges

**Burke Grammar of Motives (pentad):**
- The scene-agent ratio is the dominant ratio in this unit: hierarchic Scene generates and constrains Agent. The courtier is constituted as an agent-type by the scene of the court. The castle is not merely a setting but a generative principle from which K.'s entire situation derives.
- The grammar of "regression" (logical priority stated narratively as temporal/childhood priority) is explicitly named on pp. 241–242 as a grammatical resource; bridges to GM-era pentadic analysis of how essence is stated in narrative terms.
- Socratic erotic (referenced at pp. 230, 249) explicitly cross-references the Grammar's treatment of Plato's Phaedrus and the Socratic erotic as a pedagogical cult of courtship — the philosopher's gallant cajoling of young men into the dialectical path.
- The convertibility between social and religious hierarchic vocabulary is the rhetorical equivalent of the Grammar's discussion of the "god-term" or "ultimate" as the logical terminus of terministic hierarchies. RM-06's courtship apparatus supplies the rhetorical mechanism by which ordinary social terms acquire this ultimate dimension.

**Burke Rhetoric of Motives RM-03 (mystery/mystification):**
- The socioanagogic concept is the rhetorical operationalization of what RM-03 develops as Marxist mystification and Carlyle's mystery. Socioanagogic interpretation is explicitly positioned as "midway" between Marx's rage at mystification and Carlyle's adulation of mystery (p. 220). Where RM-03 shows how mystery functions ideologically (concealing class relations as natural), RM-06 shows how the same mystery functions rhetorically (generating the conditions for courtship-address across those relations). The two analyses are complementary: every instance of ideological mystification is simultaneously a rhetorical resource.

**Burke Rhetoric of Motives RM-04 (hierarchy, Veblen, Machiavelli):**
- The hierarchic motive in its applied forms (Veblen's invidious, Diderot, La Rochefoucauld) is presupposed here; RM-06 extends those analyses into literary case studies.

**Burke Rhetoric of Motives RM-07 (pure persuasion):**
- The unit seeds pure persuasion via Castiglione's Book IV and the Kierkegaardian dialectical lyric; RM-07 will develop the concept as the limiting case of rhetorical address.

**Calleja In-Game (Shared involvement, Ch. 6):**
- Courtship as social-motive apparatus maps structurally onto Shared involvement: the social dimension of player engagement that arises from orientation toward other players across asymmetries of rank, experience, and access. Guild initiation, mentor-mentee, elite-player allure, and cross-faction drama are the primary game-behavior correspondences. [anticipatory-projection]
- The hazing/initiation dynamic Burke analyzes (the informal, unacknowledged ritual of testing outsiders, the "trampling on mystery" by newcomers who bypass the process) is directly operative in any gaming community with established membership and prestige hierarchies. Burke's insight that mystery requires formal recognition of the initiation process — and that its absence creates uncanny suffering for all parties — anticipates research on guild dissolution, community toxicity, and the alienation of veteran players when progression barriers are arbitrarily lowered. [anticipatory-projection]
- Kierkegaard's perpetual courtship — the willingness to court in terms of the infinite, in perpetual repetition — maps onto the motivational structure of "completionist" or "eternal return" players whose engagement is sustained precisely by the impossibility of final acquisition. The distance-preserving logic of Kierkegaard's knight of faith reappears in players who, once they have achieved a goal, immediately seek a new one, or who find engagement most alive in the approach rather than the attainment. [anticipatory-projection]

**Calleja In-Game (incorporation, Ch. 10):**
- The consubstantiality logic implicit in courtship (estranged parties bound by common hierarchic substance) subtends Calleja's incorporation: the player's progressive taking-in of the game-world's social structure, including its rank distinctions and mystery auras, is a form of hierarchic consubstantiation. The newcomer who undergoes initiation is not merely learning mechanics but becoming consubstantial with the community's hierarchic order. [anticipatory-projection]
- The socioanagogic reading — detecting social hierarchy in ostensibly neutral game elements — maps onto the interpretive practice players apply when they read lore, aesthetics, and design conventions as expressions of rank. The "glow" of legendary gear, the visual distinction of prestige titles, the spatial isolation of high-level zones: these are instances of hierarchic radiance operating at the level of game-perception. [anticipatory-projection]
