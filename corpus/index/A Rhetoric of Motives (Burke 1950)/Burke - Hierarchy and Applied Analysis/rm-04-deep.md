# RM-04 Deep Analysis — Hierarchy & Applied Analysis
## *A Rhetoric of Motives* (Burke 1950), Book pp. 123–180 / PDF pp. 138–195

---

## Overview

Unit RM-04 constitutes Burke's extended applied-analysis workshop, reading a sequence of canonical authors — Empson, Veblen, Bacon/Shaw, Rochefoucauld, De Gourmont, Pascal, Machiavelli, Dante — as case studies in the rhetorical operations of hierarchy, identification, mystery, courtship, and imitation. The pivotal theoretical node is "A Metaphorical View of Hierarchy" (pp. 137–142), where Burke argues that hierarchy is not merely a sociological fact but a universal motivational and symbolic principle — the "hierarchic psychosis" that permeates all symbolic action, from theological cosmology to class literature to political conspiracy. The unit closes with an extended meditation on infancy, mystery, and persuasion that ties hierarchy inextricably to the dynamics of courtship and the theological reach of the persuasive act. Identification here deepens beyond conscious address: Burke shows how imitation, emulation, and the invidious comparison are all special cases of a more fundamental symbolic impulse toward consubstantiality.

The structural logic of the unit is cumulative: each author-reading adds a layer to the theory of hierarchy-as-motive. Empson shows how hierarchy produces the literary strategies of class embarrassment and cross-class courtship; Veblen reveals how competitive conformity (out-imitation) is the social expression of the hierarchic drive; the "Priority of the Idea" section locates the symbolic-linguistic motive as prior to the economic; the Hierarchy section provides the theoretical architecture; Diderot, Rochefoucauld, De Gourmont, and Pascal each furnish analytical templates — pantomime as bodily enactment of position, self-love as courtly formation, dissociation as unmasking of automatic identifications, casuistry as exploitation of the act/intention gap. Machiavelli then demonstrates administrative rhetoric as the synthesis of verbal and nonverbal persuasion, and the Dante section opens the question of how the hierarchic principle extends into the unconscious strata of language and identification. The final synthesis on infancy and mystery completes the arc by showing that the mystery produced by social hierarchy is not merely an epiphenomenon of class relations but is implicit in the structure of the persuasive act as such.

## Argument (page-anchored walkthrough)

**Empson on "Pastoral" Identification (pp. 123–127).**
Burke opens by positioning Empson's *English Pastoral Poetry* (also *Some Versions of Pastoral*) as an investigation into "recondite rhetoric" — the courtship between contrasted social classes that pastoral as a genre has always negotiated (p. 123). Pastoral mediates class difference without abolishing it: the "beautiful relation between rich and poor" is precisely a stylistic transcendence of conflict, not its resolution. Burke analyses how Empson's readings of Gray's *Elegy*, through what Burke considers a five-part rhetorical logic, disclose how literary "resignation" doubles as a "bid for preferment" — the bowed posture is itself a form of identification (p. 125). The modes of class embarrassment Empson catalogs — comic primness, prim irony, mock simplicity — are all under the heading of identification: the attempt of stratified persons to achieve, through rhetorical indirection, a shared symbolic field that their material positions deny them (pp. 126–127).

**The "Invidious" as Imitation: Veblen (pp. 127–132).**
Burke opens the Veblen section by positioning the *Theory of the Leisure Class* as the appropriate counterpart to Empson on the sociological-satirical side: where Empson examines the cross-class courtship of pastoral with literary finesse, Veblen treads more cumulatively, fixing on "conspicuous waste" and "vicarious consumption" as the master expressions of social advantage. Burke rates Veblen's *Theory of the Leisure Class* as valuable satire but a deficient account of motives. Veblen's diagnostic terms — "invidious," "pecuniary," "conspicuous waste" — are censorial terms masquerading as scientific neutrals (pp. 128–129). Burke's objection is not merely terminological: the deeper problem is that Veblen collapses what should be the more general concept of *imitation* into the narrower "invidious." Competitive behavior, properly understood, is a frenzied form of conformity — men attempting to out-imitate one another (p. 131). The so-called "competition" of capitalist society, when examined closely, reveals itself as zealous conformity: businesses and the families of businessmen amassing and displaying all the insignia felt proper to their status. This is not predation but imitation. Imitation is "an essentially dramatistic concept": it "makes for consubstantiality by community of ways (identification)" (p. 131). Both crude surface-imitation and subtle imitation of underlying principles are forms of consubstantiation — sharing in a common mode of being. The "pecuniary" motive should be treated as a special case of the linguistic/symbolic motive, not as a primitive predatory instinct, because money is itself a dialectical-symbolic abstraction: "under the sign of the Ladder," and hence rhetorically convertible with the patterns of "celestial hierarchy" (pp. 135–136). This moves Veblen's critique from economics to semiotics and reconnects it to the universal hierarchic principle.

**Priority of the "Idea" (pp. 132–137).**
Burke distinguishes three orders of cultural identification: (1) mechanical or accidental association (conditioned-reflex linkage of signs); (2) analogizing transfer of principles from one domain to another (the "occupational psychosis" — Burke cites Dewey and Shaw's dramatistic inversion in *Back to Methuselah*); (3) distinct specialized expressions all derived from a common generating principle without direct borrowing (Spenglerian "cultural essences"). The third order is "ideological" in the constructive sense: grasping the underlying principle allows creative re-expression across all domains. Money is the paradigm case — itself symbolic of dialectical processes, "prior" to the economic in the sense that the laws of symbols are prior to any particular property structure (pp. 136–137).

**A Metaphorical View of Hierarchy (pp. 137–142).**
This is the theoretical pivot of the unit. Burke stages a speculative myth — organisms nostalgic for the sea, a doubly regressive longing — to illustrate that the hierarchic principle is not merely the arrangement of ranks but the demand that every rank internalize the principle of gradation itself, reversing higher and lower at the level of principle (p. 138). The Christian doctrine that "the first shall be last" is not social-revolutionary utopianism but a dialectical expression of this reversibility: the "heavenly state" is the realm of pure principle, prior to any particular arrangement (pp. 138–139). Hierarchy generates a sequence of social-affective stances: ideally, love and charity between divided beings; then the ironic embarrassments and modified tributes of courtship; then tragic attempts to transform hate into love "on a higher level"; finally, conspiracy and war, which produce their own perverse pseudo-unity (pp. 139–140).

Burke formulates the key theoretical claims at pp. 141–142:
- The hierarchic principle is inevitable in systematic thought, embodied in the very process of growth and learning.
- No particular hierarchy is inevitable; hierarchies form and crumble. But the principle is "indigenous to all well-rounded human thinking."
- Hierarchy includes the *entelechial* tendency: the "top" or "culminating" stage is treated as the "image" that best represents the entire "idea" — the prince represents the principle of order.
- Though hierarchy is exclusive, the principle of hierarchy is not: all ranks can "share in it alike."
- The mystification of hierarchy proceeds by confounding the universal principle with the particular privilege of the top rank.

**Diderot on "Pantomime" (pp. 142–145).**
Diderot's *Neveu de Rameau* is read as a dramatization of hierarchic disorder. The dialogue form mirrors the author's internal division: "Lui" speaks the disorder that a perfectly symmetrical feudal hierarchy (now lost) would have organized; "Moi" represents the bourgeois-rationalist who nonetheless desires such architectonic symmetry (p. 142). "Pantomime" — obsequiousness, the postures of social position — is Diderot's term for the universal choreography of hierarchy: "all pantomime is the grand branle of the earth" (p. 144). Everyone dances their position before everyone above them in the chain. Lui's criminal aesthetic — sublime precisely in its vice — carries the "mystery" of aristocracy. Burke reads Lui as a point of convergence representing both the royal cause of disorder and its demoralizing effects (p. 144–145).

**Generic, Specific, and Individual Motives in Rochefoucauld (pp. 145–150).**
La Rochefoucauld's maxims are "a courtly rhetoric, with the individual defined by his place in an institution" (p. 147). His primary term, *amour-propre*, appears to be individualistic but is in fact a social-institutional formation: devotion to princes is a "second self-love," and Burke argues La Rochefoucauld has the two motives "ideologically reversed" — the social (courtly) motive is prior, the individual one its residue. Burke's six-moment analysis (pp. 147–149) moves from (1) biologically-grounded individual divisiveness, through (2) class thinking, (3) the idea of courting as local principle, (4) individual identity conceived in courtly terms, (5) reduction to "self-love," and (6) rediscovery of the princely/courtly principle behind that self-love. The pun on "prince"/"principle" is not accidental: the entelechial mode of thought makes the prince — the highest rung — represent the general principle of hierarchic order (p. 148). Rochefoucauld thus illuminates the rhetorical structure of all class morality, showing how self-love is already a social term for individual divisiveness (p. 149).

**De Gourmont on "Dissociation" (pp. 149–154).**
De Gourmont's method of "original dissociation" — breaking the automatic linkages between facts and abstractions that constitute rhetorical commonplaces — is praised as a mental gymnastic that clarifies how identification operates. Every commonplace secretly embeds the pattern of the "god incarnate": an abstraction ("freedom," "justice") is linked to a particular empirical condition as if divinely embodied in it (pp. 150–151). Burke's methodological qualification: De Gourmont over-relies on the image; a realistic analysis of abstractions should treat them as verbs — acts by agents in scenes — and by this route produces something closer to Marxist analysis (pp. 152–153). Dissociation is valuable not for its own sake but as a cleansing transition from enslaving old associations to rationally adopted new ones (pp. 153–154).

**Pascal on "Directing the Intention" (pp. 154–158).**
Pascal's seventh *Provincial Letter*, attacking Jesuit casuistry, illustrates the "Pauline" element at the roots of language: every abstraction is a "divinity" that can be incarnated in particular conditions, and rhetoric exploits this by selecting the intention under which an act is classified. The Jesuit slogan "direct the intention" — keep the act, change the stated motive — exemplifies how rhetoric operates in the gap between act and purpose-attribution (pp. 156–157). Burke reads this not as merely theological but as the general structure of administrative rhetoric: wherever verbal framing and material operation are both in play, intention-direction is available as a resource.

**"Administrative" Rhetoric in Machiavelli (pp. 158–167).**
*The Prince* is treated as a manual of "administrative rhetoric" — persuasion that includes nonverbal, empirical operations as well as symbolic ones (pp. 158–161). Burke's comparison with Ovid's *Ars Amatoria* shows that both manuals operate on the same rhetorical structure: topics of advantage, the manipulation of scenes, management of appearances, and the use of nonverbal conditions as signs (pp. 159–161). The key analytical payoff is Burke's use of the scene-act ratio and agent-act ratio from the *Grammar* to unlock the structure of *The Prince* (pp. 162–163): the first twenty-four chapters are all variants of scene-act ratio (act proper to situation), while the next-to-last chapter introduces the agent-act ratio (act proper to the ruler's temperament). The final chapter's exhortation reconciles the two, calling for the agent whose temperament and the times coincide — and offers the ultimate identification of ruler and ruled (p. 163). Burke reads Machiavelli's ambiguity as the ambiguity of nationalism itself: "Where conspiracy is the fact, universality must often be the fiction" (p. 166).

**Dante's *De Vulgari Eloquentia* (pp. 167–170).**
Dante's quest for an "illustrious, cardinal, courtly, and curial" language is read as thoroughly hierarchic: all four criteria carry hierarchic connotations (p. 167). The paradox that the "vulgar" (vernacular) idiom is the more "noble" because it is the speech learned in infancy — closer to the "unconscious" and to identification — sets up Dante as adumbrating a concern with the motives of the unconscious and "infancy" as rhetorical force (pp. 167–168). Burke compares Dante to D.H. Lawrence's linguistic primitivism in *Lady Chatterley's Lover*: both attempt to reclaim a pre-grammatical speech closer to the unconscious, though Lawrence's hierarchy is "essentially rightist along Carlylean lines" without Dante's architectonic symmetry (pp. 169–170).

**Rhetoric in the Middle Ages and "Infancy," Mystery, and Persuasion (pp. 170–180).**
Burke surveys McKeon's scholarship on medieval rhetoric and the crossing of rhetoric with medicine ("administrative" nonverbal persuasion), then builds toward the closing theoretical synthesis on infancy, mystery, and persuasion (pp. 174–180). The final argument: persuasion is intrinsically mysterious because communication is between different kinds, and the courtship structure is built into all such communication. The ten-step sequence (pp. 176–178) moves from persuasion-as-communication → communication between kinds → abstract courtship → reverent beseechment → prayer → theological God. The "mystery" that haunts class relations is not merely ideological mystification but an intrinsic property of the persuasive act itself, rooted in the symbolic animal's orientation toward the hierarchically arranged world of meanings (pp. 178–180).

## Key Concepts

**Hierarchy** [SPINE]. The universal motivational and symbolic principle by which social and cognitive order is arranged on a scale of "higher" and "lower." Burke argues hierarchy is not merely descriptive of social stratification but operative in all systematic thought, growth, and development. The hierarchic principle is reversible at the level of pure principle (first/last inversion), which is both its rhetorical power and its susceptibility to mystification. (pp. 137–142)

**Hierarchic psychosis**. Burke's term (implicitly, by analogy with "occupational psychosis") for the pervasive motivational disposition that arises when the hierarchic principle structures consciousness: the obsessive orientation to rank, ascent, distinction, and the "mystery" of those above and below one's position. Manifests as courtship, as comic primness, as pantomime.

**Entelechial principle**. The tendency to treat the culminating or "top" stage of any hierarchy as the representative image of the whole: the prince represents the principle of hierarchic order; the "idea" is expressed in its most fully realized instance. This makes hierarchy mystifying because the universal principle coincides with the most particular privilege.

**Identification** [SPINE]. Continues to develop across this unit as the mechanism behind imitation, emulation, and competitive conformity. Competition is out-imitation; pastoral is cross-class identification by rhetorical indirection; Dante's vernacular language is identification with the pre-grammatical unconscious.

**Consubstantiality** [SPINE]. Surfaces explicitly at p. 131: imitation "makes for consubstantiality by community of ways (identification)." The shared principle or community of practice is the substrate in which different agents become consubstantial. This unit shows how consubstantiality is achieved through imitation of *principles* (not mere insignia) and through the pastoral/courtship strategies that allow different class positions to find common symbolic ground.

**Imitation**. The master term under which Burke subsumes competition, the invidious, emulation, and identification. Dramatistic concept (not anthropomorphic): both crude surface-imitation and subtle imitation of underlying principles. The linguistic/symbolic motive is prior to and more general than the pecuniary motive.

**The Invidious** (Veblen's term, critically assessed). Veblen's term for comparison-of-persons-in-respect-of-worth. Burke argues it is a censorially partisan word where the more general word "imitation" is needed: "invidious" is a special case of imitation, not the other way around.

**Occupational psychosis** (Dewey). The imaginative transference of principles from one professional field to a general worldview; Burke's example is Shaw constructing a philosophy of creative evolution on the analogy of his craft as playwright.

**Pantomime** (Diderot). The choreography of social position — the postures, obsequiousness, and ritual deference that enact hierarchic arrangement. Burke takes it as the universal symbol of the hierarchic psychosis made visible. Dogs even sense the "mystery" of class through costume.

**Comic primness** (Empson). An ironic attitude of a privileged person who somewhat questions the conditions of his privilege but ultimately resigns himself to them — a mixture of apology and acquiescence. Paradigmatic of the embarrassments that mystified class produces.

**Directing the intention** (Pascal/Jesuit). The rhetorical device of re-attributing a performed act to a socially approved purpose rather than its actual motive. An instance of the gap between act and symbolic framing that rhetoric exploits. Burke generalizes it beyond theology to all administrative rhetoric.

**Administrative rhetoric**. Rhetoric that includes nonverbal, material operations — spectacles, military force, police clubs, medical apparatus — as well as verbal address. The nonverbal element persuades by its "meaning" (symbolic character), not merely by material effect. Machiavelli and Ovid are the chief exemplars.

**Scene-act ratio / Agent-act ratio** [cross-pipeline, from *Grammar*]. Burke deploys these Grammar terms to analyse *The Prince*: chapters 1–24 = scene-act (what act fits this scene?); chapter 25 = agent-act (what act fits this person's nature?); chapter 26 = their coincidence as the rhetorical exhortation.

**Mystery** [SPINE]. Developed here as the intrinsic product of the hierarchic courtship structure of persuasion. Mystery is not only ideological mystification of class but an intrinsic property of communication between different kinds of beings. Persuasion as courtship = communication across difference = mystery.

**Courtship** [SPINE]. The rhetorical modality of communication across social difference — always present wherever hierarchic arrangement structures relations between classes. Courtship includes the full range from outright flattery to ironically veiled challenge, from deference to conspiracy.

**Infancy**. Burke's term for the pre-verbal, sub-verbal, and unconscious strata of meaning that give symbols their resonance and mystery beyond explicit content. Linked to the "speechlessness" of the nonverbal, the unconscious, and the superverbal. Dante's valorization of the vernacular as learned "in infancy" points toward identification with the unconscious.

**The negative** [SPINE — implicit]. Present throughout in the structure of social division, the invidious comparison, and the scapegoat: the hierarchic principle "protects" its top rank by loading dyslogistic terms onto lower ranks or alien groups.

**Pure persuasion** [SPINE — anticipatory]. The terminal point of Burke's ten-step synthesis (pp. 176–178): abstract courtship between kinds, taken to its limit, becomes theology — the ultimate "addressing" of the most pure and universal being. The route from persuasion to God is the route from ordinary rhetoric to pure persuasion.

**Dissociation** (De Gourmont). The intellectual practice of severing automatic associations between facts and abstract ideals. Rhetorically valuable as a cleansing transition; methodologically limited by over-reliance on the image vs. the verb/act.

**Terministic screen** [SPINE — implicit]. Veblen's choice of "invidious" is itself a terministic screen: the censorially charged term foregrounds one aspect of competitive behavior (predatory, invidious) while screening out the more general principle of imitation and consubstantiality.

## Identification & Consubstantiation Development

Identification deepens significantly in this unit. The unit's key advance is the explicit statement that imitation "makes for consubstantiality by community of ways (identification)" (p. 131). This is not merely metaphorical: imitation of underlying *principles* (not mere surface insignia) is the mechanism by which agents become consubstantial — participating in a shared generating principle that manifests differently across different domains of activity.

The Rochefoucauld analysis extends this: self-love is a social term for individual divisiveness, and the "devotion paid to princes" is not derived from self-love but constitutive of it. This means that social identification (courtship, hierarchy) is prior to individual identity — identity is formed in and through the consubstantial field of hierarchic order. The individual is consubstantial with his class position and with the universal principle that the top of the hierarchy embodies.

The Dante section ties identification to the unconscious: the speech learned in infancy is the speech that bypasses grammatical secondarity and connects directly to the pre-verbal strata — identification with an audience at the level of the unconscious, not merely the conscious.

The closing synthesis (pp. 176–180) establishes that the drive toward identification is built into the structure of persuasion as such: communication between different kinds → courtship → mystery → theology. The attempt to overcome the original divisiveness of the nervous system through symbolic union is the deepest form of consubstantiality — it motivates all persuasion, not just the conscious rhetorical act.

## Attitude (Incipient-Act) Signals

Attitude as the 6th dramatistic term — incipient or suspended action — surfaces throughout this unit in the analysis of class embarrassment and intermediate rhetorical stances:

- **Comic primness** (p. 126): the privileged person's ironic-apologetic stance that is neither full approval nor protest — a suspended act between deference and challenge. The attitude *is* the rhetorical act.
- **The bowed posture** in Gray's *Elegy* (p. 125): resignation that simultaneously functions as appeal — an attitude that holds two potential acts (acceptance and petition for preferment) in suspension.
- **Pantomime stances** (pp. 143–144): Diderot's pantomime positions are Attitude made visible — the body's incipient acts toward superior and inferior, held in a perpetual almost-gesture of deference.
- **The directed intention** (pp. 156–157): the Jesuit device of selecting an appropriate intention after the fact is precisely an Attitude imposed retroactively on an act — the symbolic/motivational orientation that "completes" the action in the relevant register.
- **Infancy as Attitude** (pp. 174–176): the "acting-with" in an intuition (the phoebe's song in spring) is neither a perception nor a full act but an attitudinal orientation toward a wider orbit of meanings — the suspended, incipient engagement that precedes determinate action.

## Game-Behavior Application (anticipatory)

All game-application material is [anticipatory-projection] and does not represent Burke's own claims.

**Hierarchy → Calleja Shared Involvement (Ch. 6)** [anticipatory-projection]. Burke's analysis of the hierarchic principle — rank-differentiation, the entelechial tendency to treat top-rank as representative of the whole, the compulsion to position oneself vis-à-vis those above and below — maps precisely onto Calleja's Shared involvement dimension: rank systems, leaderboards, gear-score, prestige ladders, and title/achievement systems all operationalize the hierarchic psychosis within designed systems. The "out-imitation" competition Burke identifies in Veblen (p. 131) is the motivational logic of competitive ranked ladders in multiplayer games: players do not merely compete to win but to display and confirm position within a symbolic hierarchy. The entelechial pull toward the top-ranked player/guild as representative of the game's excellence parallels Burke's analysis of the prince as representative of hierarchic order.

**Hierarchic psychosis → Pentad Scene** [anticipatory-projection]. The game world as scenic order constitutes a designed hierarchy: maps, zones, dungeons, difficulty tiers, social spaces, and economic layers all instantiate the scenic arrangement of levels. The Scene (in the pentad sense) is not merely a neutral backdrop but a hierarchically organized motivational structure that generates appropriate acts from agents within it. Game designers as architects of scene-hierarchy are architects of motivational orientation.

**Imitation/consubstantiality → Calleja Spatial Involvement** [anticipatory-projection]. The practice of imitating skilled players' techniques, build strategies, pathing decisions, and aesthetic choices — community of in-game ways — is the mechanism by which players become consubstantial with the game's social body. "Out-imitation" competition (p. 131) maps onto the copy-cat meta: following the dominant strategy/build is simultaneously conformity and competition.

**Machiavelli's "Administrative Rhetoric" → Game Design as Persuasion (developer as rhetor)** [anticipatory-projection]. Burke's claim that nonverbal, material conditions can have symbolic-persuasive force because they carry "meaning" for the audience maps onto the game designer's situation: UI/UX layout, loot-drop timing, difficulty curves, spectacle events, and reward-interval engineering are all "administrative rhetoric" in Burke's sense — nonverbal operations designed with an eye to their appeal and persuasive effect on the player-audience. The developer functions as Machiavelli's prince, managing scenes (game states) to produce identification (player investment, continuation) and to prevent alienation (churn). The player is both subject and co-constitutive audience, just as Machiavelli's subjects are both the objects of rule and the audience of its rhetoric.

**Directing the intention → Quest/Narrative Framing** [anticipatory-projection]. The Jesuit device of directing the intention after the act maps onto the rhetorical function of quest objectives and narrative framing in games: the same act (kill ten enemies) is transformed into a heroic sacrifice or civic duty by the intention-direction layer of quest text and cinematics. Players participate in this re-attribution as a pleasurable form of identification.

**Pentad Agent-Act Ratio → Player Identity** [anticipatory-projection]. Burke's analysis of how Machiavelli's chapter 25 introduces the agent-act ratio — act proper to the ruler's nature — maps onto player-character identification: players choose or construct agents whose nature matches their desired mode of play, and the alignment of player-temperament with character-type produces the heightened sense of successful identification Burke describes.

**The Entelechial Tendency → Endgame / Prestige Mechanics** [anticipatory-projection]. The entelechial pull toward the "culminating stage" as representative of the whole maps onto prestige mechanics, max-level content, and "endgame" as the game's representative image. The "mystery" of those who have reached the top creates aspiration and courtship dynamics in communities oriented around high-rank players.

**Mystery/Infancy → Atmospheric and Subliminal Game Rhetoric** [anticipatory-projection]. Burke's analysis of infancy and mystery — the resonance of symbols that exceeds their explicit content — maps onto the atmospheric, musical, and environmental rhetoric of game design: the sense of something beyond the immediately visible, the mystery of undiscovered areas, the subliminal affective orientation produced by sound design, lighting, and spatial arrangement. This is Attitude (incipient act) operationalized as designed phenomenology.

**Calleja Incorporation (Ch. 10) — keystone bridge:**
The unit's explicit statement that imitation "makes for consubstantiality by community of ways (identification)" (p. 131) advances the keystone bridge: incorporation (Calleja's terminal concept for full player-game fusion) is the game-design instantiation of consubstantiality achieved through sustained community of ways. This unit provides the *mechanism* (imitation of underlying principles → shared symbolic substance) that RM-01 established as the theoretical ground.

**RM-03 (Mystification and Mystery):**
RM-04 opens where RM-03 closed (p. 123 references Marx and Carlyle on mystification) and extends their analysis via Empson's literary sociology and the Hierarchy section's theoretical synthesis. Mystery is here given its motivational-structural grounding rather than merely its ideological-critical framing. Carlyle's "mystery" of social inequality, introduced in RM-03, now becomes the "hierarchic principle" — the general symbolic form from which any particular mystification of class derives. Burke's ten-step synthesis at the close of RM-04 (pp. 176–180) provides the explicit philosophical argument that RM-03 only gestured toward: mystery is not reducible to class ideology but is intrinsic to the structure of persuasion-as-courtship.

**RM-05 (Positive, Dialectical, Ultimate Terms — forthcoming):**
The "Priority of the Idea" discussion in RM-04 (pp. 132–137) anticipates RM-05's analysis of terministic hierarchy: the arrangement of ultimate, dialectical, and positive terms is itself a hierarchic structure, and the entelechial principle by which a culminating term represents the entire genus will be central to RM-05's account of ultimate terms. The sea-myth's hierarchic reversibility (first/last, before/after) prepares for the dialectical-ultimate term analysis where positive terms are transcended by dialectical and ultimate ones.

**RM-06 (Courtship — forthcoming):**
This unit functions as the analytical preparation for RM-06's extended treatment of courtship. The ten-step synthesis (pp. 176–178) provides the philosophical architecture; RM-06's analyses of Venus & Adonis, Castiglione, and Kafka will operationalize the specific social forms of courtship that this unit has theoretically grounded. The "modified tributes of courtship" Burke identifies in the hierarchy myth (p. 139) and Empson's pastoral are the literary species of courtship that RM-06 will analyze at full length as the fundamental social-motive apparatus.

**Integration keystone (Burke × Calleja):**
The core advance of RM-04 for the integration pipeline is threefold. First, it specifies the *mechanism* of consubstantiation: imitation of underlying principles (not mere insignia) produces community of ways, which is the substrate of consubstantiality. Second, it shows that the social form this takes is hierarchic: rank-oriented, entelechially structured, mystery-generating. Third, it establishes that administrative (nonverbal) operations are part of the rhetorical apparatus — a crucial bridge to game design as persuasion, where material conditions (level architecture, loot systems, progression curves) function as symbolic operations with persuasive meaning for their audiences.
