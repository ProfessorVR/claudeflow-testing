## Overview

Burke opens *A Rhetoric of Motives* by warning that its most demanding pages come first, and by giving the reader permission to skip ahead to "Identification" on p. 19 if the literary analysis proves unwieldy. The Introduction declares the book's central conceptual move: shifting the master term of rhetoric from "persuasion" to "identification," while insisting that identification supplements rather than replaces the classical tradition. The first chapter ("The Range of Rhetoric") then enacts this declaration through close readings of Milton's *Samson Agonistes*, Matthew Arnold's poems, and Coleridge's *Religious Musings*, demonstrating how imagery of killing, suicide, and self-immolation functions as a vocabulary for transformation rather than for death per se. Burke concludes the unit by establishing that dramatic or narrative essence-terms (culminative imagery of death, ruin, ending) translate the philosophical concept of Aristotelian entelechy into temporal, story-bound form — a grammatical principle he calls "dramatic terms for essence." Throughout, "identification" operates as a latent gravitational term whose full definition is deferred to Part I proper (RM-01).

## Argument — Page-Anchored Walkthrough

**Introduction (pp. xi–xv)**

Burke announces that the book's opening is its only genuinely difficult section, and explains why: the analytical work there requires treating literary texts rhetorically and dialectically, not purely aesthetically. The Introduction performs the book's first act of scope-definition: rhetoric is not merely the craft of overt persuasion but encompasses a vast "intermediate area" of expression — neither wholly deliberate nor wholly unconscious. As an illustration Burke offers the politician who identifies his private ambitions with the public good: such a person may be partly sincere, partly self-deceived, and the rhetorical analyst cannot reduce the case to either cynical design or naive simplicity. This intermediate zone is where rhetoric lives.

Burke then announces the classical term's insufficiency. "Persuasion" has been the traditional master word, and the book will give it full respect (the classic texts that trace its implications will be reviewed). But "identification" is a more capacious organizing term — one that can accommodate persuasion's mechanisms while also capturing the motive-structures that produce social cohesion through courtship, mystification, and the magic of class relations. W. C. Blum's formulation is cited approvingly: identification as the source of "dedications and enslavements" as well as "cooperation." The Introduction closes by mapping the book's tonal range: from blunt propaganda through courtship and education to what Burke will later call "pure persuasion" — rhetorical appeal that delights in its own process without ulterior end. And identification ranges correspondingly: from the farmer-audience politician through "the mysteries of social status" to the mystic's union with ultimate being.

**"The Use of Milton's Samson" (pp. 3–6)**

The analytic centerpiece of the opening chapter. Burke reads *Samson Agonistes* not as pure poetry but as a layered site of identification: Milton-with-Samson, Samson-with-God, Puritans-with-Israelites, Royalists-with-Philistines. This multiple layering enacts identification as a structural principle: each level involves a "consubstantiating" of separate entities through shared substance (blindness, divine mission, factional cause). Burke notes that the poem works as "literature for use" — a moralistic prophecy by which the blind poet, ritualistically declaring that his blind hero will conquer, declares his own coming victory. This is use "at one remove," in the order of magic and ritual rather than instrumental propaganda. The passage about Samson "in 'self-violence'" collapsing the temple is read as reflexive action — aggression and self-destruction fused in one image — which serves as the unit's first entry-point into the transformational motive.

**"Qualifying the Suicidal Motive" (pp. 5–6)**

Burke argues that the reflexive character of Samson's act could sanction suicide, yet Milton's religion forbade it. The dramatic context — theocratic rage, divine mission, factional allegory — supplies so many modifications that "suicide" as a reduced "gist" badly misrepresents the motivational whole. Burke introduces a critical methodological principle: one cannot reduce a complex motivational recipe to a single essential strand without misinterpreting it. The dramatic terms contain dimensions that qualify and transform the surface motive. By contrast, if conditions strip away those qualifying modifiers (as happens under social strain), the ingredient that had been embedded in a rich motivational context can become "efficient" — purely itself.

**"Self-Immolation in Matthew Arnold" (pp. 7–9)**

Burke examines Arnold's *Empedocles on Etna* and *Sohrab and Rustum* as contrasting instances. Empedocles offers explicit cosmic suicide — union with mountain, sea, and stars through the volcano's crater. Sohrab is killed by his father in unknowing combat, a war-death that, when set beside Empedocles, reveals a common motivational cluster: fatal admiration for the father, expressed differently as filicide and as suicide. Burke shows that the same "attitude toward life" can generate both, because Arnold identified himself as poetic agent with both figures. The key analytical move: by treating two works as aspects of one poetic agent's motivational substance, Burke demonstrates inter-work identification — an author's consubstantiality across texts.

**"The Imaging of Transformation" (pp. 10–12)**

This is the unit's theoretical apex and the section most directly relevant to the integration pipeline. Coleridge's couplet — "Victorious Murder a blind Suicide" — suggests a point at which murder and suicide become convertible, each an image for the same motive. Burke uses this to argue for a ground-term that "transcends" both: transformation in general. The imagery of killing, he proposes, is only one family among many available for localizing the process of transformation; others include Upward/Downward Way, Exile/Homecoming, Egressus/Regressus, seasonal cycles, antitheses of Day/Night, Yes/No, Losing/Finding. The neutral point of view that sees all these as variants of a single principle — transformation — is itself a rhetorical-dialectical perspective that "transcends" the animus of any particular image. Burke then surveys how different kinds of transformation-imaging recur across literary history: Henry Adams' depersonalizing surrender to historical force is a self-immolation every bit as much as Empedocles' volcanic leap, but expressed in doctrinal or educational terms. The range of images is limited only by the imagination, but the "selective nature of existence" (the conditions of a given motivational context) favors some above others — Life and Death imagery being highest among them.

**"Dramatic and Philosophic Terms for Essence" (pp. 13–15)**

Burke turns from imagery-analysis to grammar. There is an "ultimate Grammatical incentive" behind imagery of death: the end of a narrative is a formal way of proclaiming the essence of what the narrative dramatizes. He cross-references his earlier treatment of "the temporizing of essence" in the *Grammar of Motives* (pp. 430–440) and extends it in a new direction. In the *Grammar* he had stressed essence-stated-as-origin (ancestral terms, divine parenthood, childhood regression). Here he adds the opposite: essence-stated-as-culmination (what a thing ends up being = what it essentially is). The Aristotelian entelechy is the philosophical form of this: classifying a kind by the perfection of which it is capable. The narrative equivalent is "death as transfiguration" — the tragic dignifying of a motive by identifying it with its fulfillment-end. The Christian "dying life" formula is itself a translation of entelechy into tragic terms. Burke also notes the etymological cluster: define/determine/termination share a common root ("term" = end/boundary), suggesting that all essence-definitions have a hidden affinity with ending. These observations set up the methodological vocabulary that will ground how *identification* operates: to identify with something is, among other things, to define yourself by your relationship to it — to adopt it as an ending or terminus of your motivational arc.

**"'Tragic' Terms for Personality Types" (pp. 15–17)**

An illustrative aside: Burke imagines a "neotragic" classification of personality types based not on questionnaires but on what kind of world's-end imagery a person most readily identifies with — holocaust vs. entropy vs. slow asphyxiation vs. stellar collision. The "scene-agent ratio" (cross-referenced to the *Grammar*, pp. 7–9) is the mechanism: a person can identify themselves with the character of a surrounding situation, translating one into terms of the other. The shift from personal to cosmic scale enables self-transcendence while still portraying the individual character. This passage introduces the pentad's scene-agent ratio as an active analytical instrument, and connects it to identification: the agent defines their essence by identifying with a scene (the type of ending they imagine). The factional identifications from the Samson analysis reappear in compressed form: a "recapitulation" rehearses the chain — Milton/Samson/God, Puritans/Israelites, Royalists/Philistines — as a demonstration of how identification compounds across scales.

**"Imagery at Face Value" (pp. 17–18)**

Burke closes the unit with a warning against the twin errors of naive face-value reading and over-eager psychoanalytic reduction. Imagery, taken at face value, does real rhetorical work: the adolescent "trained" by cinema to meditate on brutality acquires a vocabulary of transformation in which "growing up" = slaying troublesome motives. The sinister effect is not in the imagery per se but in the absence of formal devices that mark the imagery as artistic or fictive. The photographic medium eliminates the aesthetic distance that would otherwise qualify the motivational ingredient. Burke closes by criticizing psychoanalytic accounts that insist on locating a "killing" motive as the essential one, even where no killing appears in the explicit imagery — an error of efficient reduction that strips the context's qualifying modifications. The correct method is to take the total motivational recipe seriously, not to reduce it to a single "gist."

## Key Concepts

**identification** [spine]
The central organizing term of *A Rhetoric of Motives*, here introduced but not yet formally defined. In this unit it operates through demonstrations: Milton-with-Samson, Arnold-with-Empedocles, author-with-agent, faction-with-biblical-figure. Identification is the motive-structure by which separate entities are rendered consubstantial. The term's full definition is explicitly deferred to p. 19. `provenance: burke-direct`

**consubstantiality** [spine — implicit here, explicit at RM-01]
Not yet named as such in this unit, but enacted throughout. Milton's blindness and Samson's blindness, Puritan faction and Israelite tribe, Arnold's two poems as aspects of one poetic substance — all are instances of entities sharing common substance while retaining distinctness. The term's formal introduction is held for RM-01, but the unit's entire analytic machinery prefigures it. `provenance: burke-direct`

**transformation (imaging of)**
Burke's generic term for the process that literary death-imagery localizes or dramatizes. Transformation is the ground-motive; killing/suicide/self-immolation are particular terminological choices for it. The full range of possible transformation-images is given (Upward/Downward Way, Exile/Homecoming, seasonal cycles, antithetical pairs). Any of these can stand in for any other, from the neutral analytical standpoint; the particular choice is what reveals motivational character. `provenance: burke-direct`

**terministic screen** [spine — anticipated here]
Not named yet, but the logic is present: the particular imagery through which a writer chooses to localize transformation is a terministic screen — it determines what aspects of the motivational situation will appear and what will recede. Milton's theocratic terms screen out the suicidal ingredient; Arnold's lyric softness screens out the martial. The term itself appears later in Burke's corpus, but RM-00 provides its first operational instance. `provenance: burke-direct`

**dramatic terms for essence**
The narrative/temporal translation of philosophical essence-claims. The "end" of a story serves as the formal declaration of the protagonist's essence: to call something tragic is to define its essence in terms of its culmination. Opposed to "philosophic terms for essence" (abstract, timeless). Key move: the two are convertible by the same heuristic reversal that lets us see narrative as translation of philosophy or philosophy as translation of narrative. `provenance: burke-direct`

**philosophic terms for essence**
Abstract, timeless characterizations of a thing's kind (e.g., "man is a rational animal"). Opposed to dramatic terms for essence. In the Aristotelian entelechy, the philosophic statement of essence is in terms of a kind's perfection or finishedness. Burke shows that this philosophical form already conceals a temporal logic (finishedness = end), making it convertible with the dramatic/narrative form. `provenance: burke-direct`

**entelechy (Aristotelian)**
Cited as the philosophical underpinning of "dramatic terms for essence": classifying a kind by the perfection of which it is capable. The entelechy as philosophical concept becomes, when translated into narrative form, the "tragic end" as definition of motive. Burke uses this to show that the vocabulary of drama and the vocabulary of philosophy are formally homologous at the level of grammar. `provenance: burke-direct`

**temporizing of essence**
Cross-referenced to *Grammar of Motives*, pp. 430–440. The logical or metaphysical idea of a thing's essence gets expressed in temporal/narrative form — as origin (ancestry, childhood) or as culmination (ending, death). RM-00 adds the "culminative" pole to what the Grammar had already established as the "ancestral" pole. `provenance: burke-direct`

**suicidal motive**
One of three forms of the death-motive that Burke inventories: killing (active/homicidal), being killed (passive), and self-killing (reflexive/suicidal). The suicidal is given special analytic attention because it fuses agency and passivity, aggressor and victim, in a single figure. Milton's Samson is the paradigm case: his act is simultaneously an aggressive destruction of enemies and a self-destruction. `provenance: burke-direct`

**motivational recipe**
Burke's term for the total complex of motivational ingredients — their proportions, modifications, and contextual qualifications — that constitutes the actual motive of a work or act. One cannot reduce the recipe to a single "gist" ingredient without misrepresenting the whole. The metaphor of a recipe captures the compositional logic: the same ingredient (suicidal strand) tastes entirely different depending on the other ingredients surrounding it. `provenance: burke-direct`

**rhetoric as intermediate area**
The Introduction's key definitional move for the scope of rhetoric: an area between fully deliberate intent and fully unconscious expression. The politician who may be sincerely self-deceived inhabits this area. It is rhetoric's proper domain — and it is the domain that classical "persuasion" cannot capture but "identification" can. `provenance: burke-direct`

**scene-agent ratio**
Cross-referenced to *Grammar*, pp. 7–9. The formal ratio by which a person's character and the character of their surrounding scene are identified with each other: one's individual end is translated into the kind of world's-end imagery one prefers. The ratio enables self-transcendence through scale-shift (from personal to cosmic) while still revealing personal motivational character. `provenance: burke-direct`

**literature for use**
Burke's term for literary works that perform rhetorical-political work: overtly in the case of the *Areopagitica*, "at one remove" in the case of *Samson Agonistes* (in the order of ritual and magic rather than propaganda). The distinction between kinds of "use" anticipates the full range announced in the Introduction, from propaganda to pure persuasion. `provenance: burke-direct`

**face value (imagery at)**
The interpretive stance of accepting imagery on its own terms as having real rhetorical effect, without reduction to an unconscious "gist." Burke defends face value as methodologically appropriate against both naive consumption of violent imagery and psychoanalytic over-reading that postulates a "real" motive behind every surface. Imagery taken at face value shapes motivation; it cannot be neutralized by declaring it "merely symbolic." `provenance: burke-direct`

**dramatic subterfuge**
The mechanism by which dramatic form allows the inclusion of motivational ingredients that would be inadmissible if stated in "conceptually analytic" form. Milton's poem admits the suicidal ingredient through the dramatic subterfuge of Samson's theocratic mission; stated analytically, the same motive would have been impermissible. `provenance: burke-direct`

**persuasion (range of)**
Burke maps the full spectrum from bluntest propaganda through courtship, etiquette, education, and sermon to "pure persuasion" — rhetorical process enjoyed for its own sake. This range-map serves to show why a single master term ("persuasion") is too narrow: the upper end of the range (pure persuasion, mystification, courtship) is not adequately captured by models of deliberate intent. `provenance: burke-direct`

## Identification & Consubstantiation Development

RM-00's contribution to the identification master-term is preparatory rather than definitional. Burke explicitly tells the reader that the full term is coming on p. 19, and that the opening chapters can be read "lightly" as prologue. However, the unit enacts several identification-structures that will be formalized later:

1. **Layered personal identification**: Milton/Samson; Arnold/Empedocles; Arnold/Sohrab. The poet identifies with a literary agent, and through that agent is identified with a motivational principle (blindness-as-strength, self-abnegation-as-piety). Each identification involves shared substance while maintaining distinctness — the formal requirement of consubstantiality.

2. **Factional identification**: Puritans/Israelites, Royalists/Philistines/Dagon. The faction identifies its cause with a biblical precedent, borrowing the precedent's motivational force. Burke calls this "substantial" saying — "in saying, with fervor, that a blind Biblical hero did conquer, the poet is 'substantially' saying that he in his blindness will conquer." The adverb "substantially" here is proto-consubstantial.

3. **Inter-work identification**: Arnold's two poems are aspects of the same poetic agent, sharing "common substance of the one authorship." The agent's motivational substance flows across texts, making each work a partial expression of the same underlying attitudinal cluster.

4. **Scene-agent identification**: The scene-agent ratio already involves identification: translating one's character into the character of one's preferred "ending" is an identification of self with scene, an act of consubstantiating agent and world.

The unit thus pre-operationalizes consubstantiality before the term appears, establishing the analytic habits the reader needs to handle its formal definition in RM-01.

## Attitude (Incipient-Act) Signals

The 6th dramatistic term (Attitude as the incipient or suspended act) is not named in RM-00, but several passages signal its presence:

- The "intermediate area of expression" that is neither fully deliberate nor fully unconscious (Introduction) is exactly the zone where attitude operates: an orientation toward action that has not yet resolved into action. The politician who identifies private ambition with public good is in an attitudinal state — neither pure cynicism (a resolved stance) nor pure sincerity (transparent motive) but an incipient orientation.

- Arnold's poetry is characterized as expressing an "attitude towards life" — specifically, an attitude of pious self-abnegation toward the father. This attitudinal cluster generates the different works as its various expressions. The two poems are not separate acts so much as expressions of a stable underlying attitude.

- The adolescent who "mediates much on the imagery of brutality" (pp. 17–18) is developing an attitude — a preparatory orientation toward transformation-through-violence — before any action occurs. The imagery functions as attitudinal training: it habituates a particular incipient stance toward the process of "growing up."

- The "neotragic" personality-typing scheme (pp. 15–16) would classify persons by the eschatological imagery with which they most readily identify — i.e., by their attitudinal resonance with particular transformation-images. This is a classification of attitudes rather than acts.

## Game-Behavior Application (Anticipatory)

> All items in this section carry `provenance: anticipatory-projection`. None of the following is Burke's claim.

**"Imaging of Transformation" → Player Self-Narrative (Narrative PIM Dimension)**
[anticipatory-projection] Burke's analysis of transformation-imaging shows that a vocabulary for self-change is assembled from available cultural images. The player-character relationship in video games operates by exactly this logic: the player selects an avatar and a genre of imagery (heroic combat, survival, exploration, social management) and uses it as a narrative vocabulary for self-transformation. When a player describes themselves as having "leveled up," "survived the raid," or "built an empire," they are using game-imagery to localize and particularize the process of transformation — precisely the move Burke analyzes through Milton, Arnold, and Adams. The game's transformation-vocabulary (its imagery of death, rebirth, progress, failure) is not merely descriptive of in-game events but is the medium through which players narrate self-change. Maps to Calleja's **Narrative PIM dimension** (story-construction, self-positioning within fictional arc). Pentad term: **Act** (the specific transformation-image selected) + **Agent** (the player-avatar as site of the transformation).

**"Dramatic Terms for Essence" → Player Identity-Culmination (Narrative + Affective PIM Dimensions)**
[anticipatory-projection] Burke's argument that a thing's essence is defined by its end (entelechy-translated-into-narrative) applies directly to how players construct game-identity through culminating moments. The character who "finally defeated the final boss," who "completed the 100% run," who "survived the permadeath playthrough" — these culminative events define the player's game-identity in exactly the narrative-essentializing manner Burke describes. The ending gives the motivational recipe its character. This maps to the **Narrative PIM dimension** (identity-construction through narrative culmination) and to the **Affective PIM dimension** (the emotional charge of the culminating moment — defeat, triumph, sacrifice — as the felt expression of the player's attitudinal investment). Pentad term: **Act** (the culminating action) + **Scene** (the game-world context that makes the culmination legible as "ending").

**"Qualifying the Suicidal Motive" → Reading Game Motives Holistically (Methodological)**
[anticipatory-projection] Burke's insistence that motivational recipes cannot be reduced to a single "gist" without misrepresenting the whole is a methodological directive for game-behavior analysis. A player's "aggressive" or "competitive" behavior cannot be reduced to an aggression-drive: the full motivational recipe includes social elements (performing skill for an audience), transformational elements (enacting competence-growth), narrative elements (positioning oneself in a competitive story), and affective elements (managing frustration and satisfaction). The "face value" of play behavior — its full qualifying context — must be maintained. This maps methodologically to all six Calleja PIM dimensions as a caveat against reductive single-motive analysis.

**Scene-Agent Ratio → Player-Avatar Identification (Affective + Narrative PIM Dimensions)**
[anticipatory-projection] The scene-agent ratio — by which a person identifies their character with the character of their surrounding situation — is a direct anticipation of the player-avatar relationship. A player who chooses a dark, desolate post-apocalyptic game world is not merely selecting a genre: they are identifying their motivational character with the character of that scene. The scene-agent ratio predicts that the type of game-world a player gravitates toward will reveal their attitudinal orientation — a "neotragic" categorizing device for player types. Maps to **Affective PIM dimension** (emotional valence of player-world resonance) and **Narrative PIM dimension** (world-as-extension-of-player-self-narrative). Pentad term: **Scene** (the game-world) + **Agent** (the player).

**"Literature for Use" → Instrumental vs. Intrinsic Play**
[anticipatory-projection] Burke's spectrum from "literature for use" (propaganda, overt persuasion) to use "at one remove" (ritual, magic) to the implicit promise of pure persuasion anticipates a parallel spectrum in game motivation: from wholly instrumental play (grinding for rewards) through social-ritual play (multiplayer ceremonies, guild events) to intrinsically motivated play that "delights in the process" — which is precisely the structure Burke will later formalize as pure persuasion. This mapping is anticipatory of RM-07's pure persuasion content but is seeded here. Maps to **Affective PIM dimension** (intrinsic motivational quality) and **Social PIM dimension** (play-as-social-ritual). Pentad term: **Purpose/Agency** (the motivational end of the play-act).

## Cross-Pipeline Bridges

**Burke *Grammar of Motives* (GM pipeline)**
- Scene-agent ratio: RM-00 cross-references *Grammar* pp. 7–9 explicitly. The ratio established there as a dramatistic analytical tool is here extended into the domain of identification: identifying with the character of a scene is itself an identification-move, not merely a grammatical description.
- Temporizing of essence: Cross-referenced to *Grammar* pp. 430–440. RM-00 adds the "culminative" pole, completing the picture that the Grammar had established with only the "ancestral" pole.
- Entelechy: RM-00 deploys Aristotelian entelechy as the philosophic underpinning for dramatic terms for essence — connecting the Grammar's grammatical-scene vocabulary to the Rhetoric's terministic-essence vocabulary.

**Calleja *In-Game* (IG pipeline)**
- Incorporation (Ch. 10): The proto-consubstantiality moves in RM-00 (agent-identifies-with-figure, shares substance while remaining distinct) anticipate the incorporation bridge that RM-01 will formalize. RM-00 establishes the analytic habits needed to understand incorporation as a consubstantiating act.
- Narrative PIM dimension: The imaging-of-transformation analysis is the most direct bridge to Calleja's Narrative dimension, where players construct self-positioning stories through play.
- Affective PIM dimension: The "face value" of imagery — its real effect on motivation independent of "unconscious" reduction — parallels Calleja's insistence that affective engagement in games is not reducible to a simple pleasure/pain axis but involves complex attitudinal orientations.
