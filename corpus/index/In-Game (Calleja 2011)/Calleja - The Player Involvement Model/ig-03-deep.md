## §1 Argumentative spine of the chapter

The chapter performs three interlocking jobs in twenty pages. First, it stratifies the discourse of game experience by positing a three-tier hierarchy: attention is the precondition of involvement, which is itself the precondition of presence and immersion. This stratification reframes the immersion debate Ch. 2 had surveyed. Calleja argues that researchers have repeatedly attempted to build accounts of presence/immersion on top of an under-articulated involvement layer, and that the analytic gain to be had is from going down one floor and modelling involvement first.

Second, it constructs a six-by-two grid. Six dimensions of involvement (kinesthetic, spatial, shared, narrative, affective, ludic) cross-cut by two temporal phases (macro = off-line, between sessions; micro = in-session, moment-to-moment) yield twelve cells. The dimensions are not discrete categories. They are presented as *layered and transparent*: each modulates the perception of the others without occluding them, and the inclusion or exclusion of any one alters how the others operate. Calleja insists on this property because the empirical pattern in his interview data was of fluid blending rather than serial isolation, and because Ch. 1's Wittgensteinian commitment forbids any essentialist taxonomy of player experience. The grid is therefore a heuristic apparatus over a phenomenologically continuous field, not a partitioning of that field. This generates the chapter's signature theoretical tension — diagrammatic register pulling against experiential register — to which I return in §8.

Third, it performs a sustained negative-definitional excision (pp. 46–53) of the dominant prior vocabulary: the magic circle (Huizinga via Salen + Zimmerman, Juul), the lusory attitude (Suits), and the fun-essentialism inherited by Crawford, Koster, Salen + Zimmerman. Nearly forty percent of the chapter is devoted to clearing the conceptual ground. This is not a digression. It is the chapter's specification of what the model is *not*, performed at the moment of the model's introduction, so that subsequent chapters can deploy the six dimensions without re-litigating the prior framework.

The chapter accordingly does both positive-constructive work (building a structured account from a substrate of attentional resources, ergodic readiness, and dimensional layering) and negative-purgative work (excising the magic circle / lusory attitude / fun apparatus) in a single twenty-page span. Calleja closes by reaffirming that the experience of gameplay can be further analyzed — provided that analysis avoids a priori essentialist labels.

## §2 Methodology in full (the dissertation's empirical-adaptation seed)

The Research Process subsection (p. 36) is one of the densest single pages in the volume. Calleja compresses three years of qualitative research into a single column. The case sites are deliberately contrastive: *World of Warcraft* (Blizzard Entertainment, 2004) as the prototypical massively multiplayer on-line role-playing game (MMORPG), and *Planetside* (Sony Online Entertainment, 2003) as a massively multiplayer on-line first-person shooter (MMOFPS). The pairing samples two structurally different MMOG sub-genres — persistent world vs. instanced combat theater, character-progression vs. continuous combat-respawn, role-playing vs. ranked-objective — and gives the dimensional model a wider empirical base than a single-genre study would.

The rationale for the qualitative approach is stated as the intensely experiential and richly varied nature of the object of inquiry. Calleja names three methodological forebears for this orientation: T. L. Taylor's *Play between Worlds* (2006), Constance Steinkuehler (2005), and Lisbeth Klastrup (2004). These are the existing virtual-worlds qualitative-research lineage; they will reappear later in the chapter (Taylor and Steinkuehler especially) as anchors for the magic-circle and fun-essentialism critiques.

The primary research methods are three: extended personal participation in both game environments; numerous focus groups; and in-depth interviews with twenty-five experienced players recruited in-game. These primary methods are supplemented by four further channels: discussions with academics studying virtual worlds; participation in on-line forums; monitoring of player blogs; and viewing player-made machinima. The triangulation matters because the dimensional clusters were extracted from the convergence of these data streams, not from interviews alone.

The interview design carries the empirical weight. Interviews are semi-structured, extending over two sessions of up to two hours each. Two design choices are dissertation-relevant in particular. First, each interview took place within the virtual environment of the game about which the participant was being interviewed. Calleja's stated rationale is to stimulate participant memory and to bring the participant's relationship to the game world to the fore. The implication for dissertation methodology is that interview-in-environment is not a curiosity but a constitutive methodological move: the artifact under study is a virtual environment, and the participant's reportable relationship to it is most retrievable from within it. Second, the schedule is *loosely structured*, with a constant focus on experiences related to immersion. This combination — methodological discipline (focus) with topical openness (loose structure) — is what enables the dimensional clusters to emerge inductively rather than be imposed deductively.

The analytical procedure is iterative grounded-theoretic coding. Data were coded and analyzed between interviews; the interview schedule was updated in the intervening period to reflect input from participants. Over time, strong themes emerged, and the dimensional model is grounded on these salient features. The model is therefore an inductive abstraction from convergent qualitative data, not a theoretical posit checked against the data after the fact.

For the dissertation, this subsection is the empirical-adaptation seed. The dissertation's planned analytic engagement with games can adopt this template directly: paired case-sites contrasting on a structural axis; interviews-within-the-game; iterative coding between sessions; emergent clusters checked against ongoing data. The three named forebears (Taylor, Steinkuehler, Klastrup) are existing virtual-worlds qualitative anchors and remain extant cross-pipeline candidates for Phase 4 activation.

## §3 Macro and micro temporal phases (the six-by-two grid foundation)

The chapter's signature theoretical move is the temporal-phase distinction, set up on pp. 37–38 with the *Empire: Total War* (Creative Assembly, 2009) worked example. Selecting and controlling an eighteenth-century army in a real-time multiplayer battle — building one's army with limited funds, deploying in a stipulated zone, controlling individual units, outmaneuvering one's opponent — is in-the-moment engagement. Calleja designates this micro-involvement. Lying in bed afterward thinking through different army configurations and unit-usage strategies, or thinking through campaign-mode conquest plans during the morning commute, is out-of-the-moment engagement. Calleja designates this macro-involvement.

The macro phase is built up over pp. 38–40. Its constitutive contents are: thoughts, plans, feelings, and expectations both prior to and following the gameplay session; longer-term motivations for sustained engagement; the desire to feel surrounded by a particular kind of world; the formation of strategies to try in upcoming sessions; the ambition to develop one's abilities and outperform others; the feeling of belonging to a close-knit community; and interest in continuing to unravel a game's narrative or in exploring a newly discovered part of the world. Macro-phase examples span all six dimensions, demonstrating that the macro phase is not a single dimension's domain but the cross-cutting temporal frame of all of them. Calleja cites Karolien Poels (2009) on the over-narrow focus of game-experience research on the moment of gameplay, and Poels et al. (2010)'s focus-group study of "postgame experiences" as empirical corroboration of the macro phase.

The micro phase is built up over pp. 40–43 as the chapter's analytic center of gravity. Calleja distinguishes attention to a medium from active player input. Attention is the concentration of cognitive resources on stimuli (Fan et al. 2002), and is capacity-limited (Baddeley + Hitch 1994; Sturm + Willmes 2001) so that learned tasks become automatic and free attentional resources for other tasks. Active player input is reframed through Espen Aarseth's (1997) ergodicity. Calleja then performs his most consequential theoretical move within the chapter: ergodic effort is *first and foremost a disposition and readiness to act*, not merely physical input. This dispositional reformulation does substantial work. It captures the long pauses of strategy-game cognitive effort within the ergodic concept rather than excluding them as non-action. It captures the patient alertness of the *Red Orchestra* (Tripwire Interactive, 2006) sniper covering a street with no enemies in sight (Figure 3.2) as the dispositional form of ergodic engagement. And it captures the in-game-map planning of a *World of Warcraft* journey from Ratchet to Feathermoon as cognitive-effort ergodicity even though no input registers visibly on the screen. The cybernetic-circuit framing from Dovey + Kennedy (2006) is invoked to locate ergodic input within a feedback loop of hardware, representational layer, and underlying rules.

The macro / micro distinction has a methodological asymmetry that propagates through the rest of the book. Macro receives a single section here and a section-opening treatment in each of the dimension chapters (Chs. 4–9). Micro receives most of the present chapter and dominates the bulk of the dimension chapters. The model is more elaborated micro-side than macro-side at the moment of its own introduction. This is one of the chapter's structural tensions (T3 in the JSON).

## §4 The six dimensions: operational definitions and boundary criteria

The six dimensions receive their first canonical articulation on pp. 43–44. Each is given a tight one-paragraph operational definition that the dimension's own chapter (Chs. 4–9 respectively) will subsequently develop at length. For the dissertation's PIM Operationalization Manual, these are the anchor paragraphs. I extract the six operational definitions in ≤60-word capture-grade form below, with boundary criteria distinguishing each dimension from its closest neighbours.

**Kinesthetic** involvement is engagement with avatar or game-piece control in a virtual environment, spanning from conscious learning of the controls through to fluent internalized movement; foregrounded when controls make themselves present, either through unmastery or because a situation demands a complex sequence of actions taxing the player. Its locus of attention is the control surface, not the environment; it exhibits the unique control-presence / internalization dynamic; and it accommodates the dispositional-readiness extension developed in §3. The closest boundary neighbours are SPATIAL (which shares the avatar-in-environment phenomenology but localizes attention on the place rather than the controls) and LUDIC (which shares the strategy-game cognitive-effort phenomenology but localizes engagement on choice-and-repercussion rather than action-readiness).

**Spatial** involvement is engagement with the spatial qualities of a virtual environment via spatial control, navigation, and exploration, and accounts for the internalizing of game-spaces such that the player senses inhabiting a place rather than merely perceiving a representation of space. Its locus is the environment-as-inhabited-place. The contrast cases Calleja chooses are paradigmatic: *Pong* (Atari, 1972) as low-spatial; *The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion* (Bethesda, 2006) and *Half-Life 2* (Valve, 2004) as high-spatial. The boundary criterion against KINESTHETIC operates by attentional foregrounding (controls vs. place); the boundary criterion against NARRATIVE operates by whether the same environmental object is foregrounded as place-cue or as story-cue (decision rule R3); the boundary against magic-circle-spatial is sharp — non-navigable spaces (*FIFA 2009* stadium stands; *Battlefield 1942* outside-combat-area) are representational backdrop, not spatial-involvement loci.

**Shared** involvement is engagement deriving from awareness of and interaction with other agents (human- or computer-controlled), under the modalities of cohabitation, cooperation, and competition, including ambient awareness that one's actions occur in a social context. Its constitutive condition is the presence of other agents; remove them and the dimension drops out. The macro-signature (clan-website work, guild-political deliberation, persisting social bonds) is, per Calleja's report, the majority macro-phase content in his interview data. The boundary against LUDIC under cooperative play resolves via decision rule R4: joint goal-pursuit is primarily LUDIC with SHARED as secondary modulator; ambient social-context awareness independent of joint goal is primarily SHARED.

**Narrative** involvement is engagement with both story elements scripted into the game and emergent stories generated by ongoing interaction with the world, its embedded objects, inhabitants, and events. The dual scripted / emergent character is essential; alterbiography (developed in Ch. 7) is the emergent layer. The boundary against SPATIAL under environmental-storytelling resolves via decision rule R3 by attentional foregrounding (place vs. story-cipher). The macro-signature (speculation about how a designed narrative will develop; story-construction featuring one's character) differs from the ludic-macro (winning-strategy formation) and shared-macro (community-site work).

**Affective** involvement is engagement with the range of emotional states elicited by play — calming aesthetic response, adrenaline arousal, uncanny — including both designer-installed rhetorical strategies of affect and player-precipitated interpretations. Its object is the response-state irrespective of trigger-dimension. Per decision rule R5, AFFECTIVE always co-occurs with another dimension (which supplies the trigger) and is coded as primary only when the player's report or behavioral signature foregrounds the emotional response itself rather than the triggering content. The multi-valenced range (especially the inclusion of the uncanny) marks the dimension's structural opposition to fun-essentialism.

**Ludic** involvement is engagement with choices and their repercussions across game-stipulated, player-stipulated, and community-stipulated goals; including spur-of-the-moment decisions without overarching goal; with opportunity cost constitutive of well-balanced systems — without repercussions, actions lose their meaning. The dimension is goal-source-agnostic; it is choice-and-repercussion-structured; it is opportunity-cost-weighted. Its overlap with KINESTHETIC in strategy-game dispositional-readiness cases resolves via decision rule R1 (action-execution-readiness = KINESTHETIC; choice-evaluation-rehearsal = LUDIC; mixed cases reported with weighting).

Across all six dimensions, the **Applying the Model** section (pp. 44–46) qualifies the apparatus with Wittgensteinian permissiveness: not every dimension needs to be equally relevant to every game (the *Pong* / *Oblivion* / *Half-Life 2* spatial-intensity contrast is the standing example). The model's wagon-wheel diagram (Figure 3.1) is read here: the outer edge of each triangular segment represents full attentional resources directed to that dimension; narrowing inward triangles represent internalization (less attentional resource cost); further toward the center, more dimensions are simultaneously attended to. The diagrammatic reading thereby instantiates the layered + transparent property in a concrete attentional-economic mechanism.

## §5 The magic-circle / lusory-attitude / fun excision (pp. 46–53)

This block performs the chapter's negative-definitional work. The structural rationale for its scale (nearly forty percent of the chapter) is that Calleja's model is in part constituted by what it refuses. Each of the three excised constructs corresponds to one of three ways the prior literature has bracketed games from ordinary experience: spatially (magic circle as boundary), psychologically (lusory attitude as experiential mode), and affectively (fun as essence).

The **magic-circle critique** (pp. 46–48) begins by acknowledging the concept's wide adoption (Huizinga 1955; Salen + Zimmerman 2003; Juul 2005). Calleja's central move against Salen + Zimmerman is that one cannot adopt Huizinga's central concept while abandoning his central argument: Huizinga's whole point is that play is set aside from ordinary life, and the magic circle is the formal instrument of that apartness; Salen + Zimmerman attempt to retain the term while sidelining the apartness via their open / closed schema, generating, on Calleja's reading, an incoherent position. He marshals Ehrmann (1968) on the impossibility of bracketing "reality" against play, and Taylor (2006) on the grounded-analytic cost of magic-circle reading.

The **spatial-magic-circle critique** (pp. 48–49) engages Juul (2005)'s spatial-formal reading: the magic circle for digital games is located in the screen and the input devices. Calleja refuses this as redundant: in digital games, the only on-screen space one can act in is the navigable space of the virtual environment. *FIFA 2009* stadium stands and *Battlefield 1942* outside-combat-area are non-navigable representational backdrops; they do not require a magic-circle marker because they do not function as game-space at all. The spatial-marker function dissolves into the navigability gradient.

The **lusory-attitude critique** (pp. 49–51) engages Suits (1978) on the voluntary adoption of inefficient (rule-restricted) means to a goal. Calleja's two-pronged refusal: (1) digital games enforce rules mechanically — in digital golf, no voluntary adoption is required because the game itself prohibits picking up the ball — so Suits's criterion fails to capture digital games; (2) the criterion is circular (games require a lusory attitude; the lusory attitude is what occurs when playing games). Calleja invokes Malaby (2007) on the logical incoherence of using "play" as both mode-of-experience and form-of-activity, and Taylor (2006) on the oversimplifying effect of experientially deterministic arguments. The chapter then states the empirical finding from Calleja's own data: in analyzing the interviews he found no indication that players enter into a game-specific experiential mode. The PIM dimensions are presented as an analytically productive description that does not load the analysis with a priori assumptions.

The **fun-essentialism critique** (pp. 51–53) engages Huizinga (1955)'s claim that fun is the irreducible essence of play, and the inheritance of that claim by Crawford (2003), Koster (2005), and Salen + Zimmerman (2003). Two arguments are deployed: (1) per Malaby, Dibbell (2006), and Taylor (2006), the games=fun equation imposes a normative status that ignores the variation of experiential states; MMOG players spend extended periods of willing engagement in tedious or laborious activity (Steinkuehler 2005; Yee 2006a, 2006b); (2) fun is analytically empty — it names a clustering of positive emotions without specifying what they are or where they originate. The closing move is dialectical: Huizinga's confession that fun cannot be further analyzed is reframed as evidence of the term's fuzziness rather than as evidence of its essential status. The chapter ends by reaffirming that gameplay can be further analyzed — provided one avoids labels like fun or the magic circle.

The substantive theoretical contribution of this block is that the PIM is dimensional rather than essentialist. It refuses to define games by a single essence (fun) or to bracket them by a single boundary (magic circle). It substitutes a structured account of dimensional involvement at two temporal grains.

## §6 Applying the Model (pp. 44–46)

The Applying the Model section bridges the dimensional definitions and the magic-circle excision. Three reading-instructions emerge. First, no game need exhibit all dimensions at high intensity (the *Pong* / *Oblivion* / *Half-Life 2* contrast). Second, the wagon-wheel diagram (Figure 3.1) instantiates the attentional-economic mechanism — outer edge = full attention; narrowing inward = internalization; center = simultaneous attention to multiple dimensions. Third, the kinesthetic-control internalization sequence is the paradigm example of how attentional resources are reallocated as a dimension passes from conscious to internalized engagement, freeing capacity for spatial layout-learning, character-development planning, and other simultaneous dimensions. The model's modular + scalable property is stated here: each dimension is a broad category open to further subdivision in the chapter dedicated to it.

## §7 Cross-pipeline bridges (anticipatory across all three augmentation layers)

Per Phase 0's cite-check and confirmed by direct search of pp. 35–54, the chapter contains zero direct references to Aristotle, Heidegger, Burke, Uexküll, Merleau-Ponty, or Gibson, and no instances of "affordance," "phantasia," "pathos," "phronesis," "Funktionskreis," "Umwelt," or "Merkwelt." All three augmentation layers (Aristotle / Heidegger; Burke; Uexküll) are therefore *anticipatory projections* by the dissertation. Every cross-pipeline edge carries provenance `anticipatory-projection (dissertation)`. The mappings proposed:

**Burke pentad (anticipatory)** — KINESTHETIC → Agency (avatar control as instrumental means, with the dispositional-readiness extension capturing Agency-as-disposition); SPATIAL → Scene (place-as-inhabited as the Burkean setting; spatial internalization as Agent's coming-to-dwell-in Scene); SHARED → Agent (with co-agent ratios) (other agents as the populating field of co-Agents); NARRATIVE → Act + Purpose (scripted sequence + emergent alterbiography as Burkean Acts toward Purposes); AFFECTIVE → Attitude (the sixth pentad term capturing the dispositional coloring of action); LUDIC → Purpose + Act (choice-and-repercussion as Purpose realized through Act under opportunity-cost weighting). The *consubstantiation ↔ incorporation* theoretical move is flagged for Manual Section E and IG-10.

**Uexküll biosemiotic constructs (anticipatory; cite-check confirms Gibson is *absent* in Ch. 3, so the Gibson-mediated bridge is also anticipatory rather than mediated)** — KINESTHETIC → *Funktionskreis* (perception-action loop; internalization as the closing of the loop into automaticity); SPATIAL → *Umwelt* + *Merkwelt / Wirkwelt* (subjectively-constituted virtual environment of meaning-carriers); SHARED → *Umwelt*-overlap (intersubjective biosemiotic field of agents in overlapping non-identical Umwelten); NARRATIVE → *Bedeutungsträger* sequence (meaning-carrier seriality); AFFECTIVE → *Merktöne / Wirktöne* (qualitative tone-coloring); LUDIC → *Wirkmal* + goal-structure (effect-marker as closure-condition for functional circle). The *Umwelt ↔ virtual-environment-habitation* mapping is flagged as Layer C's signature payoff: arguably more apt than Heideggerian *In-der-Welt-sein* for the bounded, designer-constituted nature of virtual environments.

**Aristotle / Heidegger ontological grounding (anticipatory)** — KINESTHETIC → *aisthesis-kinesis* + *dunamis* / *hexis* via Bowin AMT (readiness-to-act = *dunamis*-as-*hexis*; internalization = actualization of *hexis*); SPATIAL → spatial *phantasia* via Hawhee rhetorical-vision + Papachristou deliberative *phantasia* (internalization of game-space = maintenance of spatial *phantasmata*; macro-phase route-planning = *phantasia*-deliberative); SHARED → *Mitsein* via Heidegger BCAP (being-with as ontological mode); NARRATIVE → *muthos* / emplotment (Aristotelian *Poetics*); AFFECTIVE → Aristotelian *pathē* (Rhetoric II) + Gross 2017 *Uncomfortable Situations*; LUDIC → *phronesis* + deliberative *phantasia* (practical wisdom under opportunity cost; imagistic substrate of choice-rehearsal); TEMPORAL-PHASE (macro / micro) → BCAP kairos + ekstatic temporality + Bowin AMT continuous-time (macro = anticipatory + retentional structure; micro = ekstatic moment-to-moment engagement). The temporal-phase grounding is the chapter's strongest cross-pipeline bridge candidate to the dissertation's BCAP and Bowin AMT indices.

**Phantasia Cluster (anticipatory)** — beyond the SPATIAL and LUDIC mappings above, the internalization mechanism itself parallels habituation of *phantasia* operations; the layered + transparent property of dimensions echoes rhetorical-vision imagistic overlay. These map to existing Phantasia Cluster anchors (Hawhee, Papachristou).

**Gross 2017 *Uncomfortable Situations*** — the AFFECTIVE dimension's multi-valenced range (especially the uncanny inclusion) bridges directly to Gross's situated-emotion theory and to the Berlant / Barrett / Barsalou anchors in that pipeline.

**Dalton dissertation** — the PIM is the dissertation's analytical instrument. The three-tier hierarchy (attention → involvement → presence/immersion) is the dissertation's import-ready stratification. The grounded-theoretic methodology is the dissertation's empirical-adaptation seed. Every Ch. 3 concept is a candidate dissertation-deployable construct, with provenance `dissertation-instrument` rather than `anticipatory-projection`.

## §8 Tensions and open questions

Five structural tensions structure the chapter's reception.

T1 — Anti-essentialist Wittgensteinian definitional posture vs. construction of a six-dimensional taxonomic apparatus. Calleja softens this by insisting on layered + transparent dimensions and on the non-necessity of all dimensions in every game, but the formal structure remains taxonomic.

T2 — Bottom-up grounded-theoretic emergence vs. presentation of dimensions as exhaustive. Two MMOG case sites is a thin empirical base for an exhaustive taxonomy of player experience.

T3 — Macro / micro developmental asymmetry. Micro is the chapter's analytic center of gravity; macro is comparatively under-developed at the moment of the model's introduction.

T4 — Positive-constructive vs. negative-purgative compression. Both jobs are performed in the same twenty pages.

T5 — Structural-taxonomic register (diagrammatic) vs. experiential-phenomenological register (continuous, layered, dispositional). The dissertation's adoption of the model should be alert to which register the instrument is in at any given analytic moment.

Open questions for the dissertation. (a) How should the model handle dimensions Calleja did not derive — e.g. temporal-rhythmic involvement, aesthetic-stylistic involvement — that the dissertation may need? (b) How does the macro-phase apparatus scale to games whose macro-phase content is structurally different from MMOGs (single-player narrative games; abstract competitive games)? (c) How exactly does the three-tier hierarchy (attention → involvement → presence/immersion) integrate with phenomenological accounts of being-in-the-world (Heideggerian *In-der-Welt-sein* vs. Uexküllian *Umwelt*)? (d) Calleja's interview design includes only experienced players; the dissertation may need to capture the dimensional-emergence sequence of first-time and novice players, where attentional capacity is structurally differently distributed and where the kinesthetic-internalization paradigm is itself the experiential object rather than the substrate of other dimensions. (e) The interview-within-environment design presupposes a stable persistent virtual environment to interview within; how does the methodology translate to ephemeral or session-bound games where there is no ambient game-world to host an interview? (f) Calleja's ergodic-dispositional reformulation is a substantive theoretical move that arguably exceeds Aarseth's original formulation. The dissertation should clarify whether it adopts the dispositional reformulation as a friendly amendment to Aarseth or as a distinct phenomenologically-grounded reframing that may need its own conceptual archaeology back to Aristotelian *dunamis* / *hexis* and Heideggerian readiness-to-hand.

## §9 Phase 3D Manual Section A inputs (per-dimension capture)

This section serves as the direct seed for the PIM Operationalization Manual's Section A. For each dimension I provide the operational definition (≤60 words), boundary criteria (4–6 features), macro / micro signatures, decision-rule references, and a paradigm-game placeholder for the Phase 2 dimension chapters (IG-04 through IG-09) to fill.

**KINESTHETIC.** Operational definition (53 words): Engagement with avatar or game-piece control in a virtual environment, spanning from conscious learning of the controls through to fluent internalized movement; foregrounded when the controls make themselves present, either through unmastery or because a situation demands a complex sequence of actions that taxes the player. Boundary criteria: locus = control surface (vs. SPATIAL); foregrounded under unmastery / complex-sequence; dispositional-ergodic under-determination (Red Orchestra sniper); internalized state reduces attentional load. Macro signature: recalling impressive feats of avatar control. Micro signature: control-internalization sequence; dispositional readiness. Decision rules: R1 (KINESTHETIC vs. LUDIC), R2 (SPATIAL vs. KINESTHETIC). Paradigm games: IG-04 to fill.

**SPATIAL.** Operational definition (41 words): Engagement with the spatial qualities of a virtual environment via spatial control, navigation, and exploration; accounts for the process of internalizing game-spaces so that the player senses inhabiting a place rather than merely perceiving a representation of space. Boundary criteria: locus = environment-as-place (vs. KINESTHETIC); navigation/exploration mode (vs. NARRATIVE); inhabitation vs. mere representation (Pong / Oblivion / Half-Life 2 contrast); non-navigable space = backdrop, not spatial locus (FIFA 2009; Battlefield 1942); macro = off-line spatial mental models. Decision rules: R2, R3. Paradigm games: IG-05 to fill.

**SHARED.** Operational definition (44 words): Engagement deriving from awareness of and interaction with other agents (human- or computer-controlled) in the game environment under the modalities of cohabitation, cooperation, and competition; encompasses collaborative strategy, guild-political discussion, and ambient awareness that one's actions occur in a social context. Boundary criteria: agent-presence as constitutive; ambient awareness counts; macro signature (clan / guild work, persisting bonds); three modalities span. Decision rule: R4 (SHARED vs. LUDIC under cooperative play). Paradigm games: IG-06 to fill.

**NARRATIVE.** Operational definition (33 words): Engagement with both story elements scripted into the game and story elements emergent from the player's ongoing interaction with the game world, its embedded objects, its inhabitants, and the events that occur there. Boundary criteria: story-content object (vs. SPATIAL / SHARED); dual scripted / emergent; macro = designed-narrative speculation + alterbiography construction; environmental-storytelling boundary against SPATIAL via R3. Paradigm games: IG-07 to fill.

**AFFECTIVE.** Operational definition (49 words): Engagement with the range of emotional states elicited by play — from the calming sensation of an aesthetically pleasing scene through the adrenaline rush of competitive first-person-shooter rounds to the uncanny of an action-horror episode — including both designer-installed rhetorical strategies of affect and player-precipitated affective interpretations. Boundary criteria: object = response-state; dual designer/player source; multi-valenced range (calming / adrenaline / uncanny — not coextensive with fun); macro signatures (aesthetic-residue inner peace; anticipatory excitement / anxiety). Decision rule: R5 (always co-occurs; foregrounding rule for primary coding). Paradigm games: IG-08 to fill.

**LUDIC.** Operational definition (49 words): Engagement with choices and their repercussions, where choices may be directed toward game-stipulated, player-stipulated, or community-stipulated goals, may be made on the spur of the moment without overarching goal, and where opportunity cost is constitutive of well-balanced game systems — without repercussions, actions lose their meaning. Boundary criteria: choice + repercussion + opportunity-cost object; goal-source-agnostic; repercussions-meaningfulness criterion; macro = winning-strategy + character-development planning; ergodic-disposition overlap with KINESTHETIC via R1. Decision rules: R1, R4. Paradigm games: IG-09 to fill.

**Cross-dimensional decision rules** (recapitulation): R1 (KIN vs. LUD under cognitive effort / readiness); R2 (SPA vs. KIN under heavy-navigation FPS); R3 (SPA vs. NAR under environmental storytelling); R4 (SHA vs. LUD under cooperative play); R5 (AFF coding always secondary unless response-foregrounded); R6 (macro vs. micro under hinge cases); R7 (involvement vs. presence/immersion cross-tier).
