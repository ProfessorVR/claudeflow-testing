## 1. The Chapter's Role and Method

Chapter 2 is the volume's densest-citation chapter and its principal hinge between the foundational ground laid in IG-01 ("Games Beyond Games") and the load-bearing theoretical apparatus introduced in IG-03 ("The Player Involvement Model"). Calleja undertakes a literature review of the two terms — *presence* and *immersion* — that the field has used to name what he takes to be a real and important phenomenon, namely the experiential quality of inhabiting a virtual environment. His diagnostic argument is that the terminology has accumulated incompatible meanings across overlapping research communities and that this terminological promiscuity has impeded theoretical progress. The chapter terminates in the canonical enumeration of *four conceptual challenges* (book pp.32–33) which any subsequent account — including his own incorporation-frame in IG-10 — must answer, and in a brief "Toward a Solution" hinge (pp.33–34) that announces the constructive work to come.

The chapter is methodologically a survey-critique. Calleja distinguishes two metaphorical lineages of "immersion" (absorption vs. transportation), follows each into its representative theorists, and identifies the specific moves in each account that make the lineages incompatible or self-undermining. He works with ten named pre-Calleja immersion models. The asymmetric pattern of his response to them — captured by the response-type distribution above — is the chapter's most consequential structural feature, because it pre-determines what incorporation will and will not absorb from the prior literature.

## 2. The Four Conceptual Challenges in Full

The canonical enumeration arrives at book pp.32–33 and is the chapter's load-bearing diagnostic. I render Calleja's formulations paraphrastically:

**Challenge 1 — Immersion as absorption versus immersion as transportation.** The field uses *immersion* to denote two distinct phenomena without disambiguating. The first, traceable through the OED definition (*absorption in some condition, action, interest, etc.*) and operative in Salen and Zimmerman 2003, Jennett et al. 2008, Ermi and Mäyrä 2005, Dovey and Kennedy 2006, and the first two levels of Brown and Cairns 2004, denotes a generic high-engagement state applicable in principle to gardening, cooking, crossword puzzles, or Tetris. The second, operative in Murray 1998, Ryan 2001, Laurel 1991, Carr 2006, Steuer 1992, Tamborini and Skalski 2006, Ijsselsteijn and Riva 2003, Waterworth and Waterworth 2003, Slater 2003 (technology-side), and Brown and Cairns 2004's third level, denotes being-present-in-another-place — medium-specific, virtual-environment-anchored, and carrying the system-recognition feature that game environments uniquely afford. The challenge is that researchers move between the two senses without flagging the move, sometimes within a single study. Calleja's prescription is terminological discipline: reserve *immersion* (or, better, deploy a new term — incorporation) for the transportation-sense and use *involvement*, *engagement*, or *absorption* for the first.

**Challenge 2 — Immersion in non-ergodic media.** The two senses behave differently across ergodic and non-ergodic media. Calleja deploys the Aarseth-derived ergodic/non-ergodic distinction without an in-chapter cite (the concept is operative throughout) to insist that engagement with literature, film, and painting is qualitatively distinct from engagement with game environments. The presence-research tendency to extend presence to non-ergodic media (Witmer and Singer 1998; Schubert and Crusius 2002; Gysbers et al. 2004; Marsh 2003; Lee 2004) has stretched presence to cover what Calleja calls "essentially an entirely noetic phenomenon" (p.22) — abandoning the original technological-affordance specificity (system-recognition of the user's location and actions) that motivated the term in Minsky 1980. The challenge is therefore not merely that the two media-types are different but that the analytic apparatus appropriate to one cannot simply be ported to the other.

**Challenge 3 — Technological determinism.** Specifically, fidelity-determinism: the position that hardware-fidelity determines presence regardless of interpretation, agency, or prior lived experience. The chapter's paradigm of this position is Slater's quadraphonic-orchestra example at Slater 2003, 607, which Calleja reads as exemplifying the form/content partition (presence = form, engagement-with-content = something else) by which a presence-inducing virtual environment can in principle have uninvolving content. Calleja rejects the partition: interpretive apparatus and prior lived experience are constitutive of the sense of presence itself, so form and content cannot be cleanly separated. Someone who has never attended a classical concert may not feel "present" listening to a high-fidelity orchestra recording, regardless of the system's specs. The Heidegger 1993 citation at p.21 underwrites this rejection (see §5 below).

**Challenge 4 — Monolithic perspectives on immersion.** Whether construed as absorption or as transportation, immersion is constituted by multiple experiential components arrayed on a continuum of attentional intensity. The presence-questionnaire tradition (Witmer and Singer 1998 and successors) treats presence as a single phenomenon to be operationally defined and quantitatively measured. Brown and Cairns 2004's three-degree ladder, while gesturing toward a continuum, still terminates in a discrete *total immersion* state equated with presence. Calleja's prescription is multidimensional and graded: immersion must be analyzed as a composite, not a binary on/off switch. This is the seed of IG-03's macro/micro temporal-phase distinction and of the six-dimensional structure of the Player Involvement Model itself.

The four challenges are not independent. Challenges 2 and 4 share a common rejection of monolithic, medium-indifferent analytic frames. Challenges 1 and 3 share a common rejection of analytic frames that abstract from the player's interpretive apparatus and the system's responsive structure. Together, the four diagnose what Calleja takes to be the field's collective failure: an attempt to apply pre-virtual-environment vocabulary to a phenomenon whose qualitative novelty those vocabularies cannot capture.

## 3. The Presence-Theory Critique (Displace-and-Replace)

The chapter's presence-theory critique runs from pp.18–25 and operates as a *displace-and-replace* pattern: Calleja preserves none of presence-theory's vocabulary or analytic apparatus under the eventual incorporation-frame in IG-10. Three theorists carry the weight of this critique.

**Slater and Wilbur 1997 / Slater 2003.** Slater is the chapter's primary fidelity-determinism foil. His framework defines immersion as a property of the technology ("a description of a technology that describes the extent to which the computer displays are capable of delivering an inclusive, extensive, surrounding and vivid illusion of reality"; Slater and Wilbur 1997, 606) and presence as the human reaction to this technological property (Slater 2003, 1). Calleja rejects the form/content partition by which the position is sustained: the quadraphonic-orchestra example presupposes that a listener's interpretive apparatus is not constitutive of the sense-of-presence, but Calleja argues — and his argument is the chapter's positive theoretical move at p.21 — that interpretation is not optional or modulatory but constitutive. If you have never attended a classical music concert, you may not feel "present" in the way Slater describes regardless of the system's fidelity. Slater's vocabulary is therefore displaced wholly. Incorporation does not preserve the immersion-as-hardware-property sense.

**Witmer and Singer 1998.** Witmer and Singer invert Slater and Wilbur: immersion becomes the psychological state of envelopment, presence becomes the conjunction of involvement plus immersion. Calleja treats the inversion as symptomatic — two of the field's flagship accounts use the same terms with opposed meanings. The Witmer+Singer collapse of immersion into psychological-state also enables the cross-media extension Calleja rejects under Challenge 2: once immersion is psychological state of envelopment, there is nothing barring its application to literature, film, painting, or any "continuous stream of stimuli and experiences." Incorporation does not reconstitute presence-questionnaire psychometrics.

**Lombard and Ditton 1997.** Lombard and Ditton attempt a synthetic move: they survey six characterizations of presence (social richness, realism, transportation, immersion, social actor within a medium, medium as social actor) and distill them into the common thread *perceptual illusion of non-mediation*. They then subdivide presence-as-immersion into perceptual and psychological sub-senses. Calleja grants that the synthetic move is theoretically useful but argues that when operationalized for analysis the framework reduces to feature-list pigeonholing — and offers McMahan 2003 as proof (see below). The perceptual-illusion-of-non-mediation framing has a theoretical neighbor in Bolter and Grusin 1999's *logic of transparent immediacy* (p.23), which Calleja preserves as a distinct concept-node, but the Lombard+Ditton framework itself is displaced. Incorporation works from habitation rather than from interface-erasure.

Two structural critiques of presence-theory accompany the three-theorist critique. First, the Held-and-Durlach-descended equivalence claim — that presence-in-VEs and presence-in-physical-environments are not intrinsically different — is rejected as ontological-not-merely-terminological. Calleja deploys *Second Life* (Linden Lab 2003) as counter-evidence: even open-ended virtual worlds remain designed environments, and players are not confused about that designed nature at any point in their interaction. Ijsselsteijn and Riva 2003 are quoted as the representative articulation of the equivalence-claim. Second, the cross-media extension of presence is critiqued (pp.22) as having abandoned the system-recognition feature that originally motivated the term in Minsky 1980 — presence has been stretched to cover noetic phenomena which the original technology-driven term was designed precisely to distinguish from.

## 4. The Game-Studies-Immersion Critique (Critique-and-Supersede)

The chapter's game-studies critique runs from pp.25–32 and operates as a *critique-and-supersede* pattern: Calleja absorbs specific partial insights from each theorist while replacing the overall framework with the eventual six-dimensional PIM. Seven theorists carry the weight of this critique; five constitute the canonical pre-Calleja immersion-models tracked in the pipeline manifest.

**McMahan 2003.** McMahan operationalizes Lombard and Ditton's six-categories framework on *Myst III: Exile* (Presto Studios 2001). Calleja reads the resulting analysis as descriptive feature-listing rather than analytic engine. The social-realism claims McMahan makes about *Myst III* (elevators, zeppelins, page-turning, peering at objects) apply trivially to most digital games in similar genres and so do no game-specific work. McMahan also drifts terminologically — she treats immersion as a form of involvement, diverting it from its presence-theory transportation-connotations — and Calleja flags the drift as symptomatic of broader field-wide confusion. What incorporation supersedes here is the entire three-fold classification scheme. What it absorbs is the negative diagnostic: classification without analytic resolution fails, and a successor framework must do per-game analytic work.

**Salen and Zimmerman 2003.** Salen and Zimmerman frame the *immersive fallacy*: the idea that the pleasure of a media experience lies in its capacity to sensually transport the participant into an illusory simulated reality so complete that the frame falls away. They are correct, Calleja insists, to attack the mimetic-realism imperative that this fallacy underwrites in game design. They are also correct to insist (via Gorfinkel, quoted within them) that one can become immersed in *Tetris* — that is, that immersion-as-absorption need not depend on representational mimesis. But the Tetris move sidelines spatiality as a defining feature of virtual-environment immersion: the absorption-sense and the transportation-sense are conflated. What incorporation supersedes is the absorption-sense's claim to govern the analytic vocabulary. What it absorbs is the anti-mimetic-realism insight, which surfaces in incorporation as resistance to fidelity-determinism.

**Douglas and Hargadon 2001.** Douglas and Hargadon build on Nell 1988's psychology-of-reading to distinguish immersion (a within-frame schema, directed interaction, single-script comportment) from engagement (a more distanced, critical, extra-textual perspective triggered by contradictory or schema-defying elements). Calleja praises the attentional-continuum recognition — the move from conscious attention to unconscious involvement — and rescues it under the eventual macro/micro temporal-phase distinction in IG-03. But he rejects the Balzac-Dickens-Krantz-equals-Space-Invaders move: collapsing ergodic and non-ergodic forms of engagement loses precisely the system-recognition feature Calleja takes to differentiate game-environment transportation. Book readers may imagine themselves in the space-world a literary work describes, but that world does not recognize them. What incorporation supersedes is the within-frame/extra-textual schema distinction. What it absorbs is the attentional-continuum insight.

**Brown and Cairns 2004.** Brown and Cairns propose a three-degree ladder: *engagement* (basic interest in interacting with the game), *engrossment* (emotional attachment, with loss of awareness of self and surroundings), and *total immersion* (most intense involvement, equated with presence). Calleja credits the productive identification of increasing levels of involvement as prerequisites for transportation. The flaw is the metaphor-mixing: levels 1 and 2 operate in the absorption-sense and level 3 switches to the transportation-sense, exacerbating the very definitional confusion the authors had set out to resolve. The follow-on Cairns et al. 2006 quantification study inherits the mixed-metaphor flaw and additionally over-generalizes from generic cognitive-fatigue effects (degraded performance on a tangram task following gameplay) which are not game-specific. What incorporation supersedes is the three-degree ladder. What it absorbs is the involvement-as-prerequisite-to-transportation insight, which surfaces in the six-dimensional PIM as the precondition structure underlying incorporation.

**Ermi and Mäyrä 2005.** Ermi and Mäyrä propose a multidimensional gameplay-experience model — sensory immersion (audiovisual engagement), challenge-based immersion (mental and motor skill in overcoming challenges), imaginative immersion (catchall for character-identification, narrative, world-engagement). Calleja credits the multidimensionality and the medium-specificity recognition: the model treats games as qualitatively distinct from other media. The flaw is methodological. Ermi and Mäyrä derive the meaning of "immersion" empirically from child interviews. Children naturally deploy the term in its colloquial absorption-sense; the model therefore collapses the academic-technical sense into the colloquial. The work would be unproblematic if relabeled as a study of "involvement" or "engagement"; what is problematic is using "immersion" while committing the very definitional-promiscuity charge Calleja levels at the field. What incorporation supersedes is the three-mode taxonomy. What it absorbs is the multidimensionality + medium-specificity — fully, because the PIM's six dimensions are an expansion of the multidimensional approach Ermi and Mäyrä initiate.

Two supplementary game-studies theorists round out the critique. Dovey and Kennedy 2006 use *immersion* and *engagement* interchangeably; their gameplay time-economy framing is usable but the terminology is too unspecific. Jennett et al. 2008 are cited as part of the absorption-side cite-cluster, instancing investigations that refer to presence and immersion in their virtual-environment-habitation sense and then offer a view of immersion in its more general involvement-sense.

## 5. The Heidegger p.21 Cite in Context

The Heidegger 1993 citation at book p.21 is the sole Heidegger reference in the volume — confirmed by both index (Heidegger, Martin, 21) and references (Heidegger, Martin. 1993 [1977]. *The Question Concerning Technology and Other Essays*. New York: Harper & Row.). The cite-check audit at manifest.json records three in-text hits across the whole book: the actual cite at p.21, the references entry, and the index entry. It is therefore the only ontological-grounding-related text-anchor available to the dissertation's Layer A augmentation.

The local context is the chapter's central rejection of fidelity-determinism. Calleja has just quoted Slater 2003's quadraphonic-orchestra example at the foot of p.20. He opens p.21 by arguing that fidelity-determinism underweights the role of interpretation and agency in creating a sense of presence. He then makes (paraphrastically) three connected claims: that interpretation need not be conscious; that most environmental interactions are enabled by an internalized knowledge of how aspects of the environment work; and that when an experience resists ready interpretation, our comportment becomes critically removed and we must actively think about what we are doing. To this last claim a parenthetical "(Heidegger, 1993)" is attached, with no further explication. The entire Heidegger-anchored move occupies three sentences of book p.21.

The Heidegger reference here functions as authorization for an everyday-vs-disrupted comportment distinction. One would natively gloss it as the Heideggerian shift from *Zuhandenheit* to *Vorhandenheit* — the move from ready-to-hand pre-thematic engagement to a thematized, present-at-hand stance triggered by the failure of equipmental functioning. Calleja does not use that vocabulary. He does not name the structure. He does not identify which essay from the 1993 [1977] volume is in view. He does not engage *Sein und Zeit*, Dasein, In-der-Welt-sein, or the existential-analytic apparatus that the dissertation's BCAP-grounded Layer A augmentation would activate. He does not engage Aristotelian-Heideggerian time-phenomenology, which his own macro/micro temporal-phase distinction in IG-03 would naturally invite.

The reference operates as a single one-sentence philosophical authorization — what a philosophically literate reader would call a *gesture* — rather than as a sustained ontological grounding. This is consequential. It means that every ontological-grounding edge in the IG pipeline (to BCAP, to the Phantasia Cluster, to Bowin AMT, to Uexküll *Foray*) carries the provenance flag *anticipatory-projection (dissertation)*. The p.21 cite is the one exception: it is text-anchored proxy for the dissertation's late-Heidegger-of-technology-critique bridge, but even this proxy is single-line and underdetermined. The dissertation's Layer A apparatus does not find a partner in Calleja's text; it finds, at best, a hospitable opening.

That said, the p.21 passage is the closest Calleja comes to phenomenological-hermeneutic register in the volume. The everyday-vs-disrupted distinction it sketches is structurally adjacent to the macro-vs-micro temporal-phase distinction the PIM in IG-03 will deploy: macro-phase off-line involvement is precisely the unthematized comportment of the everyday, micro-phase moment-to-moment engagement is precisely the kind of immersed coping the disruption-passage describes. The dissertation can therefore use the p.21 cite as a single hinge-point and develop the rest of the Layer A apparatus from the BCAP and Phantasia Cluster indices on the dissertation's own authority.

## 6. The "Toward a Solution" Hinge and Bridge to IG-03

The chapter's final subsection (pp.33–34) is short — under two book pages — and operates as a structural transition rather than a substantive argument. Calleja re-locates the two terms within their disciplinary origins: technologists, media psychologists, and HCI researchers use *presence*; humanists and later social scientists use *immersion*. Both terms have suffered from varied application. The first step toward progress, Calleja announces, is the "establishment of clear and precise terms" — and the rest of the chapter ends. The Player Involvement Model in IG-03 is what that announcement turns out to mean.

What the hinge does for the volume's argument structure is to license a constructive move. The four challenges of pp.32–33 have established the diagnostic; the "Toward a Solution" gesture announces that the constructive remedy will operate at the terminological-conceptual level, not at the empirical-questionnaire level (rejecting Witmer+Singer) and not at the technology-property level (rejecting Slater). What it leaves unspecified at this point is the positive vocabulary. The PIM in IG-03 will supply *involvement* as the umbrella term and the six dimensions (kinesthetic, spatial, shared, narrative, affective, ludic) as the multidimensional decomposition that answers Challenge 4. The macro/micro temporal-phase distinction in IG-03 will answer Challenge 1's absorption-vs-transportation problem by distributing the two metaphors across two distinct temporal regimes (macro = absorption-friendly off-line involvement; micro = transportation-friendly moment-to-moment engagement). The avatar-anchoring + system-reactivity claim Calleja has already built up across pp.22–28 will answer Challenge 2 by supplying the ergodic-specific structural feature presence-theory had lost. And the rejection of fidelity-determinism in §5 above will answer Challenge 3.

The IG-10 incorporation move is more distant but already prefigured. The two key concepts the chapter develops on its own authority — *avatar-anchoring* and *metaphorically habitable environment* — are proto-incorporation moves. The player who assimilates a game world into gameplay "as a metaphorically habitable environment can be thought of as being *transported* to that world" (p.27, paraphrastically). The transportation-metaphor is preserved at this stage; in IG-10 it will be reconstituted as the habitation-metaphor of incorporation. This is the structural respect in which Murray and Ryan are critique-and-rescued rather than displaced — their underlying intuitions (transportation, spatiality) survive the terminological renaming that IG-10 performs.

## 7. Cross-Pipeline Bridges

Four cross-pipeline bridges are anchored in this chapter, all carrying *anticipatory-projection (dissertation)* provenance except where noted.

**Heidegger BCAP.** The Heidegger p.21 cite is the one text-anchored proxy in the entire volume. It is, however, late-Heidegger-of-technology-critique rather than SZ-Dasein, and so the BCAP-anchored time-phenomenology bridge to Calleja's macro/micro temporal-phase apparatus in IG-03 must be carried by the dissertation on its own authority. The p.21 passage is the only place in the volume where a phenomenological-hermeneutic vocabulary is even gestured at.

**Phantasia Cluster.** Ryan's "expanse to be immersed within" and "textual world" (quoted at p.31–32) invite phantasia-spatial-imagery framing. Calleja's distinction between the *imagined scene* (where the imaginer wills events) and the *avatar-anchored game-world* (where the world reacts to the player) at pp.22–23 is, in Calleja's vocabulary, a phantasia-vs-pragmatic-perception distinction — though Calleja never uses the term *phantasia* (and the cite-check confirms zero Aristotelian vocabulary anywhere in the volume).

**Uncomfortable Situations (Gross 2017).** Brown and Cairns's *engrossment* — emotional attachment plus loss of self and surroundings (p.30) — is the chapter's principal affective-register moment, prefiguring IG-08 Affective Involvement. Calleja's caveat that Brown and Cairns "do not provide us with an explanation for why" affect and loss-of-awareness "might be" tied together is a point at which Gross's situated-emotion frame and Berlant's cruel-optimism analytic could supply explanatory leverage.

**Uexküll *Foray*.** Avatar-anchoring (pp.22–23, 27–28) and metaphorically-habitable-environment (p.27) are *Umwelt*-adjacent constructs; the system-reactivity-to-player-location feature is *Funktionskreis*-adjacent (perception-marker / effect-marker loop). The Gibson-mediation hypothesis is falsified for this chapter as for the whole volume: Calleja's two uses of *affordances* (p.20 "specific qualities and affordances of the medium"; p.32 "multiple media with considerably varied qualities and affordances for engagement") are theoretically generic and do not establish a Gibson bridge. Layer C is therefore singly-anchored anticipatory in this chapter.

**Dalton dissertation (instrument).** The four conceptual challenges (pp.32–33) supply a diagnostic checklist any dissertation immersion claim must answer. The absorption-vs-transportation partition supplies the vocabulary-discipline the dissertation will apply to its empirical adaptation. The Heidegger p.21 cite supplies the one text-anchored ontological-grounding moment the dissertation can claim is *in* Calleja rather than projected onto him.

## 8. Synthesis: Why the Asymmetric Response Pattern Matters

The chapter's response-type distribution is the key Phase 3 input for the *ig-immersion-vs-incorporation-debate-map* deliverable. The numbers are: two *critique-and-rescue* (Murray, Ryan); three *displace-and-replace* (Slater+Wilbur/Slater, Witmer+Singer, Lombard+Ditton); five *critique-and-supersede* (McMahan, Salen+Zimmerman, Douglas+Hargadon, Brown+Cairns, Ermi+Mäyrä).

The pattern is not arbitrary. The transportation-ancestors (Murray, Ryan) carry the experiential intuition Calleja's *incorporation* aims to preserve under a new vocabulary — being transported into a place, the spatiality of the place being constitutive. Their accounts are critique-and-rescued: the vocabulary is replaced, the intuition is kept. The presence-theorists (Slater, Witmer+Singer, Lombard+Ditton) carry a hardware-and-psychometrics analytic apparatus that Calleja's experientialist incorporation-frame rejects categorically; their apparatus is displaced wholly. The game-studies-immersion-as-absorption theorists (McMahan, Salen+Zimmerman, Douglas+Hargadon, Brown+Cairns, Ermi+Mäyrä) each contribute one or two partial insights — multidimensionality, attentional continuum, anti-mimetic-realism, involvement-as-prerequisite, classification-without-analytic-resolution-fails — that the PIM in IG-03 will absorb piecemeal while replacing the surrounding frameworks. They are critique-and-superseded.

The asymmetry is consequential because it pre-determines what the dissertation can claim. Any dissertation argument that invokes Calleja's incorporation framework will inherit the rescued Murray + Ryan intuitions (transportation, spatiality-as-defining-feature) without inheriting the rescued vocabularies. Any dissertation argument that takes up presence-theory under the incorporation framework must do so on Calleja's terms — the presence-questionnaire psychometric tradition is unavailable as analytic support. Any dissertation argument that takes up game-studies immersion theorists may freely cite their partial insights but should not rely on their overall frameworks as analytic engines, because the chapter has shown the frameworks to be self-undermining in just the respects the dissertation would need to lean on them.

The Manual that Phase 3 produces will draw its debate-map directly from this chapter's prior-model critique table. The asymmetry is the table's most important structural feature.
