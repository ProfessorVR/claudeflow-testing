# IG-06 Shared Involvement — Deep Analysis

*Calleja 2011, In-Game, Ch. 6, book pp. 93–112.*

## 1. Dimension operationalization

Shared involvement, as Calleja constructs it across Chapter 6, names the engagement derived from awareness of and interaction with other agents in a game environment, whether those agents are human- or computer-controlled. The dimension is the third of six in the Player Involvement Model and is the second after spatial (IG-05) that takes the structure of the virtual environment as a precondition: shared involvement requires that there be perceived others co-inhabiting the same world. The chapter is organized along the temporal-phase distinction Calleja inherits from his Player Involvement Model in IG-03: macro-phase shared involvement names the persistent off-line sociality that accumulates across sessions (guild membership, durable friendships, marriages, betrayals, reputation, governance), while micro-phase shared involvement names moment-to-moment co-presence and contest during gameplay. The micro phase is decomposed into three sub-dimensions — cohabitation, cooperation, and competition (and conflict). Calleja announces the trio with an authorial slip at p.101, writing that "there are four dimensions of shared involvement: cohabitation, competition, and cooperation" — the numeral "four" governs only three named items, and the chapter delivers exactly three subsections. This is treated as typo in the present analysis: there is no occulted fourth dimension. (See §6 below for the load-bearing significance of this slip for Phase 3 ontology.)

Calleja's operational definition can be tightened to under sixty words: *Shared involvement is the engagement arising from awareness of and interaction with other agents — human or sufficiently coded AI — in a game environment, encompassing cohabitation, cooperation, and competition, and spanning macro-phase persistent online sociality and micro-phase moment-to-moment co-presence, collaboration, and contest during gameplay.*

The dimension is bounded against adjacent dimensions by five distinguishing features. First, an agent-other-than-self condition: shared involvement engages only where the player perceives another agent inhabiting the same world; pure spatial habitation in an empty environment falls under IG-05 alone. Second, an awareness-and-interaction condition: mere ambient avatar density barely qualifies (the chapter treats this as the threshold case), but the substantive form requires perceived interaction-potential or actual interaction. Third, a social-versus-functional valence: shared involvement foregrounds social bond, reputation, performance-for-audience, or contest-with-another — even where kinesthetic and ludic dimensions are also engaged. Fourth, a persistence sensitivity: macro-phase shared involvement requires environment persistence so that reputation, friendship, and betrayal accumulate across sessions, distinguishing one-off LAN matches from MMOG society. Fifth, the dimension is indifferent to medium of communication: chat, voice channel, avatar gesture, and co-located vocal banter all count, distinguishing it from narrative involvement (IG-07), where channel-of-delivery is partly constitutive.

## 2. Macro phase: MMOG socializing

Calleja opens by historicizing multiplayer gaming. Pre-digital games were largely multi-player and public; early digital games such as *Spacewar* (Russell, 1962) and *Pong* (Atari, 1972) extended this two-player pattern through Atari VCS, the Nintendo Wii, and PC LAN gaming. With networked play and integrated console multiplayer, this trajectory culminates in MMOGs and in console virtual worlds like *PlayStation Home* (SCE London Studio, 2008). Citing Steinkuehler and Williams (2006) and Taylor (2006), Calleja identifies the support of multitudes of globally distributed players in a single environment as MMOGs' main attractor — and identifies the felt presence of those players, even as background traffic or as silent potential audience, as constitutive of the dimension's macro phase.

What distinguishes MMOGs from earlier digital games is, in Calleja's phrasing, their propensity to become full-fledged on-line societies. Friendships are started and ended, relationships formed, marriages sealed, and betrayals played out. He offers the *EVE Online* (CCP Games, 2003) Guiding Hand Social Club heist of April 2005 as a betrayal exemplar: a mercenary corporation infiltrated and sabotaged Ubiqua Seraph, extracting around US$16,500 in virtual assets. The case is small in word-count but load-bearing: it demonstrates that virtual social structures can sustain consequential, narrative-rich betrayal events readable as social acts rather than as technical exploits. Interview testimony reinforces the society-claim: Baal (*WoW*) describes the feeling that "all these thousands of characters … are actually people stuck to their PCs all over the world." Splate (*WoW*) reports moving cross-country to live with friends made in a MUD; Nombril (*WoW*) flirts with guildmates over Ventrilo and finds that attaching a voice "humanizes" them; Haelvon (*WoW*) contrasts MMOGs' goal-directed activity with chat rooms' lack thereof; Inauro (*WoW*) cites the vibrant online community as primary attraction.

Calleja draws three structural claims out of this material. End-game content design forces guild membership (the prerequisite functionality of guilds is a designer-engineered scaffold for shared involvement, not an emergent social fact). Geographically-organized guilds (the Austin, Texas *WoW* guild example) bleed in-game sociality back into out-of-game gatherings, eroding the magic-circle boundary. And, citing Castronova (2005) alongside Klastrup (2004) and Taylor (2006), Calleja identifies the social reality MMOGs constitute as a different referent for "the real" than tangible-physical-matter — goal-directed activity gives MMOG communication a context and motivation chat rooms lack.

## 3. MMOG governance: the Sara Andrews / Blizzard case

The chapter's longest sustained example occupies pp.97–99. In late January 2006, Sara Andrews announced the formation of a new *World of Warcraft* guild that would welcome gay men, lesbians, and transsexuals. Players reported the announcement to Blizzard Entertainment, which warned Andrews that her guild violated the *World of Warcraft* Terms of Use under "Harassment — Sexual Orientation," and threatened account suspension if she continued the formation process. Calleja reproduces the relevant Terms-of-Use clause (which gives Blizzard "sole and absolute discretion" to define offensive language and forbids misspellings or alternative spellings to circumvent restrictions) and Blizzard's public statement (cited from Hunter 2006) that "to promote a positive game environment for everyone … we prohibit mention of topics related to sensitive real-world subjects in open chat … This includes openly advertising a guild friendly to players based on a particular political, sexual, or religious preference." A personal-communication quote from Blizzard to Andrews provides the chapter's most pointed line: "Many people are insulted just at the word 'homosexual' or any other word referring to sexual orientation."

Calleja's analytic move on this material runs in two registers. First, an ironic register: the situation arose because Blizzard could not enforce the Terms of Use against routine derogatory use of "gay" in chat, so it was easier to corner one self-declaring entity than to monitor channels for the more common infringement. Second, a structural register: this incident, he argues, illustrates how dangerous notions like the magic circle become when used to analyze or make decisions in virtual worlds like MMOGs. The magic-circle posture is attractive to companies like Blizzard because it lets them treat MMOGs as games cordoned off from social concerns of the everyday and the problems of governance these entail. But Blizzard's self-policing policy means that what is acceptable is ultimately dictated not by Blizzard but by the general consensus of the population of the server — by the das-Man of the chat channel. (Calleja does not cite Heidegger, but the operative structure is recognizably the publicness-and-leveling of *das Man*; see §6 below.) The Sara Andrews case thus functions in the chapter as a triple anchor: (i) it operationalizes Calleja's critique of the magic circle as governance-tool; (ii) it documents the corporate platform's exposure to server-consensus enforcement; and (iii) it shows the contradiction between cordoning real-world concerns out while designing real-world social ties in for retention purposes.

After the Andrews case Calleja turns to Oldenburg's (1999) "third places" and Steinkuehler and Williams's (2006) test of the third-place hypothesis using ethnographic data from *Lineage* (NC Soft, 1998), *Lineage 2* (NC Soft, 2003), *Asheron's Call* (Turbine, 1999), and *Asheron's Call 2* (Turbine, 2002). Steinkuehler and Williams conclude MMOGs fit most of Oldenburg's criteria, but are better at bridging than bonding social capital. Since Oldenburg's complaint targets the bridging deficit, the bridging verdict is, by Calleja's lights, sufficient to satisfy the third-place claim — though Calleja qualifies that the US-centric framing may not transfer to societies that have retained off-line third spaces.

## 4. Micro-phase cohabitation

The micro-phase bridge passage (pp.100–101) draws an analogy with literary and film characters before pivoting to the coded-behavior layer that distinguishes game agents: the one-eyed rhinos in *Spore* (Maxis Software, 2008) attack grass-munching ostrich-like creatures not because the author decreed but because their coded behavior dictates attack-when-weaker-and-within-distance. Human-controlled agents shift this further — in *World of Warcraft*'s Ironforge city, the avatars running around are improv-theater actors in a larger environment, and the player knows she is interacting with humans through her avatar. As Sunniva (*Planetside*) puts it, "it somehow makes the experience more immersive/intense knowing that there's a real person behind the avatar." This human/AI distinction, once made, reorients player disposition.

Cohabitation, Calleja's first sub-dimension, anchors the player firmly in the location both spatially and socially — a walking-talking populated environment obfuscates its simulation-character. Even rudimentary AI suffices to ground a sense of cohabitation. Calleja anchors this via Reeves and Nass's (1996) media equation (people treat media interfaces as social actors automatically and unconsciously at minimal cues), corroborated by Gerhard, Moore, and Hobbs (2004) on cohabitation with rudimentary AI, and extended by Calo (2010) on the growing sociality of computer-controlled agents. The *Grand Theft Auto IV* (Rockstar North, 2008) Dimitri Rascalov betrayal of Nico Bellic is treated as evidence: players experience the event as betrayal by a double-crossing mobster rather than as interaction with a computer program (Calleja cross-cites Gerhard et al., Reeves and Nass, and Ruvinsky and Huhns 2009 on this).

MMOGs add a persistence layer. Characters exist over extended time without name or appearance changes; players accumulate positive or negative reputation; some guilds keep "kill on sight" lists for unacceptable actions such as ninja-looting (unfairly taking rare drops and leaving) or ganking (killing characters with no chance of winning for no particular gain). Social standing significantly affects gameplay decisions in MMOGs.

Cohabitation also subsumes spectatorship. In multiplayer games that allow looking through other players' perspectives in spectator mode, one's actions become a performance commented on by others. Players maintain consistent aliases across games to be recognized within a community and to accumulate reputation. Clan-entry trials use spectator-mode screening over multiple rounds. Spectators may also be co-located: in the *FIFA 2009* (EA Sports, 2008) living-room scenario, an audience cheering and gasping in the last minutes of a drawn game can deepen affective involvement and shift play style from goal-directed to visually-pleasing-or-impressive. This subsection is, in the present pipeline's reading, the chapter's most Goffmanian — performance-for-audience, audience-effect on style of action, institutionalized spectator-mode screening — and Goffman is not cited. (See §6 on the residual-phenomenology anchor sites.)

## 5. Cooperation, competition, and conflict

Cooperation, the second sub-dimension, is anchored in Manninen and Kujanpaa (2005) and Heide-Smith (2007). Co-op configurations include split-screen *Halo 3* (Bungie, 2007) in a shared living room (whose limitation is restricted spatial navigation — levels are made linear and proximity-constrained), LAN parties at private and public scale, and designed-interdependence games such as *Left 4 Dead* (Valve, 2008). *Left 4 Dead* is the paradigm case Calleja develops at length: four postapocalyptic survivors must reach a rescue point through a zombie-infested zone, supervised by an AI director that spawns mobs when players separate or stall; among special zombies, the Hunter pounces and locks the player in damage that only a teammate's shot can interrupt, and the Smoker's tongue drags the player away. The game additionally facilitates collaboration by letting players see each other through walls and by issuing a single-use medical pack each player can apply to self or other. The game's mechanics, that is, engineer collaboration into the substrate; cooperation is not optional but designed.

In MMOG cooperation, teamwork operates at two scales: groups (5–6 person base, scaling to 15/20/40 raid groups at higher levels) and guilds (which provide common chat channels supplemented by TeamSpeak or Ventrilo voice, plus virtual social events to bind members). End-game-content design forces grouping; the investment required (hours of preparation plus the quest itself) compels repeat collaboration with regular teammates. Bladerunner (*Planetside*) describes "hundreds of people 'attempting' to work together for a common goal" as a key attractor: "you get all warm and fuzzy when your team is kicking ass." All but two interview participants cited interaction with others, collaboratively or competitively, as the main reason for their MMOG participation; collaboration was the most frequently cited motivation.

Competition (Caillois 1962, "agon") and conflict round out the micro phase. Digital games have not decreased competitive impulse; they have amplified it by scaling to globally distributed real-time contests with thousands of players. Calleja's typology ranges across configurations: synchronous two-player on the same screen (*Pong*, *Combat*), split-screen on consoles (*Gran Turismo*, Polyphony Digital 1998), simultaneous shared-screen competition (*FIFA 2009*), and cooperative-competitive hybrids (*Rock Band*, Harmonix 2007, where players collaborate on a song while competing for individual scores). He distinguishes direct competition (shared goal, opposed factions) from conflict (differing objectives intersecting in environment): the *Lord of the Rings Online* (Turbine, 2007) journey from Bree to Rivendell runs through computer-controlled assailants pursuing different objectives, and Angmar quests intersect with player-controlled creatures pursuing capture goals.

Computer-controlled conflict in single-player games (*Half-Life 2*, Valve 2004; *S.T.A.L.K.E.R.*, GSC Gameworld 2007) is becoming more sophisticated relative to the *Space Invaders* (Taito, 1978) predictable-pattern baseline. Calleja turns to Malaby's (2007) social contingency, codependent on performative contingency, to explain preference for human opponents: opponents' unpredictability and the requirement to act on guesses (not just make them) drive engagement. BunkerBoy86 (*Planetside*) gives the quote anchoring this: "human element adds a lot of fun and surprises … makes it more challenging and realistic." Lim and Reeves (2006) document that human opponents increase both automatic and rationalized responses to gameplay.

AI limitations split along two axes. In strategy games like *Empire: Total War* (Creative Assembly, 2009), AI units act simultaneously on local behavioral triggers but lack holistic battlefield awareness — skirmishers flee flanking cavalry only at trigger range, not before. In FPS games, computer-controlled agents combine pinpoint accuracy with vulnerability to traps; once again, they cannot take the entire environment into account. The competitive-FPS arc (*Counter-Strike*, Valve 1998; *Call of Duty IV*, Infinity Ward 2007; *Red Orchestra*, Tripwire 2006) sustains a casual-to-professional trajectory: from random-group encounters to dedicated tournaments. Rambusch, Pargman, and Jakobsson (2007) document the shift from individual to team play in established clans, where players reframe themselves not only as individuals but as team players whose actions impact teammates; local tournaments shift the activity from leisure to (semi)professional work.

Calleja's closing move reasserts the macro/micro structure: the satisfaction of successful teamwork binds cooperation and competition into a deeply involving experience that operates simultaneously at macro (long-term engagement) and micro (moment-to-moment gameplay) phases. He notes that shared involvement, like other PIM dimensions, can be consciously attended or internalized, with cooperation and competition both requiring semi-conscious learning before mastery — and that this attentional capacity depends on prior kinesthetic mastery: a player still working out movement controls cannot allocate attention to opponent-behavior modeling. This is the chapter's strongest intra-volume cross-reference (to IG-04).

## 6. The three-not-four authorial slip — and what it means for the dissertation

At p.101 Calleja writes that "there are four dimensions of shared involvement: cohabitation, competition, and cooperation." The numeral is four; the named list contains three; the subsections that follow are three (Cohabitation, Cooperation, Competition and Conflict). No fourth sub-dimension is presented anywhere in the chapter, and the closing summary on p.112 reasserts that the interactions have been discussed "in terms of cohabitation, cooperation, and competition." The simplest reading is authorial typo. For the dissertation, the load-bearing point is twofold. First, the chapter's internal cardinality is fixed at three: any Phase 3 ontology that imports a fourth sub-dimension is unwarranted by the text. Second, the candidate fourth — spectatorship, which is the only sub-element of the chapter that hovers on the margin of distinct status — is in fact treated by Calleja as part of cohabitation, not as separate. This is itself a substantive theoretical commitment: spectator-mode trials and co-located audience effects are forms of co-presence, not a separate axis of audience-relation. The dissertation will need to decide whether to ratify or contest that commitment; the present pipeline records the chapter's choice and flags it.

## 7. Layer-A Mitsein anchor sites (residual phenomenology without phenomenologist cite)

Five sites in Chapter 6 carry phenomenologically loaded language without naming the relevant phenomenologist. The propagation note from IG-03 and IG-10 flags this as a chapter-level residual anchor concern; Chapter 6 is in fact the volume's strongest such concentration. (1) The macro-phase "full-fledged on-line societies" formulation at pp.94–95 substantively describes being-with-others-in-a-shared-persistent-world without ever citing Heidegger's *Mitsein* or *Mitwelt* (SZ §§25–27). This is the strongest Mitsein anchor in the entire In-Game volume. (2) The Sara Andrews / Blizzard server-consensus passage at pp.97–99 substantively describes what Heidegger calls *das Man* — the publicness, the leveling, the "in averageness" of inauthentic conformity-policing — without naming the construct. (3) The spectatorship subsection at pp.103–104 substantively describes Goffmanian dramaturgy — performance for co-located audience, audience-effect on style of action, institutionalized spectator-mode trials as audience-screening — without citing Goffman (whether *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* or *Frame Analysis*). This is the chapter-level Layer-A anchor site flagged by IG-03 and IG-10 propagation. (4) The Reeves-Nass media-equation passage at pp.101–102, in treating computers and computer-controlled agents as social actors at minimal cues, substantively engages intersubjectivity-with-non-human-others without citing Husserl, Schutz, or Levinas. (5) The human-vs-AI agent-recognition passage at pp.100–101 substantively engages theory-of-other-minds / intentional-stance terrain without citing Dennett or Husserl. None of these residuals is dispositive on its own; collectively they make Chapter 6 the strongest Layer-A integration target for the dissertation's planned Aristotle-Heidegger grounding work, and the strongest single-chapter anchor for the Mitsein bridge.

## 8. Anticipatory augmentation mappings

Per plan §3.D.i, the chapter's Burke pentad mapping is **Agent**, with ratios into Scene (the persistent virtual world), Act (cohabit / cooperate / compete), and Agency (the chat/voice/gesture channels). The Agent-as-locus reading is hard to avoid: Chapter 6 is literally about the human and AI agents in shared environments, and Calleja's analytic moves repeatedly turn on the Agent's perceived properties (human vs AI, persistent vs transient, reputation-bearing vs anonymous, audienced vs solitary). The Agent-Scene ratio captures cohabitation; the Agent-Act ratio captures cooperation and competition; the Agent-Agency ratio captures the communication-channel infrastructure (guild chat, TeamSpeak, Ventrilo, spectator-mode). This mapping is anticipatory-projection (dissertation), not Calleja's claim — Burke is not cited.

Per plan §3.D.i, Shared is explicitly NOT a Layer-C signature dimension under Uexküll. The signature Layer-C dimensions are Spatial (*Umwelt*), Kinesthetic (*Funktionskreis*), and Affective (*Merktöne / Wirktöne*). For Shared, a weak Uexküllian anchor remains possible via *Bedeutungsträger* (meaning-carrier): the other player is a meaning-carrier whose effect-tones (*Wirktöne*) differ qualitatively from those of a coded NPC meaning-carrier, which Uexküllianly anchors Calleja's human-vs-AI disposition shift. This is a secondary anchor, anticipatory-projection only.

Per plan §3.D.i, the primary Layer-A grounding for Shared is **Heideggerian Mitsein** (SZ §§25–27, via the BCAP corpus index). This is the strongest Layer-A bridge in the entire In-Game volume. Secondary anchors are *Mitwelt*, *das Man* (for the Sara Andrews case), and Aristotelian *koinōnia* / *philia* (for guild and friendship structures). All are anticipatory-projection — Calleja cites no Heidegger and no Aristotle in this chapter.

## 9. Cross-pipeline bridges

The strongest cross-pipeline bridge is to **BCAP / Heidegger** via Mitsein and das Man. This is anticipatory but anchored on a dense site: the chapter's macro-phase formulations and the Sara Andrews case together stage Mitsein and das Man more substantively, and over more pages, than any other site in the volume. The dissertation's Layer-A grounding work should treat Chapter 6 as primary evidence.

Intra-volume bridges run in four directions. To IG-04 (kinesthetic): Calleja explicitly stages kinesthetic mastery as an attentional prerequisite to social-contingency mapping on p.110 — the IG-04 dimension is the chapter's only explicitly named cross-dependence. To IG-05 (spatial): cohabitation is described as anchoring the player both spatially and socially (p.101), tying shared involvement to the spatial-habitation substrate of IG-05. To IG-08 (affective): the *FIFA 2009* co-located audience example is staged in p.104 as intensifying affective involvement, an explicit cross-reference. To IG-10 (incorporation): the chapter's three sub-dimensions feed forward into the incorporation rubric, where shared involvement is one of the constitutive components.

To **Burke 1945** (*A Grammar of Motives*): Agent + ratios mapping, anticipatory. To **Uexküll Foray**: secondary / weak *Bedeutungsträger* anchor only, anticipatory. To **Goffman** (no current pipeline): chapter-level Layer-A residual-phenomenology anchor concentrated at the spectatorship subsection — this is the dissertation's strongest Goffman-without-Goffman site.

## 10. Closing observations

Three observations carry forward to Phase 3 synthesis. First, Chapter 6's evidentiary weight for the Mitsein bridge is exceptional within the volume; the synthesis should not let this disappear into chapter-summary granularity. Second, the three-not-four enumeration slip is load-bearing for the book-level ontology: it fixes the sub-dimension cardinality and makes spectatorship a sub-element of cohabitation rather than a separate axis. Third, Calleja's empirical-sociological scaffold (Steinkuehler & Williams, Taylor, Castronova, Oldenburg, Reeves & Nass, Caillois, Malaby) is held remarkably consistently across the chapter and provides the densest layer of direct citation edges in the unit; the Phase 3 interlocutor map should treat the Steinkuehler-Williams / Taylor / Castronova / Caillois / Malaby cluster as the chapter's primary citation backbone.
