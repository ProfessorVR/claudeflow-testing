# IG-APX Deep Analysis: Appendix — A Tale of Two Worlds (book pp. 187–200)

## 1. The production-editorial anomaly: an appendix that was almost a chapter

A small typographic detail does substantial interpretive work in this unit. The two figures embedded in the appendix are labeled "Figure 12.1" (p. 189) and "Figure 12.2" (p. 191), yet the section is presented in the table of contents and the running heads as an appendix rather than as Chapter 12. The likely production history reads through this artifact: MIT Press almost certainly handled the section internally as Chapter 12 during typesetting and figure numbering, and only at a later editorial pass redesignated it as an appendix. The numbering survived the reclassification because regenerating figure labels carries production risk and offers no reader-facing benefit.

The interpretive payoff is not merely bibliographic curiosity. The appendix-versus-chapter ambiguity tracks a substantive ambivalence about what this material does. Read as appendix, the section is auxiliary, pedagogical, and removable: readers unfamiliar with World of Warcraft and Planetside are oriented to the games whose players Calleja interviewed. Read as Chapter 12, the section is structural, evidentiary, and load-bearing: the binary-comparative case presentation that anchors the book's analytic model to two specific virtual worlds. The textual fingerprint suggests the manuscript moved between these self-understandings during production, and the surviving figure numbers preserve the chapter-form draft beneath the appendix surface. For Phase 2 purposes the relevant point is that the case-presentation material in this section carries chapter-weight even though its packaging signals appendix-weight, which justifies treating the appendix as a full unit rather than as supplementary matter.

## 2. The binary-comparative scaffold: pedagogy on the surface, methodology underneath

The appendix opens by disclaiming its own analytic ambitions. The framing paragraph announces that the section exists for readers who have not experienced the games firsthand, and that what follows is an overview rather than an argument. This pedagogical framing should be taken seriously but not exclusively. The verified location of Calleja's qualitative-research methodology is Chapter 3, "The Research Process" (book pp. 35–36, IG-03), where he describes interview design, participant recruitment, focus-group composition, machinima and forum monitoring, and iterative coding. The appendix is not the methodological seed for the dissertation's empirical-adaptation chapter; Chapter 3 is. Recognizing this avoids the temptation to read the appendix as a tacit methods chapter.

That said, the appendix is methodologically transferable in a different sense. Its structural device — eight subsections (Genres, Topographies, The Lore, Clone Worlds, Customization, Mechanics, and the closing Planetside synthesis, plus the framing intro), each built as a symmetric WoW-then-Planetside comparison along the same axis — is a case-study apparatus. The scaffold triangulates the book's player-involvement model across two genre prototypes, demonstrating that the analytic categories the book develops in its main chapters operate across substantially different MMOG designs. As an analytic-instrument, the scaffold is portable: another scholar working with a different corpus of cases can adopt the seven-axis presentation pattern (genre, topography, lore, server, customization, mechanics, agency-and-world-mutability), populate it with their own case data, and produce a parallel triangulation. This portability is what makes the appendix a case-study-template extraction target for the dissertation, even though the methodological seed proper resides in Chapter 3.

The pedagogy-versus-methodology distinction matters because the appendix's surface rhetoric (orient the unfamiliar reader) under-represents what the section structurally accomplishes (demonstrate cross-genre applicability of the player-involvement model). A dissertation that wants to adapt the apparatus needs to read past the modesty of the framing paragraph to the comparative architecture beneath it.

## 3. World of Warcraft profile (pp. 187–200, drawing across all subsections)

**Genre and lineage.** World of Warcraft is positioned as a massively multiplayer on-line role-playing game whose lineage descends from pen-and-paper role-playing games such as Dungeons and Dragons (Arneson and Gygax 1974), with Fine (1983) and Mackay (2001) cited for the performance dimensions of that tradition. Calleja immediately complicates the role-playing tag, observing that most MMORPG players do not in fact filter their actions through fictional personas outside of dedicated role-playing servers. The genre label thus operates more as a marker of mechanical lineage (character sheets, levels, classes, quests, statistic-based combat) than as a description of player practice.

**Topography.** Azeroth comprises ninety-six regions distributed across two continents, controlled by either the Alliance or the Horde or designated as contested areas hosting both factions. Each region carries a distinct aesthetic palette, and transitions between regions are unnaturally abrupt — a few meters of land containing a dramatic blend of two regions' colors. Calleja's participants do not register this lack of mimetic realism as a deficiency. Indeed, it functions as a memory-and-orientation aid: aesthetic differentiation makes the world feel larger than it physically is. Geological coherence is sacrificed for palette legibility: the lush Feathermoon region sits hundreds of meters from the barren desert of Desolace, and Azeroth's Google-map representation reveals what Calleja describes as a quiltlike terrain. The contrast case is Dark and Light (NP Cube 2006), which pursued geological realism. Blizzard's other spatial technique is the designed channel: mountains, forests, walls, and shores create impassable boundaries that guide players along predetermined pathways while making regions feel larger than they are. The Half Life 2 (Valve Software 2004) example of a city that trails off into apparent distance while only a few streets remain accessible is offered as the broader-game-design analogue.

**Social spaces.** Azeroth's social fabric is dense. Marketplaces, auction houses, and taverns punctuate the urban centers. Players advertise wares on the trade channel, and geographic proximity between buyer and seller matters for one-to-one transactions, particularly when advantageous deals or urgently needed items are at stake. Casual socializing happens in these spaces independently of organized gameplay.

**Lore.** The Warcraft real-time-strategy trilogy supplies World of Warcraft's backstory, which eased entry for players already familiar with the franchise but produces a setting that, by Calleja's measure, falls well short of the depth of Tolkien's Middle-earth or Greenwood's Forgotten Realms. The initial Warcraft game pitted orcs against humans in a generic environment that was never intended to support a deep secondary world, and that limitation persists. More consequentially, the design does not require lore engagement for progression. Haelvon's interview (cited in the lore subsection) reports reading early quest descriptions but eventually defaulting to clicking Accept and pursuing the bare minimum. Nombril's interview reports never reading the quests at all. The functional decoupling of narrative from progression is, on Calleja's reading, structural rather than incidental.

**Server architecture (clone worlds).** Azeroth runs on thousands of clone servers, region-locked into four blocs (Americas, Australasia, Europe, Asia). Once a player purchases World of Warcraft within a region, that region's servers are the only ones available. The friction this introduces is documented through Ananke's interview, which records the work of maintaining a stable four-year friend group across SWG, World of Warcraft, and DDO as the players themselves relocated across the United States, Canada, and England.

**Customization.** Players choose a race and a character class at creation, name the character, and then make limited alterations to hairstyle, skin color, and face shape. Beyond that, avatar individuation is constrained. Inauro's interview compiles the participant complaint: at level sixty, what distinguishes one rogue from another aside from name and gear, perhaps a dagger versus sword build. The differentiation economy thus migrates from avatar to acquisition: rare clothing, armor, and items become the legible markers of player identity, an economy Calleja crystallizes in the formula that in World of Warcraft a player literally is what they wear. Long hours in-world or real-money expenditure on gold and items are the two routes to that wardrobe.

**Mechanics.** A 300-millisecond latency does not materially degrade play because character actions consist of keystroke-activated move sequences queued on the server and executed sequentially. Once a character is ordered to attack, it follows the target automatically; a frozen client during network congestion still permits the queued attack sequence to resolve. Third-person perspective is unanimously preferred among the World of Warcraft participants because it provides a wider field of vision including the character's rear. Tab-targeting permits acquiring enemies the player cannot personally see. Environmental conditions have negligible mechanical effect: only distance from targets and passable-versus-impassable terrain matter.

**Progression, questing, and griefing.** Levels gate absolute power. Quests, kills, and instanced player-versus-player battlegrounds yield experience points; the level differential between a character and its target tunes both XP gain and the radius at which mobs aggro. Quest design defaults to deliver-X, kill-X, or kill-X-until-Y-drops; participants report that quest text is often unread, with external help-sites supplying map coordinates and target locations, a practice normalized as part of the game itself. On PvP servers, the absolute-power gradient enables griefing: higher-level characters kill lower-level victims who have no chance of competing, and some practice corpse camping, slaying victims as they return to claim their corpse and effectively locking them out of the game. The practice is rule-permitted but socially frowned upon.

**Specialization and world-mutability.** Class specialization in World of Warcraft is decorative rather than tactically required: the rogue class detects traps and opens locks, but no quest specifically requires a rogue's presence. World-mutability is near-zero. Mobs, non-player characters, and resources respawn; even a successful capture of an opposition outpost on a PvP server yields no in-world reward, and when the aggressors depart the location resumes its prior existence as if nothing had happened. Solo activities (questing, crafting, auction-house economics) are well-supported; world-altering action is structurally precluded.

## 4. Planetside profile (pp. 187–200, drawing across all subsections)

**Genre and lineage.** Planetside is a massively multiplayer on-line first-person shooter, distinguished from MMORPGs principally by combat system (long-range, twitch-based, real-time) and perspective default (first-person). Calleja immediately complicates the boundary by noting that MMOFPSs commonly include character progression, classes, missions, and quests; that MMORPGs can be played first-person with real-time combat; and that the genre line tracks weapon density and setting more than any clean mechanical criterion. Comparable MMOFPS titles cited include World War II Online (Cornered Rat Software and Playnet 2001), Face of Mankind (Duplex Systems 2006), and the forthcoming Huxley (Webzen). Notably, MMOFPSs accommodate non-fighting playing styles through support classes — medics, engineers, transport drivers — that will become significant in the mechanics discussion.

**Topography.** Auraxis comprises ten continents and five caverns. Each continent carries a particular terrain theme and weather cycle, but the landscape is visually generic, and participants report difficulty differentiating continents even after a year of regular play. Ambient sound is negligible aside from weather effects. The absence of AI-based agents leaves areas without human players feeling incredibly barren. There are no urban areas — only towers and facilities — which participants raised as a felt absence. The only safe area is Sanctuary, the starting factional continent.

**Social spaces.** Auraxis has no calm social spaces. No marketplaces, auction houses, or taverns; equipment is obtained from terminals, foreclosing a player-driven persistent economy. Socializing is restricted to collaboration with outfit comrades, and the pervasive player-versus-player action makes casual gathering impractical: a shock assault or infiltration mission can launch at any moment. The world attracts more action-minded players, with social interaction routed through outfit chat channels or directed at immediate tactical operations.

**Lore.** The setting is a futuristic space-colonization gone wrong. A contingent of humans is stranded on Auraxis, their disconnection from the Terran Empire splintering the army into three factions: the Terran Empire, the New Conglomerate, and the Vanu Sovereignty. Players must pledge allegiance to one faction and join the never-ending battle against the other two. The functional disinterest pattern documented for World of Warcraft lore is not explicitly repeated for Planetside, but the structural decoupling of narrative depth from gameplay obtains: factional allegiance is mechanically consequential, but background mythology is light.

**Server architecture (clone worlds).** Auraxis runs on three clone servers — US West, US East, and Europe — with no regional restrictions on which servers players may log on to. The mobility friction documented for World of Warcraft's region-locking is therefore absent.

**Customization.** Avatar customization is more restricted than in World of Warcraft — limited to facial features, hair, and general head shape — and faction-mates look largely alike. Crucially, however, Planetside has no character classes. Identity emerges from the combination of skills and certifications acquired during play. The differentiation economy thus shifts from gear (as in World of Warcraft) to skill-bundles: how a player has elected to spend certification points constitutes their game-mechanical identity.

**Mechanics.** Latency materially matters because actions are performed in real time. A slight delay in server response means a player aims at things that were there half a second ago. Unlike Tab-target acquisition in World of Warcraft, the Planetside player must see the target, align the crosshairs, and click. Perspective is correspondingly mandatory: although third-person is technically available, Planetside participants unanimously agree it is only practicable to play first-person. Environmental effects are mechanically substantial: heavier rain occludes line of sight, advantaging infiltrator suits and disadvantaging long-distance shooters; light levels similarly affect detection; cover and elevation are crucial tactical considerations.

**Progression and specialization.** Levels exist but do not change attributes, skills, or powers. They confer certification points to assign to weapon categories or professions (hacker, medic, engineer, driver). A starting character might use three weapon types and ride a bike; an advanced character might use eight weapon types, control tanks and aircraft, and operate medical devices. Crucially, a starting character can still compete or collaborate with an advanced character given correct timing, tactics, and skill, and a handful of beginning characters will generally defeat an advanced character through superior numbers. Specialization is mechanically irreplaceable: a sabotage mission against an enemy facility requires a hacker's certifications and cannot be substituted by firepower. Baal's interview testifies to the tactical-role identity: leading platoons, flying transport aircraft to redeploy troops, engineering to repair others and set up defenses, and using infiltrator-suit-plus-wraith mobility to coordinate attacks across the battlefield. Crucially, the experience-point economy is shared: medics, engineers, hackers, and drivers receive battle-XP shares from the units they heal, repair, hack, or transport, structurally rewarding collaboration beyond one's immediate squad and platoon.

**World-mutability and agency.** Planetside provides game mechanics, rules, and uninhabited areas, but the events of the world are shaped by players. The very nature of player-generated situations often creates unbalanced scenarios — a facility defended against an army outnumbering its defenders five to one — but participants articulate this as part of the pleasure: the aim is not always to win the battle but to hold off the enemy, with the desperate defense being precisely what they enjoy. Taking a whole continent persistently disadvantages other factions because of spawn-point and connected-facility benefits. Outfits (organized player groups) materially shape outcomes; Kumacho's interview describes the Death to All (D2A) outfit during the five-server era and the Ghosts of the Revolution (GoTR) outfit on Emerald, both of which require teamspeak voice-coordination and run regular raids, and which Kumacho says can totally change the shape of a battle.

## 5. The case-study template extraction

The seven-axis scaffold the appendix uses to present its two cases is, as already noted, portable. For the dissertation's purposes the abstract structure can be lifted as follows.

**Axis 1 — Genre placement and lineage.** Where in the broader game-genre taxonomy does the case sit, and what design-historical lineage shapes its conventions? Calleja's move is to acknowledge porous boundaries while preserving the genre tag as a marker of mechanical inheritance.

**Axis 2 — Topography.** The spatial-design analysis covers regional partition, continent count, transition design (mimetic versus aesthetic), the presence or absence of designed channels and impassable boundaries, social-space density and type (marketplaces, auction houses, taverns versus equipment terminals), and the ambient texture of empty space (AI agents, weather, urbanity).

**Axis 3 — Lore.** Narrative depth, source-franchise lineage, and the degree to which engagement with backstory is structurally decoupled from gameplay progression. The participant-quotation pattern is especially load-bearing here, as scholarly claims about player engagement with lore are most credibly anchored by direct participant testimony.

**Axis 4 — Server architecture.** Clone-server topology, regional restrictions, mobility friction, and consequences for social-network continuity across player relocations. The Ananke quotation is the methodological exemplar: participant testimony as evidence for the social effects of architectural decisions.

**Axis 5 — Customization.** The avatar economy (what aspects of appearance can be altered, how meaningfully), the differentiation channel (gear, skills, certifications), and the resulting identity formula ('you are what you wear' versus 'you are what you have certified').

**Axis 6 — Mechanics.** Latency tolerance and action-system design (queued versus real-time), perspective affordance (third versus first person), environmental sensitivity (do weather and light matter mechanically), progression structure (level-gated absolute power versus certification specialization), specialization irreplaceability (decorative versus tactically required), and the experience-point reward economy (solo-rewarded versus shared).

**Axis 7 — Agency and world-mutability.** The scripted-versus-emergent ratio, the persistence of player actions on world state, and the social structures (outfits, guilds, platoons, raids) that organize coordinated player action.

Across all axes, the evidentiary pattern remains constant: analytic narration interleaved with short, screen-name-attributed participant quotations that anchor each claim about player practice or feeling to direct testimony. This is the methodologically transferable signature pattern.

The case-selection criterion the appendix supplies at its close is prototypicality. Each case must prototype its genre and, between them, the cases must encompass gameplay forms shared with other genres while exhibiting the distinguishing element under study (in Calleja's case, the massively-multiplayer element). The closing sentence — that future application of the model to other genres of games will update and expand the foundational research — is a rhetorical invitation to successor case-studies, an opening that the dissertation can take up.

## 6. Tensions: cohabitation versus competition; persistence versus cyclicality; scripted versus emergent

Three structural tensions emerge from the binary scaffolding.

**Cohabitation versus competition.** World of Warcraft cultivates cohabitation: dense social spaces, persistent player-driven economies, casual gathering, and a trade-channel auction culture. Planetside cultivates competition: equipment terminals replace economies, factional warfare displaces casual gathering, and pervasive PvP makes the world an arena rather than a polis. Social-space density correlates with economy structure and with the species of player-involvement each world cultivates.

**Persistent progression versus cyclical warfare.** The two games invert the locus of persistence. World of Warcraft accretes character progression (levels, gear) across time but resets the world (mobs, NPCs, resources respawn). Planetside flattens character progression after early-certification differences but persists the world (continental conquest accrues strategic advantage through spawn-point and facility control). This inversion is consequential for any model of player attachment: in WoW the player invests in their character against a stable world; in Planetside the player and their outfit invest in the world against the relative flatness of their characters.

**Scripted versus emergent.** World of Warcraft's quest-and-battleground economy delivers pre-scripted gameplay; Planetside provides only mechanics and uninhabited terrain, with events generated by players. This is the agency axis in its starkest form, and it is the operational substrate of the closing synthesis: where World of Warcraft minimizes unpredictable situations by virtue of tightly scripted design, Planetside provides the mechanical conditions for players to create their own events.

## 7. Cross-pipeline bridges

The dissertation's empirical-adaptation chapter is the primary bridge target. Combined with the Chapter 3 methodology (IG-03), this appendix supplies the case-study template required for the dissertation to mount its own two-or-more-case comparative analysis. The seven-axis scaffold and the participant-quotation embedding pattern are directly portable. The prototypicality justification supplies a defensible case-selection criterion.

Secondary bridge targets include a spatial-design dissertation chapter (the Topographies subsection's analysis of designed channels and aesthetic-versus-mimetic terrain), an emergent-versus-scripted gameplay chapter (the closing Planetside synthesis), a player-agency-and-persistence chapter (the world-mutability contrast), and a social-space chapter (the cohabitation-versus-competition tension).

The closing paragraph of the appendix positions Calleja's two cases as having supplied "solid foundational research" for the player-involvement model and explicitly anticipates "future application of the model to other genres" that will update and expand it. This is precisely the rhetorical opening the dissertation occupies. Calleja, in effect, names his successor's task; the dissertation accepts the brief and adapts the binary-scaffold method to a different case corpus, extending rather than replacing the analytic apparatus established in In-Game.
