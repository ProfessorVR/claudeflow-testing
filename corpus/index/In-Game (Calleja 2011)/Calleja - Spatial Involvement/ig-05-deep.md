# IG-05 Spatial Involvement — Deep Analysis

**Unit**: IG-05-SPATIAL (Calleja 2011, *In-Game*, Chapter 5, pp.73–92)
**Role in volume**: Dimension chapter (1 of 6) operationalizing the spatial axis of the Player Involvement Model.
**Phase 2 deliverable**: paraphrastic deep-analysis narrative; companion files `ig-05-deep.json` and `ig-05-deep-edges.csv`.

---

## 1. Dimension Operationalization

Spatial involvement, in Calleja's formulation, is the habitation-oriented engagement of the player with the simulated, navigable space of a virtual environment. The dimension is operationalized through a macro-phase / micro-phase pair: the macro phase is **Exploration** — the off-line and on-line drive to traverse unfamiliar simulated landscapes — and the micro phase is **Navigating Space**, which subsumes a five-fold taxonomy of spatial structures together with the qualitative transition from space to place and the special case of miniature/disembodied spatiality. What unifies the dimension is a single cumulative process: the **internalization of game-space across nested scales** (immediate / map / level / region / world) such that the environment shifts from being conceptually perceived to being phenomenally inhabited. The dimension's gatekeeping criterion is Wolf's (2009) definition of *navigable space* — interconnected simulated cells through which the avatar must move and act, with much of the network off-screen — which excludes the merely-represented spaces of non-ergodic media (literature, film) where traversal is limited to mental imagery. Six boundary criteria distinguish spatial involvement from the other five PIM dimensions: simulated-not-represented; nested-scale mapping; oriented toward where-I-am rather than how-to-move (the kinesthetic differentiator); severable from co-presence (the shared differentiator); admitting both avatar-embodied and miniature/disembodied modes; and internally carrying its own aesthetic-affective tonality (the tourist moment) which is not exported to the affective dimension.

## 2. Macro Phase: Exploration (pp.73–75)

The chapter opens by routing exploration through species-level anthropology (Lewis-Williams 1998/2002; Evans 2001) and then immediately through Tuan 1998's argument that urbanization drives romanticization of pastoral and exotic landscape. This sets the stage for Calleja's central macro-phase claim: digital games and virtual worlds *appease the exploration penchant* by offering simulated traversable expanses that exceed what non-ergodic visual media can give. The pivotal differentiation comes at lines 2529–2536: literature can prompt mental imagery of represented landscapes, but in a game world one must *literally* navigate from one point to another, not merely imagine. This is the ergodic-traversal precondition for the entire spatial dimension. Calleja gathers paradigm exploration-affect testimony from his interviews — Rheric's WoW "tourist moment," Baal's "come over a hill and, bang, you're struck by the scenery like you're on holiday somewhere exotic," and Ananke's effortful half-hour foot-traversal yielding an *achievement of sorts*. The macro phase closes by introducing what will turn out to be the chapter's most heavily-used phenomenological apparatus: the avatar's body-relative orientation — front/back/left/right, futurity-ahead, past-behind — via Tuan 2007. This is transferred, with no methodological worry, from physical to virtual environments.

## 3. The Lost-in-the-Game-World Motif: Phenomenological Weight Without Phenomenological Cite (pp.75–77)

This sub-section does the chapter's heaviest phenomenological work, and it does it *paradoxically* with zero phenomenological citation. Getting-lost is reframed as a *productive condition* for spatial involvement. The argument moves in three beats: first, the *possibility* of getting lost requires *spatial contingency*, which requires the environment to extend beyond what is immediately perceptible — Klastrup's (2004) defining element of virtual *worldness*. Second, once players realize loss is impossible, the environment collapses into a perceptible multicursal labyrinth and the scope for exploration is severely diminished; Aarseth (2005) is invoked as the anchor citation. Third — and this is the pivotal move — Tuan 2007 is quoted at length on the lost-in-forest phenomenology: when one strays from a path, *"space is still organized in conformity with the sides of my body. There are the regions to my front and back, to my right and left, but they are not geared to external reference points and hence are quite useless."* A flickering light then establishes a goal and "front and back, right and left, have resumed their meaning." Calleja transfers this directly to virtual environments: goal-directed movement (set by a quest, an in-game agent, or the player herself) gives the avatar a *meaningful* front-back-right-left, reinforcing its tangibility. This is, in disciplinary register, exactly a Heideggerian *Verlorenheit* analysis paired with a body-schema phenomenology paired with a goal-comportment phenomenology — and yet Heidegger is not cited; Merleau-Ponty is not cited; no canonical phenomenologist appears. Tuan, a humanistic geographer working downstream of phenomenology but not himself a phenomenologist, bears the entire weight. This is **Residual Phenomenology Anchor Site RPA-01** and is the chapter's largest single instance of phenomenology-without-phenomenologist.

Borges (1972) supplies the literary figure for the *designer-constituted* character of game worlds: a "labyrinth devised by men, a labyrinth destined to be deciphered by men." Malaby (2007) supplies the conceptual scaffold: maze-traversal promises a solution because the contingency is *contrived* (correct route + designed dead ends), whereas uncharted-bush contingency is *real* (no designed solution). Game-world design fabricates "complex, implicit, contingent conditions wherein the texture of engaged human experience can happen." This Malaby formulation — *designer-constituted-but-genuinely-contingent space* — is the deepest unresolved tension in the chapter and the cleanest entry point for the dissertation's Umwelt augmentation (see §10 below).

## 4. Micro Phase: Navigating Space (pp.77–78)

The micro phase pivots on three engagements: (i) learning the environment through navigation, (ii) mentally mapping the navigated space, and (iii) controlling miniature space through tactical interventions. Before treating these in succession, Calleja imports Wolf's 2009 *navigable space* definition. The definition is doing methodological work: it establishes that the rest of the chapter concerns *simulated*, not *represented*, space.

## 5. The Five Spatial Structures (pp.78–85)

Calleja's signature taxonomy classifies game spaces by **traversable rather than visible space** — this is the methodological commitment that lets him override Nitsche's (2008) labyrinth-vs-tracks-and-rails distinction, since *Gran Turismo* tracks and *Medal of Honor* corridors both structure navigable space along a single path.

**Unicursal Corridors / Labyrinths.** Paradigm: *Medal of Honor: Allied Assault* (2002) opening sequence. The beach landing appears expansive (cliffs left and right, distant horizon) but contains a single traversable path; artillery fire, strafing planes, and general chaos disguise the unicursal structure. After repeated death along the western flank, the player's cognitive map updates to register the invisible-wall barrier. This is the chapter's first introduction of Tolman 1948's cognitive-mapping concept, operationalized through Golledge and Garling (2004) as "the process of encoding, storing, and manipulating experienced and sensed information that can be spatially referenced." Liboriussen 2009 ports cognitive mapping into virtual-world navigation: the mental tool aids navigation AND provides "an overall sense of how the virtual world is structured and a sense of connectedness." Aarseth 2005's *unicursal corridors* label is imported; Fernandez-Vara 2007 on classical labyrinths offering no test is qualified (game labyrinths always contain adversity — puzzles, enemies); *Half-Life 2* (2004) is the alternating-barrier exemplar; King and Krzywinska (2006) supply the spatial-restriction-enables-other-dimensions argument that anchors the chapter to Chapters 7 (narrative) and 8 (affective).

**Multicursal Corridors / Mazes.** Branching paths and dead ends; mental mapping becomes necessary only when the maze is presented in first- or third-person and extends beyond the individual screen. *Fable* (2004) is the in-perspective paradigm: hill and rock formations channel pathways, and a desert ring with heat-exhaustion damage forms the outer barrier. *Pacman* (1980) is the omniscient-perspective counter-example: its scrolling extension generates a different kind of spatial learning, more akin to indexing a Google Maps view than to navigation-based mental cartography.

**Rhizomatic Zones.** Non-contiguous spaces linked by teleportation, framed through Deleuze and Guattari (1987): no hierarchy or center, multiplicity with no beginning or end, multiple entry points, non-linear, resilient to local damage. *Fable II* (2008) offers predefined teleport points within already-discovered areas; *Mass Effect* (2007) offers separate zones reachable only by teleport entry-point. Even contiguous spaces contain breaks-in-navigational-flow as new zones load; designers conceal these through *diegetic transit* — *Dead Space* (2008) elevator journeys are the paradigm, allowing inventory and weapon management during the zone-load.

**Open Landscapes.** Maximum navigational freedom: *The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion* (2006) as paradigm RPG; *Lord of the Rings Online* (2007) and World of Warcraft as MMOG paradigms. Restrictions tend to be rule-based (mob difficulty) rather than spatial-structural — the Hobbit-to-Rivendell example shows that the Shire-to-Rivendell journey is not spatially barred, just rule-dangerous. This sub-section carries Babic's (2007) absolute-vs-relational space argument: drawing on Martina Löw (2001), Babic distinguishes the Euclidean container-conception of space (in which arrangements of bodies and objects are objectively measurable) from the relational conception (in which space exists "through the context given by the relations and interactions of the actors and objects within"). Lefebvre 1991, Soja 1996, and Tuan 1974 cluster as the relational-space theorists. Babic associates pre-MMOG game-space with absolute design and MMOGs with relational design through persistent social interaction. Calleja reframes Babic's argument in PIM-terms: MMOG spatial dimension overlaps intimately with the shared dimension, while *Doom*-style spatial design overlaps with the kinesthetic.

**Arenas.** Nitsche 2008 supplies the category: arenas provide *"the canvas for a performance"* rather than navigational cues. Embedded competitive spaces — WoW battlegrounds, *Counter-Strike: Source* maps, *Gran Turismo* tracks, FPS multiplayer levels. The chapter's most detailed worked example unfolds here. On *Counter-Strike: Source*'s de_dust2 map, starting on the terrorist side with a shotgun against a rifle-armed opposition, the optimal route avoids the central and right corridors (long sightlines favoring the longer-range weapon) in favor of route B which offers cover. Figure 5.2 anchors the layout; Figure 5.3 anchors a flash-grenade response in which the player, blinded, presses D to step right, then S+D to move along the back wall down the steps, then mouse-left to face up the steps anticipating counter-terrorist pursuit. This is the chapter's most articulated case of *intimate map knowledge* as the precondition for tactical competition. The movement-vs-cover discipline differs by game physics: *Counter-Strike: Source*'s one-shot-kill physics makes burst-between-cover movement central, while *Unreal Tournament*'s (1999) multi-shot-kill physics makes constant motion more conducive to survival.

## 6. From Space to Place: The Tuan Engagement in Full (pp.85–90)

This is the chapter's residual-phenomenology centerpiece and the dissertation's premier Layer-C engagement site. Calleja organizes nested-scale cognitive mapping along the immediate-map / level / region / world axis: *Counter-Strike* (map), *Medal of Honor* (level), *Oblivion* (region), *World of Warcraft* (world). As cognitive maps improve, *the player's spatial disposition to them shifts from the conceptual to the inhabited* (lines 2988–2991). Less conscious orientation-attention is required; the environment becomes part of the player's immediate surroundings. Rheric supplies the testimony at lines 2996–3003: a "pretty visual person" who drives by landmarks rather than road names, who flies the Undercity-to-Tarren-Mill route in WoW, who naturally seeks landmarks ("a twist in the road here, a tree there") and feels comfort *and pride* in knowing the lay of the land. The qualitative-shift criterion is Tuan 2007 (p.73): *"When space feels thoroughly familiar to us, it has become place. Kinesthetic and perceptual experience as well as the ability to form concepts are required for the change if the space is large."* This is the chapter's gatekeeping quote and bears the full weight of the dimension's habitational claim.

Calleja stresses the *visual primacy* of digital environments: heat, smell, and varied sound cues are absent or restricted, so the visual aspect of mapping becomes more salient. He then introduces the chapter's second-major residual-phenomenology anchor (RPA-04): Lynch's (1960) *imageability*. The quoted passage runs over a dozen lines: imageability is "that quality in a physical object which gives it a high probability of evoking a strong image in any given observer." Calleja extends Lynch from physical to virtual objects and pushes a stronger claim: imageability is *even more important* in primarily visual-auditory virtual environments. The paradigm contrast is *World of Warcraft*'s Azeroth (high imageability — Rheric on the distinct palettes and textures: Silverpine's blues and greens with wood and fog; Barrens' reds and oranges with rock; Stonetalon's forested variation on the Barrens palette; Arathi and Hillsbrad's greens; Alterac's whites) against *Planetside*'s Auraxis (lower imageability — Kestra: "Most of the landscape isn't too different on a continent. All the trees look the same … if I've been fighting in one place long enough I'll get the lay of the land. But a week later I'd be just as lost as before"). Azeroth is *geographically implausible* (tropical regions abutting temperate flora) but its players accept the implausibility because the regions are distinct and memorable; Auraxis is *more geographically plausible* but its players feel less connection. This is the chapter's **fidelity-vs-meaningfulness tension** (T02): the case is decided in favor of imageability/meaningfulness over geographic plausibility, but Calleja does not theoretically resolve why this should be so.

The section closes with the indexical-map ↔ 3D-environment transition, anchored at Figure 5.4 (the Oblivion map-to-environment shift): making the transition involves a distinction between *inhabiting* the environment and a *more detached disposition which assists in learning the geography*. Increased knowledge of the world makes it feel more familiar; the freeing of attention resources allows engagement with other PIM dimensions — the explicit bridge to the multi-dimensional incorporation thesis of IG-10.

## 7. The Space of Miniatures (pp.90–92)

The chapter's final section addresses disembodied isometric spatiality, characteristic of turn-based and real-time strategy and simulation. Players are always *on the outside looking in*; agency is tied not to a single in-game entity but to a conjunction of the miniatures' capabilities and the player's disembodied-omniscient powers. *Empire: Total War* (2009): one can order troops to march across Portugal while simultaneously changing the tax rate in Silesia. *Warhammer 40,000: Dawn of War II* (2009; Figure 5.5) supplies the RTS miniature-landscape paradigm: terrain is built to support miniature interaction with defendable positions, cover, and lines of fire calibrated to weapon ranges and movement speeds. *In miniature space one can never be lost* (line 3140) — the productive lost-ness of S2 is structurally impossible here. Exploration in miniature space is an *unraveling of a pre-existing picture* rather than a habitational venture. The terrain is a *tactical* space (resource mining, special-item locations, build-zones); internalization becomes exercise in understanding and exploiting tactical potential.

Some miniature environments are purely *symbolic*: *Risk* (2009), checkers, chess. Here spatial involvement is *directly identical with spatial control* and inextricably coupled with tactical involvement. The closing summary paragraph (lines 3189–3194) crystallizes the dimension: spatial involvement defines players' engagement with the spatial qualities of a virtual environment in terms of *control, navigation, and exploration*, accounting for the process of internalizing game space which is a *powerful factor in engaging players and giving them the sense that they are inhabiting a place rather than merely perceiving a representation of space*.

This bifurcation between **habitational** and **tactical-control** modes is the chapter's structural diagonal: the first eight pages of the chapter and the From-Space-to-Place section all build habitational spatial involvement; the closing section installs a second, severable mode in which the habitational machinery does not apply. The 5-structure taxonomy holds for both modes but the qualitative transition Tuan 2007 names — space-into-place — applies only to the habitational mode.

## 8. Layer-A Residual-Phenomenology Anchor Sites

Six residual-phenomenology anchor sites are catalogued in `ig-05-deep.json` (RPA-01 through RPA-06). The pattern across all six is identical: Calleja deploys a register, vocabulary, and analytical structure that disciplinarily belongs to phenomenology — Heideggerian *Verlorenheit*, lived-body orientation in the Merleau-Ponty sense, *Wohnen* / dwelling, phantasia spatial-imagery, equipmental-comportment shift, Stimmung-aesthetic encounter — but in every case the actual citation is to Tuan, Lynch, or Tolman. None of these three is a phenomenologist in the canonical disciplinary sense. Tuan is a humanistic geographer (downstream of and indebted to phenomenology but operating within geography); Lynch is an urban-design theorist; Tolman is a behavioral psychologist. The chapter is therefore the volume's premier *residual-phenomenology* site — where the phenomenological work is being done with non-phenomenological tools. Each RPA marks a place where the dissertation's Layer-A exegetical work can substitute or supplement Calleja's existing scaffolding with the corresponding phenomenological apparatus, and where Layer-C anticipatory projection finds its richest anchoring.

## 9. Anticipatory Burke Mapping: Scene

In Burke's dramatistic pentad, *Scene* is the where-of-action — the constituted ground that contains and constrains the act. The scene-act ratio (the scene determines the act it makes possible) maps with unusual fidelity onto Calleja's spatial-structure-constrains-affordances arguments. Calleja's five-structure taxonomy is, read pentadically, a *typology of Scene as constraint on Act*: unicursal scenes channel acts linearly; multicursal scenes diversify them; rhizomatic scenes discretize them; open-landscape scenes free them; arena scenes intensify them tactically. The mapping pairs naturally with the IG-04 Kinesthetic ↔ Agency mapping — *Scene-Act* in Burke is precisely the pair that Calleja's Spatial-Kinesthetic cornerstone (IG-10) anticipates. The mapping is flagged *anticipatory-projection (dissertation)*: Calleja does not cite Burke.

## 10. Anticipatory Umwelt Mapping with Rationale — Layer C Signature Bridge

This is the chapter where the dissertation's Layer-C Uexküllian augmentation reaches its highest density of anchorability. The argument runs as follows.

Uexküll's *Umwelt* is the subjectively-constituted environment of an organism — *not* the objective world the organism inhabits, but the structured perceptual+effectual world that organism-specific perceptual signs (*Merkwelt*) and effect-signs (*Wirkwelt*) jointly constitute through the *Funktionskreis* (functional circle: perception-marker → mark-bearer → effect-marker → effect-bearer → back to perception-marker). For Uexküll, the tick's *Umwelt* is bounded by what the tick can perceive and the actions through which it can affect its environment; it is not impoverished but *complete-for-the-tick*. The biology-derived organism-relativity is the key feature: an *Umwelt* is constituted by the *match* between an organism's perception-and-effect apparatus and the environment that match brings forth as world.

Four mappings between Calleja's chapter and the Uexküllian apparatus:

(a) **Imageability ↔ Merkwelt**. Lynch's imageability is the capacity of an environment to yield strong evokable perceptual signs *to the organism that traverses it*. This is precisely the Merkwelt-relation: the environment-features that count as perceivable signs *for this organism* (a player-avatar). The Azeroth/Auraxis contrast becomes, in Uexküllian register, a contrast in *Merkwelt-richness*: Azeroth is designed for high Merkwelt-fidelity to the human-as-avatar; Auraxis is designed for high *geographic*-fidelity to a Euclidean container, which is a different (and weaker) design criterion.

(b) **Cognitive mapping ↔ Functional-circle closure**. Tolman's cognitive map (operationalized through Liboriussen) is built precisely because *action* (effect) in the environment feeds back as *perception*: every move generates new perceptual data that updates the map, which informs the next move. The cumulative process is the Funktionskreis writ across a longer time-base.

(c) **Body-relative orientation ↔ Umwelt organism-relativity**. Tuan 2007's front/back/right/left, with futurity-ahead, is exactly an Umwelt-style organism-centered spatial structure — the kind of orientation-frame an organism *brings to* the environment rather than reads off it.

(d) **Space-to-place transition ↔ Umwelt-consolidation**. Tuan 2007 (p.73)'s familiarization-yields-place describes what is, in Uexküllian terms, the *consolidation of a stable Merkwelt*: the environment's perceptual signs have been learned, the corresponding effect-signs reliably yield the expected feedback, and the Funktionskreis is now closed in a habituated rather than exploratory mode.

**The Layer-C signature claim** is therefore: *Uexküll's Umwelt is arguably more apt than Heideggerian In-der-Welt-sein for designer-constituted bounded environments*. The argument: Heideggerian In-der-Welt-sein presupposes a *shared, public, non-constituted* lifeworld that game-worlds precisely do not have — game-worlds are designed, bounded, and constituted-for-the-player by a designer-as-environment-builder. Uexküll's apparatus, by contrast, *makes designer-constituted boundedness a feature, not a bug*: the game-designer occupies the structural position of the biologist studying a tick's Umwelt — both *observe* and *build* a bounded perceptual+effectual world that an organism inhabits. The fit between designer-intentioned Merkwelt-construction and avatar-organism Merkwelt-inhabitation is the precise structural condition Uexküll's apparatus was built to describe. Heideggerian In-der-Welt-sein, with its public-lifeworld presupposition, has to be *bent* to fit designer-constituted environments; Uexküllian Umwelt fits without bending.

The mapping is flagged *anticipatory-projection (dissertation)*. Calleja cites no Uexküll; the Gibson-as-implicit-Uexküll-bridge framing from earlier plan drafts is dropped per user directive (Gibson is not cited in this book). The bridge stands or falls on direct anticipatory projection from the dissertation side. The chapter's density of phenomenology-without-phenomenologist makes the projection unusually well-supported, but it remains projection, not exegesis.

## 12. Synthetic Summary

Spatial involvement is, in Calleja's hands, the dimension along which the player undergoes a qualitative transition: from perceiving a representation to inhabiting a place. The transition is engineered through cumulative cognitive cartography across nested scales, enabled by imageable design, oriented by the avatar's body-relative front/back/right/left, and varied across a five-fold taxonomy of designer-constituted spatial structures. The chapter does this work using exclusively non-phenomenological theoretical resources — Tuan from humanistic geography, Lynch from urban-design theory, Tolman from behavioral psychology — and the gap between the disciplinary register Calleja deploys (phenomenological) and the citation pool he draws from (geography + urban design + psychology) is what makes IG-05 the dissertation's premier residual-phenomenology anchor site.

The dissertation's three augmentation layers find unusually rich anchoring here. Layer A (exegetical) can substitute or supplement Tuan/Lynch/Tolman with phenomenological apparatus at six identified RPA sites. Layer B (Burke pentadic) finds Scene-as-constraint-on-Act directly typologized by the five spatial structures. Layer C (Uexküllian) finds its signature payoff: the whole-chapter habitational-spatiality machinery maps onto Uexküll's Merkwelt/Wirkwelt/Funktionskreis/Umwelt apparatus with four discrete and individually-defensible bridges, and the meta-claim — that *Umwelt* is more apt than Heideggerian *In-der-Welt-sein* for designer-constituted bounded environments — is the dissertation's most ambitious bid against the canonical phenomenological vocabulary. The Umwelt bridge is anticipatory projection, not exegesis (Calleja cites no Uexküll, no Gibson, no Heidegger), but it is the highest-density anticipatory projection in the entire pipeline because the underlying theoretical structure of the chapter is unusually congenial to the Uexküllian apparatus the dissertation will impose on it.
