## 1. Dimension operationalization

Narrative involvement, in Calleja's Player Involvement Model, denotes the player's engagement with two analytically distinct but practically interwoven kinds of story material: the *scripted narrative* that designers write into the game (cut scenes, quest text, dialog, in-world objects, audio diaries, verbal-text streams) and the *alterbiography* that the player generates through her ongoing interpretation of, and interaction with, the game environment. Indexed to the dual-phase temporal apparatus introduced in Chapter 3, the macro phase of narrative involvement is the off-line, between-sessions anticipation and retrospection of a game's scripted material together with the accumulated alterbiography of a campaign, save file, or character; the micro phase is the moment-by-moment alterbiography-generation in which causally related segments of synthesis — combinations of game-rule outputs, representational layer, and mental imagery — are strung together into an unfolding narrative experience. Calleja states explicitly at book p. 131 that alterbiography occurs in the micro phase, supplying the chapter's micro-phase declaration in lieu of the explicitly labeled "Micro Phase:" subsection found in Chapters 4–6 and 8–9. This structural deviation is one of the chapter's load-bearing surprises and is flagged for Phase 3 ontology-synthesis. The boundary criteria distinguishing narrative involvement from adjacent dimensions are summarized in the JSON deliverable; the principal cross-dimension hazard is the boundary with ludic involvement, where quest objectives that function as reward-progression migrate into ludic territory but the same quest texts return to narrative territory when the player invests in their story content.

## 2. Macro phase: narrative and experience

Calleja opens by characterizing the discipline's two governing questions — Juul's "Do games tell stories?" (2001) and the Aarseth/Eskelinen "Are games narratives?" (2004) — as a pair of misframings that have dominated the field. He refuses both the position that games are non-narrative and the position that attributes every aspect of gaming experience to narrative; the former is described as a normative pre-determination of player experience, the latter as analytically unproductive. The approach he sets out is to focus on how stories are experienced in games while taking the formal apparatus of game narratives seriously without reducing the experiential to the formal.

He then surveys the speech-act strand of narratology represented by Chatman (1978), Genette (1980), and Prince (1982), drawing the speech-act framing from Marie-Laure Ryan's *Avatars of Story* (2006). In this strand, narrative is retelling: the story is told in retrospect, the depicted events are fully determined, and a narrator is required for narrative to exist at all — a position which excludes visual media, drama, and other performance-based forms from the category. Genette is quoted directly on the Saussurian terminology of *story* (signified content), *narrative* (signifier/discourse), and *narrating* (the producing action and its situation). The story/discourse distinction is then anchored across Metz (1974), Genette (1980), Chatman (1978), Prince (1982), and Bal (1997), and balanced against the counter-position represented by Culler (1981) and Walsh (2007) that there is no story outside the discourse that constructs it.

Calleja accepts the analytic value of the story/discourse distinction for literary narrative but argues that it is not productive for game environments. The pivot is Juul's 2001 argument that story-time and discourse-time collapse into a synchronous *now* in games: players' actions are not past or prior but happening, and what comes next is undetermined. Juul reads this collapse as evidence that games are incompatible with narrative — "you cannot have interactivity and narration at the same time" — and infers that games therefore "almost never perform basic narrative operations like flashback and flash-forward." Calleja accepts the temporal observation but refuses the inference. The argument's defect, he holds, is that Juul slides between two dimensions of narrative without distinguishing them: the moment-by-moment story generated by interaction and the pre-scripted story written into the game. Flashbacks are operative in *Max Payne* (Rockstar Toronto, 2001) and *Call of Duty IV* (Infinity Ward, 2007) at the scripted level; player-generated event sequences cannot, by definition, be pre-determined without losing the ergodicity that makes them games. With this, Calleja introduces — already at book p. 115, in italicized definitional prose embedded inside the macro-phase opening — the distinction between *alterbiography* (the story generated by the individual player as she takes action in the game) and *scripted narrative* (the pre-scripted story events written into the game). The chapter's signature contribution is thus stated long before the dedicated *Alterbiography* subsection at p. 124.

The macro-phase opening closes by turning the speech-act narratology critique against Eskelinen (2004): if print narratology, hypertext theory, film studies, and drama studies cannot be applied directly to computer games, then Eskelinen cannot build his own anti-narrative case on Genette- and Prince-derived claims that narratives require narrators. The position is exposed as self-undermining rather than refuted on its own terms. Calleja then borrows Ryan's positive reading: the arguments of Eskelinen and Juul merely imply that games tell stories differently than literature and film, not that they fail to tell stories at all — a redefinition of classical narrative is demanded, not denied.

## 3. Experiential narrative — Pearce, Salen + Zimmerman, Jenkins

The *Experiential Narrative* subsection surveys three positive accounts of narrative-in-games and qualifies each. Celia Pearce (2004) is credited with a useful point about games versus literature and film — that games revolve around play and player experience rather than around story — but her six-operator framework (experiential, performative, augmentary, descriptive, metastory, story system) is judged over-general because Pearce applies it to tic-tac-toe, basketball, Battleship, chess, and *The Sims*. A framework constructively applicable to so wide a spectrum of objects will, Calleja warns, be analytically unproductive when applied to game environments with strong narrative elements.

Salen and Zimmerman's *Rules of Play* (2003) supplies the embedded/emergent narrative distinction that Calleja substantially endorses — particularly because emergent narrative makes the dynamic, participatory, and experiential structures of games the proper site of narrative analysis. The endorsement is then qualified by a worked critique of their poker example: the dramatic tension and bluffing-narrativity Salen and Zimmerman attribute to a poker game illustrate, for Calleja, exactly the over-extension Pearce also exhibits. Calleja contrasts the poker example with an extended *Grand Theft Auto IV* (Rockstar North, 2008) walkthrough across pp. 118–119 in which Nico's date with Michelle, the clothes-store detour, the alleyway encounter, and the unintended punch and pursuit are presented as a properly grounded alterbiography: the narrative is supported by the representational and code-level structure of the environment plus the player's subjective interpretation. The poker example, by contrast, lacks a fictional environment that supports ongoing narrative generation — there are no characters and no inhabited world; players interact only with a stack of cards and a rule system. The constraint Calleja levies is therefore that narrative experience must be grounded in cyber-textual qualities — in the representational and mechanical structure of an environment with characters or world-elements supporting story-generation. Aarseth's *Cybertext* (1997) is invoked as the proper anchor for this constraint: emergent narrative must be tied to the mechanical, coded structures of ergodic texts and not allowed to drift into the experiential-in-general.

Henry Jenkins (2004) is then treated as a third positive account whose attention to spatial structures usefully constrains experiential narrative to specific qualities of game environments. Jenkins's four types — *evocative spaces*, *staging grounds*, *embedded mise-en-scène*, and *resources for emergent narratives* — are accepted as not mutually exclusive. *The Lord of the Rings Online* (Turbine, 2007) is briefly used to instance the evocative type: Tolkien's books and films have already established the world, so the environmental storytelling can proceed by intertextual metonymy. Calleja's reservation, however, is that Jenkins remains spatial in his ambition; a complete account of narrative in games must address dimensions beyond the spatial.

## 4. Scripted narrative and structures of progression

The *Scripted Narrative* subsection defines scripted narrative as the story designers want to impart, ranging from a simple world-introduction-plus-goals to complex multi-character plots. In MMOGs, quest texts couple a ludic goal (what must be done) with a scripted-narrative envelope (the world-context for doing it); players who skim the text engage at the rudimentary level while players who invest read for story. Story-driven games are characterized by coupling progression — both ludic reward and spatial exploration — with scripted-narrative advancement, and Calleja notes the contemporary design discipline of avoiding hard scripted-narrative gating (the cautionary counter-example is *The Secret of Monkey Island* [Lucasfilm Games, 1990], where missing a piece of information could make progress impossible). The subsection closes with the inventory of scripted-narrative delivery channels: cut scenes, quick-time events, in-world objects, dialog, and verbal-text streams. Endnote markers 1 and 2 (resolved against book pp. 201–204) attach to cut scenes and quick-time events respectively.

*Scripted Narrative Structures of Progression* then articulates a four-type typology — a dimension-deployable design variable Calleja effectively introduces here. *Halo 3* (Bungie, 2007) supplies the LINEAR paradigm: checkpoint-gated narrative advance, with cut scenes or character communication advancing the story at predetermined junctures while permitting tactical flexibility within each level. *Fallout 3* (Bethesda Game Studios, 2008) supplies the BRANCHING BACKBONE-WITH-SIDE-QUESTS paradigm: an open environment with side quests whose selection depends on exploration, generating more variable alterbiographies than linear games. *World of Warcraft* (Blizzard Entertainment, 2004) supplies the NO-MAIN-STORY-LINE-WITH-SIDE-QUESTS-ONLY paradigm, where dense background lore plus serial and one-off side quests carry the entire scripted apparatus. The implicit fourth type — open world with main story plus secondary storylines — is articulated in the prose without a single paradigm game (Oblivion approximates it). The typology functions as a dissertation-deployable game-classification axis for emergent-narrative analysis.

## 5. Push and pull narrative — Levine on Bioshock

The *Push and Pull Narrative* subsection takes its analytic vocabulary from Ken Levine's 2008 Games Developers Conference lecture. Push narrative is the captive-audience mode: the cut scene is the quintessential push device, suspending player action while delivering a story segment. Pull narrative is the player-elicited mode: designers embed narrative elements in the world — the tape recorders of *Bioshock* (2K Games, 2008) and the in-world books of *The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion* (Bethesda Softworks, 2006) — and rely on the player to pull the narrative to herself. Levine's recognition that some players will miss pulled narrative is accepted as a feature rather than a defect: those willing to commit to exploration are rewarded with a more powerful engagement.

Push exemplars beyond cut-scene-orthodoxy include *Half-Life* (Valve Software, 2004), where Klevjer (2002) is cited on the well-paced cut scene as a reward for completing challenging quests; and *Grand Theft Auto IV* (Rockstar North, 2008), whose scripted narrative is told primarily through cut scenes. Pull exemplars include Levine's *Bioshock* (the audio vignettes strewn through Rapture, related to the immediate environment) and *Oblivion* (its books found, purchased, or stolen across locations). Calleja then links Levine's pull narrative to Jenkins's embedded-narrative concept and identifies the cognitive correlate that the push/pull distinction implies: pull narrative requires the player to do synthetic work that integrates designer-embedded scripted material into an ongoing experiential whole — and that synthetic work is the alterbiography.

Designer-commentary as theoretical source is a methodological signature of this chapter. Sam Houser on *GTA IV* in the Edge magazine "Making of" article (2008) and Ken Levine on *Bioshock* are not academic citations; Calleja's openness to practicing-designer discourse is consistent with his interview-based qualitative-research method and is worth noting as a feature of the chapter's evidentiary economy.

## 6. Alterbiography in full — introduction (p. 115), dedicated subsection (p. 124), synthesis grounding (pp. 128–130), and intersection with scripted narrative (pp. 131–133)

The alterbiography concept must be page-anchored at four locations. The first is the italicized definitional prose at book p. 115, embedded in the macro-phase opening: the distinction between *alterbiography* (the story generated by the individual player as she takes action in the game) and *scripted narrative* (the pre-scripted story events written into the game) is introduced before any dedicated subsection unpacks it. This is unusual in Calleja's dimension-chapter rhythm and signals the conceptual stakes.

The second location is the dedicated *Alterbiography* subsection at book pp. 124–126. Here alterbiography is defined as "the ongoing narrative generated during interaction with a game environment," characterized as neither solely a formal property of the game nor a property of the player's free-roaming imagination, and specified as a cyclical process afforded by the representational, mechanical, and medium-specific qualities of the game and actuated in the mind of the player. The challenge Calleja gives himself is to account for this form of narrative generation without broadening its scope to game experience in general — the same constraint he applied earlier against Pearce and against Salen and Zimmerman's poker example. Focalization is then introduced via Genette's *Narrative Discourse* (1980) — "restriction of field" — and extended from literary narrators to game environments via player disposition. Three focalization types are articulated: *alterbiography of miniatures* (multi-entity or collective control: *Age of Empires* [Ensemble Studios, 1997], *SimCity* [Maxis Software, 1989], *Medieval II: Total War* [Creative Assembly, 2006] campaign mode, *The Sims* [Maxis Software, 2000]); *alterbiography of entity* (third-person disposition: *Max Payne* [Rockstar Toronto, 2001], *Fahrenheit* [Quantic Dream, 2005]); and *alterbiography of self* (first-person disposition: *Fallout 3* [Bethesda Game Studios, 2008], *Mount and Blade* [TaleWorlds, 2008]). Crucially, Calleja insists that disposition governs focalization, not camera angle alone — a football-manager controlling a whole team may center her alterbiography on the managerial role; a *Medieval II: Total War* player may shift focalization between miniatures, self, and entity within a single session. Figure 7.1 schematizes this typology.

The third location is the *Synthesis* subsection at book pp. 127–131, which supplies alterbiography's cognitive-phenomenological grounding. The extended *Oblivion* opening — Uriel Septim's dungeon escape, the pendant — illustrates the scripted-to-open-world transition that anchors alterbiography in a designer-supplied entry. Two contrasting interview quotations from WoW players (Rheric, who finds the parallel and concurrent storylines stimulating; Inauro, who is frustrated by an inability to affect the framework) demonstrate that alterbiography depends on individual disposition. Calleja then aligns the combinatorial power of alterbiography with Wolfgang Iser's *The Act of Reading* (1991): the reader puts together what the wandering viewpoint has divided up; the syntheses formed are of an unusual kind, neither in the printed text nor solely in the reader's imagination, and the projections of which they consist emerge from the reader while being guided by signals that project themselves into her. The terminology of "narrative props" is borrowed from Kendall Walton (1990). The alterbiography is then characterized as a casual assemblage of segments of syntheses, with the Iser-synthesis being the first building block. The tabletop-RPG worked example (the numeric appearance-4/intelligence-18 character; the striding-out-of-the-tavern declaration generating a succession of images grounded in the rule values and the declarative action) presents a synthesis-in-miniature: causally related segments of interaction with and interpretation of game rules, representation, and mental imagery. Sartre is then quoted directly twice — *What Is Literature?* (1967) on author–reader interdependence and the dialectic of writing and reading; and *The Imaginary* (1995) on synthesizing efforts becoming apparent only when representational qualities are ambiguous. The CRPG example of *Oblivion* (a strength-and-endurance-increasing avatar whose body remains visually unchanged), the *FIFA 2008* (EA Sports, 2007) management-mode example (newspaper clippings, locker-room responses, board-of-directors opinion feeding manager-career alterbiography; Ronaldinho underperformance attributed to in-game entity rather than controlling player), and the *Oblivion* Redguard/Breton racial-snobbery interpretation example (alterbiography shaped by representations not supported by simulation) round out the demonstration that alterbiography is generated through a synthesis of rules, representation, and imagination.

The fourth location is *The Relationship between Scripted Narrative and Alterbiography* at book pp. 131–133. Here the explicit micro-phase declaration appears: "Alterbiography occurs in the micro phase of narrative involvement." This single sentence does the work an explicitly labeled Micro Phase: subsection performs in Chapters 4–6 and 8–9 — a structural deviation from the dimension-chapter template that should be captured in Phase 3 ontology-synthesis. Scripted narrative and alterbiography are then specified as co-constitutive rather than mutually exclusive: alterbiography integrates the scripted-narrative elements relating to its focalized entity. The Bioshock Dr. Steinman example is the paradigmatic worked instance and the chapter's strongest single demonstration: pre-encounter audio recordings by and about Steinman, posters, and portraits prime the player with insight into the demented plastic surgeon's aesthetic ideals; when the player finally enters Steinman's surgery to find him operating on his dead assistant, the scripted narrative is absorbed into the player's alterbiography. Figure 7.3 visualizes the encounter; Figure 7.2 — "Episodes in the life of Muun in *World of Warcraft*" — uses Calleja's own Appendix-case-study WoW character as alterbiography exemplar, supplying the strongest single methodological tie between body chapters and the Appendix "Tale of Two Worlds" case profiles.

## 7. The dissolves-the-binary stance

Calleja's narratology-vs-ludology positioning is more subtle than a neutral "neither pole" reading suggests. He rejects the framing of the debate via Juul + Eskelinen anti-narrative arguments using Ryan's cognitive-narrative reframing; he endorses Salen + Zimmerman and Aarseth on the importance of ergodic mechanical structures; and he extends both via the new scripted/alterbiography distinction. The effect is not mediation between two poles but dissolution of the binary: once narrative is reframed as a cognitive/mental construct that operates differently across media and once the scripted/alterbiography distinction is in place, the question "Are games narratives?" loses its purchase — games are narrative environments in two distinguishable, jointly operative modes, and the relevant theoretical work is to specify those modes rather than to adjudicate their narrative status. This dissolution-rather-than-mediation positioning is the chapter's meta-stance and one of the cleanest substantive contributions to the 2008–2012 ludology-vs-narratology reconciliation period.

Notable absences confirm the positioning. Janet Murray is absent from Chapter 7 despite her Tier-A status in the volume — her *Hamlet on the Holodeck* operates as the agency-theorist (Ch. 4) and immersion-theorist (Ch. 2) text in Calleja's reading rather than as the narrative-theorist text. Gonzalo Frasca is absent throughout — Calleja approaches the debate via Juul, Aarseth, and Eskelinen directly, bypassing Frasca's foundational ludology essays.

## 8. Sartre and Iser as mediated-exegetical Phantasia bridge

The chapter's most analytically valuable surprise, from the cross-pipeline perspective, is the irruption of reader-response and phenomenology-of-imagination theory in the Synthesis subsection. Iser's *The Act of Reading* is quoted directly on reader-synthesis as dual-nature projection; Sartre's *What Is Literature?* (1967) is quoted directly on author/reader interdependence; Sartre's *The Imaginary* (1995) is invoked on ambiguity-revealed synthesis. These are not anticipatory bridges — they are direct, explicit citations in Calleja's own text. Together they make Chapter 7 the one PIM dimension chapter with a non-zero exegetical anchor for any cross-pipeline bridge.

The bridge is to the Phantasia Cluster (Hawhee on rhetorical vision; Papachristou on deliberative phantasia; et al.). Its provenance is not the default `anticipatory-projection (dissertation)` flag that applies to Layer A (Aristotle/Heidegger), Layer B (Burke), and Layer C (Uexküll) augmentation edges throughout the rest of the chapter. Instead the provenance is `mediated-exegetical (Sartre/Iser → Phantasia Cluster)`: exegetically anchored in Calleja's own text via the direct Sartre and Iser citations, then mediated to the Phantasia Cluster through the classical phenomenology-of-imagination → rhetorical phantasia lineage. The structure parallels the deprecated Gibson → Uexküll mediated chain that the Phase 0 cite-check falsified — except that this one survives the cite-check. Sartre and Iser are *in* Calleja's text; Gibson and Uexküll are not.

The scope of the mediated-exegetical provenance is strictly limited. Sartre and Iser do NOT anchor Layer A: alterbiography's bridge to Aristotelian muthos and to Heidegger BCAP's time-phenomenology remains wholly anticipatory. Sartre and Iser do NOT anchor Layer B: the Burkean Act/Purpose mapping remains wholly anticipatory. Sartre and Iser do NOT anchor Layer C: the Uexküllian *Bedeutungsträger*-sequence mapping remains wholly anticipatory. Only the bridge to the Phantasia Cluster carries the mediated-exegetical provenance, and it is documented as such in the edges CSV at E39–E42. For Phase 3 Manual Section E this is the dissertation's strongest non-anticipatory leverage point in the Calleja apparatus and should be treated as an exception to the otherwise uniform anticipatory-projection provenance across the six dimensions.

## 9. Anticipatory mappings — Burke, Uexküll, Aristotle

For Layer B (Burke pentad), the chapter's strongest pentad-mapping is *Act + Purpose*. Alterbiography is the unfolding Act-stream — what is done — and scripted narrative supplies Purpose — the designer-imposed teleology, the main quest, the narrative goal. The Push and Pull Narrative subsection dramatizes the Act/Purpose ratio: pushed narrative is Purpose-saturated and Act-constraining (cut scenes suspend player Act in order to deliver Purpose); pulled narrative releases Purpose to Player-Act (the player elicits the Purpose through her own exploration). The Bioshock Steinman intersection is a Burkean Act/Purpose-ratio paradigm: pre-encounter audio-diary Purpose-priming is reconfigured as Player-Act content when assimilated to alterbiography. Burke is wholly absent from Calleja's text — Phase 0 cite-check returned zero hits — so all Burke-mapping edges carry `provenance=anticipatory-projection (dissertation)`.

For Layer C (Uexküll), the strongest construct-mapping is the *Bedeutungsträger (meaning-carrier) sequence within Umwelt*. Alterbiography's central operation — stringing together causally related segments of synthesis of interaction with and interpretation of game-elements — structurally parallels Uexküll's sequence of biosemiotically active meaning-bearers within an Umwelt. NPCs, quest items, audio diaries, environmental details function as successive Bedeutungsträger. Pull narrative maps especially well: player-pulled-Bedeutungsträger are biosemiotically active; pushed cut scenes function as designer-imposed non-Bedeutungsträger interruptions of the player's Umwelt. Uexküll is wholly absent from Calleja's text — Phase 0 cite-check returned zero hits — so all Uexküll-mapping edges carry the anticipatory-projection provenance. The Phase 0 cite-check also falsified the Gibson-as-mediated-Uexküll-bridge hypothesis from plan v3 §5: Calleja uses "affordance" generically eleven times without Gibson attribution, and Gibson does not appear in either the references or the index. Layer C is therefore singly-anchored anticipatory projection.

For Layer A (Aristotelian / Heideggerian grounding), the strongest single-concept bridge in the chapter is to *muthos / emplotment* via Aristotle's *Poetics* chapters 6–10. Alterbiography is functionally Aristotelian muthos in everything but vocabulary — synthesis of incidents (*kineseis*) into a unified action with beginning, middle, and end, now distributed across game sessions and shaped by player disposition. The Synthesis subsection (pp. 128–130) makes this explicit at the cognitive level: the tabletop-RPG worked example presents muthos in miniature; the rules-representation-imagination synthesis Calleja specifies is emplotment in everything but the term. Secondary anchor: Heidegger BCAP on time-phenomenology and kairotic event-structuring parallels macro-phase off-line anticipation of scripted-narrative continuation and micro-phase moment-by-moment alterbiography generation. Calleja cites zero Aristotle and only one Heidegger (Question Concerning Technology at p. 21, not in Ch. 7); both Layer A mappings carry the anticipatory-projection provenance.

The Uexküll bridge (push/pull as Bedeutungsträger / non-Bedeutungsträger interruption, pp. 122–124, plus alterbiography as Bedeutungsträger-sequence across pp. 124–131) is anticipatory and singly-anchored after Phase 0's Gibson-bridge falsification. The Burke bridge (Act + Purpose ratio, pp. 122–124 + pp. 132–133) is anticipatory. The Dalton-dissertation bridge runs throughout: alterbiography is a directly measurable design variable for emergent-narrative analysis, and the four-type scripted-narrative-structures-of-progression typology supplies a dissertation-deployable game-classification axis.

## 11. Summary

Chapter 7 is the dimension chapter that most directly dramatizes the central methodological tension of Calleja's whole project — the need to capture experiential phenomena without dissolving them into experience-in-general. It does so by introducing the chapter's signature term (alterbiography) into the macro-phase opening, by sharply distinguishing alterbiography from scripted narrative while insisting on their co-constitutive operation, by extending Genette's focalization to game environments via player disposition, by importing reader-response phenomenology to ground the synthesis-of-rules-representation-imagination operation, and by dissolving rather than mediating the narratology-vs-ludology binary. It is also the one PIM dimension chapter where a cross-pipeline bridge can be flagged `mediated-exegetical` rather than `anticipatory-projection` — the Phantasia Cluster bridge via the direct Sartre and Iser citations in the Synthesis subsection. For the dissertation's PIM Operationalization Manual Section E, this status is the strongest non-anticipatory leverage point in the entire Calleja apparatus, and Phase 3 should treat it accordingly.
