# IG-01 Deep Analysis — "Games beyond Games" (Calleja 2011, book pp. 7–16)

**Phase 2 paraphrastic narrative. Content filter: no ≥25-word verbatim from Calleja. All page anchors refer to book pages (= PDF pages; offset = 0).**

## 1. The three traditional axes and the volume's experiential self-positioning

Calleja opens the chapter by mapping the analytic terrain of game research onto three principal axes, broadly construed: (i) the formal axis, which treats games as media objects and asks after their internal architecture; (ii) the experiential axis, which attends to the subjective texture of gameplay and the player's involvement; and (iii) the sociocultural axis, which examines gaming communities and the social practices surrounding play (p.7). The axes are not insulated: research that privileges one will draw on insights from the others, often dictated by the researcher's disciplinary background. Calleja then commits the volume to the experiential axis — but with an immediate and important qualification. The other two axes will continue to inform the analysis, since the formal and higher-level social issues surrounding games frequently illuminate the experiential ones (p.7).

This positioning is more consequential than its placement in the chapter's opening paragraph might suggest. It is the move that authorizes the Player Involvement Model of IG-03 and the incorporation thesis of IG-10. Equally important, it is the move that explains why a chapter ostensibly devoted to a methodological-definitional preliminary nevertheless spends the bulk of its pages on what looks like a structural/formal decomposition — the five-element descriptive framework. The framework is offered as a service to the experiential analysis to follow: by clarifying what is being referred to in the rest of the book when central terms like *players*, *rules*, and *environmental properties* are deployed, it equips the experiential analysis with determinate referents (p.7). This tension between announced experiential primacy and the actual object-side density of Chapter 1's argument is registered below as Tension T02.

## 2. The complexity of games and the dual-domain performance claim

Calleja's substantive opening claim is that games elude holistic categorization. Attempts to formulate stable, universal definitions of games inevitably fall short — but the omissions are themselves instructive, reminding us that games are multi-perspective objects (p.7). Games reflect aspects of the society and culture that made them while contributing to that society in the process, so understanding them is a recursive exploration into collective knowledge and social practices (p.8). This auxiliary remark licences the formal+sociocultural axes' continued analytical role even within the experiential commitment.

The more theoretically loaded claim in this subsection is the dual-domain performance thesis: the performance of a game inhabits two often-simultaneous domains — the player's subjective experience, and the visible practice of playing (pp.7–8). Calleja invokes a deliberately heterogeneous range of bodily actions to demonstrate the range of the visible domain: moving a piece on a board, pressing a button sequence on a controller, sprinting toward a goal line. He then issues the load-bearing ontological claim: a game becomes a game only when it is played — until then it is only a rule-set + props awaiting human engagement (pp.7–8). Human subjectivity, accordingly, sits at the very heart of the analytic difficulties game theorists face.

The dual-domain claim is the seed of an unmistakable phenomenological resonance that pervades Chapter 1 without ever being phenomenologically grounded. The subjective-experience / visible-practice pair is structurally cognate with the Aristotelian *aisthesis* / *praxis* duality and with the Heideggerian *Befindlichkeit* / *Rede* pair (mood-attunement and articulation). It will return at IG-03 as the macro/micro temporal-phase distinction, where the visible-practice domain unfolds in the macro phase (off-line involvement, between-session reflection, campaign planning) and the subjective-experience domain in the micro phase (*in ludo res*, moment-to-moment attunement). Calleja, however, names no phenomenologist in IG-01. This is the first instance of the **residual-phenomenology-without-phenomenologist-cite** pattern flagged in the cross-pipeline residual-phenomenology audit (Tension T03 below, propagated from IG-03 and IG-10).

To dramatize the irreducible heterogeneity of activities that 'game' subsumes, Calleja names poker, fencing, and *Grand Theft Auto IV* (Rockstar North, 2008) as falling under the general heading of games while entailing radically different forms of engagement (p.8). Digital games are then singled out as a special source of complexity, and *GTA IV* is offered as the paradigm case: it may be more accurate to describe it as a virtual environment with several embedded games + a linear storyline, in which players progress through gamelike activities — but may also opt to interact in ways that are not gamelike at all (a scenic drive, a walk with friends). Not all interactions with the objects we call games yield gamelike activities (p.8). This claim does double duty: it motivates the family-resemblance methodology that follows, and it foreshadows the digital-games-as-hybrids synthesis at the end of the chapter.

## 3. Games as families: family-resemblance and verbal disagreement

Calleja's first canonical methodological move is to adopt Wittgenstein's (1997) proposal that games be viewed not as a rigidly defined set but as a family whose members share overlapping resemblances (p.8). The strength of the family-resemblance conception is that it dispenses with any single check-list of characteristics whose satisfaction is necessary-and-sufficient for game-membership; instead, it generates a collective concept from the overlaps among various family members. Analytic boundaries may still be drawn for particular purposes, but they are admitted as artificial; when a subset of the family is characterized, those characteristics need not identify games as a whole (pp.8–9).

The motivation for this commitment is not only philosophical but procedural. Calleja imports Arne Naess's (2005, p.64) construct of **verbal disagreement** to name a methodological hazard: disputes in which disagreement turns not on the actual claims being made but on the exemplars to which those claims are being applied by different parties (p.9). When one analyst is critiquing immersion via *Tetris* (Pajitnov, 1985) as the working exemplar and another is responding with *Half-Life 2* (Valve Software, 2004) in mind, the conversation cannot move forward, because the forms of engagement afforded by the two games are radically different (p.9). The family-resemblance commitment is therefore — as the chapter's later trajectory makes explicit — a prophylactic against the verbal-disagreement pathology that recurs through the immersion debates of Ch.2 and the lusory-attitude debates of Ch.3.

The chapter's residual task is now sharply specified: deliver a descriptive framework that identifies the matrix of components combining to form games + virtual environments — without re-essentializing what family-resemblance has just refused (pp.9–10). This formulation generates the methodological tension that the chapter never fully discharges (Tension T01 below): how a descriptive framework can identify constitutive components without functioning, in practice, as a stealth-essentialism.

## 4. Process versus object: the actualization claim

The next conceptual move is the game-as-object vs. game-as-process distinction (p.9). *Settlers of Catan* (Teuber, 1995) is the paradigm. As object, *Settlers* is a set of material pieces + cards + rules; as process, it is the activity afforded by those objects + rules, indexed to a group of players in their sociocultural context. The two sides can be discussed in isolation: one can comment on the visual qualities of the hexagonal board pieces, on the colour scheme of the deck, on the value of the robber as a game mechanic, on the genealogical influence of *Settlers* on later board-game design (pp.9–10).

The distinction carries over to digital games with two adjustments: rules are coded into software rather than upheld by players, and the material substrate is hardware + code rather than tangible pieces. But the asymmetry between the dormant rule-set and its actualization-through-interaction is preserved (p.10). Calleja states the principle plainly: the dormant code, board pieces, or rule set present a potential that is actualized during gameplay (p.10). This is the chapter's most theoretically loaded sentence, and — to anyone working at the Aristotelian / Heideggerian end of the dissertation's anchoring corpus — it is a transparent *dunamis/energeia* (potency/actuality) claim made without the Aristotelian vocabulary. Heidegger's *Vollzug* (enactment) would name the actualization side equally well. The phenomenological resonance is not incidental: it is structurally constitutive of the move. Yet Calleja cites neither Aristotle nor Heidegger. The residual-phenomenology audit flag fires here.

From the actualization principle Calleja then derives an endorsement of the processual approach championed by T. L. Taylor (2006) and Thomas Malaby (2007) (pp.10–11). Malaby is quoted directly on each-game-as-ongoing-process — the game's enactment always contains the potential to generate new practices and meanings, possibly refiguring the game itself. This processuality entails continuity with other domains of experience: games need not be conceptualized as somehow experientially separate. With that, Calleja registers the volume's prospective position against the "lusory attitude" (Suits 1978, p.55) as taken up by Salen and Zimmerman (2003) and Juul (2005) — a target he tags for full critical treatment in Chapter 3 (p.11). Malaby's positive formulation supplies the contrast: games are processes that create carefully designed, unpredictable circumstances bearing meaningful, culturally shared, yet open-ended interpretations (p.11). Both the game-practice and the meaning it generates are subject to change.

## 5. The five-element analytic framework

The chapter's load-bearing analytic instrument is then introduced under the header **Digital Game Elements** (p.11). The framing claim is the volume's first explicit position-edge against named Tier-A interlocutors: rather than adhering to an essentialist definition of games like those forwarded by Juul (2005) or Salen+Zimmerman (2003), the book follows Wittgenstein in viewing games as members of an extended family that share resemblances (p.11). The framework's role is therefore explicitly NOT to supply necessary-and-sufficient conditions for game-membership; it is to identify a matrix of components whose configurations vary across the family.

The five elements are: (1) **player** — the human agent(s) engaging with the game system; (2) **representational sign** — the interpretable, signifying entity (text, image, sound) that players read; (3) **coded rules** — the rule-system, whether coded into software or socially enforced; (4) **(simulated) environmental properties** — the modeled physics of the constructed environment; (5) **material medium** — the hardware substrate that instantiates the rest (pp.11–14). Two of the elements (coded rules + simulated environmental properties) are clustered in Calleja's header description at p.11 under "structural properties of game and environment" and broken out into their own subsections at pp.12 and 13.

### 5.1 The player

Calleja's stipulation on the player element is careful. *Player* designates the human agent engaging with the game system; the term is used in conformity with game-studies convention, but explicitly not in a way that prescribes any particular experiential disposition (such as "playfulness," however that might be conceptualized) (pp.11–12). The object/process distinction returns at the player-element level: from the object-side, the player is the ideal or implied player; from the process-side, the player is the actual active player + the set of practices she deploys in interacting with the game-world and game-system, always considered relative to the social and cultural contexts of the player, which have a formative role in disposition prior to and during engagement (p.11).

The processual corollary that follows is essential: different players engaging the same structure, signs, and medium can experience a different game (p.12). Calleja's *Call of Duty IV* (Infinity Ward, 2007) death-match example demonstrates: while one player follows the conventional team-kill rules, a teammate may pursue an idiosyncratic suicide-jump-off-the-highest-building goal — both interact with the same game-object, but the resultant game-process is divergent enough to count as a different game.

### 5.2 The representational sign

The representational-sign element covers the broad category of signifying entity — alphanumeric text, imagery, sound — i.e. the interpretable surface elements that players read to interact with the game (p.12). In digital games the representational sign and the behaviour-generating code may both be "code," but they perform functionally distinct roles and warrant analytic separation. The point is preparatory for the next subsection.

### 5.3 Coded rules

Calleja imports Aarseth's (1997) *Cybertext* distinction here. In ergodic media the function of the "surface sign" bears a nontrivial relation to the mechanical operations of the internal code that generates it: surface and code are mutually intrinsic (p.12). The contrast is with trivial cases (a literary text read aloud is an audible derivative of the printed book): one level of manifestation derives from the other (p.12). The nontrivial code-to-surface relation is what makes the cybernetic sign different in kind from its print predecessors.

Rules are then characterized as a common denominator in all members of the game family, though their shape varies. In analog games, rules are stipulated by the game but their enforcement is socially dependent — generating situations in which rules are negotiated and altered by players (p.13). In digital games, rules are coded into software and thus harder to modify. Multiplayer digital games typically display a coexistence of coded + socially-negotiated rules: a *Counter-Strike: Source* (Valve Software, 2004) clan may use the standard coded ruleset but locally ban sniper rifles on its match servers, with violations producing warnings and eventual server-bans (p.13). Negotiation persists in environments with coded rules — typically as community-developed conventional supplements to the coded layer.

### 5.4 (Simulated) environmental properties

Simulated environmental properties obtain whenever a game takes place in a constructed environment that models physical properties (p.13). The model is not restricted to computer-generated environments — pen-and-paper RPGs simulate physics through their game systems (Calleja's tabletop-RPG example specifies 3D6 damage from a three-meter fall, giving the world a physical structure that adds body to the shared mental image). In computer-generated environments the physics is hard-coded: *Call of Duty IV* brick density resists 9mm rounds but is penetrated by 5.56mm M4A1 rounds; avatars run, walk, crawl at defined speeds (p.13). The digital/analog contrast: in digital, mechanics are upheld by the computer; in analog, by the players under a prescribed (often modified) system. Complexity ranges from minimal abstraction (*Pong*) to fuller-but-always-partial simulation (*Crysis*, *Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 2*) (p.14).

### 5.5 Material medium

The material instantiation shapes form + experience even with rule-content held constant (p.14). PS3-controller vs. PC-mouse inflects an RTS title; the Nintendo DS's pocket-portability + infrared linkability supports social contexts home PCs do not. The contrast is starkest with board-games-in-code: digital *Settlers of Catan* preserves the rules but loses tangible board, held cards, manipulable pieces — yielding a markedly different incarnation. Calleja deflects whether to call it "a different game altogether" in favour of the prior question: does the analytic instrument register the difference (p.14)? The redirection is a methodological corollary of the family-resemblance commitment.

## 6. Digital games as hybrids and the unit-of-analysis declaration

The chapter's synthesis arrives at **Digital Games as Hybrids** (p.14). The complexity introduced by digital gaming is still growing, and its most significant source is that a considerable proportion of what are called games today are in fact extended virtual environments containing one or several games embedded within them (pp.14–15). If the five-element framework is applied to *Grand Theft Auto IV* (Rockstar North, 2008), one sees multiple clusters of game-rules co-present within one overall environment: the material medium, signs, and environmental properties remain constant across the embedded games, but the conventional + coded rules and player-aspects vary across the embedded games (p.15). Each embedded game has its own coded rules and — in multiplayer cases — its own conventional rules.

In Wittgensteinian terms, *GTA IV*, *World of Warcraft* (Blizzard Entertainment, 2004), and *Half-Life 2* (Valve Software, 2004) belong to two families that mingle resemblances (p.15). They share features of both virtual environments and games, and thus form a subfamily derived from both. Calleja invokes Ryan (2006) as a corroborating witness: digital games (and tabletop role-playing games before them) have enabled the combination of traditional games' rule-systems with the fictional + narrative resources of the predecessor media (p.15).

The chapter's terminal declaration is the volume's unit-of-analysis fixing: the rest of the book will refer to those members of the game family that are set in virtual environments as **virtual game environments** (or **game environments** for short) — the intersection of the two broad families of virtual environments and games (pp.15–16). Digitized card games (hearts, poker), and digitized puzzle games (crosswords, sudoku), are not virtual game environments under this definition and are accordingly excluded from substantial treatment in the rest of the book. The focus is fixed on games that present the player with a virtual world for participation: *Half-Life 2* and *World of Warcraft* are named as paradigm cases (p.16).

## 7. Cross-pipeline bridges (Layer A / B / C) and propagated findings

Anticipatory bridges to the dissertation's three augmentation layers attach to Chapter 1's vocabulary. All are flagged uniformly as *anticipatory-projection (dissertation)* — none is Calleja's claim.

**Layer C (Uexküll).** Two anchors. First, the unit-of-analysis declaration — virtual game environments as the bounded intersection of game-family + virtual-environment-family — is a candidate target for *Umwelt*-grounding: every virtual game environment is a designer-constituted Umwelt with bounded perception-action affordances. Uexküllian *Umwelt* is arguably more apt than Heideggerian *In-der-Welt-sein* for the bounded, designer-fabricated nature of these environments. Second, the five-element framework admits a *Funktionskreis* re-reading: PLAYER as subject-pole; REPRESENTATIONAL SIGN bearing Merkmal-function; CODED RULES + SIMULATED ENVIRONMENTAL PROPERTIES jointly constituting the Wirkmal-system; MATERIAL MEDIUM as Trägerorganismus-analog. (Phase 0 cite-check: Calleja does not cite Gibson; Uexküll-via-Gibson bridge runs single-anchored.)

**Layer B (Burke).** Burke's pentad parallels the five-element framework structurally without parallel-by-content: PLAYER ↔ Agent; REPRESENTATIONAL SIGN ↔ Agency (partial); CODED RULES ↔ Agency (rule-as-instrument) + Purpose (rule-as-end); SIMULATED ENVIRONMENTAL PROPERTIES ↔ Scene; MATERIAL MEDIUM ↔ Scene (substrate-level). Both decompositions refuse essentialist reduction and distribute analytic load across irreducible terms whose ratios (Burke) or configurations (Calleja) vary. Burke is wholly absent from Calleja per Phase 0; mapping is creative-anticipatory.

**Layer A (Aristotle + Heidegger).** Highest-leverage anticipatory bridge in the chapter. The actualization principle at p.10 is a textbook *dunamis/energeia* move without Aristotelian vocabulary; the Malaby-quoted process-ontology at p.11 reads equally through Heideggerian *Vollzug*. Phantasia Cluster (Aristotle *De Anima* III.3) and BCAP analyses of being-in-act vs. mere-potency are the candidate grounding targets. This anchor sets up the line that will return in IG-05 (phantasia spatial-imagery re-actualization between sessions) and IG-10 (incorporation as full energeia-of-virtual-game-environment-inhabitation).

**Methodological signature, propagated from IG-03 + IG-10.** Family-resemblance is iteratively applied across the volume: at IG-01 on the game-family / virtual-environment-family intersection; at IG-03 on the PIM-dimensions as overlapping non-essential axes; at IG-10 on incorporation-as-family-resemblance-of-experiential-modes. Family-resemblance is the **volume's methodological signature**, and Chapter 1 is its constitutive origin point.

## 8. Position-against-essentialism: the volume's first explicit Tier-A POSITION-edges

The chapter's position-edge against essentialism is doubly directed in a single paragraph (p.11): Juul, *Half-Real* (2005); and Salen+Zimmerman, *Rules of Play* (2003). The stated alternative is Wittgensteinian family-resemblance. The same paragraph rolls into the unit-of-analysis fixing.

A prospective position-edge fires later against Suits (1978, p.55) on the **lusory attitude** as taken up by Salen+Zimmerman and Juul — but Calleja defers full critique to Ch.3 (p.11). The IG-01 output therefore registers C07 as Tier-3 / prospective only; the substantive critique is delivered by IG-03 as C-LA-EXC at pp.49–50. The pairing — anti-Juul + anti-Salen+Zimmerman + prospective-anti-Suits — clusters the essentialist game-definition project as the foil-set against which the volume's family-resemblance + experiential + processual + incorporation commitments will be elaborated.

## 9. Residual-phenomenology-without-phenomenologist-cite flags (Tension T03)

The flag fires three times in Chapter 1, per the propagated audit from IG-03 + IG-10. **(a)** Dual-domain performance (pp.7–8): subjective-experience / visible-practice is structurally Aristotelian (*aisthesis* / *praxis*) and Heideggerian (*Befindlichkeit* / *Rede*); Wittgenstein cited methodologically only. **(b)** Actualization principle (p.10): dormant-potential-actualized-through-gameplay is *dunamis* / *energeia* without Aristotelian vocabulary; *Vollzug* would name the actualization side equally well. **(c)** Processual approach (pp.10–11): Malaby's process-ontology parallels Heideggerian *Vollzug*-ontology + BCAP analyses of being-in-act.

The propagated pattern: Calleja's argument operates with strong phenomenological resonance while withholding phenomenological citation. (Heidegger appears only once in the whole volume — Ch.2 p.21, on tool-disturbance.) The dissertation's Layer-A anticipatory grounding is therefore creative-augmentation rather than exegetical-recovery.

## 10. Summary: what IG-01 contributes

Chapter 1's three load-bearing contributions are: (i) **methodological commitment** — Wittgensteinian family-resemblance + refusal of essentialism, motivated by Naess's verbal-disagreement hazard, iteratively applied at IG-03 and IG-10; (ii) **descriptive framework** — the five-element matrix as the analytic vocabulary against which all subsequent chapters operate; (iii) **unit-of-analysis declaration** — virtual game environments as the volume's analytical scope, with paradigm cases *Half-Life 2* and *World of Warcraft*. Chapter 1's subsection structure is preparatory-definitional rather than macro/micro-architecturally PIM-shaped (Chs.4–9 open with "Macro Phase: <subtitle>" headers IG-01 lacks). Its function is to clear the field of essentialist definitions, fix a non-essentialist descriptive vocabulary, and set the volume's analytic scope so that IG-02's immersion-critique and IG-03's PIM construction proceed without verbal-disagreement cross-talk. Read against the residual-phenomenology audit, IG-01 is the chapter where the volume's Aristotelian/Heideggerian undertow is most visible — and most consequentially uncited.
