## Discussion: the interlocutor profile

### The five-author backbone

If one reads only the index page-counts and ignores everything else, five authors immediately announce themselves as the spine of *In-Game*: **Aarseth (7 index loci), Juul (10), Salen+Zimmerman (18), Murray (5), and Ryan (7)**. These are the canonical 2005–2010 game-studies references, and Calleja's posture toward each is calibrated. With Aarseth he *extends* — taking ergodicity and quest-theory as productive openings; with Juul he *qualifies* — accepting Half-Real's hybridity without endorsing the strong-magic-circle reading; with Salen+Zimmerman he *qualifies* — gratefully using "immersive fallacy" while pushing back against its terminological consequences; with Murray he *qualifies* — accepting the agency/immersion/transformation triad but unwilling to keep "immersion" as the umbrella; with Ryan he *extends* — Ryan supplies the mediated-exegetical bridge to Iser and Sartre that Chapter 7 needs in order to invoke phenomenological imagination without having to read phenomenology directly.

### The methodological lineage Calleja explicitly names

Chapter 3 (`IG-03-MODEL`) does something rare for a game-studies monograph of this period: it *explicitly* names the qualitative-virtual-worlds precedents Calleja considers himself heir to. The named line is **T. L. Taylor (2006), Steinkuehler (2005), Klastrup (2004)** — three women working in MMO/virtual-worlds ethnography. All three reappear later: Taylor in Ch.6 + Ch.9, Steinkuehler in Ch.6 (with Williams, on third-places) and Ch.9, Klastrup in Ch.5 (spatial poetics) and Ch.6. This is the most precisely-articulated methodological filiation in the book, and the fact that all three are placed in Tier-A reflects how seriously Calleja takes them as more than citations: they are *exemplars* for how to combine in-depth interview data with theoretical synthesis on the experience of virtual-worlds.

### The presence-theory foils

Chapter 2 is densely cited and operates almost entirely as a critical literature review. The foils here are presence-theorists Calleja proposes to *contest* in the strict sense: Slater, Lombard+Ditton, Witmer+Singer. These three (with the secondary support of Schubert+Crusius, Held+Durlach, Sutherland) supply the immersion-as-presence apparatus that Calleja's incorporation thesis is designed to *displace*. Calleja's stance here is not dismissal but critical incorporation: he wants the precision of presence-theory and rejects its impoverished ontology.

### The narratological apparatus (Ch.7)

Chapter 7 contains the densest narratology engagement, but with a striking feature: most of the classical narratologists are *cited rather than engaged*. Chatman, Prince, Bal, Walsh, Culler all appear as Tier-C single-locus citations. The substantive narratological work is done through Ryan (Tier-A) — who functions as Calleja's mediated route to Iser (Tier-B), Sartre's *Psychology of Imagination* (Tier-B), and Walton on make-believe (Tier-C). The Juul-vs-Aarseth narratology debate is named but explicitly *not* adjudicated. Genette gets Tier-A treatment not for narratological substance but because Calleja invokes Genette's *Narrative Discourse Revisited* as a model-revision precedent in the Conclusion (Ch.11) — a meta-citation, not a substantive one.

### Chapter 10's atypical anchoring

The most consequential atypical feature of the interlocutor map is in Chapter 10, "From Immersion to Incorporation." Here the synthesis is anchored not in phenomenology proper but in **Lakoff+Johnson** and **Damasio** — two interlocutors who appear nowhere else in the book and whose single-unit presence belies their decisive role. This is the structural signature of the book's intellectual horizon: when Calleja needs an experientialist-embodied-cognition framework to ground "incorporation," he reaches not for Merleau-Ponty (zero citations), not for Husserl (zero), not for Schutz (zero), not for the *Sein und Zeit*-era Heidegger (zero — the only Heidegger reference is the 1977/1993 *Question Concerning Technology* in Ch.2 at p.21, deployed for "interpretive comportment" only), but for the cognitive-linguistics + neuroscience pair. This is decisively important for the dissertation's anticipatory-augmentation work: Calleja's "incorporation" is a *cognitive-experientialist* concept, not a phenomenological one in the classical sense. Any Layer A (Heidegger BCAP) or Layer C (Uexküll) augmentation must be transparent about this — Calleja did not perform the phenomenological move; the dissertation does, post hoc.

### Conspicuous absences

The Phase 0 cite-check confirms an extensive list of *expected-but-absent* interlocutors. Some are surprising; some are deeply consequential.

**Most surprising**: **Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (flow theory)** does not appear anywhere in the volume. For a book whose Chapter 8 ("Affective Involvement") engages emotion-in-gameplay, the absence of flow-theory is striking. Calleja substitutes Grodal's "pleasures of control" + Bryant+Davies's selective-exposure media psychology, neither of which is a flow-equivalent. This produces what is widely felt to be the weakest dimension chapter (it is also the shortest at 12 pages).

**Consequential for the dissertation augmentation layers**:

- **Aristotle, Kenneth Burke**: zero in-text mentions, zero references. Dissertation Layer A (Aristotle/Heidegger BCAP/Bowin AMT) and Layer B (Burke pentad + consubstantiation) are *wholly anticipatory*. There is no Aristotelian or Burkean vocabulary in Calleja anywhere.
- **J. J. Gibson** (name absent despite 11 generic "affordance" uses): the *plan v3* hypothesis that Calleja's affordance-talk would furnish a Gibson-via-affordance bridge to Uexküll is **falsified**. Calleja's affordances are pre-theoretic.
- **Jakob von Uexküll**: zero. Layer C is singly-anchored anticipatory, not doubly-anchored as plan v3 hoped.
- **Merleau-Ponty, Husserl, Schutz, *Sein und Zeit*-Heidegger**: zero phenomenology proper. The "incorporation" thesis is built on second-order cognitive science (Lakoff+Johnson + Damasio), not on first-order phenomenology.

**Surprising-but-period-consistent**:

- **Frank Biocca** (presence-theory pioneer): zero — surprising given Ch.2's saturation with presence theorists.
- **Ragnhild Tronstad**: zero — surprising given her contemporary game-studies relevance.
- **Ian Bogost, Gonzalo Frasca, Miguel Sicart**: zero — surprising for 2011 ludology coverage.
- **Tom Boellstorff** (*Coming of Age in Second Life*, 2008): zero — surprising given Calleja's MMO focus.
- **Erving Goffman**: zero direct — surfaces only through Rettie 2004's title; Pargman+Jakobsson's "frame analysis" use in Ch.9 does not cite Goffman directly.
- **Celia Pearce on *Communities of Play* (2009)**: only the 2004 *First Person* chapter is cited. Her ethnography of post-Uru diaspora is absent.

### What the interlocutor profile tells us about the book's positioning

*In-Game* is **game-studies-internal first, qualitative-empirical second, theoretically eclectic third**. The argumentative core is built from inside game studies, validated against qualitative virtual-worlds precedents (Taylor / Steinkuehler / Klastrup), and theoretically scaffolded by a small set of cherry-picked extra-disciplinary anchors (Lakoff+Johnson + Damasio for Ch.10; Caillois + Suits + Huizinga + Wittgenstein + Malaby for Ch.1, Ch.9; Tuan + Lynch for Ch.5; Ryan + Sartre + Iser for Ch.7; Grodal + Bryant+Davies for Ch.8). The book *does not engage phenomenology directly*. The book *does not engage rhetoric or classical philosophy at all*. The book *does not engage ecological psychology* (no Gibson by name).

This profile is **the constraint within which all dissertation augmentation must operate**. Every Layer A / B / C citation that engages Aristotle, Heidegger (beyond Question Concerning Technology), Burke, Uexküll, Gibson, or Merleau-Ponty is *anticipatory-projection from the dissertation*, never an exegetical commentary on Calleja's actual text.
