## 1. Dimension operationalization

Ludic involvement, in Calleja's six-dimensional Player Involvement Model, names the player's engagement with the rule system of the game through goal-pursuit and the formulation, execution, and revision of plans toward those goals — together with the receipt of associated rewards. The dimension is split, like every other dimension in the volume, across macro-phase and micro-phase. In the **macro phase**, ludic involvement is motivation: the off-line draw of the game's goals and rewards on the player's attention and time. In the **micro phase**, ludic involvement is the moment-to-moment decision-making and plan-formulation by which players work their way through a hierarchy of goals — goals set by the system, by the players themselves, or by a community of co-players. Calleja states the orientation explicitly at p. 149: in the macro phase, the dimension "revolves around the motivation for game engagement provided by the pursuit of goals and the attainment of associated rewards"; in the micro phase, it "concerns decision making and plan formulation that enables players to work their way through a hierarchy of goals either self-assigned, set by the game, or set by other players."

The chapter has eight named subsections, more than any other dimension chapter: an opening macro-phase frame (Caillois paidia/ludus + rule-systems + ergodic semiosis + orientation), then **Goals**, **The Structure of Goals**, a **Micro Phase** transition, **Plans and Goals**, **Strategies, Tactics, and Microtactics** (Calleja's analytic innovation), **Rewards**, and a closing **Ludic Involvement Summary** — the only such summary subsection among the six dimension chapters, and a structural signal that the dimension-chapter cycle terminates here and Chapter 10's synthesis begins.

Six boundary criteria distinguish ludic involvement from adjacent dimensions: (a) rule-system mediation (action-meanings depend on rules; the capture-the-flag flag illustrates the criterion in its sharpest form), (b) goal-orientation (the activity must be geared toward a specific goal — agonism is not required, as the Spore case shows), (c) choice and decision-making between alternatives (Sid Meier's "set of interesting choices" axiom), (d) iterative plan-formulation/execution/feedback/replanning loop, (e) reward-attribution (system, personal, social, or aesthetic), and (f) detectable goal-hierarchy structure (primary / sub / immediate). When more than one dimension overlaps — most commonly with kinesthetic and spatial — the criterion is whether the activity's salient organizing feature is goal-pursuit-through-rule-mediation or, instead, motor-coordination (kinesthetic) or environment-habitation (spatial). The Ananke Darkshore swim sits at the boundary: ludic (self-set goal) interlocked with spatial (exploration of game-space) and adjacent to kinesthetic (felt-agency of decide-and-do).

## 2. Macro phase — goals and rewards (pp. 147–149)

The chapter opens classically. Caillois's *Man, Play and Games* (1962) supplies the founding distinction between **paidia** — diversion, turbulence, free improvisation, carefree gaiety, an idealized pre-linguistic purity of play that "could not have any" name "because they are not part of any order, distinctive symbolism, or clearly differentiated life that would permit a vocabulary to consecrate their autonomy" — and **ludus**, the gratuitous structure imposed upon paidia by conventions, rules, and techniques, which Caillois describes as complementary to and a refinement of paidia, disciplining and enriching it. Calleja block-quotes Caillois three times across pp. 147–148, establishing the paidia/ludus binary as the chapter's load-bearing classical frame.

He then qualifies. Digital games can never attain Caillois's paidic ideal because they are coded: even the most free-form activity in a virtual environment is shaped by the rule system. Flying around Liberty City in *Grand Theft Auto IV* with reckless abandon has a degree of kinesthetic freedom, but that freedom is itself constrained by code; the player's intention is "always limited by the conventions of a designed system" (p. 148). The paidic ideal is therefore unattainable in principle, and ludus is everywhere — a structural condition of the medium.

The argument then pivots to the **rules-give-meaning thesis**, citing Salen and Zimmerman (2003) and Juul (2005) for the systemic-nature-of-games premise, with Malaby (2007) for the proposition that rule systems are designed to "generate interesting forms of contingency." Rules assign values to objects, events, and entities; the rule system is "the machine behind the representational surface" of the game, intended to create "a landscape of interesting choices." This is, structurally, the chapter's working analog for Salen and Zimmerman's "meaningful choice" — though Calleja does not deploy that exact phrase. The ergodic-vs-non-ergodic semiosis argument follows. A national flag in a film carries iconographic patriotic connotations; in capture-the-flag mode of *Call of Duty IV*, the flag becomes "the locus of a competitive struggle between two factions and a source of added excitement for players on both teams." The flag's meaning is rule-system-assigned, not iconographic; its semiotic charge is accessible only through rule-mediated interaction. This is the volume's defining ergodic-semiosis exhibit and the strongest candidate site for an Uexküllian Wirkmal (effect-marker) reading that the dissertation's Layer C augmentation will pursue.

A subtle but structurally important closing move: rules rarely focus conscious attention. The rule system "manifests itself experientially in the form of decision making and the pursuit of personal and game-defined goals." The rest of the chapter therefore addresses ludic involvement primarily through goal-hierarchies and decision-making, not through rule-system formalism. This grounds the macro/micro orientation paragraph and motivates the next subsection.

## 3. Goals — sources, structure, and the autotelic loanword (pp. 149–153)

The **Goals** subsection establishes a three-source taxonomy: goals can be **system-determined**, **individually-determined**, or **community-negotiated** in multiplayer contexts. The taxonomy is supported by an opening philosophical formulation that carries one of the chapter's most theoretically charged sentences: "Pursuing a goal can be an end in itself — an autotelic experience yielding satisfaction within a rule-based system of meaning — or it can be fueled by the desire of attaining a reward" (p. 149). The word **autotelic** is doing serious work here as a loanword. Csikszentmihalyi is not cited. This confirms the volume-wide absence of flow-theory observed across the cite-check, and it marks a precise Layer-A anchor site for the dissertation's anticipatory augmentation: pursuing-a-goal-as-end-in-itself is, in Aristotelian terms, **praxis** (end-in-itself activity, distinguished from poiesis at NE VI.5) and is the operative criterion of **eudaimonia** (the self-sufficient activity of NE I.7). The autotelic loanword without its proper psychologist citation is best bridged not back to Csikszentmihalyi but downward to Aristotle. This is one of the chapter's clearest residual-phenomenology-without-phenomenologist-cite sites.

Calleja proceeds to argue that games appeal to our tendency to organize life's complexity into goal-hierarchies (citing Egenfeldt-Nielsen, Smith, and Tosca 2008; Juul 2005, on systems modulable to participant skill); games "distil the abstract life goals that we struggle with on a daily basis into highly concrete, temporally and spatially circumscribed events," as Aki Järvinen's 2008 doctoral thesis puts it in the chapter's only major block quotation. Multiple theorists converge on goal-centrality: Juul, Salen and Zimmerman, Järvinen, Björk and Holopainen (2005).

The **three-source taxonomy** is then operationalized through *Grand Theft Auto IV*. The game's scripted-narrative imposes mission-goals with strict ludic parameters; its open environment supports player-defined goals (drive around to find a nicer car); its multiplayer dimension supports community-negotiated goals (arrange a hospital-loop race with friends). The driving-for-a-nicer-car example illustrates Schank and Abelson's (1977) **instrumental goal** — a goal that acts as a prerequisite to another goal (impress one's virtual date); or the same activity may be done "simply for its own sake," reactivating the autotelic loanword's philosophical valence.

Personal-goal scope, Calleja then argues, depends on rule-system affordance. In *Space Invaders* (Taito 1978) the activity-range is narrow, so personal-goal-setting is constrained. Sandbox titles like *Oblivion* (Bethesda Softworks 2006) and *Fallout 3* (Bethesda Game Studios 2008) widen the field substantially — in *Oblivion* one may abandon the save-the-world primary goal and pursue a criminal-career instead, the rule-system reacting in kind (notoriety grows, authorities pursue). MMOGs offer the broadest scope of all, though even there rule-system constraints persist (a first-level *World of Warcraft* character cannot freely explore because aggressive mobs will kill it).

The signature self-set-goal exhibit follows: Ananke, a research participant, recounts her *World of Warcraft* swim from Darkshore — "the better part of an afternoon" — undertaken not for system-validated rewards but for the felt satisfaction of doing something unusual and not "guided by the game mechanics." Calleja's gloss is the chapter's sole occurrence of the word *agency*: "The satisfaction of goal completion is made possible by the sense of agency created by allowing players to decide to do something and then to actually do it" (p. 153). This is a residual-phenomenology-without-phenomenologist-cite site of the cleanest kind: agency-as-felt is described phenomenologically (the felt support that decide-and-do gives to satisfaction) without any phenomenological framework (no Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, or Heidegger). Sunniva's *Planetside* quotation closes the subsection: more self-set-goal scope makes the game "more engaging … 'cause you need to plan more and think more around what you're doing." Cross-dimensional weaving is here thematized explicitly: ludic involvement is supported by spatial exploration and kinesthetic decide-and-do.

## 4. The structure of goals — cognitive-psychology hierarchy, partial rejection of McIntosh (pp. 153–155)

The **Structure of Goals** subsection imports the cognitive-psychology consensus on hierarchical goal-organization (Bell and Huang 1997; Locke and Latham 1990; McIntosh 1996; Rosenbloom and Newell 1983), bundling four sources into one citation cluster. The common distinction is between **primary goals** and **subgoals**, with immediately-attainable subgoals pursued instrumentally toward primary goals (Houser-Marko 2007). Järvinen (2008) applies the distinction to games; Björk and Holopainen (2005) identify it as a recurring design pattern and observe that player-awareness of the lower-to-higher-order linkage raises satisfaction.

The *Fallout 3* cascade is the worked example: primary goal find-fugitive-father; subgoal travel to a distant radio station; sub-subgoal procure better equipment to survive the route; sub-sub-subgoal find a town with a shop (Megaton); further down, earn bottle-cap currency; further down, find a job. The chain gives the player's actions meaning and motivates continued engagement. Crucially, the game's rule system supports an **alternative-personal-primary-goal**: a player who has lost all faith in humanity may decide to murder every living human in the world; the rule-system supports the goal because corpses persist. By contrast *Far Cry 2* (Ubisoft Montreal 2008) cannot support this kind of personal primary goal because enemies respawn infinitely. Rule-system design therefore determines which personal primary goals are attainable — a clean instance of the agency-vs-rules tension's structural-conditioning resolution: the felt freedom to set one's own primary goal is exactly bounded by the rule-system's permission.

A balancing move follows. Game-life-goal relativity: the entire game-activity can itself be a subgoal of out-of-game primary-goals (relaxation, self-worth-via-competition); a game's primary goal "tends to be only a subgoal in the larger life of the player." But certain everyday-life features of goal-hierarchies do not translate to games. William McIntosh's claim that hierarchically-higher goals are more abstract is **partially rejected** for games (p. 155): primary game-goals are typically concrete and explicitly communicated for design reasons. The highest-MMOG-level primary goal is "measured at all times by the system and displayed explicitly in order to keep players striving for their goal." Oriel's *World of Warcraft* quotation anchors the numbers-game ethos: "Then came the numbers … quickly getting higher levels, higher skill points, better armor … making more money for bigger items …" The implicit position: game-design's concretization of primary goals is itself an analytic feature of the medium, not a deviation from cognitive-psychology's hierarchy thesis.

## 5. Micro phase — plans, the iterative loop, and Spore's dual-goal (pp. 155–157)

The brief **Micro Phase** transition section deploys Sid Meier's "set of interesting choices" aphorism (quoted via Rollings and Morris 2000, p. 38). This is the chapter's central micro-phase framing axiom. Two observations matter for the dissertation's purposes. First, the Meier quotation reaches Calleja only via a game-design-practice book; it is not from a Meier primary source. Sid Meier is a designer (the *Civilization* series), not a theorist. Second, the Meier aphorism functions as Calleja's working analog for Salen and Zimmerman's "meaningful choice" — but Calleja does not use the Salen and Zimmerman phrase. The implicit reasoning is that rule-system-mediated choice is the only kind of meaningful choice (compare the rule-system-makes-meaning argument at p. 148 lines 5205–5214), but the term-of-art bridge is the design-practice quotation, not the game-studies theoretical one. The dissertation should treat "meaningful choice" as an anticipatorily-reconstructed bridge-concept, not as an exegetical anchor.

The **Plans and Goals** subsection introduces the **iterative planning loop**: players formulate subgoal plans, execute, receive feedback on likelihood-of-success, then continue or modify the plan, sometimes restart, with successful subgoals advancing the chain (typically up the hierarchy). This loop is the structural backbone of micro-phase ludic involvement, and it is the cleanest site in the chapter for the dissertation's Aristotelian phronesis + deliberative phantasia (Papachristou) bridge: deliberate-among-action-possibilities, choose (prohairesis), execute, receive feedback, re-deliberate. The bridge is exact in its iterative structure.

Worked examples: a *World of Warcraft* journey from Ratchet to Feathermoon, with plans amended on the spot when unforeseen player-faction skirmishes erupt en route; auction-house pricing decisions in *WoW*; defensive strategy in *Counter-Strike: Source*; army-allocation for the siege of Aragon in *Medieval II: Total War*. The *Spore* (Maxis 2008) creature-customization case is theoretically important: a personal goal (fit a specific mental image of what the creature should look like) and a systemic goal (build a functionally aggressive creature) can co-exist within the same activity. The activity is ludic involvement iff it is geared toward a specific goal — *agonism is not required*. This is a quiet but crucial boundary clarification.

## 6. Strategies, tactics, and microtactics — Calleja's analytic innovation (pp. 157–159)

This subsection houses the chapter's distinctive theoretical contribution and one of the few places in the volume where Calleja makes a wholly conceptual move beyond synthesizing prior frameworks. The argument runs as follows.

The primary-goal/subgoal distinction entails a parallel plan-level distinction. Plans formulated in pursuit of primary goals are **strategies**. Plans formulated in pursuit of subgoals are **tactics**. And — Calleja's introduced term — plans formulated *during* the moment of action, under conditions of short temporal-distance between formulation, execution, feedback, and evaluation, are **microtactics**.

The hierarchy is not categorical but a heuristic gradient. The fencing extended example (pp. 157–158) makes the gradient operational. Primary goal: be ranked in the county's top three. Strategy: build a training regime and choose tournaments carefully. Tactical level (per tournament-bout): "Disrupt the opponent's attacks while retreating, and use counterattacks to score safe points." Microtactic level (per individual point): "He is out of balance, with the foil moving clockwise; lunge with a counterclockwise evasion of the blade." The microtactic exemplar is formulated as inner speech — a residual-phenomenology of moment-of-action deliberation, and the cleanest Aristotelian phronesis + deliberative phantasia anchor in the chapter. The inner-speech form is itself structurally phantastic: the agent images the situation (opponent's balance + foil-direction) and projects an alternative motor response (counterclockwise evasion) prior to execution. Papachristou's reading of deliberative phantasia as imaginative-projection of action-possibilities fits the example with near-perfect specificity.

The **temporal-distance qualification** is essential to the typology and is what gives it analytic traction: the strategy/tactic/microtactic distinction is useful only where there is short temporal distance between plan-formulation, execution, feedback, and evaluation. Turn-based board games do not benefit from microtactic vocabulary because players have time to think tactically. In real-time game environments where players must act, the microtactic concept identifies "decisions to immediately perform certain actions instead of others." For the dissertation's purposes, the qualification means the typology is a graded gradient tied to temporal-feedback granularity rather than a categorical taxonomy.

The **FIFA 09 Ultimate Team extended case** (pp. 158–159) is the chapter's most elaborated single design-economy analysis. Personal primary goal: build an all-Italian team and win an online cup against other human players. The subgoal cascade interlocks four scarce resources: contract-time (each match played reduces it), money (earned by playing matches, but matches reduce contract-time), staff (manager, head-coach, goalkeeper-trainer, physiotherapist, fitness-instructor — all of whom cost money and contract-time), and player-morale (which requires playing-time but again competes for contract-time). The structure is **ludic-economy** reasoning — Calleja does not use the phrase but the interlocking-scarce-resources structure is clean, anticipatory ludic-economy. The dissertation should capture this as a portable instrument: ludic-economy ≈ Aristotelian techne plus phronesis under interlocking scarce-resource constraints, with primary-goal-as-telos. Tactical level: match-management subgoals (win the match while avoiding player injuries / send-offs). Microtactic level: the right-winger faced by a defender — short-pass-to-right-back, dribble-and-cross, or cross-immediately, decided in fractions of a second.

The strategy/tactics/microtactics hierarchy is a **primary dissertation-instrument candidate**: portable as a coding scheme for player-deliberation analysis. Its mapping onto Aristotelian phronesis is clean: strategy ↔ distal-deliberation (council-deliberation among long-term ends), tactic ↔ situated-tactical-deliberation, microtactic ↔ prohairesis mediated by deliberative phantasia. The mapping is anticipatory, not exegetical — Calleja has zero Aristotle hits per the Phase 0 cite-check.

## 7. Rewards — the eight-category implicit typology (pp. 159–165)

The longest subsection of the chapter. Calleja's opening move is to subjectivize reward-value: the same cut-scene delights one player and annoys another; a rare magical hammer is precious to one player and trivial to another with a rack of better ones. Reward-value depends on the player's wants, needs, and expectations. This subjective-value framing is structurally Burkean (the rhetorical-amplification of motive by the system) and structurally Uexküllian (Tonkreise — qualitative coloring of perceived objects by purposive context).

What follows is, implicitly, an **eight-category rewards typology** that Calleja does not formalize but the dissertation should formalize as a portable coding scheme. (1) **Performative-enhancing**: rewards that improve avatar or miniature abilities or grant new ones. The *Planetside* skill-point ability-economy is the paradigm — each level yields a skill-point that buys access to armor, weapons, vehicles; skills compound synergistically (infiltrator-suit + reconnaissance-aircraft → drop-onto-tower-rooftop-and-hack). The skill-economy is itself a ludic-economy. (2) **Mobility-enhancing**: efficient or attractive navigation of large worlds; horses/ponies/vehicles in *Oblivion*'s Cyrodill or *Lord of the Rings Online*'s Middle-earth. (3) **Visibility / spatial**: omniscient-viewpoint games (RTS) reward players with added map-visibility (fog-of-war abilities). (4) **Spatial-area-unlock**: space itself functions as reward — *GTA IV*'s mission-gated areas. (5) **Intrinsic-points**: points/score assigned for successful action, building toward a quantifiable high-score (*Space Invaders*); *Counter-Strike: Source*'s "Monster Kill" exaggerated sound-effect is the rhetorical-amplification exemplar, and the chapter's strongest Burkean motivation-rhetoric exhibit — the system rhetorically amplifies the player's Purpose+Act conjunction. (6) **Social-acclamation (achievements)**: console-platform achievement systems unlock predefined-goal completions to a public profile, generating bragging-rights. (7) **The numbers-game**: scores increasing, level-meters filling, gear-stats accumulating; participants describe continuing-past-intent to reach a new level and "logging off with the satisfaction of knowing that a concrete game goal was reached." Aprile's *World of Warcraft* quotation on grinding — "[It's] pretty much the only way to level. … So, I do what I have to do to reach my goals" — anchors the numbers-game's force: the lure of next-level reward makes admittedly-tedious activity feel worthwhile. The numbers-game applies beyond MMOGs to football-team / city / army quantification. (8a) **Competitive rewards**: most-kills in *Counter-Strike: Source*; first-guild-to-defeat-a-raid-boss in *World of Warcraft*. (8b) **Collaborative rewards** (class-orientation): *Lord of the Rings Online*'s Burglar class can conceal friends; *World of Warcraft*'s Priest healing class is designed to assist others — class-selection is a goal/reward orientation commitment. (9) **Scripted-narrative-as-reward**: *GTA IV*'s Nico-story cut scenes work because the script, voice-acting, and character-depth carry quality; *Metal Gear Solid 4*'s non-skippable hour-long sequences are the anti-paradigm. (10) **Aesthetic-beauty**: *GTA IV*'s harbor at sunrise; *Flower* (ThatGameCompany 2009) makes aesthetic beauty an explicit designed reward — sequence-completion bursts the surroundings into color (Figure 9.3 anchor). Calleja explicitly locates aesthetic-beauty rewards on the calmer side of the affective dimension, completing the bridge to Chapter 8.

The affective-response paragraph (p. 165) bridges into Chapter 10's synthesis. Success/failure of goal-attainment is an emotionally charged experience because outcomes "reflect" the player's "dexterous, cognitive, or social abilities." The dimension is dominantly stimulation-directed in contemporary games, though the *Flower* paradigm shows the calming-aesthetic pole is also available to designers.

## 8. Ludic Involvement Summary — the closing rules-as-meaning-enabler thesis (pp. 165–166)

The chapter closes with a subsection found nowhere else in the volume's dimension chapters. Three short paragraphs. Ludic involvement is "one of the most fundamental cornerstones of a game" — compelling because of the landscape of cognitive challenges games are apt at presenting and their associated rewards. Ludic involvement is engagement with the choices made in the game and the repercussions of those choices. The choices may be system-stipulated, player-set, or community-negotiated; or they may be spur-of-the-moment with no overarching goal-link. The summary's final sentence is the tightest one-line statement of the rules-as-meaning-enabler thesis the volume offers: **"Without repercussions, actions lose their meaning, and thus their execution generates no excitement."** This is, implicitly, the resolution of the agency-vs-rules tension that the chapter has carried throughout: rule-mediated repercussion is constitutive of meaningful action, not opposed to it. The line is the closest Calleja comes to a Salen-and-Zimmerman "meaningful choice" formulation, though the canonical phrase is not used. The final paragraph signposts Chapter 10: now that the six dimensions have been considered in turn, the model can address the four challenges raised in Chapter 2, and a solution to the presence/immersion question will emerge — the incorporation thesis.

## 9. Tensions

**Tension 1 — rules-as-constraint vs. rules-as-meaningful-context.** The same paragraph (p. 148) carries both: "the intention of the player is always limited by the conventions of a designed system" AND "Rules give meaning to actions and objects in the game world." The tension is real but Calleja's implicit resolution is that the two clauses state the same thesis from opposite directions: restriction is constitutive of meaning. The closing summary line "Without repercussions, actions lose their meaning" (p. 166) is the tightest one-line resolution. From this position, an implicit Salen-and-Zimmerman "meaningful choice" argument is reconstructable, although Calleja does not deploy the phrase.

**Tension 2 — ludic-agency-as-felt vs. narrative-determination / system-determination.** The chapter uses "agency" only once (p. 153) and treats it as the felt-experiential residue of decide-and-do under rule-system permission. Yet pp. 154–155 show that alternative-personal-primary-goals are conditional on rule-system support: *Fallout 3* allows murder-everyone (corpses persist), *Far Cry 2* does not (infinite respawn). Felt-agency is structurally pre-empted by the designer's choice of respawn-rule. Calleja stops short of Sicart-style ethical-rule-design analysis. The residual-phenomenology-without-phenomenologist-cite flag applies: agency-as-felt is described phenomenologically without any phenomenological framework anchor.

**Tension 3 — Caillois paidic ideal vs. coded-game inescapability.** Caillois's paidia is the idealized pre-linguistic purity of play; Calleja immediately concedes that digital games can never attain this ideal because they are coded. The tension is structural backdrop, not resolved in the chapter.

## 10. Anticipatory augmentation mappings

**Burke pentad — Purpose + Act (with Scene-as-rule-context).** Goals = pentadic Purpose (the "for what / toward what end" of action). Moment-of-gameplay actions = pentadic Act (the "what done" under Purpose's organization). The rule system = pentadic Scene (the framing context lending Act its motive-intelligibility). The strategy/tactics/microtactics three-tier hierarchy is itself a pentadic decomposition of how Purpose organizes Act at descending temporal scales: distal-Purpose / proximal-Purpose / immediate-Act. The *Counter-Strike: Source* "Monster Kill" audio-amplification is a paradigmatic Burkean motivation-rhetoric exhibit: the system rhetorically amplifies the player's Purpose+Act conjunction. Calleja's repeated framing of goals as the *experiential* manifestation of the rule system (p. 149) matches Burke's claim that pentadic Purpose is what gives Act its motive-intelligibility. This chapter is the volume's premier Burke-pentad-mapping site after Kinesthetic-Agency. Provenance: anticipatory-projection (dissertation).

**Uexküll — Wirkmal + Funktionskreis + Tonkreise / Umwelt-as-rule-mediated-action-field.** Uexküll's Funktionskreis decomposes the perception-action loop into Merkmal (perception-marker) and Wirkmal (effect-marker). Calleja's ludic-involvement micro-phase is paradigmatically Wirkmal-organized: actions are not raw motor-execution but rule-validated effector-signs that carry game-system meaning. The capture-the-flag flag (pp. 148–149) is the volume-defining Wirkmal exhibit — physical-screen-pixel-of-flag becomes effector-sign-of-ludic-objective via rule-system mediation. The goal-structure analysis maps onto Uexküll's claim that the animal's Umwelt is organized by its biological purposes (Tonkreise): the player's game-Umwelt is organized by primary-goal / subgoal / microtactic decomposition. The Sid Meier "set of interesting choices" aphorism aligns with Uexküll's claim that the animal's Umwelt is a structured field of action-possibilities, not a neutral perceptual flux. The Spore creature-customization case (p. 156) exhibits dual-Umwelt structure — function-tuning (biological-purpose Umwelt) and mental-image-tuning (aesthetic-purpose Umwelt). Calleja has zero Uexküll vocabulary. Provenance: anticipatory-projection (dissertation).

**Aristotelian phronesis + deliberative phantasia (Papachristou) + eudaimonia/praxis-for-its-own-sake (NE I.7 + NE VI.5).** This chapter is the volume's premier phronesis-anchor site. Papachristou's reading of phronesis as embodied-deliberative-capacity-applied-to-particulars, mediated by phantasia (deliberative-imagination of action-possibilities), is the cleanest ontological grounding for Calleja's strategy/tactics/microtactics hierarchy. The fencing case (pp. 157–158) and the *FIFA 09* case (pp. 158–159) are paradigmatic phronesis-exhibits: agent confronts particular situation, deliberates among action-possibilities (via deliberative phantasia — the inner-speech microtactic example is structurally phantastic), chooses (prohairesis), acts, receives feedback, re-deliberates. The microtactic concept identifies decisions to immediately perform certain actions instead of others — structurally identical to Aristotelian prohairesis. The iterative process of planning/execution/feedback/replanning (p. 156) is a moment-by-moment exercise of practical reasoning. **Additionally**: the autotelic-loanword-without-Csikszentmihalyi residual-phenomenology site (p. 149) is best bridged via Aristotelian eudaimonia and praxis-for-its-own-sake — NE I.7's self-sufficient-activity criterion and NE VI.5's distinction between praxis (end-in-itself) and poiesis (end-outside-itself). Pursuing a goal as an end in itself is, in Aristotelian terms, praxis. Calleja has zero Aristotle hits. Provenance: anticipatory-projection (dissertation).

**Heidegger BCAP — phronesis-as-aletheic-disclosure-of-the-actionable-particular + ekstatic-temporal-attunement + kairos.** BCAP's analyses would deepen Calleja's iterative planning loop; kairos (the right-moment-to-act) is directly relevant to microtactic-as-immediate-action-choice. Provenance: anticipatory-projection (dissertation).

**Bowin AMT — motion-at-different-scales.** Bowin's analyses of continuous motion and aisthesis-kinesis interaction supply the kinematic substrate for the microtactic concept; the strategy/tactics/microtactics hierarchy descends in temporal granularity in a way that parallels Aristotle's motion-at-different-scales. Provenance: anticipatory-projection (dissertation).

## 11. Cross-pipeline bridges

Beyond the augmentation layers, the chapter offers several specific cross-pipeline bridge sites. The **Phantasia Cluster** (Hawhee + Papachristou) finds its single richest Calleja-side anchor in the inner-speech microtactic example (p. 158) — the imaginative-projection of an alternative motor-response prior to execution is exactly Papachristou's deliberative phantasia. Hawhee's rhetorical-vision work bridges to the Ananke Darkshore-swim self-set-goal (p. 152), where phantastic goal-construction precedes any system support.
