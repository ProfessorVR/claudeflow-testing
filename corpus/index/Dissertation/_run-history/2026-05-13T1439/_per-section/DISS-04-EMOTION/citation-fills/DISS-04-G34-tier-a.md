## What (paired primary+secondary footnote)
> Aristotle famously describes the *pathē* as movements of soul (*psychēs kinēseis*) at *Rhetoric* II.1, 1378a20 ("feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements, attended by pain or pleasure"). The classification is in productive tension with *DA* I.4 408b1–30, where Aristotle warns "it is the man who is angry, not the soul; thought is impassible" — limiting in what sense soul itself can be the bearer of *kinēsis*. The dissertation's framing tracks the secondary literature: Caston (1996, pp. 26–32) reads the *pathē* as second-order kinetic changes "indirect effects" of sensation; Heidegger in BCAP §SS17 treats the *pathē* as "*ginomena en tē psyche*" — modes of becoming co-given with *hēdonē* and *lupē*.

## Why
The *pathē*-as-soul-*kinēsis* thesis requires both Aristotle's own *Rhet.* II.1 statement and acknowledgement of the *DA* I.4 408b restriction. The Caston (1996) reading triangulates by routing through the *On Dreams* echo-mechanism.

## Verbatim (≤200 words secondary)
"Phantasia derives from sensation in two respects: (i) CAUSALLY it derives from a particular sensation; (ii) its CHARACTER derives from the character of that sensation. Hence *Rhetoric* 1.11, 1370a28 describes *phantasia* as 'a weak sort of sensation'" (Caston 1996, pp. 46–47).
