> ‘There would be no time without the soul, except that there might be that, of which time is a number, i.e. change.’ (Physics IV.11, 219b1–2)

On what I shall call the strong reading, Aristotle is claiming that, in the absence of souls, there would be no time at all, but there would still be change. On the weak reading, he is making the more modest claim that, in the absence of souls, there would be nothing that *counts* as time, although there would be something that is sufficiently like time for it to be the basis of temporal truths. I shall argue for the strong reading.

…Aristotle’s thought, I suggest, is that the existence of time depends on the existence of souls capable of counting, because time is essentially something that can be counted. Change, by contrast, does not depend for its existence on being counted, and so does not depend on the existence of souls.

I defend a ‘strong’ reading of this dependence claim, according to which there would quite literally be no time at all if there were no souls, as opposed to a ‘weak’ reading on which the dependence is merely conceptual or epistemic.

**Verbatim (Perplexity-returned — REQUIRES SOURCE VERIFICATION)**:

> Aristotle’s notorious statement that ‘if there were no soul there would not be time’ (219b1–2) has encouraged the idea that he is committed to the outright non‑existence of time in a soulless universe. But the context suggests a weaker claim. What would be missing, Aristotle says, is time ‘as number’, since there would be no soul to do the counting; what would remain would be the changes themselves, with their before‑and‑after structure. Thus, strictly speaking, the before‑and‑after of change does not *require* a countersoul, even if what is properly called ‘time’ does.

**Verbatim (Perplexity-returned — REQUIRES SOURCE VERIFICATION)**:

> The passage at 219b1–2, where Aristotle remarks that without soul ‘there would not be time, except that there would be change’, need not be read as implying that time is ontologically absent in a soulless world. Rather, what is denied is the actuality of time as an item of arithmetic—time as *arithmos*—since there would be no one to perform the act of counting. The temporal order given by the before and after in movement is in no way threatened by this counterfactual; what is at issue is our practice of articulating that order in numerical form.
