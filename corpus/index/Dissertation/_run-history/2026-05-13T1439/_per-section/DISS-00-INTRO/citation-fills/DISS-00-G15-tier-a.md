## What (secondary-source footnote; author-prominent)
> As Heidegger explicitly insists, "*Pathos* in relation to the *zoe praktike meta logou* is a *being-taken* of being-there. Being-there is taken with that which is there in the world — *from without*, but from without *in the sense of the world as the wherein of my being*" (Heidegger 1924/2009, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* p. 133). The pathē are accordingly "not 'psychic experiences' and not 'in consciousness'" but a being-taken of human beings "in their full being-in-the-world" (pp. 133–134).

## Why
The introduction's anti-internalist thesis is structurally a Heideggerian reading of Aristotle. BCAP p. 133 is the locus where Heidegger most pointedly refuses the cognitivist/internalist construal of *pathos*; quotation locks the argument to a textual anchor rather than a paraphrase.

## Verbatim (≤200 words secondary)
"The *pathe* are not 'psychic experiences' and not 'in consciousness'; they are a being-taken of human beings *in their full being-in-the-world*. ... Aristotle's critical remark (*De an.* A 1, 403a 3 sqq.): 'to say that the soul gets angry is the same as to say that the soul builds a house. It would be better to say not that the soul has pity or learns or believes, but that the human being does the psyche.' Soul is here conceived as *ousia*, insofar as the being-taken of beings as living things is expressed in the *pathe*" (BCAP pp. 133–134).
