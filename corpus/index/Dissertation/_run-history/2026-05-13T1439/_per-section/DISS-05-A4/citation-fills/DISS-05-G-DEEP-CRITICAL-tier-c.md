# Heidegger's Reinterpretation of Aristotelian Praxis-Hexis as Cultivated Emotional Resolution: Contesting the Mechanical Reflex Theory

## Executive Summary

Martin Heidegger's early engagement with Aristotelian ethics, particularly his 1924 lecture course on basic concepts of Aristotelian philosophy (Grundbegriffe der aristotelischen Philosophie, GA 18), presents a decisive reinterpretation of the relationship between praxis, hexis, and emotional experience that fundamentally contests the traditional reading of Aristotelian virtue acquisition as mechanical habituation. Rather than understanding the cultivation of virtue through hexis as the development of automatic reflexive behaviors divorced from cognitive and affective deliberation, Heidegger's phenomenological reading—grounded in his emerging existential analytic of Dasein—reveals that praxis represents a temporally structured form of becoming that integrates intentional emotional understanding, the capacity for existential resolution, and responsive engagement with particular circumstantial demands. This report synthesizes Heidegger's destructive retrieval of Aristotle's ethical vocabulary with contemporary scholarly reconstructions by Pierre Aubenque, Thomas J. Sheehan, Nancy Sherman, William McNeill, and others to demonstrate how the doxa-content conditional gating of pathē-arousal constitutes an irreducibly affective but nonetheless intelligible structure of human action that resists reduction to mechanical causation. The central argument traces how Heidegger's temporalization of hexis—his interpretation of the "frequently" (πολλάκις) of habituation not as quantitative repetition but as existential repetition grounded in historicality and finitude—opens a philosophical space for understanding ethical becoming as continually renewed resolution rather than fixed disposition, thereby recovering an authentically Aristotelian phenomenology of virtue that remains obscured in intellectualist and mechanistic readings alike.

## Aristotle's Conception of Praxis and Hexis in Nicomachean Ethics VI.5

To properly situate Heidegger's innovative reinterpretation, we must first establish the interpretive context of Aristotle's original formulation of the praxis-hexis relationship as it appears in the Nicomachean Ethics. The fundamental philosophical distinction Aristotle draws among human activities provides the conceptual scaffolding upon which all subsequent virtue ethics rests. According to the received Aristotelian taxonomy, human beings engage in three basic modes of activity: *theoria*, which aims at truth; *poiesis*, which aims at production; and *praxis*, which aims at action itself.[1] This tripartite division proves consequential precisely because it establishes that not all human doing constitutes the same genus of activity; action that produces an external artifact or that pursues theoretical knowledge operates according to fundamentally different principles than action undertaken for its own sake as the proper mode of human flourishing. The ethical significance of this distinction cannot be overstated, for if praxis constitutes the highest realization of human potential, then understanding its structure becomes synonymous with understanding how human excellence itself becomes possible.

Nicomachean Ethics VI.5 addresses the intellectual virtues that govern deliberation and decision-making within the domain of praxis, a passage that proves crucial for understanding the integration of reason, emotion, and habituated response within virtuous action. Aristotle's discussion of *phronesis*—practical wisdom—in relation to the character virtues emerges from his recognition that neither theoretical knowledge (*episteme*) nor mere technical skill (*techne*) suffices to explain how a virtuous agent acts well in particular circumstances.[10] The virtue of character, according to Aristotle's formulation, is a *hexis*, which may be understood as a disposition, state, or condition that inclines the person toward appropriate feelings, desires, and actions in response to concrete situations.[30] Crucially, Aristotle specifies that this hexis is "a state intermediate between extremes" and that its acquisition "results from habit."[5] The doctrine of the mean, far from endorsing mechanical averaging or rigid conformity to predetermined rules, requires that the virtuous person develop a refined sensitivity to what the situation demands—an ability that manifests itself in having the right emotion, to the right degree, directed at the right object, at the right time, in the right manner.[30]

The relationship between hexis and pathos—between the stable dispositional state and the affective dimension of human experience—cannot be understood as one of mechanical cause and effect. Rather, as contemporary Aristotelian scholars have increasingly recognized, the emotional life constitutes an intrinsic dimension of virtue itself.[47] When Aristotle characterizes virtue as involving the capacity to feel fear, anger, compassion, and other emotions appropriately, he indicates that the cultivation of virtue necessarily includes the cultivation of one's emotional responsiveness to the world.[7] The intellectual virtue of phronesis works "in close collaboration with moral virtue, and thus with our affectivity or pathos," suggesting that practical wisdom and emotional attunement operate as equiprimordial dimensions of excellent action rather than as separable domains of cognition and feeling.[3] This integrated understanding of hexis as encompassing both rational deliberation and affective response proves central to appreciating what Heidegger sought to recover in his phenomenological retrieval of these Aristotelian concepts.

Furthermore, Aristotle's account in Nicomachean Ethics VI emphasizes that virtuous action requires both the disposition (hexis) to act virtuously and the practical wisdom (phronesis) to perceive what the situation requires. This perceptual dimension of virtue—the ability to discriminate the particulars of a situation and to apprehend what response appropriately addresses those particulars—distinguishes virtuous action from both mere compliance with rules and from the exercise of technical skill. As Sherman argues in her influential reconstruction of Aristotelian habituation, the process through which virtue becomes established in an agent involves "critical practice" dependent upon "cognitive capacities of choice, perception, and deliberation" rather than operating as a "mechanical process that chronologically precedes more reflective and cognitive moral development." This rehabilitation of habituation as fundamentally cognitive and deliberative, even when undertaken by developing agents who do not yet possess complete reflective capacities, directly challenges interpretations that would reduce the hexis to an automatic behavioral pattern.

## Heidegger's Destructive Reading of Aristotle in GA 18 (1924)

Heidegger's phenomenological engagement with Aristotelian philosophy in the 1924 lecture course on Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy represents far more than mere historical scholarship. Rather, Heidegger undertakes what he terms a "destructive retrieve" of Aristotelian thought—a methodological appropriation designed to excavate the existential structures underlying Aristotle's conceptual apparatus while simultaneously exposing how subsequent metaphysical tradition has obscured or distorted these original insights.[28] This destructive retrieval forms an integral component of Heidegger's broader project in Being and Time and the surrounding lecture courses of the early-to-mid 1920s, wherein the analysis of Dasein's being provides the fundamental ontological framework for rethinking the nature of human action, emotion, understanding, and temporality.[40]

The specific focus of Heidegger's inquiry into Aristotelian ethics concerns the possibility of reconceiving praxis and hexis not as mechanical psychological phenomena but as existentially meaningful modes of disclosure and becoming. As Heidegger's students and later commentators have documented, his reading of Aristotle in GA 18 proves revolutionary precisely in its insistence that praxis constitutes the primary sphere within which human being-in-the-world reveals itself.[45] Where traditional interpretations had relegated practical knowledge to an inferior status relative to theoretical contemplation, and where later psychological and ethical readings had reduced hexis to automatic habit, Heidegger recognizes that the structure of praxis opens a fundamental dimension of human existence that philosophical tradition has systematically undervalued and misinterpreted.
