# The Architecture of Virtue in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics: Per-Virtue-Pathos Pairings and the Praxis-Hexis Framework

This comprehensive report examines one of the most distinctive features of Aristotelian virtue ethics: the structured relationship between particular virtues and their corresponding emotions or psychological states, what may be termed the per-virtue-pathos (per-virtue-passion) architecture. At the heart of Aristotle's ethical theory lies a sophisticated understanding of virtue as neither mere habit nor abstract knowledge, but rather as a stable state of the soul—a hexis—that disposes the agent toward appropriate emotional and volitional response to the circumstances of life. By examining the specific pairings of courage with fear, megalopsychia with honor-seeking emotion, and aidōs with shame, this report demonstrates how Aristotle constructs a systematic account of moral excellence grounded in the proper ordering of the human emotional and rational faculties. Furthermore, the report engages with recent and influential secondary scholarship—particularly the work of Nancy Sherman, Pierre Aubenque, Sarah Broadie, and David Bostock—to illuminate how virtue should be understood not as a passive state but as an active condition, a praxis-hexis, in which the virtuous person continually actualizes excellence through deliberation, choice, and action. This architectural reading reveals that for Aristotle, virtue is fundamentally about the integration of rational judgment with appropriate emotional response, mediated through the intellectual virtue of practical wisdom (phronesis), such that the virtuous agent becomes capable of both perceiving and responding to the salient features of the moral situation in ways that constitute genuine human flourishing (eudaimonia).

## The Conceptual Architecture: Virtue, Pathos, and Hexis in Aristotelian Theory

### The Framework of Hexis: Active Disposition Rather Than Passive Habit

To understand Aristotle's account of the virtue-pathos pairing, one must first grasp his conception of virtue as a hexis, a term that has been persistently mistranslated and misunderstood in English-language scholarship.[9] The word hexis, derived from the Greek root meaning "possession" or "having," designates a relatively stable arrangement or disposition that is fundamentally different from a mere passive state or transient feeling.[50] As Aristotle himself clarifies in the Categories, hexis belongs to the class of dispositions but distinguishes itself through its permanence and active character, standing in contrast to more superficial conditions such as heat or cold that are readily removable and changeable.[4] When understood properly, hexis functions as an active condition, a state in which something must actively hold itself, and this active dimension proves absolutely central to comprehending Aristotelian virtue.[9] The virtues are hexeis precisely because they are not passive conditions of the soul that befall us without our participation, but rather enduring dispositions that require continuous actualization through choice and action.

This distinction between the passive reception of emotion and the active disposition toward appropriate emotional response marks a crucial feature of Aristotelian virtue that many modern interpreters overlook. As Joe Sachs has emphasizing in his translation work, rendering hexis as "active condition" rather than "habit" or "state" helps preserve Aristotle's revolutionary insight that virtue involves the soul's capacity to hold itself in a certain relation to the circumstances of life.[9] This is not a matter of mindless routine or blind habituation, but rather the cultivation of a sophisticated responsiveness grounded in both rational understanding and emotional education. The person of virtue does not merely react to situations; rather, through the formation of character via habituation, practice, and rational reflection, the virtuous person comes to perceive the salient features of moral situations and to respond to them with appropriate emotion and action. This active holding of oneself in the right condition constitutes the fundamental nature of virtue as a hexis.

### The Doctrine of the Mean and Emotional Appropriateness

Central to Aristotle's architectural understanding of virtue is his doctrine that every virtue lies between two extremes—one of excess and one of deficiency—and that virtue consists in finding and choosing the appropriate intermediate position relative to the particulars of the situation.[24] This doctrine has frequently been misunderstood as a quantitative measure, a simple mathematical mean between two quantities, but such an interpretation fails to capture Aristotle's more nuanced and contextual understanding of virtue. As Aristotle himself emphasizes, the mean in question is "the mean relative to us," not an arithmetic mean, and it is "determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it."[24] The intermediate position that constitutes virtue is contextual, particularistic, and responsive to the nature of the specific emotional or volitional matter at hand.

For the virtuous person to occupy this intermediate position, they must calibrate their emotional responses according to multiple parameters: the right objects, the right amount, the right time, the right manner, and the right aims.[29] Consider, for instance, that one must feel fear toward genuinely fearful things, to the degree that fear is warranted, at times when danger is present, in ways that promote prudent action, and for the sake of preserving one's virtue and noble ends. These multiple dimensions of appropriateness cannot be reduced to simple quantitative rules; rather, they require the development of practical wisdom, a capacity to perceive what is situationally relevant and to respond proportionately. As Aristotle states in a passage central to understanding this architecture, "the man, then, who faces and who fears the right things and from the right motive, in the right way and from the right time, and who feels confidence under the corresponding conditions, is brave; for the brave man feels and acts according to the merits of the case and in whatever way the rule directs."[40] The "rule" here is not a rigid formula but the flexible standard that practical wisdom provides.

### The Pathos-Virtue Nexus: Emotions as Objects of Virtue

A fundamental feature of Aristotelian virtue theory that distinguishes it from many subsequent moral philosophies is the recognition that emotions themselves constitute proper objects of virtue rather than obstacles to be overcome or states to be eliminated.[27] Aristotle does not advocate for the Stoic position that would come to dominate later moral thought, in which virtue consists in the suppression or eradication of the passions in favor of pure reason. Rather, for Aristotle, emotions are judgment-laden phenomena that involve cognitive components and that can be felt appropriately or inappropriately depending on the circumstances and the character of the person feeling them.[27] This means that emotions are not external to virtue but rather integral to what it means to be virtuous. The virtuous person is not one who has managed to eliminate fear, anger, or shame, but rather one who has learned to feel these emotions in the right ways, toward the right objects, at the right times, and for the right reasons.

This insight has profound implications for understanding how Aristotle structures his account of individual virtues. Each virtue is paired with a particular emotion or class of emotions—what may be termed its characteristic pathos—and virtue with respect to that emotion consists in the development of an appropriate responsiveness to that emotional phenomenon. The virtue is not the emotion itself, nor is it the complete absence of the emotion, but rather a hexis that orients the person toward appropriate emotional experience and expression. As Nancy Sherman has argued in her influential study The Fabric of Character, Aristotelian virtue fundamentally consists in the proper integration of emotional life with rational judgment, such that "excellence of character is constituted both by the sentiments and by practical reasoning."[5][5] The virtues are, in this sense, ways of being emotionally intelligent, of having developed through habituation and rational reflection the capacity to perceive what emotions are called for and to feel them in measured and appropriate ways.
