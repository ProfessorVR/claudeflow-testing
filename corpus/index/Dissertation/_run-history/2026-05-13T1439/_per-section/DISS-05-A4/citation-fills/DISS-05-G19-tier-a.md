## 1. Claim and anchor

The §1.5 claim: each *pathos* has its possible *hexis*-correlate — courage as the *hexis* of fear, magnanimity as the *hexis* of anger, friendliness as the *hexis* of kindness; *aretē*, in the most general sense, is the cultivation of the *hexis* that fits the *kairos*. This **virtue-pathos pairing** thesis is the architectural ground for the chapter's claim that the *Rhetoric*'s catalog of *pathē* maps onto the NE's catalog of virtues via the hexis-as-mean-of-pathos formula.

### 2.2 Heidegger GA 18 §17–§18 (hexis-as-mean-of-pathos)

**Pipeline**: `corpus/index/Heidegger - Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy/`
**Unit**: **U-FP3c** §17b-beta (pp. 124–127, *mesotēs* analysis)

Per `phase2-u-fp3c.md` §17b-beta:

> Hexis is itself a basic determination of *aretē*: "according to its being-origin, *aretē* is a *hexis*, a being-composed as to..., in terms of the moment" (NE B 5, 1106a 13). … For the being of human beings, there is no *meson kat' auto*: "for our being, characterized by particularity, no unique and absolute norm can be given" (p. 126). **The mean is not a property of a *pragma* but the aptitude for *maintaining the mean*, which means nothing other than *seizing the moment*.**

## 3. Verbatim from corpus/index

### From `aristotle-ne-03.json` (NE III.7, 1115b17–24) — courage as mean of fear:

> The man, then, who faces and who fears the right things and from the right motive, in the right way and from the right time, and who feels confidence under the corresponding conditions, is brave; for the brave man feels and acts according to the merits of the case and in whatever way reason directs.

### From `aristotle-ne-04.json` (NE IV.5, 1125b29–1126a3) — mildness (praotēs) as mean of anger:

> The man who is angry at the right things and with the right people, and, further, as he ought, when he ought, and as long as he ought, is praised. This will be the good-tempered man, then, since good temper is praised. For the good-tempered man tends to be unperturbed and not to be led by passion, but to be angry in the manner, at the things, and for the length of time, that the rule dictates.

### From BCAP §17b-beta (p. 124, the hexis-pathos mean-mechanism):

> The relation of *hexis* and *aretē* is sharpened through *mesotēs*. The term comes from medicine: the healthy condition of human beings as a *meson*. Aristotle transposes this medical concept to ethics with a concrete glance toward the specific difference in the basic sense of being dealt with (p. 125). … The mean is now taken as *hexis* in the sense of *taxis*, the "being-apportioned" of that which comes up for resolution. It is **not a fixed property but a way of comporting oneself**, aiming at the *mesou stochastikē* — the "being-composed that sees and is open to the situation" (NE B 5, 1106b 9; 1106b 28).

## 6. Author-review request

The "magnanimity = hexis of anger" pairing is non-standard. Aristotle's actual mean-of-anger virtue is *praotēs* (mildness, NE IV.5); *megalopsychia* (magnanimity, NE IV.3) is the mean-of-claim-to-honor, with no direct pairing to anger. The §1.5 prose should be reviewed:
- If the author intends *praotēs*, replace "magnanimity" → "mildness" or "good temper" (*praotēs*)
- If the author intends a non-standard reading where magnanimity is the hexis-correlate of anger via the "great-souled person's deserved indignation" theme, this is a substantial interpretive move requiring its own flag
