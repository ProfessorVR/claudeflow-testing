## Item 1 — §1.4 L107 `pathe/pathos?` user-internal uncertainty marker

### Full sentence in current §1.4

> "The *Rhetoric*'s definitions confirm this composite structure: each emotion is a desire of a certain kind, attended by pain or pleasure, directed at a certain kind of object, under a certain evaluative description. The composite is not several parallel components stacked together but a single articulated determination in which the cognitive evaluation, the conative orientation, the hedonic tonality, and the physiological alteration are mutually constitutive aspects of one and the same `\textit{pathe/pathos?}`."

### Philosophical / grammatical analysis

The sentence ends with a singular reference ("one and the same X") to a single concrete emotion (fear, anger, pity, shame). Tension:

- **Grammatical**: *pathē* is the Greek plural; *pathos* is the singular. "One and the same *pathē*" is grammatically ill-formed.
- **Terminological** (per dissertation's §1.4 ¶2 register-key established in marathon): *pathos* is reserved for the general/ontological sense (Met Δ 21 alterability); *pathē* is reserved for the Rhetoric's concrete catalog (anger/fear/pity/shame).
- **Conflict**: The grammatically-correct singular is *pathos*, but *pathos* triggers the equivocation the chapter carefully disambiguates.

### Three options (ranked by recommended preference)

#### Option A (recommended — preserves register without grammatical strain)
> "...mutually constitutive aspects of one and the same *pathos*-token of the *pathē* catalog."

*Why*: explicitly names the catalog-membership ("of the *pathē* catalog") while using the grammatically-singular *pathos* as a token-of-type construction. Forecloses equivocation: this *pathos* is a member of the *pathē* class, not the general ontological sense.

#### Option B (preserves register but adds qualifier)
> "...mutually constitutive aspects of one and the same *articulationally concrete pathos*."

*Why*: uses the dissertation's coined umbrella term "articulational concretion" as the disambiguating qualifier. The singular *pathos* paired with "articulationally concrete" foregrounds the catalog register without name-collision.

#### Option C (sidesteps Greek terminology — least preferred)
> "...mutually constitutive aspects of one and the same emotion."

*Why*: avoids the equivocation by using the English. Loses some technical density but is the safest if the chapter's register-discipline is paramount.

#### NOT recommended:
- "...one and the same *pathē*" — grammatically wrong (plural-as-singular).
- "...one and the same *pathos*" (bare) — re-equivocates with the general-ontological sense the chapter carefully separated.

## Item 2 — §1.3 L9 `\footnote{Quotation, definition, point.}` placeholder

### Context
The L9 footnote anchors a Burke / Hazlitt identification discussion; the adjacent footnote already contains the Hazlitt RoM 83-84 quotation. The placeholder is for **Burke's own definition** of identification.

### Burke RoM (1950) availability
- **Source PDF**: `corpus/rhetorical_ontology/Burke, Kenneth - A Rhetoric of Motives_(1950)_[My Copy].pdf` (verified present, ~13,886 lines of OCR text)
- **Canonical pages**: RoM pp. 19-22 ("Identification" + "Identification and 'Consubstantiality'") and RoM pp. 55-56 ("Identification" subsection in "Traditional Principles of Rhetoric")

### Three candidate quotations (with verified RoM pagination)

#### Candidate A — The canonical "consubstantiality" definition (RoM pp. 20-21)
> "A is not identical with his colleague, B. But insofar as their interests are joined, A is identified with B. Or he may identify himself with B even when their interests are not joined, if he assumes that they are, or is persuaded to believe so... Here are ambiguities of substance. In being identified with B, A is 'substantially one' with a person other than himself. Yet at the same time he remains unique, an individual locus of motives. Thus he is both joined and separate, at once a distinct substance and consubstantial with another." (Burke, *RoM*, pp. 20-21)

*Why*: this is THE canonical Burke identification-definition cited in nearly all Burke scholarship; explicitly introduces consubstantiality; pairs cleanly with §1.3's Hazlitt 83-84 quote on identification.

#### Candidate B — Identification as compensatory to division (RoM p. 22)
> "Identification is affirmed with earnestness precisely because there is division. Identification is compensatory to division. If men were not apart from one another, there would be no need for the rhetorician to proclaim their unity. If men were wholly and truly of one substance, absolute communication would be of man's very essence." (Burke, *RoM*, p. 22)

*Why*: this passage articulates the rhetorical/dialectical function of identification (against the backdrop of division) — particularly resonant if §1.3's argument frames identification as overcoming separation.

#### Candidate C — Identification as common-ground rhetorical operation (RoM p. 55)
> "You persuade a man only insofar as you can talk his language by speech, gesture, tonality, order, image, attitude, idea, identifying your ways with his. Persuasion by flattery is but a special case of persuasion in general. But flattery can safely serve as our paradigm if we systematically widen its meaning, to see behind it the conditions of identification or consubstantiality in general." (Burke, *RoM*, pp. 55-56)

*Why*: more pragmatic / less metaphysical; foregrounds identification as a rhetorical operation rather than an ontological condition; appropriate if §1.3's framing is procedural-rhetorical.
