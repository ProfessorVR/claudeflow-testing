# Phase 2 Paraphrastic Narrative — DISS-04-EMOTION

**Section**: §1.4 — Emotion: The Form of Desire Under Evaluative Disclosure (E-motion is Motion)
**Word count target**: 1,500–3,500 | **Actual word count**: ~2,950
**Purpose** (per Plan §8.1.2): paraphrastic reconstruction of the section's argument in the user's own voice; verifies the analyst has understood the argument before flagging weaknesses, and supplies the skeleton for Phase 3 cross-section synthesis.

---

The section opens with what its title-claim announces: emotion is the form desire takes when desire is disclosed under an evaluative description. The wordplay of E-*motion*-is-*Motion* is not ornamental; it is the section's compressed thesis. *Pathos*, on the reading I am developing in agreement with Heidegger, is a *kinēsis* of the soul, not an inner state housed beneath the surface of rational operations. It is, in Heidegger's formulation at *BCAP* 133, "a being-taken of human beings in their full being-in-the-world." Bodily states — anxiety, joy, the boiling about the heart — are not symptoms tagged onto a pre-existing psychic event; they belong to the very being of beings whose way of being is that of a finite, embodied, speaking life. Emotion is therefore inseparable from *phantasia* (DA III.3–8), and the etymology of the English word already encodes this kinetic ontology: *emotion* derives from Latin *emovēre*, a moving-out, with the noun-forming suffix -ion naming not a static feeling but the *event* of being-moved. The deeper Proto-Indo-European root *meue-* links *emotion* directly to *motion*, *momentum*, and *motivate*. Even at the level of etymology, the word announces what Aristotle and Heidegger philosophically secure: emotion is being-moved-by-appearance, the soul's kinetic exposure to what matters.

But *pathos* in Aristotle is not univocal. Before any constructive account can begin, a terminological reckoning is required. Aristotle uses *pathos* in at least four registers across his corpus: an ontological sense (*Met.* Δ 21, 1022b15 — pathos as alterability, the determination of any being that admits of being-affected); a perceptual-affective sense (*DA* III.7, 431a8-10 — the pleasure/pain valence co-given with *aisthēsis* itself, in which the soul "makes a sort of affirmation or negation, and pursues or avoids"); a rhetorical-emotional sense (*Rhet.* II.1, 1378a20-22 — the determinate higher-order emotions of the catalog: anger, fear, pity, shame); and a bodily-physiological sense (*DA* I.1, 403a30-b1 — the "boiling of the blood and warm substance about the heart" by which the formal structure of an emotion is realized in matter). These four senses overlap and shade into one another, and what one says of *pathos* at one register cannot, without further argument, be transferred to another.

I therefore introduce three coined terms to track the analytic distinctions that the chain requires. The most general is **basic affective valence**, the basic hedonic tonality co-given with every moment of being-there — with every perceiving, every thinking, every considering, and with every *pathos* and *pathē*. **Pathos simpliciter** is basic affective valence in the case where the object of sense is present: the hedonic coloring that accompanies actual perception. **Resonant orexis** is basic affective valence in the case where the object of sense is no longer present: the residual hedonic tonality that forms part of the *resonant kinēsis* persisting after the encounter. Both *pathos simpliciter* and *resonant orexis* belong to **basic affective valence**; the *pathē* of the *Rhetoric*, by contrast, are more articulationally concrete. The architectural thesis is that these are not two ontologically separate categories but **two articulational concretions of one ontological structure** — the preservative *paschein* (DA II.5, 417b2-7) realized at minimal articulation (pre-propositional but not pre-positional) and at maximal articulation (*doxa*-mediated, propositionally specified). This is the chapter's central distinction, and it is what fills a gap that Heidegger's lecture course leaves unaddressed.

Heidegger's treatment of *pathos* in *BCAP* recovers the structural unity that runs from alterability through being-moved-by-anger or fear, and his retrospective citation of the *Rhetoric* in *Being and Time* §29, H.138 — the *Rhetoric* as "the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being-with-one-another" — is decisive. But Heidegger's gloss does not distinguish the *pathē* of the *Rhetoric* from the basic affective valence of perception in a way that the chain I am developing requires. The drinking case Aristotle gives in *Movement of Animals* — perception, basic appetite, immediate action — is not *pathos*-less; the thirsty perceiver is already disposed toward the water as pursue-able. Without an articulational distinction internal to the *pathos*-structure, drinking becomes either a counter-example to the single-structure thesis or a phenomenon falling outside Heidegger's analysis. The articulational distinction resolves both: basic affective valence and the *pathē* are two concretions of one ontological structure, the same preservative *sōtēria*-*paschein* realized at different degrees of cognitive articulation.

The unifying ground is Aristotle's distinction in *DA* II.5, 417b2-7 between two senses of *paschein*. One is destruction by an opposite; the other is preservation in which a potential is brought into its actuality. Hearing a sound is *paschein* of the second kind: my hearing is brought to its full being, not destroyed. Being-moved by anger or fear is also preservative *paschein*. There is no mode of being-there from which pleasure or pain is absent; both basic affective valence and the *pathē* are species of preservative *paschein*. The axis along which they differ is, however, multi-dimensional: hedonic intensity (how pleasant or painful), existential weight (how mattering), and cognitive articulation (how propositionally specified) are three independent dimensions. The *pathē* are not simply more *intense* than basic valence; they are more *articulated*, and — often, not always — more existentially weighted. The drinking case may involve high hedonic intensity without propositional articulation; the considered grief one carries quietly may carry high articulation and weight without acute intensity. What separates basic affective valence from the *pathē* is therefore not a categorical division between two ontological strata but graduation along this multi-dimensional axis: an *articulational* graduation rather than a difference in kind. Walking down a dark alley one experiences diffuse uneasiness; on seeing a hooded figure with a perceived weapon, this passes into determinate fear. The hedonic tonality is unpleasant in both; what changes is the episode's articulational concretion.

The articulational distinction also recovers a pattern Heidegger's own intellectual trajectory presupposes. His retrospective placement of the *Rhetoric* as the first hermeneutic of mood, and his identification of the pathē as "the ground out of which speaking arises, and which what is expressed grows back into" (*BCAP* 176), parallels the *Being and Time* analytic of attunement (*Befindlichkeit*) as disclosing Dasein "prior to all cognition and volition" (SZ §29, H.135). The finding-oneself of the lectures becomes the attunement of *Being and Time*; the grounding direction is identical in both texts. The actualization chain reinstates that direction at the *pathos*-grade: basic *pathos simpliciter* is pre-*doxa*, pre-*logos*, prior to all cognition and volition; the chain therefore exhibits basic affective valence as the disclosive ground from which *doxa* and the *pathē* proceed.

The four senses of pathos in *Met.* Δ 21, glossed by Heidegger as a progression from alterability through actuality of alteration to harmful alteration to magnitude of harm, articulate *pathos* as the structure of being-occurrable-to that belongs to the way of being of a finite living being. All four senses are satisfied at the level of basic affective valence: *aisthēsis* is constitutionally alterable (sense 1); the *resonant aisthēma* and *pathos simpliciter*/*resonant orexis* are the actualities of perceptual and appetitive faculties (sense 2); pain is recognized within the normal sensory range, and excess sound deafens, excess light blinds, confirming harmful alteration at the perceptual level (sense 3); Aristotle's doctrine of perception-as-having-a-logos (*Sense* 7, 447b4-5) establishes a magnitude-structure (sense 4). Heidegger's reading of the fourfold as progressive narrowing specifies the magnitude-axis along which the same structure is realized at varying intensities and articulations — the same three-dimensional axis the articulational-concretion thesis specifies.

The primary definition of emotion in Aristotle's corpus is in the *Rhetoric*, not the *De Anima*: "the emotions are all those feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements, and that are also attended by pain or pleasure" (II.1, 1378a20-22). Heidegger reads this as a three-fold articulation: *metaballontes* (undergoing change); *diapherousi pros tas kriseis* (differentiating-ourselves-from-ourselves via position-taking); and the co-givenness of pleasure and pain as being-disposed-as-higher-or-lower (*BCAP* 115). The position-taking moment is not the externally-judged-from-without; it is the position-taking of being-there from out of the changed disposition. Crucially, the three-fold articulation is not first introduced at the level of the *pathē*. The second moment is already operative at the perceptual level: Heidegger reads perception itself as *mesotēs* with the character of *kritikon* — a being-positioned, a *dynamis* in the sense of *kritikē* (*BCAP* 126). Aristotle confirms this when he states that the perceiving soul makes an "affirmation or negation" and pursues or avoids (*DA* III.7, 431a8-12). Basic affective valence — the organism's pre-propositional orientation toward the pleasant and away from the painful — is therefore co-given with perception from the earliest moment of the chain. It satisfies the differentiation aspect pre-propositionally but not pre-positionally; *doxa* later re-articulates this position-taking propositionally.

What concretizes the *pathē* is *doxa* — the involuntary belief that the object is genuinely fearful, or genuinely slighting, or genuinely shameful — and the concretization is immediate: there is no perceptible interval between the formation of the belief and the onset of emotion (*DA* III.3, 427b21-24). The involuntariness of *doxa* is what gives emotion its phenomenological character as something that *befalls* the agent. One does not choose to be afraid; one finds oneself afraid because one has involuntarily taken-something-to-be threatening. *Doxa* is not a deliberate formation of a considered judgment; it is the involuntary taking-of-something-as-being-the-case, closer to perceptual recognition than to discursive inference. When the agent perceiving by sense that the beacon is fire recognizes the beacon-as-enemy because it is moving (*DA* III.7, 431b5-6), the *doxa* "this signals danger" may form almost instantaneously, and fear may follow without any perceptible interval.

The enmattered-accounts analysis of *DA* I.1, 403a25-b19 supplies the bodily moment of the architecture. The dialectician defines anger as desire for retaliation; the physicist as boiling of blood about the heart. Neither account is sufficient without the other. Anger is the desire for retaliation *realized in* the boiling of blood; the *pathē* are meaning-articulations whose meaning is inseparable from their bodily occurrence. Heidegger insists, "No division between psychic and bodily acts!" (*BCAP* 134). The shaking and the blood-rush are not symptoms of a hidden judgment; they are part of the way the judgment is bodily *there*. The bodily form is *logos*-articulated and the *logos* is bodily-bodied-forth — a double articulation. The MA causal chain — sense or thinking → imagination → desire → affections → organic parts → movement — confirms emotion's somatic-preparatory function: emotions are the preparation of the organic parts, the desire realized in physiological change (MA 8, 701b33-702a3). The same boiling of blood that DA names as the material component of anger is what MA names as the *pathē*-step preparing the organic parts.

One aspect of the *Rhetoric*'s definition remains unresolved: how can emotion modify judgment if *doxa* is a necessary condition for emotion's concretization? The answer is the **synchronic-diachronic distinction**. The chain runs synchronically from A₀ to A₄ within any single moment of *pathos*-actualization. But each actualization persists as dispositional ground for what follows, and emotion-once-concretized alters the conditions under which subsequent *doxai* are formed. The seminal text is *On Dreams* 460b3-11: under the sway of an active emotion the controlling sense — the corrective faculty that intercepts misleading *phantasmata* — is impaired, and *phantasmata* that would otherwise be filtered are taken as true. The result is a self-reinforcing recursion: initial *doxa* concretizes initial *pathē*; *pathē* impairs corrective faculty; subsequent *phantasmata* pass through unopposed; new *doxai* concretize further *pathē*. The recursion is asymmetric in time: prior *pathē* modulate present *doxa*-formation, which feeds forward into present *pathē*. The feedback loop is a *diachronic* modulation of *synchronic* transitions, not a new synchronic transition.

Within the diachronic register two distinct mechanisms operate. The first — **Stimmung-saturation** — modulates the *input* to *doxa*: a sustained *pathos* persists as tonal coloring that biases subsequent perception, so the basic valence delivered to *doxa* is shaped by prior residue. The cowardly person sees enemies in shadows. The second — **corrective-faculty impairment**, the *On Dreams* mechanism — modulates the *gating* of *doxa* itself: emotional excitement disables the controlling sense, and *phantasmata* normally intercepted pass the corrective gate unopposed. These are commonly conflated under "the feedback loop," but they operate at different points of the chain: mechanism 1 at the basic-valence input, mechanism 2 at the corrective gate within the A₂→A₃ transition. Both are diachronic modulations; neither adds a fresh synchronic transition. This temporal-dispositional persistence is what Aristotle analyzes under *hexis* (NE II.1, 1103a16-17, 1103a31-b2 — moral excellence comes about as a result of habit, through repeated actualization of the chain itself) and what Heidegger names *Geworfenheit*, thrownness (SZ §29, H.135-137; SZ §68b, H.340 — *Stimmung* temporalizes primarily in the past-modality of *Gewesenheit*, the having-been that currently conditions disclosure). The lectures' formulation that "*hexis* can have its genesis only in being-there itself" (*BCAP* 122) is the prefiguration of what *Being and Time* names *Geworfenheit*. The feedback loop is not a structurally distinct third direction; it is the thrownness-character of the chain. The chain runs synchronically from A₀ to A₄, but each moment's starting node is itself the having-been-shaped of prior runs: synchronically directional, diachronically saturated.

Emotion's specific causal function at M₃→A₄ under evaluatively complex conditions is the concretization of *orexis* into a determinate form of desire directed at a specific object under a specific evaluative description. The three-factor schema of *DA* III.10 is preserved without revision — *orexis* as moved mover, the realizable good as unmoved originator, the animal as that which is moved — but the moved mover gains an articulational concretion the bare formulation does not require. Fear is not something that happens before the desire to flee; fear *is* the desire to flee insofar as it is structured by the cognitive evaluation of an anticipated destructive evil and experienced in the somatic alteration of chilled blood. Anger is not something that happens before the desire to retaliate; anger *is* the desire to retaliate insofar as it is structured by the perception of an unjustified slight and experienced in the somatic alteration of heated blood. Each determinate emotion individuates *orexis* by specifying which conative orientation corresponds to which evaluative disclosure of the apparent good, and each draws upon a determinate pattern of somatic preparation (cooling of fear, heating of anger, sympathetic pain of pity) that configures the body for the action the emotion calls for. Where the apparent good is straightforwardly pleasant or painful — the drinking case of MA 7, 701a32-33 — basic affective valence suffices, and the chain runs without internal complexity. The conversion is reserved for the case in which *doxa* has done its articulational work.

The chapter therefore arrives at a **five-fold ontological structure** of which each *pathos* is a determinate concretion. (1) **The disposition**: each *pathos* is a determinate manner of finding-oneself-disposed — anger as finding-oneself-as-having-been-slighted-by-a-particular-person, shame as finding-oneself-as-having-fallen-short-before-an-audience-that-matters. (2) **The toward-which and the toward-whom**: each *pathos* has determinate intentional structure, both world-side and social-ontological — anger toward the slighter, pity toward the suffering, shame before the audience that matters. The *toward-whom* slot opens the *Mitsein*-dimension on which Heidegger's recovery of the *Rhetoric* turns. (3) **The bodily form**: hot rush of anger, cold of fear, hot flush of shame, welling of eyes in pity — constitutive bodily articulation, not symptomatic accompaniment. (4) **The temporal range**: each *pathos* co-temporalizes having-been, present, and projected-futural, with a primary ecstasis giving the affect its distinctive phenomenal character. Fear is primarily futural (anticipated evil), grounded in the having-been of the dispositional *oiesthai*, felt presently as bodily *tarachē*. Anger is primarily having-been (the slight has already occurred), recoiling in present pain, projecting forward in the prospective pleasure of vengeance. Shame, by Aristotle's own formulation in *Rhetoric* II.6, 1383b13-15, ranges across "bad things, past, present, or future." Pity is primarily present, unfolding past causes and the futural projection "might befall ourselves." (5) **The disclosive function: bringing to *krisis*, bringing to *logos***: anger brings the slighted to confront, fear brings the threatened to confer, shame brings the fallen-short to defend or reform, pity brings the witness to alleviate. Every *pathos* carries a *krisis*; only the *pathē* bring to *logos*, to speech with another, and it is this *logos*-articulation that distinguishes them.

The temporal range bears the chapter's heaviest ontological weight and is grounded directly in both Aristotle (De Memoria 449b10-30 on the three temporal modes of soul's relation to objects; Rhetoric I.11, 1370a28-33 on imagination grounding memory and expectation) and Heidegger (Being and Time §68 on the three ecstases; GA 18 §21 on fear as futural disclosure; GA 18, 175 on courage as fear's hexis-correlate; GA 18, 131 on the three rhetorical genres — deliberative, forensic, epideictic — corresponding to the three temporal orientations). Struever's gloss of *Alltäglichkeit* as "timefulness," the care for tense that defines practical life (Struever in *Heidegger and Rhetoric*, ed. Gross, pp. 110-113), names what the *pathē* themselves internally articulate.

The philosophical center of gravity of the five-fold is the claim that the *pathē* are the **ground of *logos*** — Heidegger's most concentrated recovery of Aristotle: "The *pathē* are not merely an annex of psychical processes, but are rather the ground out of which speaking arises, and which what is expressed grows back into" (*BCAP* 176). Speaking is not a master operation that draws on the *pathē* as resources; it arises out of the *pathē* and returns to them. Rhetoric is the cultivation of the dispositional ground that brings being-there to discourse; Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is therefore not a manual of persuasion-techniques but a phenomenology of disclosive structures through which being-there finds itself in the *polis*. The *pathē* are not psychological phenomena added to a prior account of cognition; they are the prior. They are the modes in which being-there has its world disclosed, on the basis of which both cognition and speaking become possible.

The *pathē* are, moreover, co-original with the *hexeis*, both fundamental concepts of being articulating the being-structure of beings whose way of being is one of being-affected-and-disposed [verbatim and BCAP page to be supplied manually per `feedback-missing-source-placeholder.md`]. Each *pathos* has its possible *hexis*-correlate: courage as the *hexis* of fear, magnanimity as the *hexis* of anger, friendliness as the *hexis* of kindness; *aretē* in the most general sense is the cultivation of the *hexis* that fits the *kairos*. The five-fold structure of any given *pathos* is therefore not a snapshot but a moment within a temporally extended cultivation; the *pathē* are the hinge between the singular event of being-affected and the durable ethical character that consolidates from many such events.

Finally, the disclosive role of the *pathē* depends on a feature the chapter names several times: emotion responds to appearance, not to corrected judgment. If *phantasia* supplies the appearance, *doxa* is the moment of apophantic assertion — taking-as-true; the *pathos* that follows is keyed to the appearance, not to whatever subsequent rational correction may apply. The sound mistaken for a rattlesnake produces real fear, even though the corrected judgment will dissolve the mistake. Art produces emotion, because the *phantasmatic* representation suffices for the affective movement even when the audience knows the represented event is unreal. Emotion can contradict subsequent judgment, as when knowing the rope is not a snake fails to neutralize the startle. Because *phantasia* generates appearances beyond the immediately present, the temporal depth of emotional life follows directly: to remember grief is to feel grief again; to anticipate pleasure is to feel pleasure now in relation to a not-yet. Fear is paradigmatically "pain or disturbance from an imagination of an evil to come" (*Rhetoric* II.5, 1382a22-23); the feared event does not exist, the *phantasma* does, and the soul moves as though the feared thing were present. *Phantasia* is the condition of possibility for the soul's being-moved by absences, possibilities, appearances, and counterfactuals — the condition of possibility for the entire range of higher-order *pathē*. To persuade, accordingly, is not primarily to offer proofs but to reshape the listener's *phantasmatic* field; the rhetor supplies images — concrete, narrative, affectively charged — that ground the subsequent formation of *doxa*, the movement of emotion, the act of judgment. Rhetoric is "the discipline in which the self-elaboration of Dasein is expressly executed" (*Heidegger and Rhetoric*, ed. Gross, p. 1).

Emotion, in its full Aristotelian sense, is the kinetic, affective, evaluative dimension of how the world appears to a finite, embodied, speaking being: grounded in *phantasia*, shaped through involuntary *doxa*, felt as pleasure or pain, prepared somatically, and expressed as motion toward or away from what the soul takes to be significant. Emotion is not an add-on to rationality, nor a disruption of cognition, nor a mere physiological disturbance, but the way the soul's self-movement discloses significance at all. The five-fold structure — disposition, intentionality, bodily form, temporal range, disclosive function — specifies the architecture of this disclosure for each of the determinate *pathē* the *Rhetoric* catalogs. Emotion, in its full Aristotelian sense, is the affective architecture of being-in-the-world. The cultivation of the *hexeis* corresponding to each *pathos* — the practical-ethical task of shaping one's dispositional ground over time — is the work that the next chapter takes up; the *kairos*-relativity of the appropriate *pathos*, the question of which emotion fits which situation, follows from the *hexis*-analysis; and the rhetorical case-studies through which the five-fold does its concrete diagnostic work occupy the chapters thereafter.
