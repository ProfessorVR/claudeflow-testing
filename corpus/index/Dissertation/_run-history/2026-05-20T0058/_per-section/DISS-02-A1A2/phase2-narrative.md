## I. The Architectural Function of §1.2

§1.2 is the decisive hinge of the actualization chain: it is at the perceptual transition from $A_1$ to $A_2$ that the physical world first acquires phenomenal determinacy for the ensouled body, and here that the material conditions for *phantasia* and every subsequent cognitive operation are laid down. The section's task is therefore architectural rather than expository. It deploys the hylomorphic analysis of Aristotelian *aisthēsis* as the structural ground from which the chapter's central proprietary thesis on perception — the *dual-trace thesis* — can be extracted. The destination is a precise specification of *what is deposited at A_2*: a single enmattered affection in the organ, one in substrate and two in being, comprising a *resonant aisthēma* (the persisting sensory form) and a *resonant orexis* (the persisting appetitive valence). What is laid down here is what *phantasia* will operate on at $A_3$.

The section opens by specifying $A_1$ not as a single state but as a constellation of first actualities. Three preconditions must obtain: the sensible object actively exercising its proper quality (with Aristotle's *Metaphysics* IX.6, 1048b10–34 distinction between "capable of being seen" and "being seen"); the sense-organ holding its capacity in first actuality at the second grade of the §1.1 potentiality schema — the level of the *hexis* poised for activation; and the medium properly disposed to transmit the sensible form, with the medium's affection sequential as *Sense and Sensibilia* 6, 447a3–7 makes explicit. Sound is genuinely successive; light, exceptionally, is transmitted instantaneously (446b8–9). Receptivity at this stage is structured, not inert. Heidegger's *Geworfenheit* (*Being and Time* §29, H.135) provides a structural parallel — Dasein finds itself always already disposed before any reflective judgment — and the structural-readiness point holds equally for the Aristotelian sense-faculty. Gibson's ecological framework offers a complementary contemporary register, restricted to structural affordances since Gibson is anti-mediation whereas Aristotle's hylomorphism is a doctrine of formal mediation.

## II. Hylomorphism and Discrimination

The central thesis is form-without-matter: the sense faculty receives the sensible form without the matter, as wax takes on the impress of a signet without the iron or gold (*De Anima* II.12, 424a18–24). The eye in seeing red does not become materially red; it becomes isomorphic with the quality. The sense and its organ are the same in fact but not in essence — the organ is a spatial magnitude, the sensory power a form or capacity in a magnitude (424a24–29). The sensory power is a *logos* — a proportion of the contraries proper to the sense's domain (II.11, 424a4–5; III.2, 426a27–b8). Each sense is constituted by a proportion: eye between light and dark, ear between high and low pitch. Perception is the organ's taking-on of the ratio the perceptible quality instantiates: the formal matching *is* the perceptual act. The destruction condition at III.2, 426b3–8 follows directly: when stimulus exceeds the organ's proportional range, the organ is affected as matter rather than as sense; the motion is real and genuine damage, but it is not perception. The destruction condition presupposes the hylomorphic analysis — if perception were merely material alteration, there would be no principled difference between perceiving and being damaged.

Heidegger's reading of Aristotelian perception in the *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* radicalizes the analysis structurally. *Aisthēsis*, in his treatment of *De Anima* B 11, is itself a *kritikon* — a *mesotēs* with the character of *kritikon*, an ability-to-separate one thing from another, a being-positioned toward possible objects that is itself a *dynamis* in the sense of *kritikē* (BCAP p. 126). Perception is not a passive imprinting but a discriminative event: in receiving the form, the sense faculty simultaneously exercises its critical capacity. Aristotle himself confirms the reading at *De Anima* III.7, 431a8–12, where the perceiving soul makes "a sort of affirmation or negation." White's secondary-literature correlate identifies *to kritikon* as the unifying capacity that connects perception, *phantasia*, and thought. The hylomorphic reception of form is therefore not a mere passive imprinting but a structurally discriminative event in which form-reception and discrimination are not two operations but one. What persists after the perceptual act is the form the faculty has received, sustained in the organ's material substrate. Frede's analysis of *phantasia* contests the reductive reading that *phantasiai* are "mere epiphenomena, the lingering after-images of sensations" (Frede 1992, p. 282); the resonant trace is rather a structured residue retaining formal character while being materially reconstituted in the organ's ongoing physiological activity.

## III. The Activity of Mover and Moved as One

§1.2 inherits from §1.1 a formal principle of the highest architectural importance: the activity of the mover and the activity of the moved are one and the same activity, though they differ in their being. The motion-level statement is *Physics* III.2, 202a13–20; the formula is "one in substrate, different in being" (III.3, 202a21–24; 202b13–14). Aristotle applies the formula directly to perception at *De Anima* III.2, 425b26–27: "the activity of the sensible object and that of the sense is one and the same activity, and yet the distinction between their being remains." The illustration: "a man may have hearing and yet not be hearing... but when that which can hear is actively hearing and that which can sound is sounding, then the actual hearing and the actual sound come about at the same time" (425b28–426a1). The actual hearing and the actual sound are the same actuality under two descriptions.

The "one in substrate, different in being" formula is *load-bearing across the section*. Introduced at the motion-level (*Physics* III.2–3), redeployed at *De Anima* III.2 for perception, applied again at III.7 to the coordination of perception and appetite, and reapplied in the dual-trace thesis below. The formula's persistence across contexts reflects the chain's architectural unity: a single ontological pattern — numerical identity under plural formal descriptions — iterates across levels, each application specifying a new relation that the substrate-level unity secures. The unity of mover and moved establishes a principle of correlation: the sensible world and the sentient organism are correlative poles of a single actualization. Heidegger's existential reading sharpens the point: Dasein is constituted by its *In-der-Welt-sein*, and the receptivity that characterizes Dasein's being-in is structured openness — the structural openness of a comporting being already disposed toward what it can receive (*Being and Time* §15–§18). The sense-organ's first actuality at $A_1$ instantiates this structural openness at the level of perceptual capacity.

## IV. Enmattered Paschein in Two Senses

Although form-without-matter is the defining hylomorphic characteristic of perception, the reception is not purely formal. Perception is an enmattered *alloiōsis* — a qualitative alteration occurring in and through the material constitution of the sense organ (DA II.5, 416b33–417a1). The eye is physically affected by light, the ear by sound, the flesh heated or cooled. These physiological changes are not mere accompaniments but the material substrate of the formal reception. The soul, as the form of a natural body having life potentially within it (II.1, 412a19–22), is inseparable from the body whose form it is.

Two kinds of *alloiōsis* must be distinguished, and Aristotle is explicit at *De Anima* II.5, 417a21–b2: "we do not use 'to be acted on' in one single sense, but in two: one is the destruction of one of two contraries by the other, the other is the preservation of what is potential by what is actual and like it." The first kind is corrupting; the contrary supplants what was there. The second is preservative — the geometer learning geometry, the builder acquiring the art, the sense-organ receiving a sensible form. Perception belongs to the preservative kind. When the eye sees red, the eye is not destroyed or replaced by red; its capacity to see is actualized, and the actualization is a fulfilment of that capacity rather than its negation (III.7, 431a3–5: "in the case of sense clearly the sensitive faculty already was potentially what the object makes it to be actually; the faculty is not affected or altered" in the corruptive sense).

The structural register at issue is *paschein* — being-affected — and the perceptual *alloiōsis* is the perceptual instance of *paschein* in its preservative mode. The full frame is given by Aristotle's fourfold articulation of *pathos* at *Metaphysics* Δ.21, 1022b15–21: (1) a quality in respect of which a thing can be altered; (2) the already actualized alterations; (3) especially injurious alterations; (4) experiences pleasant or painful when on a large scale. The four senses articulate *pathos* as the structure of *being-occurrable-to* belonging to the way of being of a finite living being. Heidegger reads the fourfold as a progressive narrowing — from general alterability through the *energeia* of alteration to the determination of an alteration as harmful, to the magnitude of the harm — and gathers the four under a single thesis that *pathos* in its widest reach names the structure by which a living being is brought into its proper actuality through encounter with the world (BCAP pp. 131–132). Each of the four senses is satisfied at the perceptual level: alterability is the constitutive feature of the perceptual *dynamis*; actual alterations are realized in the resonant *aisthēma*; harmful alterations operate at the limit where excess destroys the *logos* (III.2, 426a30–b8); magnitudes are the structural feature by which perception admits of intensity. The fourfold and the preservative-*paschein* distinction are taken up at the level of higher-order *pathē* in the emotion section; here the fourfold serves as the frame for the perceptual case alone. Two consequences follow: the perceptual *alloiōsis* is enmattered through and through, and the preservative character ensures that the form received *leaves a trace*.

## V. Resonant Kinēsis: The Persisting Impression

The completion of the perceptual act does not terminate the *kinēsis* initiated by the sensible object; it deposits a persisting impression in the ensouled body. *De Anima*: "even when the sensible objects are gone the sensings and imaginings continue to exist in the sense-organs" (III.2, 425b23–26). *On Dreams*: "the affection due to their operation is present in the organs of sense not only when the perceptions are actualized, but even when they have departed" (2, 459a24–28). The mechanism is illustrated by a physical analogy: "that part which has been heated by something hot, heats the part next to it, and this propagates the affection onwards to the starting-point... since actual perceiving is a qualitative change" (459b1–6). Aristotle names the residual affections *aisthēmata* — the material of dreams, after-images, imagination, and the beginnings of memory (460b2–3).

The chapter adopts the term *resonant kinēsis* for the residual motion understood as a whole. The coinage marks two features: first, the trace is *kinēsis* — a motion, not a static imprint, with the ongoing character of all Aristotelian *kinēsis*; second, it is *resonant* — it continues reverberating within the organ like sound continuing in a stilled bell, retaining the formal structure of the original motion while operating in the absence of its originating cause. White captures the reverberating character precisely (described as a "lingering, resonating, echoing presence of sensible forms freed from their original matter," p. 498). The *resonant kinēsis* is not the same in kind as the original perceptual act; it is a continuing exercise of the same formal structure, sustained by the organ's material substrate in the original cause's absence.

A terminological distinction must be made explicit at this point. The chapter operates with two related but non-synonymous terms: *pathos simpliciter* and *resonant orexis*. *Pathos simpliciter* names the perceptual event itself in its affective aspect — the basic-valence dimension of the perceptual actualization as an event, the pleasant-or-painful that is co-given with every perceiving. *Resonant orexis* names the trace of that *pathos simpliciter* persisting after the object's withdrawal — the basic valence as the dispositional residue deposited by the perceptual event in the organ. The two are related as actuality to residue. Von Uexküll's concept of *Umwelt* offers a structurally productive analogue: the persisting impression actively shapes the organism's subsequent encounters, determining what it notices, what it ignores, and how it responds (von Uexküll, *A Foray*, pp. 222–224). The analogy is restricted to the basic-valence level, where it holds equally for rational and non-rational animals.

## VI. The Dual-Trace Thesis

The persisting impression deposited by the completed perceptual act is not a single, undifferentiated residue but a *dual trace* — the resonant *aisthēma* (the persisting sensory form) and the resonant *orexis* (the persisting appetitive valence). Every perception is simultaneously a discrimination of a sensible quality and an evaluation of that quality as pleasant or painful; every perception accordingly deposits both a formal trace and an affective trace. The dual trace is *one in substrate* — a single enmattered affection in the organ — and *two in being*, corresponding to the perceptual and appetitive faculties' distinct ways of being.

The argument proceeds along two converging lines. The first is *analytic*. Aristotle establishes the entailment at *De Anima* II.3, 414b1–6: "If any order of living things has the sensory, it must also have the appetitive ... whatever has a sense has the capacity for pleasure and pain and therefore has pleasant and painful objects present to it, and wherever these are present, there is desire." Every act of sensation analytically entails affective valence. Aristotle develops the coordination directly at III.7, 431a8–14: "to perceive then is like bare asserting or thinking; but when the object is pleasant or painful, the soul makes a sort of affirmation or negation, and pursues or avoids the object ... the faculty of appetite and avoidance are not different ... from the faculty of sense-perception; but their being is different." Perception and appetite are numerically one in substrate but differ in being. The residual motion that persists must inherit this dual structure. The enmattered-accounts argument from DA I.1, 403a25–b19 supplies independent confirmation: the affections of soul are *logoi enyloi*; if the residual motion is an enmattered *pathos* with two formally distinct aspects, the residue preserves both.

The second line is a *modus tollens*. If the residual motion were exhausted by its formal-epistemic aspect — if what persisted were only the resonant *aisthēma* — then mere retention of sensible form would suffice to originate pursuit and avoidance. But Aristotle insists that bare perception is "like bare asserting or thinking"; formal content alone is motivationally inert. The source of all movement is appetitive: "that which originates movement must be specifically one, viz. the faculty of appetite as such" (III.10, 433b10–14). The motivational autonomy of the appetitive trace is therefore a structural requirement of Aristotle's own commitment to appetite as the source of animal movement.

What the *resonant orexis* preserves is what the chapter names *hedonic tonality* — the pleasant-or-painful aspect that is co-given with every perceiving and persists after the object withdraws. The term is grounded in Heidegger's reading of Aristotelian *hēdonē* in BCAP. Heidegger insists that *hēdonē* is what it is "in the moment," not in time as a determinate span (BCAP p. 164); and that *hēdonē* and *lypē* are co-given with every mode of being-there — with every *pathos*, every perceiving, every thinking (BCAP p. 166). Hedonic tonality is not an add-on but a constitutive aspect of the perceptual event; the *resonant orexis* is the persisting trace of this constitutive aspect.

The dual-trace thesis delivers the structural payoff for the same-chain-identity claim established in §1.1. The chain runs through perception → *phantasma* → desire-actualization not because two parallel chains have been collapsed into one but because perception itself, in its very structure, deposits both a formal and an appetitive trace. The perception-to-movement chain *is* the actualization-of-desire chain — not by stipulation, but because the perceptual *pathos simpliciter* analytically entails the appetitive aspect that drives subsequent action. The dual trace deposited at $A_2$ is *basic valence* in its perceptual instance; the higher-order *pathē* that Aristotle catalogs in the *Rhetoric* arise only downstream of *doxa*'s ratification at $M_2 \rightarrow A_3$, where the cognitive apparatus articulates the perceptual basic valence into the determinate evaluative concretions the emotion section develops. The structural distinction between *pathos simpliciter* as basic valence and the determinate higher-order *pathē* is load-bearing for the chapter.

## VII. A_2 as Ground of A_3

The completed perceptual act, together with the dual residual trace it deposits, constitutes $A_2$ — not merely an endpoint but a *threshold*: the state of the ensouled body after perception has occurred. The architectural role follows from the iterated chain-structure: each completed actuality is simultaneously the terminus of its antecedent motion and the unmoved originator of its successor. $A_2$ is the terminus of the perceptual motion from $A_1$ and the unmoved originator of the phantastic motion $M_2 \rightarrow A_3$. *Phantasia*, on Aristotle's canonical definition at *De Anima* III.3, 428b10–17, is a *kinēsis* arising from actual sensation and necessarily similar in character to the sensation from which it arose. The similarity is grounded in the *resonant kinēsis*: the *phantasma* inherits both the formal aspect (resonant *aisthēma*, making the image recognizable) and the affective aspect (resonant *orexis*, making the image matter to the perceiver). Without the resonant *aisthēma* there would be no formal structure for *phantasia* to preserve, recombine, or transform; without the resonant *orexis* the *phantasma* would be a mere formal schema rather than the charged image that drives desire, aversion, and deliberation.

## VIII. Section Architecture Recap

§1.2's contribution to the chain is double. *Architecturally*, it specifies $A_2$ as the threshold from which the chain's next stage can originate. *Conceptually*, it grounds the dual-trace thesis in textually-explicit Aristotelian commitments: (a) form-without-matter, (b) the preservative kind of *paschein*, (c) the one-in-substrate-different-in-being identity of perception and appetite, (d) the persistence of residual *aisthēmata*. The thesis is interpretively-flagged but extensively citation-anchored: 44 of 160 claims rest on T1 primary citations; the remaining 95 T3 claims all carry explicit interpretive flagging.
