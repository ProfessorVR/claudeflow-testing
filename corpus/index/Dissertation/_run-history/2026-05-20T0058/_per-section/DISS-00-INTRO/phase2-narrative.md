# Phase 2 Paraphrastic Narrative — DISS-00-INTRO

**Section**: §1.0 — Introduction: Rhetorical Phantasia: The Soul's Temporal Medium
**Word count target**: 1,500–3,500 | **Actual word count**: ~2,650
**Purpose** (per Plan §8.1.2): paraphrastic reconstruction of the introduction's argument in the user's own voice; verifies the analyst has understood the argument before flagging weaknesses, and supplies the skeleton for Phase 3 cross-section synthesis.

---

The introduction frames a thesis that the rest of the dissertation will work to discharge: Aristotle's *phantasia* is best understood not as a peripheral faculty of imagination but as the soul's **temporal medium** — the kinetic and affective capacity through which the world enters the soul as continuous, narratively-synthesized experience, and through which the soul, in turn, navigates, recombines, and re-presents that experience to itself across past, present, and future. The chapter to which this introduction is prefatory traces the architectural sequence — the *actualization chain* — through which that experiential continuity is generated, with *phantasia* standing at its temporal center as the faculty that sustains the cohesive narrative within which every subsequent motion of the soul becomes intelligible.

The introduction opens with the canonical framing of *phantasia*'s place in *De Anima*. Aristotle's central project in *De Anima* is to answer "What is the soul, and what does it do?" (*DA* I, 402a5-7), and within that project *phantasia* occupies a position that resists easy translation. It is, on the most-cited textual definitions, "that in virtue of which an image arises for us" and a faculty "in virtue of which we discriminate and are either in error or not" (*DA* III.3, 428a1-3); it is the capacity through which images serve thinking as "if they were contents of perception" (*DA* III.7, 431a15-17); and it is what allows the soul to calculate and deliberate "what is to come by reference to what is present" (*DA* III.7, 431b6-8). The secondary literature converges around this functional position. Dorothea Frede's threefold — capacity, activity-or-process, and product-or-result — names the structural ambiguity of the term, which is unified only by the broader sense of "appearance" (Frede 1992, 279). Martha Nussbaum's phrase, that *phantasia* "ties abstract thought to concrete perceptible objects or situations" (Nussbaum, 265), and Debra Hawhee's note on the term's translation difficulties (Hawhee 141) frame the term's semantic span. Kevin White's interpretation, on which the introduction leans most heavily, locates *phantasia* as an intermediary between sensation and thought, persisting after sensory experience ceases and storing the impressions on which deliberation operates: "once having gained a foothold in time by means of *phantasia*, *nous* is capable of putting its survey of the past into the service of a calculation of the future" (White 1985, 502).

What the introduction adds to this received picture is the architectural reading that *phantasia*'s function is fundamentally **temporal-kinetic**. The fundamental temporal claim is made explicit at line 17: *phantasia*, the introduction argues, "lies at the core of human being through its capacity to create a continuous narrative of experience, enabling humans to navigate their reality cohesively in time." White's observation that "in the noetic or human soul, *phantasia* is a step in man's progression beyond time altogether, enabling him at certain points and for certain periods to pass beyond the confinements of sense to a consideration of the timeless nature of things" (White 503) is invoked as ratifying this temporal centrality.

The introduction then makes its first architectural move: a **four-event temporal analysis** of *phantasia*'s operation. At T₁, the perceiver encounters a sensible object — an apple is seen, a sound is heard — and this encounter initiates a movement into the sensory apparatus. At T₂, the soul undergoes *kinēsis* in the act of perception itself: the perceptual capacity is actualized by the object of sense. At T₃, "a kind of inner likeness, a resonant motion, is generated and remains in the soul" — and crucially, the introduction insists this is NOT memory; it is a *re-presenting* of a past perception detached from the original object. At T₄, at some later moment, *phantasia* "draws upon or reactivates the resonant motion to generate an internal image (*phantasma*)" — in an act of recollection, imagination, reasoning, or opinion-formation.

The four-event analysis is the introduction's most compressed statement of the entire chain that the chapter develops, and the term "resonant motion" at T₃ is a precursor of the coined term *resonant kinēsis* that the methodology subsection will name canonically and that §1.2 will define formally. *Phantasia*, the introduction concludes from this analysis, is "neither a mere residue of sensation nor an autonomous cognitive act; rather, it is the mediating power through which sensory motion persists, transforms, and becomes available for higher-order operations of the soul." To understand *phantasia* adequately, the introduction insists, we must first situate it within the broader ontological framework of motion and time — "the very conditions under which anything can appear, endure, and recur within the soul's experience."

What the four-event analysis is meant to capture is a structural feature of the soul's temporal life: the continuity between perceiving and remembering, between remembering and imagining, between imagining and deliberating. The movement or procession of each completed actualization, the introduction insists at line 26, "represents not a series of discrete events within a series of discrete temporal moments but a continuous unfolding in which each stage is the necessary condition for the next" — much, the introduction adds, as Heidegger's analysis of temporality reveals Dasein's Being as always already stretched along between past, present, and future. The Heideggerian gesture here is brief; the dissertation's deeper engagement with Heidegger is deferred to subsequent chapters (most extensively to §1.4 and to a forthcoming "Chapter X" that the introduction names with a `???` placeholder). What the introduction needs from Heidegger at this moment is the formal-temporal claim that lived experience is stretched, continuous, and ecstatically structured — a claim Aristotle, on the introduction's reading, gestures toward without articulating in Heideggerian terms.

The introduction then footnotes — at the inlinenote at line 30 — Papachristou's analysis of the *three grades of phantasia* in *De Anima*: an indeterminate or *aoristos phantasia*, a perceptual *phantasia* shared with non-rational animals, and a deliberative or *bouleutikē phantasia* belonging to rational beings alone. These distinctions, the inlinenote acknowledges, will be examined in Chapter IV; the introduction's claim at this point is only that the three grades correspond to different temporal depths of the *phantasma*'s formation, ranging from the near-immediate afterimage to the complex deliberative image. The voluntariness of *phantasia* — its susceptibility to being summoned at will — and its relation to belief (*doxa*) are similarly deferred to subsequent chapters.

The framing subsection closes with the **dissertation's central thesis**, restated at line 36: anchored in the principle of motion and time, *phantasia* "marks the pivotal transitional process whereby the world, through sensation, enters the soul as kinetic and affective resonance, and whereby the soul, through the activation of *phantasmata*, becomes capable of internally navigating, recombining, and imaginatively transforming that world." The thesis carries an immediate rhetorical implication: to influence someone's *phantasia* is "to shape not merely their beliefs, but the very structure of their perceptual and affective reality — altering how they experience, interpret, and anticipate the world as an integrated, temporally unfolding whole." Rhetoric, on this framing, is not the manipulation of judgments but the cultivation of the temporal-affective-imaginative field within which judgments form at all.

The **Methodology subsection** (lines 39-48) then makes the chapter's most consequential commitments. The first is anti-peripheral: *phantasia* is not a faculty for daydreams but the faculty on which every waking act of cognition depends, "for the soul never thinks without an image" (*DA* III.7, 431b2). The second is architectural: *phantasia* is "the temporal medium through which the soul's successive perceptual instants are held together as single cohesive narrative, binding the remembered past, the attended present, and the anticipated future into the continuous temporal horizon within which human life... unfolds." The third — the most ambitious — is the **anti-internalist thesis**: the soul is not a cognitive faculty that subsequently enters into rhetorical or affective relations with its world; it is constituted, from the moment of its first perceptual encounter, as a faculty whose kinetic and affective dimensions are constitutive rather than supplementary. The capacity for being-affected, the introduction insists, "is not a secondary feature added to cognition but the mode of being that cognition itself specifies under a different *logos*."

This last formulation is what the highlighted text at line 41 makes visible: the soul under cognition and the soul under emotion are not two souls but one soul under two different *logos*-descriptions. The phrasing borrows the language of Aristotle's enmattered-accounts doctrine (*DA* I.1, 403a25-b19) — the dialectician describes anger as a desire for retaliation; the natural philosopher as a boiling of blood about the heart; both describe the same event under different *logos*-articulations — and extends it to the relation between cognition and being-affected as a whole. Cognition and emotion, on the introduction's reading, are not separate faculties in metaphysical communication; they are the same psychic event under different descriptive specifications. The full defense of this rhetorical framing, the introduction acknowledges, is developed in a chapter referred to with `???` — a placeholder for what will become Chapter X's engagement with Heidegger, Rickert, Uexküll, and Burke. For the present chapter's purposes, the framing supplies the motivating stakes: the actualization chain is not a metaphysically neutral apparatus but the structure through which the rhetorically constituted soul unfolds its perceptual and appetitive capacities in engagement with a world that matters to it.

The Methodology then names the chapter's specific architectural project: a **sustained interpretive reading** of Aristotle's account of *aisthēsis*, *phantasia*, cognition, and appetitive action as a single architectural sequence — "a progressive chain of actualizations through which the journey from sensible object to completed action is depicted as a series of iterated motions linking completed actualities." Each node in the chain is an actuality; each link is a motion; each motion produces the actuality that the next motion will presuppose. This *actualization chain*, the introduction explicitly disclaims, is not given by Aristotle in any single treatise: it is the user's interpretive construction, a unified architectural reading that integrates Aristotle's scattered analyses across *De Anima*, *Physics*, *Metaphysics*, *Sense and Sensibilia*, *On Memory*, *On Dreams*, *Movement of Animals*, and the *Rhetoric*.

The chain organizes Aristotle's analyses into **five principal nodes**: A₀ (motion and time as the first actuality); A₁ (the preconditions for perception — sensible object actually exercising its sensible power, sense-organ in its first actuality as capacity, rational souls existing in the world); A₂ (completed perception together with the dual residual trace it deposits — *resonant aisthēma* and *resonant orexis*); A₃ (the *phantasma* proper, generated from the *resonant kinēsis* at A₂); and A₄ (the completed cognitive actuality produced when the rational cognitive apparatus engages the *phantasma* — intellection, memory, deliberative or speculative thinking, or the committal dimension of *doxa*). Subsequent stages — the *orectic* motion through which the cognitive actuality, when it discloses a realizable good, mobilizes *orexis* into determinate appetitive action — are traced in the chapters on emotion and action and are not numbered in this enumeration.

The methodology subsection lists the architectural components Aristotle himself supplies — the three-factor schema (*DA* III.10, 433b12-25); the definition of motion (*Phys.* III.1, 201a10-14); the definition of time (*Phys.* IV.11, 219b1-2); the priority-of-actuality doctrine (*Met.* IX.8, 1049b12-1050a16); the hylomorphic account of perception (*DA* II.12, 424a17-24); the treatment of residual motion (*On Dreams* 2, 459a24-460b3); the dependence of thought on images (*DA* III.7, 431a15-17 and III.8, 432a3-9); the enmattered-accounts framework (*DA* I.1, 403a25-b19); and the practical syllogism (*MA* 7, 701a7-35). The architectural work of the rest of the chapter, the introduction acknowledges with disarming candor, is to integrate these components into a unified reading "and to advance several interpretive readings that go beyond what Aristotle explicitly states": the ontological incompleteness of time without soul (developed in §1.1); the dual-trace thesis (developed in §1.2); the orthogonal committal dimension of *doxa* (developed in §1.3); and the gate-keeping condition for *doxastic* commitment (developed in §1.3 and §1.4).

The final subsection, **The Kinetic Schema: A Three-Factor Structure of Perceptual Motion** (lines 49 onward), then begins the chapter's substantive work by establishing the ontological prerequisites. *Phantasia*, the introduction insists at line 51, ontologically depends on *aisthēsis* in an asymmetrical relationship — it is "impossible without sensation" (*DA* III.3, 428b11-12). Before analyzing *phantasia*, we must analyze *aisthēsis*; before analyzing *aisthēsis*, we must establish the three ontological components that constitute all perceptual events: actuality, motion, and time. The relationship is asymmetric. Actuality is prior in account and substance (*Met.* IX.8, 1049b4-10): the potential is intelligible only through the actuality it is potential *for*, and each motion takes its name from its terminus, not its starting point (*Phys.* V.5, 229b25). Motion is the fulfillment of a potential *qua* potential (*Phys.* III.1, 201a10) — the ontological hinge connecting one completed actuality to the next. Time is the number of motion with respect to before and after (*Phys.* IV.11, 219b1) — derivative from motion and therefore doubly dependent on actuality. The chain's structural sequence proceeds chronologically because that is how the ensouled body lives through the event; but at each node, the completed actuality is what makes the preceding motion — and its temporal measure — intelligible. The list is read forward; but it is **understood backwards**.

The three-factor schema itself is then introduced via the canonical *DA* III.10 quotation (433b12-25): "All movement involves three factors, (1) that which originates the movement, (2) that by means of which it originates it, and (3) that which is moved." In its absolute sense, the term "unmoved mover" belongs to the Prime Mover of *Metaphysics* XII (1072a23-26) and *Physics* VIII (256a-260a); but Aristotle deploys the schema, the introduction observes, with **relative unmoved movers**. At each level of motion, something functions *as* the unmoved originator relative to that particular motion-event, without being the cosmological Unmoved Mover. In perception, the sensible object plays this role; in cognition, the *phantasma*; in appetitive action, the realizable good disclosed by *doxa*. The schema is, on the introduction's reading, **scale-invariant**: the same structure applies at the cosmic level (Prime Mover → spheres → sublunary motion) and at the psychological level (sensible objects → sense-faculty → perceiver). The three factors are formally identical; only the domain changes. This, the introduction insists, "is not accidental; it reflects Aristotle's conviction that psychic events are a species of natural motion, subject to the same structural principles as any other *kinēsis* the natural world exhibits."

The interlock of the actuality-first architecture and the three-factor schema is then made precise: **the completed actuality at each node is what serves as the relative unmoved originator of the next motion.** A₀ (motion and time as first actuality) is what lets A₁ be what it is. A₁ (the sensible object exercising its sensible power, together with the sense-organ in its first actuality) is the unmoved originator of the perceptual motion A₁ → A₂. A₂ (the completed perceptual act with its dual residual trace) is the unmoved originator of the *phantastic* motion M₂ → A₃. A₃ (the *phantasma* proper, crystallized by *phantasia* at the central organ) is the unmoved originator of the cognitive motions M₃ → A₄. A₄ (the completed cognitive actuality), when its content discloses a realizable good, is the unmoved originator of the *orectic* motion through which appetitive action proceeds. Each completed actuality, the introduction concludes, is **both** a terminus that *names* the motion preceding it *and* an unmoved source that *initiates* the motion following it. Motion takes its name from its end; the end, once achieved, becomes the beginning of what comes next.

The chain, accordingly, is not a sequence of different *kinds* of events but the **same motion-structure iterated** at increasing levels of complexity. At each stage, the moved mover is both patient and agent (*Phys.* III.3, 202a21-28) — the hinge that converts one motion into the next. Between each pair of hinges stands a completed actuality. Time measures the passage from node to node, and is itself intelligible only through the actualities that bound it.

Kenneth Burke's reading of Aristotle's *entelechy*, the introduction notes at line 66, provides an interpretive resource for understanding how the chain licenses itself. Burke observes that the *entelechy* introduces "another kind of priority, namely the 'principle' involved in a given form" — a priority that runs opposite to temporal sequence. A stage that follows another in time may be prior in form and substantiality: "man is 'prior' to boy because man has already attained its complete form whereas boy has not" (Burke, *Grammar* 261). The distinction Burke draws — between *temporal priority* and *priority in form and substantiality* — is, the introduction insists, crucial for the chain's structure. Each actuality in the chain stands in both relations to its neighbors. The chain is, accordingly, not a linear sequence of self-sufficient stages but a **nested architecture** in which each actuality licenses and is licensed by others in a reciprocal relation of temporal and formal priority. This reciprocal structure is what makes the chain *genuinely iterated* rather than merely sequential.

The introduction closes with the **chapter roadmap** at line 68: A₀ establishes the ontological ground (motion as *energeia ateles*, time as the countable number of motion); A₁ → A₂ develops the preconditions for perception, the hylomorphic reception of sensible form, the unity of mover and moved as a single *energeia* under two *logoi*, perception as enmattered preservative *alloiōsis*, the persisting residual motion (*resonant kinēsis*), and the dual-trace thesis; M₂ → A₃ traces the secondary actualization through which the residual trace is taken up by *phantasia* and constituted into the *phantasma* proper; A₃'s subsequent sections analyze the cognitive engagements of the *phantasma* at M₃ → A₄ — intellection, memory, deliberative-speculative *phantasia*, and the orthogonal committal dimension of *doxa*; the treatment of emotion and appetitive action — how the completed cognitive actuality, when it discloses evaluatively complex content, flips the latent *resonant orexis* into the determinate higher-order *pathē* — follows the cognitive analysis; the chapter closes with a synthesis that situates the chain within the dissertation's wider rhetorical project and prepares the transition to Chapter X's engagement with Heidegger, Rickert, Uexküll, and Burke. The diagram (a full TikZ rendering filling the rest of the section's file) supplies the visual articulation of the architecture the introduction names.

What the introduction promises, then, is a chapter that begins with Aristotle's ontology of motion and time, works through the architecture of perception and the residual motion that perception leaves behind, traces the formation of the *phantasma* and its cognitive engagements, accounts for the formation of opinion and the involuntary onset of emotion, and closes with the structure of completed action and the recursive return to a transformed world. The introduction commits the chapter to *phantasia* as the temporal medium of the soul, to motion and time as the ontological substrate of everything that follows, and to the rhetorically constituted soul as the framing within which the entire architecture is to be understood. The chapter's signature interpretive moves — *energeia ateles*-temporality, the dual-trace residual motion, the *phantasma*'s inheritance of dual structure, the orthogonal dimension of *doxa*, the articulational concretion of basic affective valence into the *pathē*, the synchronic chain saturated diachronically by character — are all forward-promised here, with their specific delivery deferred to §§1.1, 1.2, 1.3, and 1.4 respectively. Some promises (Chapter X's full rhetorical-ontology defense) await chapters not yet written. The introduction's gold-standard function — to frame, to commit, to forward-promise — is performed at the level of architecture; what remains to be done is the citational and stylistic alignment that brings the introduction up to the developed gold-standard of §1.4 and the specific Aristotelian textual ground that §§1.1-1.4 supply.
