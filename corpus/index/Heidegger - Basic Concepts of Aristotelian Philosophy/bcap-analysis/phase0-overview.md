# Phase 0: Bird's-Eye Overview of Heidegger's *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* (GA 18)

**Source**: Martin Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, trans. Robert D. Metcalf and Mark B. Tanzer (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2009). GA 18. Summer semester 1924, University of Marburg.

**Date**: 2026-03-08

---

## 1. Course Overview

*Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* records Heidegger's 1924 Marburg summer lecture course, delivered three years before the publication of *Being and Time* (1927) and two years after the pivotal 1922 essay known as the "Natorp Report" (*Phänomenologische Interpretationen zu Aristoteles: Anzeige der hermeneutischen Situation*). The course continues the project announced in that earlier text: a phenomenological-hermeneutic retrieval (*Wiederholung*) of Aristotle's fundamental ontological concepts, pursued not as antiquarian history of philosophy but as a confrontation with "the matters themselves" as Aristotle encountered them. Heidegger's stated aim is explicitly *philological* rather than philosophical in a systematic sense -- he seeks to understand "some basic concepts of Aristotelian philosophy in their conceptuality" by engaging directly with the text of the Aristotelian treatises (p. 3). The lecture thus operates at the intersection of classical philology, phenomenology, and what would later crystallize as fundamental ontology.

The organizing textual anchor for the course is *Metaphysics* Delta (Book 5), which Heidegger treats as Aristotle's own compendium of basic concepts -- a treatise that "consists simply of definitions of these basic concepts" (p. 3). Heidegger walks through the thirty chapters of Delta in the Introduction (pp. 3-4), enumerating terms such as ἀρχή, αἴτιον, στοιχεῖον, φύσις, ἀναγκαῖον, ἕν, ὄν, οὐσία, ταὐτά, ἀντικείμενα, πρότερα/ὕστερα, δύναμις, ποσόν, ποιόν, πρός τι, τέλειον, πέρας, διάθεσις, ἕξις, πάθος, στέρησις, ἔχειν, ἐκ τινος, μέρος, ὅλον, κολοβόν, γένος, ψεῦδος, and συμβεβηκός. But the course does not simply reproduce Delta's inventory; it asks after the *ground* of these concepts -- "how they have arisen, as well as *how* they have so arisen" (p. 4) -- and insists that their "specific conceptuality" can only be understood by attending to the matters, contexts, and modes of determination from which they emerge. This methodological commitment -- that concepts must be traced back to their indigenous ground in the being-there (*Dasein*) of human beings -- is what connects the 1924 lectures to the existential analytic of *Being and Time*.

The course divides into two Parts. The First Part (§§3-22, pp. 9-181) undertakes a "preliminary understanding" of the "indigenous character of conceptuality" by explicating being-there as being-in-the-world, oriented specifically toward Aristotle's practical philosophy: rhetoric (*Rhetoric*), ethics (*Nicomachean Ethics*), psychology (*De Anima*), and natural philosophy (*De Partibus Animalium*). This long middle section -- especially Chapter 3 on rhetoric, where λόγος as πίστις, the πάθη (including φόβος, ἡδονή, λύπη), δόξα, ἦθος, and ἕξις receive extended treatment -- constitutes Heidegger's most sustained engagement with Aristotle's rhetorical and practical writings. The Second Part (§§23-28, pp. 183-222) then executes a "retrieving interpretation" of the basic concepts proper, centering on κίνησις as ἐντελέχεια τοῦ δυνάμει ὄντος (*Physics* Gamma 1-3) and the cluster of ontological concepts -- ἐνέργεια, δύναμις, στέρησις -- that articulate the being of movement. The trajectory of the course thus moves from the existential-rhetorical ground of conceptuality (First Part) to the ontological payoff in the analysis of movement (Second Part), a structure that anticipates the movement from everydayness to temporality in *Being and Time*.

## 3. Provisional Claims List

Based on §§1-2 and the TOC structure, Heidegger appears to defend the following theses:

**C1. The "basic concepts" of Aristotelian philosophy are not free-floating logical categories but are rooted in the being-there (*Dasein*) of human beings as being-in-the-world.**
This is the master thesis of the course, announced in the title of the First Part and pursued through the long rhetorical-practical analysis of §§9-22. Conceptuality has an "indigenous character" (*Bodenständigkeit*) that can only be recovered by returning to the existential ground from which concepts arise.

**C2. Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is not a merely technical discipline but a fundamental hermeneutic of everyday being-with-one-another (*Miteinandersein*).**
The extraordinary length of First Part Chapter 3 (§§13-22, over 110 pages) signals that Heidegger treats rhetoric as central, not peripheral, to Aristotle's ontology. The πάθη, πίστεις, and δόξα analyzed there are not ornamental but disclose the structure of being-there in its everydayness.

**C3. The philological aim of the lecture -- understanding basic concepts "in their conceptuality" -- is itself a philosophical act, not an antiquarian exercise.**
Heidegger distinguishes his approach from both Jaeger's developmental philology and systematic philosophy (§§1-2). Philology here means "the passion for knowledge of what has been expressed" (p. 4), and it operates under six demanding presuppositions that include the conviction that Aristotle "actually has something to say" and that conceptuality is "the substance of all scientific research."

**C4. *Metaphysics* Delta (Book 5) provides the textual key to Aristotle's basic concepts, understood as a compendium of definitions that must be traced back to their ground.**
The Introduction's enumeration of Delta's thirty chapters (pp. 3-4) establishes the framework; but the course then asks *how* these concepts arose and what mode of being-there they articulate, rather than merely cataloguing them.

**C5. κίνησις (movement), interpreted as ἐντελέχεια τοῦ δυνάμει ὄντος, is the fundamental ontological concept for Aristotle -- the key to the meaning of being.**
The entire Second Part builds toward this claim via *Physics* Gamma 1-3. The trajectory from the practical-rhetorical ground of the First Part to the ontological analysis of movement in the Second Part suggests that κίνησις is the culmination and payoff of the course.

**C6. The πάθη (affects) are not merely psychological states but ontological determinations of being-there that disclose how human beings are "in" the world.**
The extended treatment of πάθος in §§16-21 (via Met. Delta 21, De Anima A1, Rhetoric B1-5, NE K1-5) -- including specific analyses of φόβος, ἡδονή, and λύπη -- points toward the existential analytic of *Stimmung* (attunement) in *Being and Time*.

**C7. The human being is defined as ζῷον λόγον ἔχον -- a living being that has speech -- and this definition must be understood through the structure of being-with-one-another.**
§9 treats this definition via *Politics* A2 and *Rhetoric* A6/11. The human being's being is essentially speaking-being and being-with-others, not isolated rational subjectivity.

**C8. ὁρισμός (definition) is not a logical operation but a "determinate mode of being-in-the-world" -- a way human beings orient themselves toward beings.**
§8 recasts definition from a formal-logical procedure into an existential comportment, grounding logic in ontology.

## 4. Candidate Tensions

**T1. Heidegger's existential reading vs. Aristotle's own categorical/eidetic framework.**
Heidegger consistently translates Aristotle's concepts into the idiom of being-there, being-in-the-world, and factical life. Whether this retrieval genuinely recovers what Aristotle means or projects Heideggerian structures onto the Greek text is the fundamental tension running through the entire course. Watch especially: the treatment of οὐσία (§7), the reading of ψυχή as being-there (§9), and the interpretation of κίνησις as the meaning of being (§§26-28).

**T2. The primacy of rhetoric/practical philosophy (First Part) vs. the primacy of physics/ontology (Second Part).**
The course devotes roughly 175 pages to practical-rhetorical material and only 40 pages to the ontological analysis of movement. Yet the Second Part is presented as the "retrieving interpretation" -- the payoff. The tension is whether the rhetorical-practical material is genuinely preparatory (as Heidegger's structure implies) or whether it constitutes the more original contribution that the Second Part merely formalizes.

**T3. Philological aim vs. philosophical presuppositions.**
Heidegger claims the lecture has "no philosophical aim at all" but is purely philological (§2, p. 5). Yet the six presuppositions he immediately articulates -- including that "conceptuality constitutes the substance of all scientific research" and that "history and the historical past have the possibility of giving a jolt to the present" -- are deeply philosophical commitments. The tension between the stated humility of the philological aim and the ambition of the presuppositions deserves sustained attention.

**T4. The 1924 lectures as pre-*Being and Time* laboratory vs. independent Aristotle interpretation.**
The Translators' Preface frames the Marburg lectures as showing "how the key concepts of Heidegger's groundbreaking work were developed" (p. xi). But reading the course solely as a staging ground for BT risks missing what is distinctive about Heidegger's 1924 encounter with Aristotle -- particularly the centrality of rhetoric and the πάθη, which receive far less attention in BT itself. The tension between teleological reading (toward BT) and immanent reading (the course on its own terms) must be managed throughout the analysis.

**T5. Multiple registers of πάθος: ontological, rhetorical, ethical, physiological.**
πάθος appears in at least four distinct registers across the course: as a mode of rhetorical proof (πίστις), as an ontological determination of being (Met. Delta 21), as the subject matter of the φυσικός (De Anima A1), and as the ground of ethical habituation (ἕξις, ἀρετή). Whether Heidegger successfully unifies these registers or whether they remain in productive tension is a key question for the analysis, especially given the dissertation's concern with πάθος and rhetoric.
