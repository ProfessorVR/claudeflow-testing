## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### A. §1 -- The Philological Purpose: Aristotle's Basic Concepts and *Metaphysics* Delta (pp. 3--4)

- The lecture aims at understanding *some* basic concepts of Aristotelian philosophy "specifically through an engagement with the *text* of the Aristotelian treatises" (p. 3). Not all concepts, but precisely those "primary matters" with which Aristotelian research is occupied.
- *Metaphysics* Delta (Book 5) serves as the organizing compendium: it "consists simply of definitions of these basic concepts" (p. 3). Following Jaeger, the fourteen treatises of the *Metaphysics* are not to be treated as a unified "system."
- Heidegger enumerates the thirty chapters of Delta (pp. 3--4): ἀρχή, αἴτιον, στοιχεῖον, φύσις, ἀναγκαῖον, ἕν, ὄν, οὐσία, ταὐτά, ἀντικείμενα, πρότερον/ὕστερον, γένος, δύναμις, ποσόν, ποιόν, πρός τι, τέλειον, πέρας, διάθεσις, ἕξις, πάθος, στέρησις, ἔχειν, ἐκ τινος εἶναι, μέρος, ὅλον, κολοβόν, γένος (second sense), ψεῦδος, and συμβεβηκός. This enumeration is not mere inventory; it establishes the field of concepts whose "ground" and "specific conceptuality" must be recovered.
- The decisive methodological demand: "We must see the *ground* out of which these basic concepts have arisen, as well as *how* they have so arisen" (p. 4). The basic concepts will be considered "in their specific conceptuality so that we may ask *how the matters themselves meant by these basic concepts are viewed, in what context they are addressed, in which particular mode they are determined*" (p. 4).
- This investigation is *no philosophy*, much less a history of philosophy; it offers "gaining insight into the fundamental exigencies of scientific research" (p. 4). Philology here is defined as "the *passion for knowledge of what has been expressed*" (p. 4).
- Jaeger's developmental approach is acknowledged (fn. 2--3 cite *Aristoteles* [1923] and *Studien zur Entstehungsgeschichte* [1912]) but implicitly circumscribed: the lecture's concern is conceptuality, not intellectual biography. The philosopher's character and personality are curtailed -- "our only interest is that he was born at a certain time, that he worked, and that he died" (p. 5).

### B. §2 -- The Presuppositions of the Philological Purpose (pp. 4--7)

- The philological aim "naturally brings with it a number of *presuppositions*" (p. 5). Heidegger lists six, escalating from the particular to the methodological:
  1. **Aristotle has something to say**: He holds a distinctive position "not only within Greek philosophy, but within Western philosophy as a whole" (p. 5).
  2. **We are wrong in some respect**: Honest self-assessment is required; we are "not yet so advanced" that nothing remains questionable.
  3. **Conceptuality is the substance of all scientific research**: Not intellectual acumen but "*he who has chosen science has accepted responsibility for the concept*" (p. 5).
  4. **Science concerns the existence of human beings**: Science is "not an occupation, not a business, not a diversion, but is rather *the possibility of the existence of human beings*" (p. 5). It carries "definite presuppositions that anyone who seriously moves in the circle of scientific research has to bring along with him."
  5. **Self-reliance**: "Human life has in itself the *possibility of relying on oneself alone*, of managing without faith, without religion, and so on" (p. 5).
  6. **Faith in history**: A methodological presupposition -- "that *history and the historical past have the possibility, insofar as the way is made clear for it, of giving a jolt to the present or, better put, to the future*" (p. 6).
- These presuppositions apply even though the lecture is "only pursuing philology" (p. 6). Philosophy is "better situated today" precisely because it operates "outside of the basic presupposition that everything is just as it should be" (p. 6).
- Heidegger invokes Aristotle as witness for the demarcation of the *manner* of treating philosophy. The aim is to implant "the *instinct for what is self-evident* and the *instinct for what is ancient*" (p. 6).
- The tripartite distinction from *Metaphysics* Gamma 2 (1004 b 17 sqq.): Aristotle distinguishes between διαλεκτική, σοφιστική, and φιλοσοφία. All three "are concerned with the same issues" (p. 6), but φιλοσοφία "distinguishes itself from both of them in its way of approaching these issues, namely, in the way it deals with the same object" (p. 6).
  - φιλοσοφία differs from διαλεκτική "in the mode of the possibility" (τῷ τρόπῳ τῆς δυνάμεως, 1004 b 24) to which it lays claim.
  - διαλεκτική "makes a mere attempt" (1004 b 25) to ascertain that which could be meant by the λόγοι, "a 'running through' (διαπορεύεσθαι) of that which could perhaps be meant" (p. 7, cf. Plato, *Sophist* 253 b 10).
  - The δύναμις of διαλεκτική "is, in contrast to philosophy, a limited one" (p. 7). Still, διαλεκτική "is geared toward the laying out of that which is meant" (p. 7).
  - σοφιστική: "it appears to be philosophy 'but it is not'" (φαινομένη, οὖσα δ' οὔ; 1004 b 26).
- The conclusion: "we are treating of philosophy *in the mode of investigating what ultimately could be meant*" (p. 7). What is decisive is arriving at "a preliminary understanding of that which is meant by philosophy" (p. 7).

## 3. Key Concepts

### 3.1 Conceptuality (*Begrifflichkeit*)

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **Name** | Conceptuality |
| **Greek form** | Not a Greek term per se; Heidegger's own framing category |
| **Definition** | "The substance of all scientific research" (p. 5); the specific mode of determination through which matters are addressed, viewed, and articulated in concepts. |
| **Type** | Methodological / meta-ontological |
| **Dependencies** | Presupposes: (a) that concepts are not free-floating but arise from a ground; (b) that the ground is the being-there of human beings. |

## 4. Main Positions

### U-Intro-P1: The philological aim is philosophically loaded

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **ID** | U-Intro-P1 |
| **Claim** | The lecture's stated aim is philological -- understanding basic concepts in their conceptuality -- but this philological aim already carries philosophical presuppositions and operates within the horizon of philosophy understood as investigating "what ultimately could be meant." |
| **Premises** | (1) Philology is "the passion for knowledge of what has been expressed" (p. 4). (2) Six presuppositions ground this passion, including that conceptuality is the substance of science and that science concerns human existence. (3) The lecture treats philosophy "in the mode of investigating what ultimately could be meant" (p. 7). → **Conclusion**: Philology and philosophy are not externally related; the philological engagement with Aristotle's text is itself a philosophical act. |
| **Aristotelian texts** | Met. Delta (enumerated); Met. Gamma 2, 1004 b 17--26 |
| **Status** | Explicit (stated clearly at pp. 4--5 and p. 7) |

### U-Intro-P2: Concepts must be traced to their ground

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **ID** | U-Intro-P2 |
| **Claim** | The basic concepts of Aristotelian philosophy cannot be understood merely as definitions in a compendium (Met. Delta); they must be traced back to the *ground* from which they arise and to the specific manner of their arising. |
| **Premises** | (1) Met. Delta provides definitions of basic concepts (p. 3). (2) "We must see the *ground* out of which these basic concepts have arisen, as well as *how* they have so arisen" (p. 4). (3) This requires asking "how the matters themselves meant by these basic concepts are viewed, in what context they are addressed, in which particular mode they are determined" (p. 4). → **Conclusion**: Conceptuality is not self-explanatory; it requires a genetic or genealogical investigation into its conditions. |
| **Aristotelian texts** | Met. Delta (general) |
| **Status** | Explicit |

### U-Intro-P3: Philosophy is distinguished by its mode of capacity, not its subject matter

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **ID** | U-Intro-P3 |
| **Claim** | Philosophy, dialectic, and sophistic deal with the same issues (τὰ αὐτά), but philosophy is distinguished by its τρόπος τῆς δυνάμεως -- its mode of capacity or way of approaching the matter. |
| **Premises** | (1) Aristotle's tripartite distinction at Met. Gamma 2, 1004 b 17 ff. (2) σοφιστική and διαλεκτική "are concerned with the same issues as is φιλοσοφία" (p. 6). (3) φιλοσοφία differs "in the mode of the possibility" (p. 6). (4) διαλεκτική is a "mere attempt" (πειραστική); σοφιστική is "apparent but not real" (φαινομένη, οὖσα δ' οὔ). → **Conclusion**: The demarcation of philosophy is not topical but modal -- it concerns how, not what. |
| **Aristotelian texts** | Met. Gamma 2, 1004 b 17--26; cf. Plato, *Sophist* 253 b 10 |
| **Status** | Explicit (Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's own distinction) |

### U-Intro-P4: Science presupposes the existence of human beings

| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| **ID** | U-Intro-P4 |
| **Claim** | Science is "the possibility of the existence of human beings" (p. 5); it is not an occupation or business but carries existential presuppositions about self-reliance, responsibility for the concept, and faith in history. |
| **Premises** | Presuppositions 3--6 from §2 (pp. 5--6). → **Conclusion**: The ground of conceptuality is ultimately existential: human being-there in its capacity for self-reliance, historical openness, and responsibility for conceptual rigor. |
| **Aristotelian texts** | Not directly cited; Heidegger's own framing |
| **Status** | Explicit but programmatic (will be developed in Part I) |

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

**T1. The philology/philosophy tension.** Heidegger insists the lecture is "no philosophy" but only philology (p. 4), yet the six presuppositions of §2 -- especially that science concerns "the existence of human beings" and requires "self-reliance" and "faith in history" -- are manifestly philosophical commitments. The concluding sentence of §2 resolves this by redefining the lecture's mode: "we are treating of philosophy *in the mode of investigating what ultimately could be meant*" (p. 7). But the tension between the modesty of the philological claim and the ambition of the existential presuppositions remains productive throughout the course. [INTERP-low: Heidegger himself stages this tension deliberately.]

**T2. Jaeger and the unity of the *Metaphysics*.** Heidegger accepts Jaeger's verdict that the *Metaphysics* is not a unified work and that attempts to find a "system" must be curtailed (p. 5). Yet the lecture's own procedure -- reading Delta's definitions back into their existential ground -- implicitly proposes a different kind of unity: not systematic but genealogical. Whether this genealogical unity is compatible with or undermines Jaeger's developmental thesis is left unstated. [INTERP-high]

**T3. The status of the six presuppositions.** Heidegger concedes it is "questionable whether one can really get into presuppositions of this sort in a lecture" (p. 5). The presuppositions are listed but not argued for; they function as existential commitments that the listener either brings or does not. This raises the question of whether the lecture's method is circular (presupposing what it aims to demonstrate) or hermeneutically spiral (entering the circle at the right point). [INTERP-low: Heidegger will thematize the hermeneutic circle explicitly in later units.]

**T4. The scope of "basic concepts."** The Introduction enumerates all thirty chapters of Delta but announces that only *some* will be treated. The principle of selection is not made explicit here. The course's actual trajectory -- moving through rhetoric, ethics, and practical philosophy before arriving at κίνησις -- suggests that the selection is governed by the question of being-there, but this is not yet stated in §§1--2. [INTERP-low]

**T5. διαλεκτική as "serious" but "limited."** Heidegger notes that "διαλεκτική is serious, but it is only the seriousness of an attempted investigation of what ultimately could be meant" (p. 7). The precise nature of this limitation -- and how philosophy surpasses it -- is left for later development. The distinction turns on τρόπος τῆς δυνάμεως, but this phrase is cited rather than analyzed. [INTERP-low]
