# Phase 2 Analysis: U-FP3a -- Speaking-Being, Rhetoric, and Pistis

**Unit**: U-FP3a (§13--§14, pp. 71--92, PDF pp. 86--107)
**Source**: Martin Heidegger, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy*, trans. Robert D. Metcalf and Mark B. Tanzer (Bloomington: Indiana University Press, 2009). GA 18.
**Date**: 2026-03-08

**One-sentence thesis**: Heidegger demonstrates that the human being's determination as ζῷον λόγον ἔχον grounds both the possibility of hearing (and thus falling into idle talk) and the threefold structure of πίστις in Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, thereby establishing rhetoric as the hermeneutic of concrete everyday being-there rather than a formal discipline of persuasion.

---

## 1. Section-by-Section Summary

### §13. Speaking-Being as Ability-to-Hear and as Possibility of Falling (pp. 71--77)

Heidegger begins Chapter Three by returning to the definition of the human being as ζῷον λόγον ἔχον. The being-there of human beings is being-with-one-another, and this being-with-one-another has its basic possibility in speaking -- specifically, in speaking-with-one-another as expressing-oneself in speaking-about-something (p. 72). The λόγον ἔχον is "only superficially clarified" and requires deeper investigation.

The first key move is the claim that the being-there characterized as λόγον ἔχον is not merely a speaking but also a *hearing*. In being-with-one-another, one can be the speaker and the other the hearer; ἀκούειν ("hearing") is genuine αἴσθησις whether or not it is connected with θεωρεῖν (p. 72). The human being is not only a speaker and a hearer but is for itself a being *that hears itself*: "Speaking, as self-expression-about-something, is at the same time a speaking-to-oneself" (p. 72). Therefore the definition of λόγον ἔχον contains within itself that the human being has λόγος in the mode of *hearing this, its own speaking* (p. 72). Heidegger identifies concrete phenomena in the *Nicomachean Ethics* A13 that exhibit this: παράκλησις ("incitement"), νουθέτησις ("making notable"), ἐπιτίμησις ("reproach") -- modes of natural speaking-with-one-another in which the other does not merely take notice but *takes up*, *follows*, *reflects on*, and *repeats* what is said. The result: "in the being of the human being as concernful lies the possibility of listening to its speaking" (p. 72).

The second key move introduces the double-meaning of ἄλογον. Heidegger identifies a critical ambiguity in the full determination of the being of human beings (NE A13, 1102 b 29--34): ἄλογον has (1) the sense of not-being-in-the-mode-of-listening-to-speaking, and (2) the sense of not standing in the context of speaking at all, as with θρεπτική (nourishing, reproducing), which is entirely unrelated to λόγος (p. 73). Genuine speaking is being λόγον ἔχον κυρίως -- the ability-to-listen-to-speaking characterizes it as a being-possibility. This leads to the partitioning of the possible ἀρεταί into (a) ἀρεταί διανοητικαί (thinking-through, reckoning-through -- the basic possibility of considering, scientific research, and the βίος θεωρητικός) and (b) ἀρεταί ἠθικαί, where ἦθος means not "moral" but "comportment" -- how the human being offers himself, how he appears in being-with-one-another (p. 73).

The third and most consequential move is the introduction of *falling*. Heidegger argues that discourse is "the genuine possibility of being-there" and that "precisely this speaking is also the possibility in which being-there is *ensnared*" (p. 74). Being-there can "become absorbed in the immediate, in fashions, in babble" -- and this absorption through language is "the *basic danger of their being-there*" (p. 74). The proof of this is the existence of *sophistry*: sophistical speaking is the "predominant possibility of speaking" that is "taken seriously by sophistry" (p. 74). Heidegger invokes Protagoras's principle (τὸν ἥττω λόγον κρείττω ποιεῖν) and Nietzsche's characterization of Greek as "the most speakable of all languages" to drive home that the Greeks were "completely under the dominion of language" by the fourth century BC (p. 75).

Heidegger then argues that Aristotle stood in "the most extreme opposition" to this falling: λόγος is λόγος οὐσίας, "speaking about the matter as to what it is" (p. 75). Logic originates from the seriousness about the possibility of speaking; the Greeks "were serious about the possibility of speaking" not from the outside but "from out of Greek living itself" (p. 75). Rhetoric is therefore "nothing other than the interpretation of concrete being-there, the hermeneutic of being-there itself" (p. 75) -- it is the discipline in which the self-interpretation of being-there is explicitly fulfilled.

The section concludes with a passage from *De Anima* B4, where Heidegger shows that the being-determinations (θρεπτικόν, γεννητικόν, αἰσθητικόν, νοητικόν, ὀρεκτικόν) are divided up according to λόγον ἔχον -- ἄλογον (pp. 76--77). The definition of the human being as ζῷον λόγον ἔχον turns out to be "of much wider significance than it seemed at first" (p. 77): (1) ζωή πρακτική μετὰ λόγου defines the being itself; (2) the being-possibilities that human beings can have at their disposal are divided according to this definition; (3) genuine speaking with the world, the ὁρισμός, is designated as λόγος. The λόγον ἔχον is twofold: (1) ἐπιστημονικόν and (2) λογιστικόν -- speaking in the sense of theoretical considerations and speaking in the sense of λογίζεσθαι, deliberating (p. 78).

### §14. The Basic Determination of Rhetoric and λόγος Itself as πίστις (pp. 78--92)

Heidegger opens §14 by asking how, from Aristotle himself, we can recover the idea that speaking-being was the basic phenomenon of Greek being-there. He notes that the *Rhetoric* is older than the Aristotelian *Rhetoric ad Alexandrum* (attributed to Anaximenes by Spengel), and that genuine reflection on speaking is traced to the Sicilian orators Teisias and Korax (p. 78). The question driving the section: "In what way is λέγειν the basic determination of being-there itself in the concrete mode of its being in its everydayness?" (p. 78).

#### §14a. The Basic Definition of Rhetoric as the Possibility of Seeing What at Each Moment Speaks for a Matter (pp. 78--81)

Aristotle defines rhetoric in *Rhetoric* A2 as a δύναμις, not a τέχνη (p. 78). Heidegger emphasizes: the genuine definition is δύναμις, even though Aristotle more often calls it τέχνη. Ῥητορική is "the possibility of seeing what is given at the moment; what speaks for a matter that is the topic of discourse, the possibility of seeing at each moment what can speak for a matter" (p. 80). A δύναμις, unlike a τέχνη, does not have a demarcated subject area (ὑποκείμενον) and does not convey determinate knowledge of a specific domain (pp. 79--80). Rhetoric is distinguished from σοφιστική: the latter is a mode of knowing-one's-way-around discourse ἐν τῇ δυνάμει, whereas ῥητορική is maintained ἐν τῇ προαιρέσει (p. 79). The ῥήτωρ is a δυνάμενος θεωρεῖν -- one who can see "to see" περὶ ἕκαστον τὸ πιθανόν (p. 79).

Rhetoric does not deal with matters themselves but with the *concrete situation* -- "that which speaks for something speaks for the conviction that the one discoursing wants to cultivate in others" insofar as it can speak for the πιστεύειν (pp. 80--81). Πιστεύειν is "holding in a view" -- cultivating a δόξα, a "view" that governs the everydayness of being-there, always modifiable, not an insight but an orientation within being-with-one-another (p. 81). The relation between πιστεύειν and πιθανόν is analogous to that between ἀληθεύειν and ἀληθές -- the unconcealable-being-that-is-there (p. 81). Ἀληθεύειν is the basic phenomenon toward which Heidegger is headed; πίστις is that which is conducive to the cultivation of a πιστεύειν, and through it one can see and learn about the πιθανόν (p. 81).

#### §14b. The Three πίστεις ἔντεχνοι: ἦθος, πάθος, and λόγος Itself (pp. 81--84)

Aristotle distinguishes πίστεις ἄτεχνοι (what cannot be brought about on our account: νόμοι, μάρτυρες, βάσανοι, συνθῆκαι, ὅρκος -- Rhetoric A15) from πίστεις ἔντεχνοι: "that which speaks for a matter that we know our way around, which we have appropriated and have at our disposal" (p. 85). The ἔντεχνοι touch upon λόγος insofar as λέγειν is that which is in our power (p. 85).

The three ἔντεχνοι correspond to a threefold possibility of taking λόγοι (Rhetoric A2, 1356a 1 sqq.):

1. **ἐν τῷ ἤθει τοῦ λέγοντος** -- "in the comportment of the speaker" (p. 82). The one giving discourse is himself a πίστις in his ἦθος, his comportment. Aristotle says λόγος must be maintained so that it turns the speaker into someone trustworthy; ἦθος must become visible through the discourse itself; πίστις must arise out of discourse itself (p. 82). The previous treatises held that ἦθος "contributes nothing to what is πιθανόν," but Aristotle insists that one's comportment is "the most excellent" πίστις (p. 83).

2. **ἐν τῷ τὸν ἀκροατὴν διαθεῖναί πως** -- "in the bringing-into-a-disposition of the hearer" (p. 82). The hearer also belongs to λέγειν. How the hearer is disposed -- the διάθεσις of the hearer -- determines his κρίσις, his "view" (p. 82). This πίστις, lying on the side of the hearer, is treated in detail in Aristotle's *Rhetoric* Book 2, Chapters 2--20 as the doctrine of the πάθη. The πάθη are not states pertaining to ensouled things but "a disposition of living things in their world, in the mode of being positioned toward something, allowing a matter to matter to it" (p. 83). They play a fundamental role in the determination of being-in-the-world, of being-with-and-toward-others.

3. **ἐν αὐτῷ τῷ λόγῳ** -- "in λέγειν itself" as πίστις, the basic function of being-there itself (p. 82). Information is given about the matter itself: διὰ τὸ δεικνύναι -- the manner and mode, the matter-of-factness or lack thereof, of the speaker himself (p. 82).

The ἄτεχνοι have their sense as πίστεις "only insofar as they are oriented" toward the ἔντεχνοι (p. 84).

#### §14c. Λόγος Itself as πίστις (pp. 84--92)

**α. The Three Forms of Hearer and the Three Types of λόγος** (pp. 84--87)

Aristotle comes to the basic determination of λόγος in Rhetoric A3: speaking has its τέλος in the ἀκροατής, the "hearer" (p. 84). Speaking is *communication*; a discourse reaches its end only when taken as communication. On the basis of the distinct ways a hearer can be, Aristotle determines *three distinct types of λόγος* (p. 84). The general structure of λόγος consists of three aspects: (1) the speaker, (2) "that about which" (the subject exhibited), (3) the πρός ὅν, the hearer "to whom" one speaks (p. 84). The τέλος is in the hearer. The λόγοι are distinguished by the modes in which the human being in the πόλις can be a hearer.

There must be *three forms of hearer*: the θεωρός -- the "onlooker" at a festival, not in the sense of stupidly sitting there but as κριτής, one who forms an opinion about what he sees (p. 85). The κριτής can be: (a) ἐκκλησιαστής -- one who takes part in the people's gathering, deliberating about what should come; (b) δικαστής -- the judge, "the θεωρός about what now is" (p. 85).

Consequently three distinct λόγοι: (1) **συμβουλευτικός** -- "deliberative speaking," speaking in the people's gathering (p. 85); (2) **δικανικός** -- "judicial discourse," the discourse of accuser and defendant (p. 85); (3) **ἐπιδεικτικός** -- "eulogy," an exhibiting that lets the human being be seen in his life, not a court judgment but a showing (p. 85).

All three operate in *two directions*: deliberative discourse can be (a) προτροπή or (b) ἀποτροπή; judicial discourse: (a) κατηγορία or (b) ἀπολογία; exhibitive discourse: (a) ἔπαινος or (b) ψόγος (pp. 85--86).

The three distinct εἶδη are further characterized with respect to **χρόνος** -- the *time-character* of their about-which (p. 86): (1) ὁ μέλλων for deliberation -- "the near future, the upcoming, what will be, at which deliberation aims"; (2) ὁ γενόμενος for the δικαζόμενος -- "what has happened"; (3) ὁ παρών -- "what is present." The being-character of the about-which of the three types: συμβουλευτικός concerns the συμφέρον as opposed to the βλαβερόν; δικανικός concerns the δίκαιον as opposed to the ἄδικον; ἐπιδεικτικός concerns the καλόν as opposed to the αἰσχρόν (p. 86).

Heidegger draws particular attention to the temporality implicit in this structure: "Everydayness itself is manifested within a fundamental basic-structure: its *temporality*. Being in itself as concern and concernful speaking is *temporal*" (p. 90). The characters of beings encountered in the environing world come to language as determinations of beings marked by temporality -- the συμφέρον, δίκαιον, and καλόν are temporal determinations of being.

**β. Rhetorical Speaking with παράδειγμα and ἐνθύμημα as Paralleling Dialectical Speaking with ἐπαγωγή and συλλογισμός** (pp. 87--92)

Heidegger identifies rhetoric as a παραφυές of διαλεκτική -- a "shoot" (offshoot) that grows up alongside (Rhetoric A2, 1356a 25 sqq.), specifically alongside the discussion of ἤθη, which belongs within πολιτική (p. 87). Ῥητορική is ἀντίστροφος τῇ διαλεκτικῇ -- "turned against dialectic" (p. 87). Neither is an ἐπιστήμη ("concrete knowledge"); both are possibilities of "furthering," "procuring" discourse (p. 87).

Aristotle approaches the third πίστις -- λέγειν itself -- by distinguishing definite possibilities within λέγειν: exhibiting something *by adding an example* or by *substantiating a definite thesis* (p. 87). Δεικνύναι through λόγος is something dual: (1) **παράδειγμα**, and (2) **ἐνθύμημα** (as the rhetorical form of συλλογισμός) (p. 87). This distinction parallels the διαλεκτική distinction between (1) ἐπαγωγή and (2) ἀπόδειξις/συλλογισμός (p. 87).

**Ἐπαγωγή** is the "leading-up-to" -- "through what is at each moment," what is there immediately, "to what is on the whole" (p. 91). Heidegger lists its advantages: (1) πιθανώτερον -- it "speaks more for itself"; (2) σαφέστερον -- "more perspicuous"; (3) κατὰ τὴν αἴσθησιν γνωριμώτερον -- "more familiar with respect to direct perceiving"; (4) τοῖς πολλοῖς κοινόν -- "common to most, to the average among human beings" (p. 91). Παράδειγμα corresponds to ἐπαγωγή; it is a "leading-to-something" that puts the matter before one's eyes through a concrete case (p. 88). Παρά means "what is present, that which stands before one, what is shown, directly put forward, demonstrated by example" (p. 88).

**Συλλογισμός** has "more penetrating power" (βιαστικώτερον), is more suitable "when it is a matter of speaking and questioning as to things, 'speaking to them'" (p. 91). In the *Topics*, Aristotle defines συλλογισμός as a λόγος [an ἀποφαίνεσθαι, a "letting-see"] in which something else comes about from what is set down as known (p. 88). The difference between ἀπόδειξις and dialectical συλλογισμός: in ἀπόδειξις the ὑπόθεσις proceeds from ἀληθές -- what lies open in its being-thus; in dialectical συλλογισμός it proceeds from ἔνδοξον -- "in δόξα" (p. 88). The ἔνδοξον is defined as "that which appears this way or that way to everyone or to most, to most or to the intelligent among them" (p. 89). The ἐνθύμημα proceeds from ἔνδοξον and refers back to it: "what has already happened, what is present, what is conducive, what is fitting and what is not fitting, the beautiful and the ugly" (p. 89).

Heidegger concludes §14 with a crucial observation about the *concreteness* of rhetoric's exhibiting-character. The being of which one speaks in everyday discourse is characterized by "more or less" (μέγεθος) and indefiniteness (ἀόριστον) -- the aspect of δυνατόν and ἀδύνατον (p. 90). What is spoken of day-by-day proceeds not from theoretical axioms but from ἔνδοξον -- views that life itself has cultivated (p. 90). The ἐνθύμημα must be *simple* (ἁπλοῦς), with fewer premises and shorter chains of inference, because "the hearer to whom one speaks in the public assembly is 'simple'; he has short-winded thinking" (p. 90). Rhetoric is thus not purely formal discourse but "is *related to the being of the being-with-one-another of human beings*" (p. 92). It stands within πολιτική; it "disappears" into politics and "is hidden in the shape of politics" (p. 92). The genuine business of πολιτική can only be carried out on the basis of a *concrete experience of being-there itself* -- "knowing-the-way-around in everyday being-there" -- which the sophists, however, merely simulate (p. 92).

## 3. Positions (Hermeneutic Claims)

### U-FP3a-P1: λόγον ἔχον entails hearing, and hearing entails the possibility of falling

**Textual basis**: "Speaking, as self-expression-about-something, is at the same time a speaking-to-oneself. Therefore, the definition of λόγον ἔχον further contains in itself that the human being also has λόγος in the mode of *hearing this, its own speaking*" (p. 72). "Precisely this speaking is also the possibility in which being-there is *ensnared*. ... to be absorbed in what is ordinary, *to fall* into the world in which it lives, became, through language, the *basic danger of their being-there*" (p. 74).

**Premises -> Conclusion**:
1. The human being is ζῷον λόγον ἔχον (definition).
2. Speaking-with-one-another implies both a speaker and a hearer.
3. The speaker is also a hearer of its own speaking (self-expression is speaking-to-oneself).
4. Hearing involves ὄρεξις -- concern is directed *after* what is spoken (λεγόμενον).
5. Therefore being-there is in its very structure exposed to the possibility of being absorbed in idle talk.

**Aristotelian texts**: NE A13, 1102b 29--34; De Anima B4.
**Status**: Explicit in Heidegger's text.

### U-FP3a-P2: ἄλογον has a double meaning that is constitutive, not accidental

**Textual basis**: "Ἄλογον has a double-meaning in the full determination of the being of human beings: (1) not to be speaking in the sense of listening to speaking, (2) not to stand in the context of speaking at all, as θρεπτική" (p. 73).

**Premises -> Conclusion**:
1. ἄλογον in sense (2): θρεπτική, reproduction -- entirely unrelated to λόγος.
2. ἄλογον in sense (1): ὄρεξις -- not speaking *genuinely* but only insofar as it listens to the speaking.
3. Genuine speaking is being λόγον ἔχον κυρίως -- the ability-to-listen characterizes a being-possibility.
4. Therefore the double sense of ἄλογον is not a lexical accident but reveals the structure of human being: some capacities are utterly outside λόγος, while others are within its orbit precisely as the possibility of listening (or not listening) to speaking.

**Aristotelian texts**: NE A13, 1102b 28 sq.; De Anima B4, 415a 22 sqq.
**Status**: Explicit.

### U-FP3a-P3: Rhetoric is the hermeneutic of concrete being-there itself

**Textual basis**: "Rhetoric is nothing other than the interpretation of concrete being-there, the hermeneutic of being-there itself. That is the intended sense of Aristotle's rhetoric" (p. 75). "Speaking in the mode of speaking-in-discourse -- in public meetings, before the court, at celebratory occasions -- these possibilities of speaking are definitively expounded instances of customary speaking, of how being-there itself speaks" (pp. 75--76).

**Premises -> Conclusion**:
1. The original sense of rhetoric was lost in Hellenism and the Middle Ages.
2. Rhetoric deals with how being-there speaks in its everydayness (public assembly, court, ceremony).
3. The self-interpretation of being-there is explicitly fulfilled in rhetoric.
4. Therefore rhetoric is not a school discipline but the systematic self-interpretation of concrete being-there.

**Aristotelian texts**: Rhetoric A1--3.
**Status**: Explicit. This is Heidegger's boldest claim in the unit.

### U-FP3a-P4: Rhetoric is δύναμις, not τέχνη -- it has no demarcated subject area

**Textual basis**: "Aristotle defines rhetoric in Book 1, Chapter 2 as a δύναμις. ... This definition is asserted despite the fact that Aristotle more often designates it as τέχνη. ... Ῥητορική has *no subject area* that can be demarcated in any way. Because it does not, it should not be designated as τέχνη" (pp. 78--79). "It does not have to cultivate a definite conviction about a matter, to set it to work with others. Rather, it only sets forth a possibility of discourse" (p. 79).

**Premises -> Conclusion**:
1. A τέχνη has a determinate ὑποκείμενον (medicine treats health, arithmetic treats numbers).
2. Rhetoric has no such ὑποκείμενον; its cultivating of πιστεύειν involves as many matters as language itself.
3. Therefore rhetoric is properly δύναμις -- a possibility of seeing what speaks for a matter at each moment -- not a τέχνη.

**Aristotelian texts**: Rhetoric A2, 1355b 25 sq., 1355b 33 sq.
**Status**: Explicit.

### U-FP3a-P5: πίστις is structured threefold according to speaker, hearer, and λόγος itself

**Textual basis**: "Of those ways of speaking-for-something that can be imparted through discourse itself, there are *three types*, in accordance with a three-fold possibility of taking λόγοι. 1. ἐν τῷ ἤθει τοῦ λέγοντος... 2. ἐν τῷ τὸν ἀκροατὴν διαθεῖναί πως... 3. ἐν αὐτῷ τῷ λόγῳ" (p. 82).

**Premises -> Conclusion**:
1. The general structure of λόγος has three aspects: speaker, subject-matter, hearer.
2. πίστεις ἔντεχνοι are accomplished through discourse itself.
3. Therefore the three πίστεις correspond to: (a) the speaker's ἦθος, (b) the hearer's πάθος/διάθεσις, (c) λόγος itself as exhibiting the matter.

**Aristotelian texts**: Rhetoric A2, 1356a 1--20.
**Status**: Explicit.

### U-FP3a-P6: The three types of λόγος are determined by the being of the hearer and are intrinsically temporal

**Textual basis**: "On the basis of the distinct ways that a hearer can be, Aristotle determines *three distinct types of λόγος*" (p. 84). "The three distinct εἶδη along with their possibilities of outcome are summarily characterized with respect to χρόνος; they are distinguished by the *time-character* of that about which they speak" (p. 86). "Everydayness itself is manifested within a fundamental basic-structure: its *temporality*" (p. 90).

**Premises -> Conclusion**:
1. The τέλος of speaking lies in the hearer (communication).
2. The hearer can be: deliberator (about what is coming), judge (about what happened), onlooker (about what is present).
3. Each type of λόγος is thus characterized by a distinct χρόνος: μέλλων, γενόμενος, παρών.
4. Therefore the three types of λόγος reveal that everydayness itself has a temporal basic-structure.

**Aristotelian texts**: Rhetoric A3, 1358a 37 sqq., 1358b 1--22.
**Status**: Explicit.

### U-FP3a-P7: παράδειγμα/ἐνθύμημα parallel ἐπαγωγή/συλλογισμός but from ἔνδοξον rather than ἀληθές

**Textual basis**: "Δεικνύναι through λόγος is something dual: (1) παράδειγμα, (2) συλλογισμὸς ῥητορικός, or here as ἐνθύμημα.... This distinction is found, correspondingly, in the διαλεκτική... namely, (1) ἐπαγωγή, (2) ἀπόδειξις (συλλογισμός)" (p. 87). "In dialectical συλλογισμός it proceeds from ἔνδοξον" (p. 88). "The ἐνθύμημα proceeds from an ἔνδοξον" (p. 89).

**Premises -> Conclusion**:
1. Rhetoric parallels dialectic as ἀντίστροφος and παραφυές.
2. Scientific ἀπόδειξις proceeds from ἀληθές (the unconcealed); dialectical συλλογισμός proceeds from ἔνδοξον.
3. Rhetorical ἐνθύμημα likewise proceeds from ἔνδοξον -- views that life itself has cultivated.
4. Therefore the rhetorical modes of exhibiting operate within the domain of everyday δόξα, not theoretical truth, yet genuinely show the being of what is spoken about.

**Aristotelian texts**: Rhetoric A2, 1356a 25 sqq.; Topics A1, 100a 25--b 23.
**Status**: Explicit.

## 4b. BT Anticipations

### BT-ANT-1: Falling (Verfallenheit) and Idle Talk (Gerede)

Heidegger's discussion of falling on pp. 74--75 directly anticipates *Being and Time* §35 (idle talk) and §38 (falling). The claim that discourse is both the genuine possibility of being-there and simultaneously the possibility in which being-there is "ensnared" -- absorbed "in the immediate, in fashions, in babble" -- maps onto BT's structural analysis of Verfallenheit as a basic existentiale, not a moral failing. The identification of sophistry as the proof that this falling is a permanent structural possibility anticipates the BT thesis that falling belongs to the being of Dasein as such. [INTERP-low: Heidegger's own language ("falling," "danger," "ensnared," "babble") is virtually identical to BT terminology.]

### BT-ANT-2: Befindlichkeit and the πάθη

The treatment of πάθη as "a disposition of living things in their world, in the mode of being positioned toward something, allowing a matter to matter to it" (p. 83) directly anticipates BT §29 on Befindlichkeit (state-of-mind/attunement). Heidegger explicitly insists that affects "are not states pertaining to ensouled things" but are ways of being-in-the-world. This structural determination of affect as a mode of being-positioned-in-the-world is the Aristotelian ground of Befindlichkeit. [INTERP-low: Heidegger makes the ontological character of πάθη explicit.]

### BT-ANT-3: Temporality of Everydayness

The identification of the three types of λόγος with three temporal dimensions (μέλλων, γενόμενος, παρών) and the explicit claim that "everydayness itself is manifested within a fundamental basic-structure: its *temporality*" (p. 90) anticipates BT Division Two's analysis of temporality as the meaning of care. The three temporal ecstases of Dasein (future, having-been, present) are here discovered within Aristotle's *Rhetoric*. [INTERP-high: Heidegger does not explicitly use the BT term "ecstases," but the structural parallel is unmistakable and appears intentional.]

### BT-ANT-4: δόξα, πιστεύειν, and the Hermeneutics of Everydayness

The characterization of πιστεύειν as "holding in a view" (cultivating a δόξα) that governs everydayness -- "always modifiable," "not an insight but an orientation within being-with-one-another" (p. 81) -- anticipates BT's account of the "they" (das Man) and average everyday understanding. The ἔνδοξον as "what appears to everyone or to most" directly anticipates the structure of average everyday interpretation in BT §27. [INTERP-low: Heidegger's own phrasing closely tracks the BT analysis.]

## 5. Tensions & Open Questions

### T-FP3a-1: Rhetoric as hermeneutic vs. rhetoric as falling

Heidegger simultaneously claims that rhetoric is "the hermeneutic of being-there itself" (p. 75) and that the predominant possibility of speaking -- sophistry -- is the "basic danger" of being-there. How can the same phenomenon (speaking-in-discourse) be both the site of genuine self-interpretation and the occasion of falling? Heidegger's answer seems to be that these are structurally co-original: the same λόγος that discloses is the λόγος in which being-there can become absorbed. But the precise relation between rhetoric-as-hermeneutic and rhetoric-as-occasion-of-falling remains underdetermined. [INTERP-low]

**Links**: U-FP3a-P1, U-FP3a-P3, BT-ANT-1.

### T-FP3a-2: δύναμις vs. τέχνη -- Aristotle's own inconsistency

Heidegger acknowledges that Aristotle "more often designates" rhetoric as τέχνη despite the genuine definition being δύναμις (p. 78). This tension within Aristotle's text is noted but not resolved. Heidegger privileges the δύναμις definition as "genuine" without fully explaining why Aristotle himself oscillates. [INTERP-low]

**Links**: U-FP3a-P4.

### T-FP3a-3: The πιστεύειν/ἀληθεύειν analogy

Heidegger asserts that the relation between πιστεύειν and πιθανόν is "analogous" to that between ἀληθεύειν and ἀληθές (p. 81). But πιστεύειν operates in the domain of δόξα while ἀληθεύειν is directed toward unconcealedness. Heidegger says "we will come back to this on another occasion" (p. 81) but does not fully work out whether this analogy implies a deficient mode or a co-original structure. The question is whether πιστεύειν is a *derivative* form of ἀληθεύειν or stands alongside it as a distinct mode of disclosure in everydayness. [INTERP-high]

**Links**: U-FP3a-P3, BT-ANT-4.

### T-FP3a-4: The temporal structure of the three λόγοι -- how far does the analysis reach?

Heidegger identifies μέλλων, γενόμενος, and παρών as the temporal dimensions of the three types of discourse and claims this reveals "the temporality" of everydayness (p. 90). But he does not here develop whether these three correspond to genuine temporal ecstases or merely to ordinary time-reckoning. The question of whether rhetorical temporality is authentic or inauthentic remains open in this unit. [INTERP-high]

**Links**: U-FP3a-P6, BT-ANT-3.
