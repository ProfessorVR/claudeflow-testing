| Field | Value |

| Scope | §3--§5 (pp. 9--14) |
| Density | High (programmatic; sets methodological frame for entire lecture course) |

**One-sentence thesis**: Heidegger argues that definition (*ὁρισμός*), properly understood not as a scholastic-logical technique but as *λόγος οὐσίας* -- a speaking that discloses the being of a being in its being -- constitutes the original ground of conceptuality, and that recovering this ground requires interrogating Aristotle's basic concepts according to (1) the concrete experience in which they arise, (2) the guiding claim of the phenomenon, and (3) the tendency toward intelligibility through which they are unfolded.

## 4. Main Positions

### U-FP1a-P1: Scholastic logic obscures the original character of definition

Heidegger opens by insisting that "logic" as traditionally received is not a genuine confrontation with things but a scholastic compilation of rules. Neither Plato nor Aristotle knew "logic" in this degraded sense. The consequence is that the concept of definition operative in the tradition -- definition as specification of genus and differentia -- has been severed from its roots in actual research and reduced to a technique of "logical perfection." Kant is credited as the sole figure since Aristotle who makes logic "vital" in the context of actual research, yet even Kant's treatment consigns definition to the "General Doctrine of Method" and thereby treats it as merely methodological, a means of lending "precision to knowledge." Heidegger's critical point: Kant recognizes that the definitio, understood as genus proximum et differentia specifica, "does not come into play for genuine definition" -- that is, the nominal definition through comparison falls short of the real definition that determines the What of the matter from its "first ground" or "inner possibility." This already points beyond logic toward ontology. [INTERP-low: The claim that Kant's insight points beyond logic toward ontology is supported by Heidegger's own framing but goes slightly beyond what Kant himself states.]

### U-FP1a-P2: The concept is rooted in concrete experience, not theoretical abstraction

Through the Kantian distinction between intuition (representatio singularis) and concept (representatio per notas communes), Heidegger develops the example of the "savage" who sees a house but has no concept of it. The savage knows the house is *for* something but not *what* it is. The one who "knows their way around" represents something general: we who know the use can make a concept of it. The concept thus "goes beyond answering the question of *what* the object is" and opens onto the question of *conceptuality* itself -- how one understands *what* something is, where this question originates. This signals Heidegger's core methodological commitment: conceptuality is not a logical abstraction but is grounded in the concrete dealings of life with its world.

### U-FP1a-P3: ὁρισμός is λόγος -- definition is a mode of speaking that discloses being

The decisive retrieval occurs in §5b. Heidegger translates ὁρισμός not as "definition" in the scholastic sense but as "limitation" -- a λόγος that addresses the matter "itself in that which it is" (καθ' αὐτό). It is a γνωρισμός, a "making familiar with a being in its being." The genuine function of λόγος is ἀποφαίνεσθαι, "bringing a matter to sight." Speaking is never solitary; it is always speaking *to someone*, *with others*, *about something*. In each case it is a "speaking out of oneself" (spreche ich mich aus). Definition, then, is not a logical operation on genera and differentiae but a mode of disclosive speech oriented toward exhibiting the being of beings.

### U-FP1a-P4: λόγος is the fundamental determination of the being of the human being

Heidegger reads ζῷον λόγον ἔχον not biologically but ontologically. ζωή is itself "a concept of being," "a mode of being," "being-in-a-world." A living thing is not simply at hand (vorhanden) but "is in a world in that it has its world." The being-in-the-world of the human being is "determined in its ground through speaking." Thus λόγος is not an attribute added to an animal nature but the fundamental mode in which human being is disclosed. This grounds the entire lecture course: if the basic concepts of Aristotelian philosophy are to be understood in their "indigenous character," they must be traced back to λόγος as the originary mode of human being-in-the-world. [INTERP-high: The explicit connection between this passage and the program of the entire lecture course is an interpretive extrapolation; Heidegger implies but does not fully state this architectonic role at this point in the text.]

### U-FP1a-P5: Three aspects of conceptuality as methodological program

In §4 Heidegger lays out the three guiding questions for interrogating Aristotle's basic concepts: (1) the *basic experience* (concrete, pre-theoretical) in which the concept arises; (2) the *guiding claim* (Anspruch) of the phenomenon -- whether the determinations are drawn from the phenomenon itself or imposed from available theories; (3) the *tendency toward intelligibility* -- how the phenomenon is unfolded, what "claim of intelligibility" is demanded, and whether the explication is measured by the phenomenon or forced upon it. These three aspects function as a hermeneutic grid that structures the analyses of subsequent units.

## 5. Tensions

### T1: Kant as revitalizer vs. Kant as still-traditional

Heidegger praises Kant as "the only one who lets logic become vital" since Aristotle, yet immediately shows that Kant's treatment leaves definition in the "General Doctrine of Method" -- a merely methodological device that cannot reach genuine definition. This tension is productive: it motivates the return behind Kant to the Greek ground. The question of whether Kant truly "revitalizes" logic or merely refines the scholastic framework is left unresolved; Heidegger uses Kant as a stepping stone and then discards the Kantian frame entirely.

### T2: Definition as logical technique vs. definition as ontological disclosure

The entire unit is structured around this tension. The scholastic tradition (and Kant, to a degree) treats definition as a logical procedure: genus + differentia specifica. Heidegger insists that ὁρισμός is originally λόγος οὐσίας, a speaking that discloses the being of beings. The tension is not resolved but rather *performed*: Heidegger moves from the logical characterization (§3) through a transitional questioning of conceptuality (§4) to the ontological retrieval (§5b).

### T3: Universals controversy as Greek vs. scholastic problem

In §5a, Heidegger notes that the Realism/Nominalism controversy "also has its origin in determinate concrete contexts of Greek philosophy, or better in scholastic misunderstandings thereof." This tension between the genuine Greek origin and the scholastic distortion is flagged but not developed. It implies that the predicables themselves must be re-read in their Greek sense rather than through the medieval framework -- a task deferred to later units.

### T4: ζωή as ontological vs. biological

Heidegger insists that ζωή in ζῷον λόγον ἔχον "should not be thought in biological, psychological, social-scientific, or any such terms." Yet the very word ζῷον carries its biological etymology. The tension between the ontological reading Heidegger demands and the natural-historical sense of the Greek term is acknowledged but resolved by fiat: ζωή "refers to a mode of being, indeed a mode of being-in-a-world." Whether this reading is philologically sustainable or is itself a Heideggerian imposition remains a question the text does not address.
