# PHX-04 — Victor Caston, "Why Aristotle Needs Imagination" (Phronesis 41.1 [1996], 20–55; accepted July 1995)

## § Overview and Caston's functional-explanatory thesis

Caston's article begins with the diagnostic puzzle that has structured the secondary literature on *De Anima* 3.3 since Freudenthal (1863): Aristotle introduces *phantasia* in this chapter as a "distinct, new capacity" of the animal soul, but the chapter's serpentine argument nowhere announces in a forthright way *why* this introduction is needed. The standard secondary-literature move — Caston names the British Empiricists, Kant, and Wittgenstein as foils (book p. 21) — is to import a settled later concept of imagination and use it to retrofit Aristotle's text. Caston rejects this reverse-illumination strategy. Aristotle stands at the beginning of the history of philosophy of mind; *phantasia* was not yet an established topic; therefore, to recover what Aristotle had in mind, one must ask "why Aristotle needs *phantasia* in the first place" (book p. 21).

The article's master thesis is announced in compressed form in the opening four pages (book pp. 20–23) and worked out across nine numbered sections, a conclusion, and seventy-one footnotes: Aristotle introduces *phantasia* to solve the *problem of error*. The problem of error is the problem of explaining how the content of mental states could ever diverge from what is actually in the world. *De Anima* 3.3 begins by posing this problem as a difficulty for Aristotle's *predecessors* — for the Presocratic theorists of like-by-like cognition — but Caston's signature exegetical move is to show that *the same complaint applies to Aristotle's own theory of sensation and conception*, which Aristotle regards as the most basic forms of perception and thought. *Phantasia*, with its more complex aetiology, is the move Aristotle makes to evade the dilemma that ensnares him along with his predecessors (book pp. 22, 38–41). The strategy works only if Aristotle can show two things: (a) *phantasia* is distinct from sensation and conception, and (b) its content genuinely admits of error. The remaining sections of *De Anima* 3.3 — and, on Caston's reading, *De Insomniis* 1–3, *Metaphysics* 4.5 + 9.10 + 5.29, and the *De Motu Animalium* psychological passages — set out to deliver exactly these two demonstrations.

Caston's argument is also, secondarily, an argument about intentionality. *Phantasia* is the faculty Aristotle uses to account for how mental states can be *of* or *about* things — for content more generally. Sensation and conception are intentional, but they cannot provide a general model for intentionality, because most intentional states permit their content to fail to correspond to what is in the world. *Phantasia* is required as the *common currency* of intentional content below the threshold of conceptual and propositional thought.

## § Section-by-section synthesis

**Section I, "The Position of *De anima* 3.3"** (book pp. 22–24), justifies *phantasia*'s otherwise puzzling placement in the *De Anima*'s architectural plan. The treatise's first half is foundational (predecessors 1.1–1.5, definition of soul 2.1–2.3); the second half ascends through the basic powers along the hierarchy of living kinds — plant powers (2.4 nutrition, growth, reproduction), perception (2.5–3.2), and the conceptual-reasoning powers exclusive to humans (3.4–3.8). Other powers (desire, action: 3.9–3.11) are deferred since they are nearly coextensive with the perceptual powers and add no important taxonomic distinction. *Phantasia* breaks this pattern: it belongs to every (or almost every) animal but is treated separately at the critical juncture between perception and thought. Caston rehearses G. H. Lewes's notorious 1864 verdict on the *De Anima* (the chapter, like the rest of the treatise, could be transposed without affecting symmetry) as a foil — fn. 10 cites Lewes's "no progression, no culmination" assessment with sardonic relish — but rejects the editorial-accident hypothesis as untenable for a generally coherent treatise. *Phantasia* must be needed at this precise stage. The opening sentence of 3.3 (427a17–20) recalls Aristotle's two leitmotif criteria for soul — change in place and conceiving/thinking/perceiving — and Caston notes that the first third of 3.3 is a concerted attack on the predecessor thesis that thought is crucially LIKE perception. The attack turns on the problem of error.

**Section II, "The Thesis that Thinking and Perceiving are 'the Same'"** (book pp. 24–26), reconstructs the *endoxon* Aristotle sets up to attack. The thesis is supported by two reasons: a general similarity (both are cognitive attitudes — "the soul discriminates and knows something that is," 427a20–21) and, more weightily, the ancients' formula τὸ φρονεῖν καὶ τὸ αἰσθάνεσθαι ταὐτὸν εἶναι ("thinking and perceiving are the same," 427a21–22), buttressed by citations of Empedocles (DK 31 B 106, B 108), Parmenides, Anaxagoras, and Homer (*Odyssey* 18.136). Caston notes the evidence is frail: none of the cited fragments mentions perception, and the second is not even about thinking in original context. Aristotle is committing his predecessors to a thesis they did not explicitly hold — a charitable interpretive move Caston flags. Caston distinguishes a weaker reading (1) "thinking consists in the same kind of process as perceiving" from a stronger reading (1') "thinking does not differ from perceiving in any way at all," and notes Theophrastus' formula τὸ αὐτὸν ἢ παραπαλήσιον ("the same or nearly the same," De sens. 10, 502.7–9 Diels Doxogr.) supports the weaker reading. Aristotle then offers two further reasons on his predecessors' behalf: (2) thinking and perceiving are both bodily processes, and (3) one thinks and perceives like by like. Neither is necessary, but each is sufficient for (1).

**Section III, "The Problem of Error"** (book pp. 26–32), is the polemical heart of the article. Aristotle now turns to *attack* the thesis. He directs attention to the widespread phenomenon of error and challenges his predecessors: "they should have said something about being in error, since this is particularly endemic to animals and the soul spends a lot of time in this state" (DA 3.3, 427a29–b2). Caston devotes a methodological note (fn. 20) to the comparatives οἰκειότερον and πλείω: Aristotle reads his predecessors as committed to error being *more* prevalent than veridical cognition, which is in tension with Aristotle's own general epistemological commitment (perceptual apparatus naturally discriminates real differences in the environment, *De sens.* 1, 436b18–437a3, cf. *De an.* 3.12, 434a30–b1). The taunt is a polemical exaggeration in the service of the dilemma.

The dilemma proper (DA 3.3, 427b2–5): given their views, Aristotle's predecessors must accept either (i) all appearances are true, or (ii) error is contact with what is unlike (since like-by-like is the schema of recognition). Both are *reductios ad absurdum*. Caston traces the affinity to Plato's *Theaetetus* 151E–157D — the Burnyeat 1990 reading at fn. 22 — where Socrates examines knowledge-as-perception (thesis A) linked to all-appearances-true (thesis B) and the "Secret Doctrine" of constitutive interaction. But Aristotle's interests are psychological, not epistemological; the structure of the arguments is different; and Aristotle's conclusion is *not* that all appearances are true.

Caston then offers his signature decomposition of the like-by-like thesis. (3) is broken into two sub-theses, *logically independent of one another*: (3a) "one perceives or thinks whenever like is affected by like" (the causal claim) and (3b) "whatever one perceives or thinks on a given occasion corresponds *exactly* to whatever brings that state about" (the content-determination claim). Empedocles accepts both; Anaxagoras, whose intellect is "simple, unmixed, and pure" (*DA* 1.2, 405a16–17; 3.4, 429a18), accepts (3b) without (3a). From (3b) alone follows (4) cause-correspondence on every occasion, and if applied to all token mental states, (5) all appearances are true. Error becomes impossible. The only restriction strategy available — applying (3b) to types but not every token — leaves a *lacuna* in explaining the content of *nonveridical* states; and since these must still occur as a result of *some* interaction, they fall back on the second horn (error as unlike-by-unlike).

The brief sub-section III.B (book pp. 31–32) handles the second horn. Aristotle cannot rule it out as absurd — he acknowledges error exists. The compressed objection "both error and knowledge of contraries seem to be the same" (427b5–6) is, on Caston's reconstruction, a *reductio ad absurdum*: if error were unlike-by-unlike, then for contraries (where contrary states are recognized from their contraries — *EN* 5.1, 1129a17ff., cf. *DA* 3.6, 430b21–24; *DA* 1.5, 411a3–7), error and knowledge would coincide. The argument relies on a tacit premise (the contraries-rule) which Aristotle's opponents could deny, but Aristotle treats their refusal as eristical. The further significance: Aristotle himself, faced with the dilemma, *cannot accept the second horn*. He is driven toward the first horn, which he considers anathema. The problem of error constitutes "the real challenge" for him.

**Section IV, "The Thesis that All Appearances are True"** (book pp. 33–35), tightens the dialectical screws. (5) does not follow from (1) alone — neither (1) nor (2) suffices. (3) is the load-bearing premise. But Aristotle's own treatment in *Metaph.* 4.5 (1009b12–17) goes a different route, via (6) "thinking and perceiving are both alterations" (ἀλλοιώσεις). This wider premise captures Anaxagoras (who denies (2)) and Theophrastus' summary of Empedocles/Democritus. Caston unpacks *Metaphysics* 4.5, 1009b17–31 at length: Aristotle's predecessors held thought changes with bodily condition, and so all mental contents are determined by bodily alteration. The Empedocles, Parmenides, and Homer citations stress that thinking is "in a different state" (ἀλλοῖος) depending on bodily condition. The Anaxagoras case is the limit case: his intellect is incorporeal but he is still committed to cause-content correspondence via (3b). Democritus, in Theophrastus' De sensibus 60–67, 73–78 (= 515.13–519.4, 520.24–522.25 Diels Doxogr.), is read by Aristotle as vacillating between "every appearance true" and "no appearance true" (Metaph. 4.5, 1009b11–12; 1.2, 404a26–30). Both characterizations land at the same place: differences over the nature of the external world only affect the valence, not the structural absence of a true/false distinction among mental states.

**Section V, "Aristotle's Dilemma"** (book pp. 36–41), is the pivot of the article — the demonstration that Aristotle's critique rebounds on himself. Aristotle is committed to (1): "the intellect is related to the objects of conception in just the same way as the sense can sense to the objects of sense" (*DA* 3.4, 429a16–17). The disanalogies between intellect and sense (intellect not the activity of a bodily organ, *DA* 3.4, 429a18–27; *De gen. anim.* 2.3, 736b28–29) do not free him: incorporeality does not explain how error is possible. Aristotle also endorses a restricted version of (3) at *DA* 2.5, 417a17–20 + 418a3–6: theorists like Anaxagoras are right to the extent that the sense must INITIALLY be unlike the object in actuality, but must be POTENTIALLY like and during sensation BECOMES like. During-sensation likeness commits Aristotle to cause-content correspondence: sensation is clearly *about* its cause. The same structure governs conception (*DA* 3.4, 429a13–18; 430a3–5; 3.8, 431b28–29; *Metaph.* 12.7, 1072a30). In both sensation and conception, content and cause coincide.

Caston then notes (6) bites for Aristotle too. His technical reservation about describing sensation as "alteration" (sensation is rather a "progression towards itself and towards realization," εἰς αὐτὸ γὰρ ἡ ἐπίδοσις καὶ εἰς ἐντελέχειαν, *DA* 2.5, 417b6–7) is a worry about the *technical* sense of ἀλλοίωσις (change away from a thing's nature). In the broader sense of "being in a different state" (ἀλλοῖος), Aristotle remains vulnerable. He even endorses a version of (3b) explicitly: the object of a given act of sensation must exist (*DA* 2.5, 417b24–26).

The pivotal admission, on Caston's reading: there *is* a sense in which all appearances are true for Aristotle — namely, all those appearances that arise from sensation and conception, the most basic operations. This is not an embarrassment; it is the *restriction strategy*. The infallibility of basic operations is consciously bitten as a bullet, via the θιγγάνειν ("making contact") metaphor at *Metaphysics* 9.10, 1051b24–25: sensation and conception of incomposites either work or not at all. The point is that (3b) holds only for *certain types* of mental states; for OTHER kinds, Aristotle can evade the disastrous consequences of the first horn. Hence the catalogue (fn. 46, book p. 41): imagination, association, memory, expectation, thought, reasoning, deliberation, desire, action, passions, dreams — all require *phantasia*, all can be at variance with reality. Thought itself, in any role involving predication or inference, also goes beyond the basic level (only the conception of simple incomposites and indivisibles is infallible: *DA* 3.6, 430a26–27, b27–28; *Metaph.* 9.10, 1051b15–32, 1052a1–4). The conditions are now in place for the positive account: Aristotle must show that *phantasia* is not, like Molière's *virtus dormativa*, merely a problem redescribed as a solution.

**Section VI, "Distinguishing Capacities"** (book pp. 43–46), reconstructs Aristotle's battery of distinguishing arguments in 427b6–428b9. *Phantasia* is shown to differ from (a) sensation (428a5–18); (b) conception (428a17); (c) knowledge (428a18); (d) belief (427b16–24, 428a18–24); (e) the complex of belief and sensation (428a24–b9, with Plato's *Sophist* 264AB and *Timaeus* 52A as the explicit target). Caston identifies two distinct argument-types in Aristotle: (i) *extensional* — showing two powers are not always coinstantiated; (ii) *in-being* — showing two powers differ "in being" (τῷ εἶναι) even if always coinstantiated. The latter (*DA* 3.9, 432a31–b1; *De insomn.* 1, 459a16–18) is needed because phantasia is in fact coextensive with sensation, and yet must be distinct from it. Extensional arguments succeed against the identifications with thinking/belief/knowledge (not all animals reason: 427b7–8, b12–14; phantasia exists where reason does not: 428a24, b13–14). The decisive *in-being* argument turns on falsehood: sensation, conception, and knowledge are always true (427b12, 428a11–12, a17–18); phantasia can be false (428a11–12, b17–18); belief can be false too (428a12, b17) but differs from phantasia in extension and in requiring conviction (πίστις, 428a19–24). Caston works out the *phantasia*-vs-belief distinction in particular (book pp. 44–45): belief is constrained by what we judge to be right; *phantasia* freely conjures up images independent of what we judge to be true. *Phantasia* "stands midway between" the intellectual and the perceptual; it does not require conviction; it bears content in a neutral way; it can remain emotionally unaffected by its contents (3.3, 427a23–24) where belief affects us immediately (427a21–22).

**Section VII, "Phantasia and Error"** (book pp. 46–52), supplies the positive aetiology. Aristotle now turns to the third and final section of *DA* 3.3 (428b10–429a9). Causal factors continue to explain content, but DIFFERENTLY than for sensation: this is what permits falsehood, and consequently the plasticity of animal behavior (428b10–17). The aetiology in compressed form: in sensation, an object (αἰσθητόν) produces an initial stimulation (αἴσθημα) in peripheral sense organs; this change travels to the central sense organ and produces the sensory experience (αἴσθησις). But a change can give rise to FURTHER changes — indirect effects. The "change brought about by the functioning of sensation" (κίνησιν ὑπὸ τῆς ἐνεργείας τῆς αἰσθήσεως, 428b13) is necessarily similar to the sensation, and from this it follows that the second change can be false as well as true, making varied animal behavior possible (428b14–17). Later Aristotle explicitly identifies this second change as *phantasia* (428b25–26, 429a1; *De insomn.* 1, 459a17–18). *Phantasia* derives from sensation in two respects: (i) CAUSALLY it derives from a particular sensation; (ii) its CHARACTER derives from the character of that sensation. Hence *Rhetoric* 1.11, 1370a28 describes *phantasia* as "a weak sort of sensation" (αἴσθησίς τις ἀσθενής).

Caston's diagrams make the structure perspicuous. Diagram 1 (book p. 47): when one shouts in a canyon, the initial air-disturbance produces hearing; that same disturbance, reflected off the canyon wall, produces a second hearing — the echo. Diagram 2 (book p. 48): in cognition, the object (αἰσθητόν) produces a sensory stimulation (αἴσθημα) in the peripheral organ; this produces the experience (αἴσθησις) in the central organ; AND it produces the phantasma (also delivered to the central organ as a side-effect). The phantasma is the echo. It is directly caused by the αἴσθημα, not by the original object; its relation to the object is indirect, via the αἴσθημα.

The critical move (book pp. 49–50): the phantasma is *not essentially* linked to its causal origin. Its content is determined not by its *causal antecedents* but by its *causal powers* — its capacity to affect the central organ. These causal powers are sometimes modified on the way to the central organ — for example, by alcohol, sickness, or the conditions of dreaming (*De insomn.* 3, 461a8–24; *Problemata* 30.14, 957a5–15, a25–35). Caston's vivid example (book p. 49): morning observations of a salamander may produce phantasmata which linger in the system. Hours later, the effects of a half-bottle of whiskey may alter these phantasmata so that, in dreaming, they cause one to see not a salamander but an immense fire-breathing dragon. A dragon could not have been a causal ancestor — dragons do not exist. But these phantasmata have the *ability to affect the central organ* the way it WOULD be affected, were one to see a dragon. The causal history is irrelevant, except per accidens.

Caston formalizes the resulting account: (P) "For any phantasma φ and time t, the total effect φ can produce at t on the central sense organ is the same as the total effect some sensory stimulation s could produce on the central sense organ, were s to occur; and at t, φ is about whatever s would be about." This is a *subjunctive*, possibility-based account of mental content — radically different from the causal-history accounts that dominated philosophy of mind in the late twentieth century. The phantasma is about whatever a sensation with the same causal powers would be about, *even if no such sensation occurs and no such object exists*. Content thus diverges completely from causal ancestry and from what is actually the case in the world. *Phantasia* can be false.

Caston confirms (P) textually from *De insomniis* 2, 460b18–27: error occurs "not only when an object of perception is effecting change, but also when the sense is undergoing change itself — whenever, that is, it should happen to be changed in just the same way as [it would be] by an object of perception" (460b23–25). Caston's footnote 66 carefully glosses the ἀδήποτε relative-clause as introducing a same-content phrase, against Ross's misreading. *Metaphysics* 5.29, 1024b23–24 supplies further confirmation: a scene-painting and a dream are both "something, but not what they produce a phantasia of" (τι ἀλλ' οὐχ ὧν ἐμποιεῖ τὴν φαντασίαν), and for this reason can be called false. Content and cause split; falsehood follows.

The closing of section VII (book pp. 51–52) draws out three consequences. (i) The content of a phantasma is initially exactly similar to the content of the sensation that spawned it; later changes are relevant only insofar as they alter causal powers comparable to possible sensations. Phantasia thus remains *quasi-perceptual* — similar in TYPE of information to perception, capable of producing experiences phenomenally like perceptual experiences. (ii) But phantasmata are NOT themselves viewed, NOT little pictures of objects in the world; they are CHANGES in the perceptual system, individuated by their causal powers. (iii) The phenomenal experience need not actually occur for the phantasma to have the content it does — content is constituted by causal powers, not by manifest experience.

This non-pictorial reading allows Caston to distinguish the type of falsehood in *phantasia* from the propositional/linguistic falsehood Aristotle analyses at *Metaphysics* 9.10, 1051b1–6 (cf. *Metaph.* 6.4, 1027b20–22; *DA* 3.6, 430b1–2). Linguistic falsehood depends on combination of concepts not matching combination in the world; *phantasia*'s falsehood is not combinatorial. Nor are concepts at issue (*DA* 3.8, 432a10–14). This is significant because it permits an account of intentionality that does NOT presuppose the conceptual structure that makes language and thought possible. It can account for intentional states that fall below this threshold — animal mental life (*DA* 3.3, 428b16–17, 429a5–6) and much that occurs in humans (3.7–3.8). Even deliberation and higher cognition, which DO require concepts and inference, *build on phantasia*: the forms which are thought are "in" phantasmata (*DA* 3.7, 431b2; 3.8, 432a4–6, a12–14, 431a14–16). By making phantasia (rather than belief) the common currency among mental states, Aristotle emphasizes a form of intentionality more BASIC than the conceptual.

**Section VIII, "Phantasia and Reliability"** (book pp. 52–53), addresses specific conditions of truth and falsity. Reliability depends on which type of perception is involved — proper perceptibles (colors, flavors), common perceptibles (shapes, motion), or incidental perceptibles. The latter two are already error-prone due to the involvement of other fallible mental states including phantasia itself. For proper perceptibles, Aristotle's standing thesis is that sensation of them is always true (427b12, 428a11; 2.6, 418a11; *De sens.* 4, 442b8–10; *Metaph.* 4.5, 1010b2–3) — but he adds the curious qualification "or it has falsehood to the least possible extent" (ἢ ὅτι ὀλίγιστον ἔχουσα τὸ ψεῦδος, 428b19). Caston: this qualification is inexplicable on Aristotle's own account of sensation; it is explicable only if *phantasia* is implicated. The staring-at-white-or-green example (*De insomn.* 1, 459b7–13) confirms this: error in proper perception is mediated by phantasia. The further correlations among types-of-perception, concurrence, and reliability yield bizarre consequences (fn. 69): a phantasia originating from a false sensation of a proper object will be TRUE so long as concurrent; a phantasia originating from a true perception of a common/incidental object will be FALSE even if concurrent. Caston flags but does not fully resolve these consequences. They depend on contingent assumptions about how perception and phantasia typically function in real-world situations — not part of the core account of error.

**Section IX, "Conclusion"** (book pp. 54–55), gives the summative statement. The opening of *DA* 3.3 tells us what the chapter is about. Predecessors cannot account for error because they bind content too closely to cause. Aristotle's own account of sensation and conception is prey to the same problem. There is nothing wrong with some mental capacities being infallible, even foundational ones, so long as the content of OTHER mental states is not accounted for in the same way. Aristotle solves this by introducing a new capacity — *phantasia* — which depends on sensation (causally and in content) but successfully evades the dilemma. The more complicated aetiology (causal POWERS rather than causal ANCESTORS) provides an account that does not require the existence of the object — framed subjunctively, in terms of the type of effects an object WOULD have on the central sensory organ IF it were sensed. This is sufficient for basic kinds of error and provides a basis for the content of most intentional states without appealing to concepts or predication.

The polemical close: *phantasia* is NOT a "locus of non-paradigmatic sensory experience" (Schofield 1978, explicitly contested at fn. 70). The point is precisely that error is so commonplace, so NORMAL, in animal life, that psychology must account for it — and this cannot be done on the causal account Aristotle offers for sensation and conception. To account for ordinary, paradigmatic animal behavior, a higher degree of intentionality is required, and this requires *phantasia*. The crucial question whether this account forms the basis for a fully adequate theory of content is deferred to forthcoming works ("Aristotle and the Problem of Intentionality" and *The Problem of Intentionality in Ancient Greek Philosophy*, Cambridge UP forthcoming, fn. 67). Caston signs off "Brown University."

## § The Protagorean antirealism confrontation: *Metaphysics* IV and *De Anima* III.3

A central interpretive move of Caston's article is to read *De Anima* III.3 as the psychological half of a project whose epistemological half is *Metaphysics* IV.5. The Protagorean thesis "every appearance is true" is the antirealist target in both. *Metaphysics* IV.5, 1009b12–17 gives Aristotle's diagnosis: "because they suppose perception is thought and this is alteration, perceptual appearance is of necessity true." The route there is via (6) thinking-and-perceiving-as-alterations, which captures Anaxagoras even though he denies bodily-process (2). In *DA* III.3, the route is via (3) like-by-like, which is the route the Empedocles citations support. Both routes terminate in (5) all-appearances-true. Where *Metaph.* IV.5 confronts the antirealism on epistemological grounds, *DA* III.3 confronts it on psychological grounds: error is a *datum* that any theory must account for if it is to count as a psychological theory at all (book p. 29, opening of §III A).

The Platonic parallel is acknowledged but kept at arm's length. *Theaetetus* 151E–157D (esp. 152C; the Secret Doctrine at 152D ff., 153E) argues that knowledge-is-perception (thesis A) is linked to all-appearances-true (thesis B) and the Heraclitean Secret Doctrine (thesis C). Burnyeat 1990 (fn. 22) is Caston's guide to the mutual implication of theses A and B. But Aristotle's argument differs structurally: instead of the Secret Doctrine, Aristotle appeals to (2) and (3); instead of concluding all-appearances-true, Aristotle uses it as one horn of a dilemma. The Platonic-Aristotelian shift: from an epistemological argument about the conditions of knowledge to a psychological argument about the conditions of intentional content.

Caston's reading of Aristotle's tactical use of the dilemma in *DA* III.3 — as a polemical exaggeration ("animals spend MORE time in error than in veridical cognition") in the service of forcing the predecessors onto one of two unacceptable horns — is one of his most acute exegetical contributions. The literal force of οἰκειότερον/πλείω (book p. 27 + fn. 20) commits Aristotle's predecessors to a counter-Aristotelian view about the predominance of error. Aristotle's own epistemological commitment (in *De sens.* 1, 436b18–437a3) is that the perceptual apparatus naturally discriminates real differences. The dilemma's polemical exaggeration serves to make the predecessors' commitments visible.

## § *Phantasia* as required by appearance-fallibility: Caston's core argument

Caston's positive thesis can be stated in five steps. (1) Aristotle inherits the like-by-like model from his predecessors: in sensation and conception, the cognitive faculty becomes like its object in actuality during the act, and the content of the resulting state is determined by what brought it about. (2) This model entails that whenever such states occur, their content corresponds exactly to their cause: they are infallible with regard to their proper objects (proper perceptibles for sensation; incomposites for conception). (3) Aristotle is willing to bite this bullet for basic operations — the θιγγάνειν metaphor at *Metaph.* 9.10 explicitly endorses an all-or-nothing model for the most basic forms of cognition. (4) But Aristotle ALSO recognizes that animals spend much of their lives in error, and that most mental states (imagination, memory, expectation, thought beyond the simple, reasoning, deliberation, desire, action, passions, dreams) admit of falsehood. (5) These cannot be explained by the like-by-like model; they must be explained by a faculty with a more complex aetiology — a faculty whose content can diverge from its cause. *Phantasia* is exactly this faculty.

The internal mechanism: *phantasia*'s content is determined by causal POWERS rather than causal ANCESTORS. The phantasma is an indirect, echo-like effect of the αἴσθημα; its causal powers can be modified by alcohol, sickness, dreaming, or simply by time. What it is about is determined by what type of sensation it has the powers to mimic — not by what produced it. This subjunctive account is what allows content-cause divergence; and content-cause divergence is what allows falsehood. The salamander-to-dragon counterexample (book p. 49) is the rhetorical and theoretical climax: a phantasma originating in a salamander-observation can come to be about a dragon — an object that could never have been a causal ancestor.

The deep significance of Caston's reading: *phantasia* is not a marginal phenomenon to be tucked into a corner of Aristotelian psychology. It is the SUBSTRATE of all higher intentional states. It provides a non-propositional, sub-conceptual form of intentionality on which the conceptual and propositional structures of belief, language, and reasoning depend. The forms which are thought are "in" phantasmata (*DA* 3.7, 431b2; 3.8, 432a4–6, a12–14, 431a14–16). By making *phantasia* the common currency among mental states, Aristotle locates the basic level of mental content below the threshold of concepts.

## § *Phantasia* and the appearance/truth distinction

Caston's most innovative philosophical contribution is the reading of *phantasia* as containing its OWN internal grounds for distinguishing true-appearance from false-appearance. The Protagorean cannot make this distinction because for Protagoras every appearance is just what it is — the antirealist has no resources to mark some appearances as deviations from reality. Aristotle's solution is not a coherence theory (true appearances are those that cohere with other appearances) nor a representationalist gap-theory (true appearances are those that successfully refer to mind-independent reality, false appearances those that fail). It is rather a *causal-powers semantics* internal to the perceptual system: a phantasma is TRUE when it matches the causal powers of an actual sensation that itself corresponds to an external object; it is FALSE when its causal powers correspond to no actual external object, or to an object different from what is in fact present.

This is, in late-twentieth-century terms, neither a pure "wide content" nor a pure "narrow content" account. The content of a phantasma is determined by its causal powers (which are internal/intrinsic), but those causal powers are themselves understood by reference to the type of sensation they would mimic (which is externally individuated). The account thus permits the same content to be tokened in different external circumstances (the dragon-phantasma in waking observation of a dragon-puppet or in whiskey-altered dreaming) while preserving the truth-evaluable character of the content.

The non-propositional character of *phantasia*'s falsehood (book pp. 51–52) is essential to this account. Linguistic falsehood, on Aristotle's analysis at *Metaph.* 9.10, 1051b1–6, is a matter of combining concepts in ways that diverge from how things are combined in the world. *Phantasia*'s falsehood is not combinatorial; it is a misalignment between cause (or what would be the cause, under the relevant counterfactual) and content. This is significant because it permits an account of intentionality that operates below the threshold of language. Animals — and humans much of the time — operate at this level.

## § Comparison with Nussbaum 1985, White 1985, and Frede 1992

Caston's article positions itself in three different ways relative to the cluster.

**Versus Nussbaum 1985 ("Phantasia in Action"):** Caston does not engage Nussbaum's 1985 essay directly. He cites only the 1978 first-edition *De Motu* commentary (fn. 1). Substantive convergences nonetheless exist. Both treat *phantasia* as a "horizontal" faculty cutting across the perception-thought divide; both reject the reduction of *phantasia* to mental imagery in the empiricist sense; both insist on *phantasia*'s necessity for the full range of cognitive/practical/passional states. But the methodological gulf is wide. Nussbaum reads *phantasia* primarily as the faculty of seeing-as that mediates between desire and action — her interest is action-theoretic and ethical. Caston reads *phantasia* primarily as a solution to the theoretical problem of how mental content can diverge from cause — his interest is metaphysical and content-theoretical. Where Nussbaum's centerpiece is the *De Motu Animalium* (especially DMA 6–7 on the practical syllogism), Caston's centerpiece is *DA* 3.3 (especially 428b10–429a9 on the aetiology of phantasmata). Caston's footnote 46 catalogues *phantasia*'s explanatory roles across imagination, association, memory, expectation, thought, reasoning, deliberation, desire, action, passions, and dreams — an inventory that substantially overlaps Nussbaum's, but in Caston's hands becomes evidence for the content-theoretical thesis rather than the practical thesis.

**Versus White 1985 ("The Meaning of Phantasia in *De Anima*, III, 3–8"):** Caston does NOT cite White anywhere. The two essays are independent contemporaneous productions in different scholarly cultures — White in the Thomistic-philological tradition (with German philological backbone from Bonitz, Freudenthal, Hicks), Caston in the analytic philosophy-of-mind tradition. Caston's *Phronesis* publication (the leading Anglophone Aristotle journal) and White's *Dialogue* publication (the leading Canadian philosophy journal) belong to different scholarly networks. The two essays converge on (1) the basic structure of *phantasia*'s grounding in sensation, (2) its role as substrate of higher cognition, (3) its role in temporal mediation (White: phantasia gives stability against the now-fleetingness of aisthesis; Caston: phantasmata persist after the cause has departed), and (4) the refusal to reduce *phantasia* to ordinary "imagination." But they diverge sharply on emphasis. White's centerpiece is the "picture-vs-encounter" distinction at *DA* 3.3, 427b21–24 (phantasia "coolly regards" affecting scenes as one would a picture). Caston's centerpiece is the dilemma at 427b2–5 and the formal causal-powers account (P) at book p. 49. White treats *phantasia* as ennobling — the source of the "familiar interior landscape" that orients human cognition toward the noetic. Caston treats *phantasia* as analytically necessary — a posit required to evade an explanatory dilemma. White's reading is Thomistic-humanist; Caston's is analytic-philosophical. The non-overlap of citation networks is striking: Caston's Burnyeat, Modrak, Wedin, Schofield, Frede stable does not appear in White's apparatus, and White's Bonitz, Freudenthal, Augustine, Aquinas-implicit stable does not appear in Caston's. The two essays demonstrate the bifurcation of late-twentieth-century Aristotle scholarship along Anglophone-analytic vs. continental-Thomistic lines.

**Versus Frede 1992 ("The Cognitive Role of Phantasia in Aristotle"):** Caston cites Frede only in the opening literature footnote. Both essays insist on *phantasia*'s cognitive role and resist its reduction to mental imagery. Both engage Schofield, Modrak, and the Nussbaum-Rorty volume tradition. The divergence: Frede emphasizes *phantasia*'s intellectualist dimension — its role in concept-application, in the recognition of types, in the structure of predication. Caston emphasizes *phantasia*'s SUB-conceptual dimension — phantasia as non-propositional intentionality, the substrate of perceptual content BELOW the threshold of language and thought (*DA* 3.3, 428b16–17, 429a5–6). Caston's reading is more sharply anti-intellectualist about *phantasia*'s basic operation while granting that higher cognition uses phantasmata.

**Caston's polemical positioning:** Caston's explicit targets are Schofield (rejected at fn. 70 for the "non-paradigmatic" framing), Modrak (rejected at fns. 7 + 48 + 55 for intellectualist/propositional readings), and Wedin (rejected at fn. 49 for making *phantasia* a condition of sensation of proper perceptibles). His allies are Burnyeat (Theaetetus), Cashdollar (incidental perception), Kenny (illusion-argument), and Engmann (broader falsity). The cluster picture: Caston extends Nussbaum/Frede on *phantasia*'s cognitive role and Engmann on broader falsity, while contesting Modrak/Wedin on intellectualist readings and Schofield on marginal-vs-paradigmatic status.

## § Cross-pipeline bridges (Aristotle corpus, Heidegger BCAP GA 18 §§14–22, Dalton dissertation chapters)

**Bridge to the Aristotle corpus ingestion.** Caston's analytic apparatus — the formal numbered theses (1) through (6) + (P), the dilemma reconstruction, the diagrams — supplies a rigorous logical scaffold for the DA 3.3 passages already indexed in the corpus. The Bekker loci catalogue above (40+ DA passages, 5 Metaphysics passages, multiple De Insomniis, De Memoria, De Motu, De Sensu, De Interp., Rhet. references) substantially overlaps with the Aristotle KU set and provides a content-theoretical lens — distinct from the action-theoretical lens of Nussbaum and the noetic-preparation lens of White — for the same passages. The salamander/dragon counterexample (book p. 49) supplies a vivid illustration available for use in dissertation prose dealing with Aristotelian content/cause divergence.

**Bridge to Heidegger BCAP GA 18 §§14–22.** Heidegger's reading of *pathos* and Aristotelian psychology in the BCAP intersects with Caston's reading at a precise point: Aristotle's analysis of how *appearance* functions as the substrate of practical and theoretical comportment. Where Heidegger (continental-phenomenological register) reads *phantasia* as part of the structure of *Befindlichkeit* and prereflective disclosure, Caston (analytic register) reads *phantasia* as the substrate of non-propositional intentionality. The convergence is striking: both see *phantasia* as operating BELOW the threshold of explicit predication, providing the precognitive ground on which higher cognition builds. The divergence: Heidegger reads this through the lens of *aletheia* and disclosure; Caston reads it through the lens of intentional content and the truth-conditions of mental states. The BCAP §§14–22 passages on the structuring of *pathos* in Rhetoric II.2–6 (Aristotle's locus, on Caston's reading, for *phantasia* in passions, fn. 46) supply a productive bridge: Caston's content-theoretical reading complements Heidegger's existential reading without displacing it.

**Bridge to Dalton dissertation chapters.** Caston's signature thesis — that *phantasia* contains its own internal grounds for distinguishing true from false appearance, evading both Protagorean antirealism and representationalist gap-theories — provides a precise philosophical anchor for any chapter dealing with the rhetorical ontology of appearance, the phenomenology of false-appearance, or the relation between *phainomena* and *aletheia*. The non-propositional intentionality reading (book pp. 51–52) is particularly useful for chapters dealing with prereflective rhetorical attunement or the conditions of false-appearance in mediated environments (cf. White's contemporary coda on man-made phantasmata, book pp. 504–505 — White and Caston converge tacitly here, though by entirely different routes). The salamander/dragon example offers a concrete touchstone for any discussion of how content can detach from causal ancestry in the experience of mediated appearance.
