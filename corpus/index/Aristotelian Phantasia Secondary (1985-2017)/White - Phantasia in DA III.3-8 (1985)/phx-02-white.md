## § Overview: White's interpretive orientation

Kevin White's 1985 essay is a sustained, close textual reading of *De Anima* III.3–8 — the chapter on *phantasia* (III.3) and the immediately following chapters on *nous* (III.4–8). White's overarching thesis is straightforward but consequential for the cluster: in this Aristotelian text-region, *phantasia* is treated not as a self-standing topic but as a *preliminary*, *preparatory* condition for the analysis of human *noein*. The whole essay is governed by Aristotle's announcement at DA III.3, 427b15–16 — that there is no thought without *phantasia*, just as there is no *phantasia* without sensation — and by the structural fact that III.3 is wedged between the completed treatment of *aisthesis* (II.5–III.2) and the impending treatment of *nous* (III.4–8) (pdf p. 1).

White's interpretive sensibility is Thomistic in a distinctive sense. Aquinas is not named in the running text, but White's *method* — the careful sequencing of soul-powers, the lexical-genealogical patience, the willingness to import Latin scholastic terminology where it illuminates (Bonitz's *species rei obiectae* vs. *eam actionem qua rerum imagines animo informamus*, pdf p. 4), and the striking comparison to St. Augustine's *Confessions* X.8–26 on *memoria* (fn. 67, pdf p. 16) — sets him within the Catholic-Aristotelian reception. White is also unusually open to the modern continental tradition: footnote 11 (pdf p. 3) deploys an expansive note on the *phaenomena realia* of Leibniz, Kant's *Erscheinung*, and the "phenomenologies" of Hegel, Husserl, and Heidegger.

The Anglophone tradition with which Nussbaum (PHX-01) most centrally contends — Ross, Hamlyn, Wittgenstein, Sartre, Dennett — does not figure in White's footnotes. Nussbaum, however, *does* appear, three times (fns. 16, 63, 80, pdf pp. 4, 15, 19), but always as the author of the *1978 first edition* of *Aristotle's De Motu Animalium*. White does not cite, and apparently did not see, Nussbaum's *1985 second-edition* essay that is PHX-01. The two 1985 essays were thus independent contemporaneous productions, working from overlapping textual bases toward methodologically mirrored conclusions.

## § Section-by-section synthesis

### Introduction (pdf 1–2, book 483–484)

White locates DA III.3 at "a critical juncture" — a transitional moment between the treatments of sensation and thought. The incidental, intermediate placement of the *phantasia* chapter, White contends, is itself an exegetical clue: Aristotle treats *phantasia* not for its own sake but because the analysis of human thought *requires* it. The chapter on *phantasia* is implicitly a chapter on *man* — the De Anima's particular concern with the human soul and the activity of *noein* emerges most thematically here (pdf 2).

### § 1. *Phantasia* and "Appearing" (pdf 2–7, book 484–489)

The methodological opening is a refusal to translate. "Imagination," White argues, fails as a rendering of *phantasia* on three grounds (pdf 2–3): (a) the etymological connotation of imitation/resemblance carried by *imago*/*imitor* — alien to the *phantasia*-family; (b) the post-Galenic, faculty-psychological inner-sense tradition that locates "imagination" in a brain organ — foreign to Aristotle; and (c) the modern poetic and Romantic register of "imagination" as "extraordinary power of insight, spiritual vision, or discovery" — a connotation as remote from Aristotle as the previous two.

White then pursues the etymology. Aristotle himself derives *phantasia* from *phaos* ("light") at DA III.3 429a3–4 (pdf 3). Whether or not the derivation is philologically exact, both words ultimately trace to the verb *phao* ("to give light, shine, beam, especially of the heavenly bodies"), from which come *phaino* ("show"), *phainomai* ("appear"), *phantazo* ("make visible, show oneself"), and the verbal nouns *phantasia* and *phantasma*. Genealogically, then, *phantasia* would mean the prominent or noticeable self-presentation of something to eyesight, but it quickly acquired a broader meaning encompassing "a presentation to consciousness, whether immediate or in memory, whether true or illusory" (pdf 3, glossing Liddell-Scott).

The crucial structural feature of the *phantasia*-family for White is the *object/subject polarity* (pdf 4). Citing Bonitz's *Index Aristotelicus* (1955), White distinguishes the *species rei obiectae* sense — the *look* of the heavenly body or color — from the *eam actionem qua rerum imagines animo informamus* sense — the action of the soul. Freudenthal's German parallel: *Aussehen/Erscheinung* vs. *Vorstellung/Einbildung*. White announces that he will focus exclusively on the second, subjective, psychological sense: the action by which the soul presents something to itself. This narrowing matters: it cleanly demarcates the essay's scope and aligns *phantasia* with the genesis of *phantasmata* "within us."

Aristotle's near-tautological formula at DA III.3 428a1–2 — *kath' hen legomen phantasma ti hemin gignesthai* — is read with care (pdf 5). White extracts three points: (i) Aristotle describes *phantasia* by the ordinary way of speaking; (ii) *phantasia* is made to account for the *genesis* of the *phantasma*; (iii) the *phantasma* involves the transfer of an appearance from its origin in *that which appears* into another medium. While Plato uses *phantasmata* for outer reflections (in water, e.g. *Rep.* VI 510a, *Tim.* 46a), Aristotle reserves *phantasma* for *inner* appearances, where we ourselves become the medium into which the reflected appearances are cast, transformed, and separated from their originals (pdf 5). The inner appearance, then, is *for* and *contained by* the individual alone. The *kath' hen* construction, read through *Metaphysics* V.18 1022a19–20 (where *kath' ho* equals *aition*, cause), signals that *phantasia* is an underlying causal action of the soul accounting for the presence of *phantasmata* in us (pdf 5).

The section closes by situating *phantasia* against the Platonic-Protagorean opposition between *ousia* and *genesis* (pdf 7). *Phantasia* arises in the gap opened between sensing and thinking, between flux and being — a gap to which Aristotle's distinction between sensation and knowledge is itself a response.

### § 2. The Context of the Discussion of *Phantasia* (pdf 7–11, book 489–493)

White structures this section in two subsections.

**§ 2.1 The Plan of the *De Anima*** (pdf 7–9). White rehearses the pre-thematic remarks on *phantasia* scattered through DA I and II: I.1 403a8–10 (thinking is intimately related to *phantasia*); II.2 413b22–23 (*phantasia* and desire concomitant with sensation); II.3 414b16 (the place of *phantasia* among the soul-powers is "unclear"); III.8 420b29–33 (*phantasia* involved in voice); III.2 425b24–25 (*phantasiai* persist in sense-organs after objects are gone) (pdf 7).

White then surveys the unstable architecture of the latter half of the treatise: nutrition (II.4), sensation (II.5–III.2), *phantasia* (III.3), thought (III.4–8), locomotion (III.9–11), and supplementary remarks (III.12–13). The plan of III.3–11 admits at least three readings: (a) by grades-of-life; (b) by *to kritikon* (the "distinguishing power," collecting thought and sensation) versus *to kinetikon* (the locomotive power), the major division at III.9 432a15–17; or (c) by powers peculiar to soul versus powers common to soul and body (pdf 9–10). White's verdict: *nous* is "a continuing and unifying theme throughout III, 3–11" (pdf 11, fn. 39).

**§ 2.2 The Introduction of DA III.3** (pdf 10–12). Close reading of 427a17–b14 — Aristotle's distinction of thinking from sensing — followed by the abrupt introduction of *phantasia* at 428a15–16. White identifies two basic sources of potential confusion between *phantasia* and *noein* (pdf 11–12):

1. *Both can occur in the absence of a sensible object*. The mnemonist arranging mental images at 427b18–20 illustrates this. Sensation requires the physical presence of its object; *phantasia*, like thinking, has for its immediate object "something interior which remains and may be arranged for consideration in the absence of sensible things" (pdf 11). Both thinking and *phantasia* thus have "an interiority and an independence of the sensible world which makes them resemble one another" (pdf 11).

2. *Both can serve as inner springs of animal activity*. White quotes DA III.3 429a4–8 in Hett's Loeb translation (fn. 44): brutes act in accordance with *phantasiai* because they have no mind, while men act so when the mind "is temporarily clouded over by emotion, or disease, or sleep." Aristotle even allows that *phantasia* "may actually be called a certain *noesis*" (DA III.10 433a9–12).

Aristotle's distinguishing strokes are equally brisk. *Phantasia*'s exercise "rests with us" (as the mnemonist's images do), while judging (*doxazein*) involves a reference beyond us to truth and falsity. And — White's *signature* exegetical move — when our judgment confronts something fearful or terrible we are immediately moved, while *phantasia* "coolly regards such affecting scenes as one would a picture" (DA III.3 427b21–24, pdf 12).

### § 3. The Definition of *Phantasia* (pdf 12–16, book 494–498)

After the introductory excursus, White reconstructs Aristotle's positive definition. *Phantasia* is rooted in *aisthesis* — the close resemblance between sensation and inner appearance is most dramatically evident in dreaming, where the dreamer takes himself to be actually seeing and hearing (DA III.3, *De Insomniis* 1, *Rhet.* I.11) (pdf 12). Yet Aristotle pointedly remarks at *Metaphysics* IV.5 1010b3 that *phantasia* is not the same as *aisthesis*. The identification of the two is Protagorean, associated with the doctrine of the truth of *phainomena* and the flux of *ousia*/*genesis*, and is rejected by both Plato and Aristotle (pdf 12–13).

The decisive distinguishing point is temporal-physiological: an "appearing" (*phainetai*) may occur when the sense-powers are inoperative — in dreams or with eyes closed — so *phantasia* cannot be the same thing as sensation (DA III.3 428a5–16); yet *phantasia* does not occur *without* sensation, since both take place in the same subjects and are directed to the same objects (428b11–13). The precise relation between these two activities yields the definition.

White then unfolds Aristotle's four-faceted account of *aisthesis* (pdf 13–14): (a) a passive "being moved" (*kineisthai*), "altered" (*alloiousthai*), "acted upon" (*paschein*); (b) a process of actualization (*energein*); (c) an assimilation (*homoiotai*) of the sense-power to its object, glossing the saying "like is known by like"; (d) the reception of sensible form *without* matter, like wax taking the imprint, but not the material, of a seal. From the *first* of these features alone — that sensation is a certain physical motion (*kinesis*) — Aristotle constructs the *phantasia*-definition: *phantasia* is **a movement produced by sensation actively operating** — *kinesis hypo tes aistheseos tes kat' energeian gignomenes* (DA III.3 428b10–11, 429a1–2, pdf 14). This secondary motion necessarily resembles sensation (which is an assimilation to the sensible object), but continues in the absence of the sensible object and the inoperancy of the sense-power.

White's signature metaphor follows: *phantasia* is "a reproductive faculty" through which "the momentary, stillborn life of sensations is perpetuated in their offspring" (pdf 14). This biological-generative image reframes the kinesis-definition as a perpetuation, a survival of sensation in its likeness.

The *De Insomniis* deepens this account. White takes time over 459a15–22 (pdf 15): the imaginative (*phantastikon*) is the same as the sensitive faculty (*aisthetikon*) although different in essence (*einai*); *phantasia* is "the process set up by a sense-faculty in a state of activity" (*he hypo tes kat' energeian aistheseos gignomene kinesis*). A dream is "some sort of mental image (*phantasma*) which appears in sleep"; dreaming belongs to the sensitive faculty but to it *qua* imaginative. Aristotle further explains the persistence of *pathos* after sensation through the analogies of serially-moved projectiles and alloiosis-by-transmitted-heat (pdf 14, *De Ins.* 2).

The closing paragraph of § 3 (pdf 16) consolidates: *phantasia* is "a dimension of the life of all animal souls." Beyond reception, the animal soul is "a *preserver* and a *storehouse* of the sensible forms of things" (pdf 16). The lingering, resonating, echoing presence of sensible forms freed from their original matter is "the essence of *phantasia* for Aristotle." It is here that White inserts the explicit Augustinian parallel: Aristotle's *phantasia* "resembles very much the *memoria* eloquently described by St. Augustine: *venio in campos et lata praetoria memoriae, ubi sunt thesauri innumerabilium imaginum cuiusce modi rebus sensis invectarum*…" (fn. 67, *Confessions* X.8–26).

### § 4. *Phantasia* and *Nous* (pdf 16–21, book 498–503)

The longest and architecturally most important section of the essay. White's task: to show *how* *phantasia* subserves *noein*.

He begins (pdf 16–17) with the Aristotelian parallelism: like sensation, thinking is a "being acted upon" (*paschein*) which is possible because thought is receptive (*dektikon*) and potentially (*dunamei*) identical with the forms (*eide*) of beings (DA III.4 429a13–18). Both cognitive powers are characterized not by any *a priori* structure but by "a specific emptiness, openness, and susceptibility to the forms of things" (pdf 16). The crucial difference: thought is wholly *unmixed* (*amige*) — free of any admixture with matter and body, "whether in the power or in its object."

DA III.8 (pdf 17, 431b21–432a1) extends this parallel: the soul is in a way all the beings (*onta*); the beings are either sensible (*aistheta*) or intelligible (*noeta*); knowledge is in a way the knowable objects, sensation is in a way the sensible objects. The soul's two principal cognitive powers, while empty of themselves, are receptive to the assimilating influence of the *eide* of beings.

**The central question** (pdf 18). Why does *nous* depend more immediately on *phantasia* than on *aisthesis*? Why are *phantasmata* serviceable to *noein* as *aisthemata* are not? White answers in the register of time. *Aisthesis* is wholly confined to "the now" (432a3–4): "even to the extent that the sensible beings show some degree of constancy, our sensitive apprehension of them is inevitably momentary and changing. *Aisthesis* continually offers to *nous* its object, but only to at once withdraw it and substitute a different object" (pdf 19). Even if *nous* could chase *aisthesis* through its transformations, the essence glimpsed in rapid succession would not be the unmoving, timeless *einai* which *nous* requires of its object.

*Phantasia* is the human soul's response to this dilemma. It "lends to the appearances a stability which they do not have of themselves" (pdf 19). By means of preserved *phantasmata*, *nous* is enabled to continuously behold its timeless object even when the mediating sensible thing is past, absent, or in motion. Preserved *phantasmata* are also necessary to learning (*Physics* IV.10–14; *EN* X.4 1174a13–17): without retention and accumulation of past encounters, there could be no acquisition of knowledge, but only a forgetful succession of insights. The corollary White draws is striking: "of itself the power of thought has no memory" (pdf 19). Both *aisthesis* and *nous* are purely present actualizations — *aisthesis* because of its confinement to the now, *nous* because of its orientation to the timeless. Retention is wholly the work of *phantasia*.

*Phantasia* extends *nous* into the future as well (pdf 19, DA III.7 431b2–10). The thinking faculty (*noetikon*) thinks the forms (*eide*) in the mental images (*phantasmata*); just as in sensation pursuit/avoidance is defined, so the soul, occupied with *phantasmata*, is moved; sometimes "by the images (*phantasmasin*) or thoughts (*noemasin*) in the soul, just as if it were seeing, it calculates and plans for the future." Once *nous* has gained "a foothold in time" by means of *phantasia*, it can put its survey of the past into the service of a calculation of the future. The importance of experience for practical wisdom rests on this preserving activity.

The geometrical analogy (pdf 20) develops Aristotle's image at *De Memoria* 1 449b31–450a9. *Phantasia* resembles Plato's "painter in the soul" (*Philebus* 39b1 ff.). As a geometer with a portable slate uses a specific right-angled triangle to deduce a truth about all triangles, so the noetic soul considers truth at its leisure — not because it has no need of paradigmatic sensible images, but because it can produce its own such images in the activity of *phantasia*. *Phantasia* preserves determinate quantity; *nous* disregards this and disengages the immaterial "being" of material beings.

White inserts a discreet polemical observation here (pdf 20). The geometrical comparison may indicate that Aristotle is responding to *Republic* VI's divided-line account (510a1, e2), which also compares a certain kind of thinking (*dianoia*) to the image-making of geometers. But while Plato's Socrates attempts to describe a *higher* kind of thinking (*noesis*) that makes no use of images, Aristotle, "like the Glaucon of the *Republic*, seems to have difficulty in conceiving the possibility of such a pure and imageless thought" (pdf 20–21). Aristotle's *nous* is image-bound in a way Plato's is not.

The section closes (pdf 21) on the continuity-of-noesis theme. Thinking, according to Aristotle, is an activity which "of its very nature tends towards a constant, uninterrupted actualization" (DA III.5 430a22) and which man can perform most continuously (*EN* X.7 1177a23–25). *Phantasia* provides "some secure foundation, however intermittent, for this divine operation in the life of a perishable being" (pdf 21). White's dialectical formula: while *phantasia* may be "the source of the failure of human *noein* to fully realize itself, it is also what makes this activity possible at all" (pdf 21).

The final paragraph of § 4 (pdf 21–22) consolidates: *nous* and *aisthesis* are strikingly similar in their empty receptivity and total, present-directed absorption in the forms of beings. The union of these two presents — and hence the unity of the human soul — requires the mediating activity of *phantasia*, which does not merely *receive*, as do *nous* and *aisthesis*, but also *retains*. By allowing the movement of sensation to persist, the impermanent sensible beings, in their reflections within us, are caused to imitate the fixed *noeta*; human thinking is thereby enabled to imitate the noetic life of the gods.

### Conclusion (pdf 21–23, book 503–505)

White draws three threads. First, *phantasia* pervades human life. Thinking is impossible without concurrent *phantasia*; when thought is eclipsed by emotion, disease, or sleep, man acts according to *phantasia* as brutes do. *Phantasia* "in its hidden interaction with the passions" plays a significant role in moral and political life (*Rhetoric* II.2–6 on anger, fear, confidence, shame; *De Insomniis* 2 on the cowardly, erotic, angry; *Rhetoric* I.11 on the "self-image" as spur to victory, honour, excellence). *Phantasia* continues in sleep, perhaps more intensely. White's emblematic formula (pdf 22): "man never ceases to take his bearings in the world by means of *phantasia*."

Second, the lexical genealogy returns as a cosmic-orientational closure (pdf 22). As the roots of *phantasia* connote light, visibility, and heavenly phenomena, so the objective sense of *phantasia* in Aristotle names the milky way, comets, shooting stars, planets, lightning, rainbows — celestial occurrences which orient man in the cosmos (*Meteor.*, *De Mundo*, *Problemata*, *De Coloribus*, *De Sensu*). Colors, too, each have their own *phantasia*. These visible phenomena lend resonances that carry over into the subjective sense.

Third, the culminating formulation (pdf 23) — White's distinctively Thomistic-humanist contribution. *By internalizing and preserving such features, the power of *phantasia* provides man with a familiar interior landscape* which, in contrast with the sensible beings in themselves, he may consult with confidence in its permanence. The relative permanence conferred on the sensible beings by *phantasia* is "necessary and interesting to man in many practical ways," but its *highest* function is to provide "an index of orientation and differentiation to the power of *nous*." It is this highest function that points to the motive for the *De Anima*'s digression on *phantasia* and "to the deepest meaning of the inner appearances in the life of the human soul."

White ends with a coda of contemporary urgency (pdf 23). Aristotle's account is "of particular relevance in our own time, when the scene of outer appearances has been overlaid and intensified by an unprecedented proliferation of man-made *phantasmata*." Artificial *phantasmata* are "in large part appearances of man himself," formed from the beginning to become inner appearances and thereby to affect the course of human thought, passion, and action. Hence the paradox: *phantasia*, "the private and inner side of man's contact with the sensible beings," has its greatest significance "in our waking and public life."

## § The picture-vs-encounter distinction (DA III.3 427b21–24)

White's *signature exegetical move* deserves separate attention. Aristotle, having distinguished thinking from sensing, must distinguish *phantasia* from *judgment* (*doxazein*, *hypolepsis*). He offers two indications. The second is the picture-vs-encounter contrast: when our judgment confronts something fearful or terrible we are *immediately moved*, while *phantasia* "coolly regards such affecting scenes as one would a picture" (paraphrasing 427b21–24; pdf 12).

The locus matters out of proportion to its size. For White, it articulates *phantasia*'s characteristic detachment from the immediate practical involvement of judgment. *Phantasia* is the soul's distance from the affective immediacy of encounter — the cool spectator-stance toward what would otherwise move us. This detachment is the structural condition for *phantasia*'s mediating function: precisely because *phantasia* is not the encounter-with, it can preserve, present, and serve as the substrate of *nous*.

This makes the picture-vs-encounter distinction the *litmus test* of phantasia-from-judgment differentiation in White's reading. It also explains White's general comfort with the lexical-cosmic register: *phantasia*'s detachment makes it the soul's mirror of the *panorama of nature*, the cool internal-cosmos register that culminates in the interior-landscape thesis.

## § *Phantasia* as preparation for *noein*

White's structural thesis — already announced in his Introduction and progressively unfolded — is that DA III.3–8 should be read as a continuous *single* argument in which the *phantasia*-chapter prepares the *nous*-chapters. Three considerations sustain this reading:

1. *Architectural placement*. The *phantasia* chapter is inserted between *aisthesis* and *nous*, not given its own organic slot in the soul-power hierarchy. Aristotle, White notes, does not even isolate *phantasia* as a distinct power constituting a grade of life — it is a "dimension" of the animal soul, the same faculty as *aisthetikon* but different in *einai* (DA *De Ins.* 459a15–22).

2. *Definitional dependence*. The kinesis-definition (428b10–11) makes *phantasia* a *secondary* motion produced by sensation. *Phantasia* presupposes *aisthesis* downward and prepares *nous* upward — it is the structural link.

3. *Functional service*. The argument of § 4 — *phantasia* lends stability to appearances, supplies *nous* with a timeless-object substrate, retains the past, projects the future — completes the case. *Phantasia* exists in the human soul for the sake of, and in the service of, *noein*.

This reading is methodologically the *inverse* of Nussbaum's (see below): where Nussbaum's framing essay sees *phantasia* as primarily a faculty operative in *action* (III.9–11, *De Motu*), White stays within the cognitive-theoretical arc (III.3–8) and treats practical phantasia only in footnote 80.

## § Comparison with Nussbaum 1985

White and Nussbaum publish in the same year (1985) on the same theme but from methodologically inverted positions, and — crucially — they appear to have worked independently of one another. White cites Nussbaum's 1978 *De Motu Animalium* commentary three times (fns. 16, 63, 80), but does *not* cite, and seemingly did not see, the 1985 second-edition reframed essay that constitutes PHX-01. Nussbaum's own footnote 71 acknowledges only Schofield (1978) as a parallel.

**Convergences:**

1. *Lexical priority of phainetai/phainomena*. Both refuse the imago/imitor framing of "imagination" and trace *phantasia* through the *phainomai* family back to the appearance-event.
2. *Centrality of the kath' hen formula (428a1–2)*. Both essays treat this as the definitional anchor.
3. *Reading of the einai-formulation (DA III.3 / De Ins. 459a15–22)*. Both treat this as the crux of phantasia/aisthesis relation, though they parse it differently.
4. *Reservation about kata metaphoran (428a1)*. Nussbaum makes this an explicit polemic; White brackets transferred uses to focus on the subjective sense.
5. *Endorsement of DA III.8 432a3–10* — the soul never thinks without a *phantasma* — as a substantive Aristotelian thesis about thinking.

**Divergences:**

1. *Textual base*. White confines himself to DA III.3–8; Nussbaum expands to DA III.9–11 + *De Motu Animalium* 6–8. White's footnote 80 contains the entire treatment of practical *phantasia* — exactly the territory Nussbaum makes central.
2. *Picture-vs-encounter (427b21–24)*. Decisive for White, barely registered by Nussbaum. The detachment-criterion that grounds White's reading is effaced by Nussbaum's "seeing-as" framing.
3. *Parsing the einai-formulation*. White reads it via the secondary-motion-from-primary-motion physics; Nussbaum reads it via the road-up/road-down and arete/dikaiosune analogies (one capacity under two descriptions).
4. *Systematic role of phantasia*. For White, *phantasia* is the *temporal mediator* between *aisthesis* (the now) and *nous* (the timeless), providing stability, retention, projection, continuity. For Nussbaum, *phantasia* is the *interpretive how* of perception — the "seeing-as" that supplies the action-explanation with its object.
5. *Philosophical horizon*. White's interior-landscape culmination is Thomistic-humanist with continental sympathies (Heidegger and phenomenology cited in fn. 11). Nussbaum's seeing-as conclusion is Wittgensteinian-analytic-Anglophone (Wittgenstein, Sartre, Ishiguro, Dennett, Goodman are her live interlocutors).

The two essays are most usefully read as the *theoretical-cognitive* (White) and the *practical-motivational* (Nussbaum) limbs of a complete 1985 reassessment of *phantasia*. They are mutually illuminating wherever they touch the same Bekker passages, and the cluster as a whole gains from being read in their tension.

## § Cross-pipeline bridges

**Aristotle corpus (rhetorical ontology).** White's essay is a high-density reading of DA III.3–8 in a single sitting; for a Dalton-style chapter on the soul-powers (Chapter 3 of Heidegger's BCAP, the Aristotle chapter of the dissertation), White provides the cleanest *single* exposition of: (a) the *phantasia* kinesis-definition; (b) the *phantasia*–*aisthesis*–*nous* three-power architecture; (c) the temporal-mediation function of *phantasia*; (d) the cosmic-light etymology of the *phantasia*-family. The picture-vs-encounter locus (427b21–24) is also a natural connector to *Rhetoric* II.2–6 passages on the passions, of relevance to the rhetorical-ontology pipeline.

**Heidegger BCAP GA 18 (Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy, 1924).** Heidegger's Marburg lectures read DA III.3–8 with attention to the *phainesthai*-genealogy and to *phantasia*'s relation to *nous* — exactly the loci that govern White. Both readers (independently) emphasize the temporal structure of *phantasia* as the soul's response to *aisthesis*'s confinement to the now. White's interior-landscape formulation (pdf 23) is structurally close to Heidegger's *Befindlichkeit*-as-orientation language, though White is Thomistic-humanist and Heidegger is fundamental-ontological. The picture-vs-encounter passage is also where Heidegger's *Pathos*/*Phantasia* problematic in BCAP §§ 16–18 intersects White's reading.

**Dalton dissertation chapters.** White is a load-bearing source for any treatment of: (i) the *phantasia*–*nous* axis in the dissertation's Aristotle chapter; (ii) the "interior landscape" / "panorama of nature" theme as a Thomistic-Aristotelian register for the soul's orientation in the cosmos; (iii) the contemporary-relevance coda on man-made *phantasmata*, which provides a clean classical-philosophical bridge to media-theoretic and rhetorical-political concerns. White's restraint of *phantasia* to the theoretical-cognitive register also serves as a sober foil to Nussbaum's action-theoretic expansion: the dissertation can hold both readings in productive tension.
