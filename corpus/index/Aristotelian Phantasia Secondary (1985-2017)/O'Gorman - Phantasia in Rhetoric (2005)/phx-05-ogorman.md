## § Overview and the epideictic-amplification thesis

O'Gorman's 2005 essay is the first sustained contemporary attempt by a rhetorician to relocate the centre of gravity of *phantasia*-in-*Rhetoric* scholarship from Book III's *lexis* alone (where the conversation has typically clustered around *pro ommatōn poiein* at 1411b) onto the broader epideictic function articulated in *Rhetoric* I.3 and I.9. The opening move (p. 16) takes the conjunction at 1404a — Aristotle's remark that *lexis* "has some small necessary place in all teaching" and that "all these things are forms of outward show [*phantasia*] and intended to affect the audience" — and treats it not as a marginal disparagement of style but as the textual lock between Aristotle's psychology (the *De Anima* account of *phantasia*) and his rhetorical theory.

The thesis announced at p. 18 has three coordinated parts. First, *lexis* and *phantasia* together reveal the visual quality of Aristotelian rhetorical style. Second, the *lexis*-*phantasia* tie reveals a basic visual component in Aristotle's conception of civic discourse, "one that is seen most clearly in those discourses Aristotle calls epideictic." Third, this in turn suggests a "rhetorical order" in Aristotle's theory of rhetoric, one that "qualifies interpretations of that theory that stress its linearity and rationalism." The epideictic function, "marked by imaging and a spatial logic, emerges from my reading as having a primal and foundational role in Aristotle's conception of public discourse." The remainder of the essay unfolds this triadic claim in four sections: a reading of *phantasia* in *De Anima*; a reading of *lexis* as *phantasia*; an argument for epideictic as visual and primal; and a coda using Pericles' Funeral Oration as exemplum.

The signature contribution — what makes this essay PHX-05's distinctive intervention — is the identification of *megethos* (magnitude/greatness) as the lexical-conceptual centerpiece of epideictic amplification, and the corresponding identification of *phantasia* as the psychic operation by which audiences "size up" magnitude through "comparative seeing." Where prior rhetorical scholarship (Newman 2002, Rosenfield 1980) had attended to "bringing before the eyes" as a stylistic device, O'Gorman embeds *pro ommatōn poiein* within the larger architecture of *auxesis* (amplification) at *Rhet*. 1368a, and thus shifts the question from "how does *lexis* produce vivid images?" to "how does epideictic discourse furnish the corporate phantasmatic ground on which deliberative and judicial rhetoric subsequently operate?"

## § Section-by-section synthesis

**Introduction (printed pp. 16–18 / PDF pp. 2–3).** O'Gorman opens from the *antistrophos*-passage of *Rhet*. I.1 but quickly redirects to the 1404a passage in Book III — "a statement in the *Rhetoric* less discussed, yet still important." The interpretive lens is the *De Anima*, "Aristotle's seminal account of the senses and their relationship to *psychê* (often translated 'soul')." The argument will read the *Rhetoric* alongside *De Anima* to disclose *phantasia* as "a tie between [Aristotle's] art of rhetoric and his psychology and phenomenology (anachronistic though these terms are)." Quintilian's *Institutio Oratoria* 4.2.29, 32 is invoked as the Latin-rhetorical witness that classical readers themselves derived rhetorical *visiones* from Aristotle's account.

**§ *Phantasia* in *De Anima* (printed pp. 18–23 / PDF pp. 3–5).** O'Gorman surveys the *De Anima* account through the modern controversies. He endorses Nussbaum's broadening of *phantasia* beyond "mental image" to "appearances in a broad sense" while preserving (with Schofield 1992 and Hett 1935) the priority of sight that scholars like Sorabji 1972 had emphasized in their image-theoretic readings. The texts canvassed include: *DA* 427b on *phantasia*'s distinctness from thought and sensation, and on the *spectator-at-a-picture* analogy; *DA* 428a on *phantasia* as a *dunamis* or *hexis* "by which we judge"; *DA* 428b on *phantasia* as a "movement produced by sensation actively operating"; *DA* 429a's etymological derivation of *phantasia* from *phaos* (light); *DA* 432a's formula that *phantasmata* are "like *aisthemata*, only without matter"; and *DA* 434a on *bouleutikē* as the capacity to combine many *phantasmata* into one. Dorothea Frede's tripartite gloss is foregrounded: *phantasia* designates capacity, on-going appearance, and what appears (Frede 1992, 279).

The summary at p. 22 fixes three load-bearing features: *phantasia* is intimately tied to sight and is a kind of "*psyche*-sight"; it is "essential to all animal action and all human thought, including deliberation, contemplation, and creativity"; and yet it is fallible — most active "when we do not perceive distinctly" (*DA* 428a). The trio — visual character, mediating function between sense and mind, epistemic fallibility — becomes the bridge to the rhetorical reading, since the same trio underwrites Aristotle's ambivalence about *lexis* in Book III.

**§ *Lexis* as *Phantasia* (printed pp. 23–26 / PDF pp. 5–6).** O'Gorman parses the 1404a conjunction. Two translations are weighed: Kennedy's "outward show" and Freese's "*mere* outward show." O'Gorman sides with Kennedy, on the grounds that elsewhere Aristotle does not treat *phantasia* as a disparaging term, even if pre-Aristotelian Herodotean usage (7.10) and late Hellenistic Polybian usage had pushed the noun toward "ostentation." Aristotle's *phantasia* here, like its *De Anima* counterpart, names a real operation rather than a pejorative façade.

Two parallels between *De Anima* *phantasia* and Rhetorical *lexis* are then constructed. *Form*: both are dynamic, both are visual, both turn on a vocabulary of manifestation. *Rhet*. 1404a couples *lexis* with *saphēs* (clarity) and *dēlōsai* / *dēloi* ("to make visible or manifest"). *Pro ommatōn poiein* at *Rhet*. 1411b corresponds to *tithēmi pro ommatōn* at *De Memoria* 450a ("the man who is thinking puts a finite magnitude before his eyes") and to *pro ommatōn tithemenon* at *Poetics* 1455a (the poet should put the scene before his eyes). Per Sara Newman 2002, *pro ommatōn poiein* is a "lexical form of *energeia*, a type of motion (*kinēsis*) realized through the capacity of the mind for seeing things in action" — a formulation O'Gorman adopts as a cornerstone (p. 24, citing *Rhet*. 1411b and *Metaph*. 1048b on the *energeia*/*kinēsis* distinction).

*Effect*: both *lexis* and *phantasia* act on the emotions. Aristotle's rhetorical emotion-definitions are themselves *phantasia*-definitions: *phobos* (fear) is a "sort of pain or agitation derived from the *phantasias* of a future destructive or painful evil" (*Rhet*. 1382a); *aischynē* (shame) is "*phantasia* about a loss of reputation" (*Rhet*. 1384a); and honor depends on each person's *phantasian* that he has the qualities of an important person (*Rhet*. 1371a). This emotion-*phantasia* coupling is grounded in *DA* 427b's account of *pathos* as the kind of *phantasia* "that lies in our power" — the affective state can be deliberately conjured by manipulating one's imagination. O'Gorman concludes the section with *Rhet*. 1354a's warning about warping the jury into anger or envy or pity, and 1404a's identification of *lexis* as artistic (*entechnon*) — twin acknowledgments by Aristotle that the very feature that makes *lexis* psychically efficacious is also what makes it dangerous.

**§ Epideictic as visual and primal (printed pp. 26–35 / PDF pp. 6–10).** This is the longest and most consequential section. The opening move (p. 27) maps the *De Anima* psychic order — *phantasia* as "lower" or "primal," shared with brute beasts, prior to *nous*, but foundational for *bouleutikē* — onto a *rhetorical* order in which epideictic, "the most overtly lexical of the rhetorical practices," occupies a primal place. The argument proceeds in two steps: the basic visual nature of epideictic, then the reasons for its primal place in public discourse.

The visual nature is anchored in *Rhet*. 1368a's account of amplification (*auxesis*): "Amplification, with good reason, falls among forms of praise; for it aims to show superiority, and superiority is one of the forms of the honorable.... In general, among those classes of things common to all speeches, amplification is the most at home in those that are epideictic; for these take up actions that are agreed upon, so that what remains is to clothe the actions with greatness [*megethos*] and beauty [*kallos*]." O'Gorman lists the means of amplification: analogy (1363b), arguments for relative superiority (1368a), metaphor (1412a), and the lexical devices of expansiveness (1407b), the period (1409a–b), and "bringing before the eyes" (1411b–1412a) — all "may be invoked to expand the subject and make it superior."

## § The *megethos* centerpiece — Farrell anchor and Aristotle *Rhetoric* I.9 etymological work

At the centre of Aristotle's conception of amplification, O'Gorman argues, stands *megethos*. The Farrell anchor is the key citation: per Thomas B. Farrell 1995 (186), *megethos* is "the root of a great many recurring issues in Aristotelian inquiry." It is "morphologically and conceptually tied to important Aristotelian themes like the more and the less, the big and small, and the virtues of magnificence (*megaloprepeia*) and greatness of *psychê* (*megalopsychia*)." In the *Rhetoric*, especially in Book I, *megethos* "as a quality relative to time, place, and circumstance, addresses the realm of appearances. The speaker who invokes *megethos* sketches out the relative magnitude of public appearances; 'dressing up' is also a form of 'sizing up.'"

The psychic correlate of this rhetorical "sizing up" is *De Anima* 428b: per Frede 1992 (286), *phantasia* "provides a kind of comparative seeing... such that what appears immediately before the eyes (the sun, which looks no bigger than my thumb) can be sized up via *phantasia* by comparing and contrasting it mentally to other objects (my thumb, treetops, chimneys)." The epideictic speaker performs *megethos* by orchestrating a spatial logic of comparison: more/less, big/small, near/far. This is what distinguishes epideictic's lexical-spatial logic from the linear-sequential logic of enthymeme and example that dominates deliberative and judicial rhetoric (*Rhet*. 1392a).

The auditor of such epideictic discourse is named *theōros* (viewer/beholder) at *Rhet*. 1358b, not *kritēs* (judge). O'Gorman traces the denotational range of *theōros* from athletic spectator to philosophical contemplator, and notes that *theōrō* (the verbal form) is repeatedly tied in *De Anima* to *phantasia* — most paradigmatically at 427b: "But in imagination [*phantasian*] we are like spectators looking at something dreadful or encouraging in a picture." Lawrence Rosenfield 1980 had concluded that the epideictic auditor is a kind of witness who sees with the "mind's eye"; O'Gorman accepts the formula but corrects its underwriting. Where Rosenfield gave the auditor a Heideggerian-contemplative role as witness to Being, O'Gorman has her be a witness to images lexically produced. The *theōros* sees not Being but a corporate *phantasma* fashioned in *lexis*.

Two functions of epideictic discourse follow (p. 31). First, "discourse has an epideictic function when it draws on *phantasia* to shape the affective ground of deliberation and judgment. This power is derived from the particular relation of *phantasia* to the emotions." Drawing on Fortenbaugh 1975 and Nussbaum 1994 on the cognitive theory of emotions, O'Gorman shows that emotions are tied to *doxa* (opinion), that *doxa* is the efficient cause of emotion (Fortenbaugh 1975, 15), and that *phantasia* — per *DA* 428a — "is to form an opinion [*doxa*] exactly corresponding to direct perception." A lexically produced image therefore exerts emotional power because audiences come to assent to the image's *doxa*. Second, "discourse also has an epideictic function when it conspicuously invokes the spatial logics of combination and comparison predicated on magnitude" (p. 31). This is the *megethos*-function: epideictic produces corporate *phantasmata* of scale that subsequently underwrite practical/pragmatic deliberation.

The *megalopsychos* of *NE* 1123b functions in O'Gorman's exposition as the paradigm-case (p. 32): "If *phantasia*, especially in epideictic amplification, is a means by which *megalopsychia* appears as such in public, this appearance will underlie pragmatic judgments about the actions of the 'great-souled' person." Epideictic, that is, lays "the phenomenological ground of pragmatic rhetorics."

## § *Phantasia* in *Rhetoric* Book III: *lexis* devices, period, metaphor, "bringing before the eyes"

The Book III machinery is treated not as the proper home of *phantasia* but as a sub-set of devices serving the larger epideictic-amplificatory function. *Lexis* devices of expansiveness (1407b), the period (*periodos*, 1409a–b), metaphor (1412a, *Metaph*. 1048b on the *energeia*-character of metaphor), and *pro ommatōn poiein* (1411b–1412a) are each construed as means by which *megethos* is rendered visible. *Pro ommatōn poiein* is reread, via Newman 2002, as a "visualizing effect that lies in sense perception" — not a literary ornament confined to *elocutio* but a psychological process Aristotle theorizes in *De Anima* and deploys in the *Rhetoric*. The identity between private mental deliberation and rhetorical spectatorship "lies in the fundamental *phantasmatic* form of each activity — both put before the mind's eye *phantasmata*." Words, through *lexis*, "can be moved so as to activate the phantasmatic capacities of audience members." What is absent before the eyes physically is made present to the mind through *lexis*, in the same way that an individual may "put" images before her mind while imagining.

## § The epideictic function as the proper home of *phantasia*

The cumulative force of O'Gorman's argument is to demote Book III's *lexis*-devices to instruments of a larger function while elevating the epideictic species — long regarded as Aristotle's least favored or most marginal rhetorical practice — to functional primality. The classificatory difficulty noted by Richard Lanham 1991 (*A Handlist of Rhetorical Terms*, 164) — that epideictic does not fit cleanly between deliberative and judicial — is reframed: Aristotle's account of epideictic is generically inadequate (as Schiappa 1999 had argued) but is best read as a *function* rather than a *genre*. Jeffrey Walker 2000 had argued that epideictic is generically and historically prior, the rhetoric of "belief" that underlies the rhetoric of practical decision-making. O'Gorman aligns with Walker but shifts the explanans: "epideictic's role lay not in shaping presuppositions but in providing common images for a people and shaping their sense of scale" (p. 33).

The axiomatic statement (p. 34) is the conclusion of the essay's central argument: "*the primal function of public discourse is the phantasmatic*. When rhetoric produces corporate *phantasmata*, these *phantasmata* form the basis of the deliberative discourse of the polis." Aristotle's higher cognitive operations — *nous*, contemplative reasoning, the political virtues — do not suppress or disregard *phantasia* but build on the mental images cultivated by it. The analogue at the rhetorical level: deliberative and judicial rhetoric build on the corporate *phantasmata* produced by epideictic discourse.

## § Comparison with Nussbaum 1985 and anticipated rhetoric-cluster siblings

In relation to **Nussbaum 1985** (PHX-01), O'Gorman is a friendly extension. Both papers correct Sorabji 1972's pictorial-empiricist reading of *phantasia*. Both privilege the *phainesthai*-stem ("what appears") over the narrow "image." Both treat *phantasia* as the faculty that mediates perception and judgment through emotion and desire. The papers' methodological registers differ: Nussbaum, a philosopher/classicist working from *MA* + *DA* III.9–11 + *EN* + *EE*, develops an action-theoretic vindication of *phantasia* as the faculty in virtue of which "phainetai-F" registers for a sentient creature. O'Gorman, a rhetorician working from *Rhet*. I.3 + I.9 + III.1–11 + *DA* + *De Memoria* + *Poetics*, develops a rhetorical-functional vindication of *phantasia* as the operation by which civic discourse produces corporate images of magnitude. Nussbaum's claim that *phantasia* has scope as wide as *phainomena* is the philosophical lemma; O'Gorman's claim that public discourse has a phantasmatic function is the rhetorical application. Nussbaum is cited at p. 19 (printed) endorsingly on phantasia as the faculty of appearances "in a broad sense," and again at n. 5 and n. 17 on the visual paradigm and on the emotions.

The two papers are methodologically separable but substantively complementary. O'Gorman's distinctive contribution is the move into the *epideictic* function — a move Nussbaum does not undertake, since *Rhet*. I.9 and the rhetorical species fall outside her action-theoretic frame.

Looking forward to anticipated cluster siblings: O'Gorman's account anticipates **Debra Hawhee 2011** ("Rhetoric's Sensorium" / Rhetorical Vision, PHX-07 in the cluster file) on the sensory-perceptual grounding of rhetorical *phantasia*. Both authors share the rhetorical disciplinary register and both work the *lexis*/*phantasia* coupling. O'Gorman's *megethos*-centred account also anticipates **Francisco Gonzalez 2006** (likely PHX-06) on *phantasia* as Aristotle's solution to the problem of bridging perception and intellection in rhetorical contexts. Where Nussbaum/Frede/Caston/White compose the philosophy-side of the cluster, O'Gorman, Hawhee, and Gonzalez compose the rhetoric-side; O'Gorman is the earliest of the rhetoric-side contributions and serves as its programmatic statement.

In relation to **White 1985** (PHX-02) and **Caston 1995** (PHX-04), O'Gorman cites both at n. 6 among visual-emphasis readers of DA III.3 but does not engage either substantively. White's emphasis on DA III.3–8 as a coherent doctrinal locus, and Caston's analytic vindication of *phantasia* as an *epistemic* faculty, run on a different disciplinary track from O'Gorman's rhetorical-functional project. Cross-citation is acknowledgment of philosophical company, not substantive engagement.

## § Cross-pipeline bridges

For the **Aristotle pipeline**, O'Gorman's *Rhetoric*-side bibliography surfaces a set of Aristotle loci often peripheral to the *De Anima*-centric phantasia conversation: *Rhet*. I.9 (epideictic + *auxesis*), the entire 1360b–1368a sweep on the topoi of magnitude/the good, 1411b–1412a (metaphor + *energeia*), and the *De Memoria* 450a / *Poetics* 1455a pair. These three loci together compose a rhetorical-poetic-mnemonic triangle around *pro ommatōn* that the philosophy-side commentaries (Sorabji, Wedin, Caston) tend to underplay. The pipeline can use O'Gorman's apparatus to populate Aristotle-Bekker locus nodes with cross-work edges (rhetoric↔memory↔poetics) that are not visible from the *DA* III.3 commentary tradition alone.

For the **rhetorical-ontology pipeline** (HR-01 GROSS *Starring the Text* and successors), O'Gorman's notion of *phantasia* as a *function* — operative across deliberative/forensic/epideictic genres rather than confined to one — anticipates the move from genre-classification to functional-classification that characterizes contemporary rhetorical ontology. The "primal function of public discourse is the phantasmatic" formulation can be productively coordinated with Gross's account of textual ontology and with rhetorical-ontological treatments of public-sphere image-production. The Loraux 1986 citation on "imaginary Athens" provides a hinge into political-theoretical accounts of constitutive rhetoric.

For potential bridges to **Heidegger and Rhetoric** (a parallel cluster in this corpus), O'Gorman's correction of Rosenfield 1980 is significant. Rosenfield had given the *theōros* a Heideggerian-contemplative gloss as witness-to-Being. O'Gorman's substitution — *theōros* as witness-to-phantasmata-lexically-produced — pulls Aristotelian rhetorical *theōria* away from a contemplative ontological reading and back toward a phenomenological-perceptual one. This is a precise locus where a Heidegger-Aristotle conversation about *phantasia*, *theōria*, and disclosure could be developed.
