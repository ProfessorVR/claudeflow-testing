## § Overview: Papachristou's interpretive orientation

Christina S. Papachristou's 2013 *Journal of Ancient Philosophy* article delivers a single, targeted, and methodologically conservative thesis: in *De Anima* Book III, Aristotle distinguishes **three**, not two, kinds (or grades) of *phantasia*. The standard reading, anchored on DA III.10, 433b29 (*φαντασία δὲ πᾶσα ἢ λογιστικὴ ἢ αἰσθητική* — "all *phantasia* is either calculative or sensitive"), gives a binary taxonomy: calculative (in rational animals) and sensitive (in irrational animals). Papachristou's contention is that this binary is incomplete. There is a third, lower grade — *indefinite* or *indeterminate phantasia* (ἀόριστος φαντασία) — found in the imperfect animals (τὰ ἀτελῆ): zoophytes, molluscs, and similar creatures with only the contact sense. The locus is DA III.11, 433b31–434a5, where Aristotle himself hesitates: "How could they have *phantasia*? Or is it that as their movements are indefinite (ἀορίστως), they have *phantasia*… but indefinitely?" The textus receptus for the tripartite reading is not, however, primarily the Greek; it is Latin. Papachristou's load-bearing source is Thomas Aquinas's *Sentencia libri De anima*, Liber II (pdf p. 16, fn. 57), in which Aquinas develops the technical pair *phantasia indeterminata* / *motus phantasiae* and the foundational distinction *animalia imperfecta* vs. *animalia perfecta*. The criterion is retention: in imperfect animals, the *motus phantasiae* does not remain after the apprehension of the sense; in perfect animals, the motion remains even after the sensible thing is gone.

Papachristou's article is, in short, a Greek-philological argument with a Thomistic backbone. The disciplinary affiliation is the long Catholic-Aristotelian tradition (Aquinas, Phillipe, Roth, Kocourek). The ancient interlocutors are Themistius (*Paraphrasis* 5.3.122.11–12; 5.3.90.5–8), Philoponus (*In De Anima* 15.255.4–5, 15.492.12, 15.592.26–29), Simplicius (11.242.17–18), Alexander of Aphrodisias (*De Anima* 81.24–25), and Sophonias (55.27–32). Modern English-language secondary literature (Ross, Hicks, Hamlyn, Polansky, Schofield, Wedin, Nussbaum, White, Frede, Caston, Modrak, Turnbull) is integrated, but not as the primary interpretive scaffold; it is the Latin commentary tradition that does the load-bearing work. Aquinas's *Corpus Thomisticum* is the only website cited in the article's bibliography (the URL is given: http://www.corpusthomisticum.org/can2.html), a fact small in itself but emblematic of the methodological commitments.

A further distinctive feature: Papachristou bridges the Aristotelian discussion to contemporary cognitive science. Footnote 94 (pdf p. 25) connects the two-register content of calculative *phantasmata* — propositional (DA III.11, 434a7–8: "πότερον γὰρ πράξει τόδε ἢ τόδε") and pictorial or quasi-pictorial (DA III.7, 431b6–8: "ὥσπερ ὁρῶν λογίζεται") — to the contemporary mental-imagery debate between the Analog/Pictorial Representation Account (Kosslyn, Sheppard) and the Propositional Representation Account (Pylyshyn, Fodor). This is an unusually interdisciplinary move within the cluster: neither Nussbaum 1985 (PHX-01) nor White 1985 (PHX-02) nor Frede 1992 (PHX-03) nor Caston 1995 (PHX-04) attempts the cognitive-science bridge.

The article is also the third or fourth piece in a sustained scholarly project. Papachristou's 2008 Ph.D. thesis (*The Cognitive Process in the Aristotelian Philosophy*, Thessaloniki, in Greek) provides the dissertational substrate (fn. 20 cites Part 4, Ch. 3, pp. 273–300). Her 2006 paper on *Phantasmata* and Kosslyn (in Sfendoni-Mentzou ed.) is cited at fn. 98 for the cognitive-science bridge. Her 2008 paper on the brain in Aristotle's theory of sense-perception (Trier *Antike Naturwissenschaft* volume) is cited at fn. 20. Her 2009 conference paper on Aristotelian *phantasia* in Stoic philosophy and Romanes (Athens) is the immediate Greek-language predecessor to the 2013 English article. The 2013 paper is thus a focused, mature distillation of a single sub-thesis from a longer research programme.

## § Section-by-section synthesis

### Introduction (pdf 1–3, book 19–21)

The opening is precise. *Phantasia* (φαντασία) is one of the parts (μόρια) or faculties/powers (δυνάμεις) of the soul that cannot exist apart from sensation (αἴσθησις) and thought (διάνοια). Its function and connection with *phantasmata* (φαντάσματα) plays a significant role in the psychological treatises of the Aristotelian Corpus. The article's purpose is then stated in remarkably explicit terms (pdf 1): "to examine the concept of *phantasia* in Book III, Chapter 3 of *De Anima*, and to show that the Stageirite philosopher distinguishes THREE and NOT TWO kinds of *phantasia*, starting from the lowest, which is found in imperfect creatures, to the highest, which appears only in the human beings." The methodological plan unfolds in three steps: (I) Aristotle's concepts of the parts/faculties of the soul and their correlation with classes of living beings; (II) *phantasia* as (a) the faculty by which a *phantasma* is presented to us (DA III.3, 428a1–2), (b) the faculty distinct from sensation/opinion/knowledge/intellect, and (c) a type of motion (κίνησις) generated by actual perception (DA III.3, 429a1–2); careful interpretation of φάντασμα; (III) demonstration of the three-kinds thesis.

### § I. Parts (μόρια) or Faculties/Powers (δυνάμεις) of the Soul (pdf 3–9, book 21–27)

The section opens with the hylomorphic foundation: soul as ἐντελέχεια ἡ πρώτη σώματος φυσικοῦ ὀργανικοῦ ("the first actuality of a natural organic body," DA II.1, 412b5–6). Soul is form (μορφή/εἶδος); body is matter (ὕλη); together they constitute one composite substance. The soul is inseparable from the body. Within this unified hylomorphic substance Aristotle distinguishes parts/faculties corresponding to different classes of living beings; these form a unified whole. DA III.9, 432a22–24 acknowledges an aporia: in a sense the parts of the soul are "innumerable" (ἄπειρα φαίνεται).

Three faculties emerge from three convergent texts: *De Generatione Animalium* II.3, 736b8–14 (nutritive/sensitive/rational); DA II.4, 415a14–18 + III.9, 432b6–7 (the τριμερής ψυχή formula — εἰ δὲ τρία ἡ ψυχή, "if the soul is tripartite"); *Ethica Nicomachea* I.7, 1098a1–5 (nutritive-and-growth/perceptive/active-element-with-rational-principle). Papachristou then expands the three to a six-fold scheme:

- **(1) Nutritive (θρεπτική) = Reproductive (γεννητική)** — common to all living beings (DA II.4, 416a19–20); works better in sleep (*Eth. Eud.* II.1, 1219b22–23).
- **(2) Sensitive (αἰσθητική)** — in all animals; does not exist apart from nutritive in plants; exists in actuality not in potentiality (DA II.5, 417a6–7).
- **(3) Rational (νοητικόν) or Discursive (διανοητικόν) and Mind (νοῦς)** — only in humans (DA II.3, 414b16–19). Here Papachristou marshals the Greek commentators: Philoponus, *In De Anima* 15.255.4–5, glosses διανοητικόν as δυνάμει νοῦς ("potential mind"), analogous to παθητικός νοῦς ("passive mind, which becomes all things," DA III.5, 430a14–15). The active or actuality mind (ἐνεργείᾳ or ἐντελεχείᾳ νοῦς = ποιητικός νοῦς) is the superior part. Simplicius and Alexander of Aphrodisias corroborate (footnotes 18–19): passive mind is matter-like (ὑλικός νοῦς, Alex. *De Anima* 81.24–25); receptive of intelligible objects (τὰ νοητά). Footnote 20 cross-references Papachristou's Ph.D. thesis: she argues DA III.5 distinguishes not just two minds but that the duality of a single mind parallels the distinction between physical brain (matter contained in skull) and the energetic function of thought.

To these three Papachristou adds three further faculties from DA II.2–3:

- **(4) Locomotive (κινητικόν κατὰ τόπον)** — added at DA II.2, 413b11–13; also called "progressive motion" (πορευτική κίνησις, DA III.9, 432b8–14).
- **(5) Appetitive (ὀρεκτικόν)** — added at DA II.3, 414a31–32. Includes desire (ἐπιθυμία), spiritedness (θυμός), wish (βούλησις). DA III.10, 433a21 confirms the appetitive faculty as cause of movement. Critical for the article's later argument: DA III.10, 433b27–29 — *ὀρεκτικὸν δὲ οὐκ ἄνευ φαντασίας* ("the appetitive cannot exist without *phantasia*").
- **(6) Imaginative (φανταστικόν)** — added at DA III.9, 432a28–432b4. Aristotle himself notes that it is distinct from the other parts but difficult to say with which of the parts it is identical.

Papachristou's Table 1 (pdf 9) presents the six-fold scheme: (1) Nutritive/Reproductive, (2) Appetitive (desire, spiritedness, wish), (3) Sensitive, (4) Locomotive, (5) Imaginative, (6a) Rational/Discursive → Passive Mind, (6b) Mind → Active Mind acting on Passive Mind.

### § II. *Phantasia* (φαντασία) and *Phantasma* (φάντασμα) in *De Anima* III.3 (pdf 10–15, book 28–32)

*Phantasia* is the main subject of DA III.3. The textus receptus is DA III.3, 428a1–2: *εἰ δή ἐστιν ἡ φαντασία καθ' ἥν λέγομεν φάντασμά τι ἡμῖν γίγνεσθαι* ("*phantasia* is the faculty/power by which a *phantasma* is presented to us"). Papachristou's translation policy is to leave φαντασία untranslated (fn. 32, pdf p. 10): "I prefer to leave the word untranslated. I believe that the word 'imagination' does not convey Aristotle's notion of '*phantasia*' as honestly and as understandably as possible."

The contemporary view defines imagination as "the capacity or power of the mind to create, to recombine or reproduce and to call up mental images of objects, events, faces or scenes, which are not present to the senses." Aristotle's concept is wider. Ross's list of six functions of *phantasia* is canvassed (Ross 1923/1995, p. 90–91, cited at fn. 38): (a) formation of after-images, (b) memory (μνήμη), (c) recollection (ἀνάμνησις), (d) dreams (ἐνύπνια), (e) in relation to desire (ἐπιθυμία), (f) in relation to thought (τὸ νοητικόν). Malcolm Schofield's signature claim is registered (fn. 39): "it was Aristotle who gave the first extended analytical description of imagining as a distinct faculty of the soul" — the imaginative part (τὸ φανταστικὸν μόριον), which cannot be independent of the body (DA I.1, 403a8–10).

A dissenting voice is registered but not endorsed: Cornelius Castoriadis (fn. 35, *The Imaginary Institution of Society*, pp. 174–175) argues that "Aristotle discovers the imagination philosophically — *phantasia* — but what he says about it thematically, when he treats it *ex professo* (fixing the imagination in its alleged place, between sensation and intellection, of which it would be a reproduction, and intellection, thereby governing for 25 centuries what everybody thinks about it) is of little consequence next to what he has truly to say about it, which he says elsewhere, and which he has no way of reconciling with what he thinks about *phusis*, the soul, thinking and being."

Aristotle differentiates *phantasia* from sensation (αἴσθησις) and thought (διάνοια) at DA III.3, 427b14–15. This differentiation is consequential: it entails that Aristotle's *phantasia* differs from Plato's *Sophist* account of *phantasia* as "a mixture of sensation and judgement" (σύμμειξις αἰσθήσεως καὶ δόξης, *Sophist* 264a4–b3, fn. 42). Instead, *phantasia* is a kind of motion (κίνησις) in the soul caused by the activity of sensation: *ἡ δὲ φαντασία κίνησίς τις δοκεῖ εἶναι καὶ οὐκ ἄνευ αἰσθήσεως γίγνεσθαι ἀλλ' αἰσθανομένοις καὶ ὧν αἴσθησίς ἐστιν* (DA III.3, 428b11–13; cf. 429a1–2). It involves ὑπόληψις (supposition) — a term Philoponus (*In De Anima* 15.492.12) glosses as inclusive of knowledge (ἐπιστήμη), practical wisdom (φρόνησις), and opinion (δόξα). Hamlyn calls ὑπόληψις "a difficult word to translate since it appears to express a very general notion which functions somewhat as the notion of judgement did in the writings of the Absolute Idealists." Hamlyn-Polansky and Hicks translate it as "supposition" (fn. 45).

*Phantasia* is a πάθος (affection) — and crucially, this affection is in our own power (ἐφ' ἡμῖν): "it is up to us to imagine, but it is not up to us to believe, for the belief that we hold must be either false or true" (DA III.3, 427b14–21). Here Papachristou pivots to the article's most striking lexical move, the phrase πρὸ ὀμμάτων γὰρ ἔστι τι ποιήσασθαι ("for it is possible to set something before our eyes"), which means our ability voluntarily (ὅταν βουλώμεθα) to set mental images before our eyes — "as do those who use memorization techniques [μνημονικοῖς τιθέμενοι] based on sight and the powers of visualization [εἰδωλοποιοῦντες]." The mnemonic art is attributed to Simonides of Ceos (556–469 BCE). *Phantasia* thus emerges as associated, even identified, with **visual imagery** — "the ability to form mental images, or to 'see with the mind's eye.'" The example Papachristou offers: if someone asks us to describe a lion not physically present, we find ourselves "looking at" or "visualizing" lions with "our mind's eye."

The section closes with Papachristou's two-register translation policy for φάντασμα (pdf 14–15). The word appears twelve times in DA. The two registers: (a) "representation" or "image" when φάντασμα relates only to the *phantasia* faculty (e.g. DA III.3, 428a1–2); (b) "mental representation" or "mental image" when φάντασμα is described by the philosopher as the substratum upon which the mind works (e.g. DA III.7, 431a16–17: διὸ οὐδέποτε νοεῖ ἄνευ φαντάσματος ἡ ψυχή — "wherefore the soul never thinks without a *phantasma*"). The distinction will matter for the tripartite scheme, since the third (calculative) kind operates precisely in register (b).

### § III(a). Indefinite/Indeterminate *Phantasia* (Ἀόριστος Φαντασία) (pdf 15–17, book 33–35)

The central tripartition opens at DA III.11, 433b31–434a5 (pdf 15): "*Σκεπτέον δὲ καὶ περὶ τῶν ἀτελῶν, τί τὸ κινοῦν ἐστιν, οἷς ἀφῇ μόνον ὑπάρχει αἴσθησις, πότερον ἐνδέχεται φαντασίαν ὑπάρχειν τούτοις, ἢ οὔ;…φαντασία δὲ πῶς ἂν ἐνείη; ἢ ὥσπερ καὶ κινεῖται ἀορίστως, καὶ ταῦτ' ἔνεστι μέν, ἀορίστως δ' ἔνεστιν*" — "We must also consider what produces movement in the imperfect animals (τὰ ἀτελῆ), which have no sense but touch, whether they have *phantasia* or not… But how could they have *phantasia*? Or is it that as their movements are indefinite/indeterminate (ἀορίστως), they have *phantasia*… but indefinitely?"

Three commentator-witnesses ground the third kind:

1. **Themistius** (*Libros Aristotelis de Anima Paraphrasis* 5.3.122.11–12): "*φαντάζεται ἀορίστως, ὥστε ἔχει μὲν φαντασίαν, ἀδιάρθωτον δὲ καὶ συγκεχυμένην*" — imperfect animals (τὰ ἀτελέστερα ζῷα), the lowest forms of animals, imagine indeterminately (ἀορίστως); they do have *phantasia*, but in an inarticulate (ἀδιάρθωτον) and confused (συγκεχυμένη) form.

2. **Philoponus** (*Aristotelis de Anima* 15.592.26–29): zoophytes (ζωοφύτοις — "animals that resemble plants," such as corals, sea anemones, sponges, molluscs) have ἀορίστως *phantasia*, because the movements of these creatures are ἀόριστον (indeterminate); their movement contracts and expands more or less, indefinitely (ἀορίστως λέγει ἀντὶ τοῦ ἀμυδρῶς καὶ πεπλανημένως).

3. **Thomas Aquinas**, *Sentencia libri De anima*, Liber II (Corpus Thomisticum, ed. Roberto Busa SJ, Taurini 1959; cited at fn. 57, pdf p. 16). The Latin passage is given in full in the footnote: "*Videtur tamen hoc esse contrarium ei quod supra dixerat: quia si pars decisa habet sensum et appetitum, habet etiam phantasiam; si tamen phantasia est idem cum imaginatione, ut videtur. Dicendum est igitur, quod **animalia imperfecta**, ut in tertio dicetur, habent quidem phantasiam, sed **indeterminatam**, quia scilicet **motus phantasiae** non remanet in eis post apprehensionem sensus: in **animalibus autem perfectis** remanet **motus phantasiae**, etiam abeuntibus sensibilibus. Et secundum hoc, dicitur hic quod imaginatio non est eadem omnibus animalibus. Sed quaedam animalia sunt, quae hac sola vivunt, carentia scilicet intellectu, et directa in suis operationibus per imaginationem, sicut nos dirigimur per intellectum.*"

R. A. Kocourek's English translation (cited at fn. 67, pdf p. 20): "Nevertheless, this seems to be contrary to what he said above: because if a part cut off has sense and appetite, it also has phantasy; provided that phantasy is the same as imagination, as it seems. It must be said, therefore, that imperfect animals, as is said in the third book, do really have phantasy, but it is one which is indeterminate because the motion of phantasy does not remain in them after the apprehension of the sense; however in perfect animals the motion of phantasy remains even after the sensible thing is gone. And according to this it is said here that imagination is not the same for all animals. But there are certain animals which live by this alone, lacking the intellect and being directed in their operations by imagination, just as we are directed by the intellect."

This is the load-bearing Latin passage of the entire article. From it Papachristou extracts the four technical terms that organize her tripartite scheme: *phantasia indeterminata*, *motus phantasiae*, *animalia imperfecta*, *animalia perfecta*. The criterion separating the first kind from the second is precisely the *motus phantasiae* and its retention or non-retention.

Papachristou's gloss (pdf 17): imperfect or indefinite creatures, which have no sense except that of touch, possess an indefinite/indeterminate kind of *phantasia*. Representations of touch (*phantasmata*) — tactile representations — are usually diffuse and indefinite, and they do not remain in imperfect animals after the sense object is gone. Polansky's note on ἀφή at 434a1 is registered (fn. 58): the word may apply to both touch and taste. Table 2 (pdf 17) visualizes the scheme: Imperfect Animals (zoophytes, molluscs, etc.) → Only the Contact Sense → Indefinite/Indeterminate *Phantasia* → *Phantasmata* (representations of touch) diffuse and indefinite, do not remain after the sense object is gone.

### § III(b). Sensitive *Phantasia* (Αἰσθητική Φαντασία) (pdf 17–22, book 35–40)

Sensitive *phantasia* is generated by sense-perception (αἰσθάνεσθαι); it does not occur without sense-perception. It is found in all animals other than man — namely the irrational animals (DA III.10, 433b29–30: ταύτης μὲν οὖν καὶ τὰ ἄλλα ζῷα μετέχει; III.11, 434a5–7: ἡ μὲν οὖν αἰσθητικὴ φαντασία, ὥσπερ εἴρηται, καὶ ἐν τοῖς ἄλλοις ζῴοις ὑπάρχει).

A productive question: if sensitive *phantasia* is shared by all animals — even the imperfect ones — then what is the difference between the indefinite (ἀόριστος) and the sensitive (αἰσθητική) kind? Papachristou defers the answer through an excursus on desire (ἐπιθυμία) and animal movement. David Ross identifies two kinds of desire: wish (βούλησις, rational, for future good) and appetite (ὄρεξις/ἐπιθυμία, irrational, for present pleasure-mistaken-for-good). Aristotle stresses appetite as the cause of animal movement: κινεῖται γὰρ τὸ κινούμενον ᾗ ὀρέγεται, καὶ ἡ ὄρεξις κίνησίς τίς ἐστιν ἢ ἐνέργεια (DA III.10, 433b17–19). The critical text: ὀρεκτικὸν δὲ οὐκ ἄνευ φαντασίας (DA III.10, 433b28–29) — the appetitive cannot exist without *phantasia*. Appetite moves the animal only with the mediation of *phantasia*; and this kind of *phantasia* is what Aristotle calls sensitive *phantasia*.

The distinction between indefinite and sensitive *phantasia* is now resolved through Aquinas (fn. 67). Very slight traces of sensitive *phantasia* are found in indefinite animals — they can discriminate which objects are pleasant or unpleasant on contact — but they cannot sense at a distance, and (as Aquinas says) "the motion of phantasy does not remain in them after the apprehension of the sense." In animals other than indefinite creatures — animals possessing more than one sense and so capable of discriminating distant objects — the product of *phantasia*, the *phantasma*, REMAINS in them even after the sense object is gone. The difference between indefinite and sensitive *phantasia* rests, then, on capacity to **perceive and retain** a sense object. Table 3 (pdf 19) visualizes: Irrational Animals → Senses of Taste, Touch, Smell, Sight, Hearing (objects in contact AND at distance) → Sensitive *Phantasia* → *Phantasmata* retained after sense object gone.

The section then addresses what Hamlyn calls the "puzzling" passage at DA III.3, 428a8–11: εἶτα αἴσθησις μὲν ἀεὶ πάρεστι, φαντασία δ' οὔ. εἰ δὲ τῇ ἐνεργείᾳ τὸ αὐτό, πᾶσιν ἂν ἐνδέχοιτο τοῖς θηρίοις φαντασίαν ὑπάρχειν· δοκεῖ δ' οὔ, οἷον μύρμηκι ἢ μελίττῃ ἢ σκώληκι ("Sense is always present, but not *phantasia*. But if [*phantasia*] was the same in actuality [as sense], it would be possible for all beasts to have *phantasia*; but it seems not to be the case; as the ant, the bee, and the *scolex*"). Hamlyn registers the difficulty: "it is doubtful whether Aristotle would have denied imagination to ants and bees" (fn. 69). Papachristou agrees with Hamlyn and marshals four counter-textual witnesses:

1. *Historia Animalium* I.1, 488a7–10: ants and bees are included among the POLITICAL animals (πολιτικά: man, the bee, the wasp, the ant, the crane).
2. *De Partibus Animalium* II.2, 648a6–7: bees and similar animals are more intelligent (φρονιμώτερα) than many other animals.
3. *Metaphysica* I.1, 980b22–25: bees are prudent/intelligent (φρόνιμα) and have memory (μνήμη); "the former are more intelligent and capable of learning than those which cannot remember."
4. *De Memoria* I, 450a12–17: memory involves *phantasia* (ἡ δὲ μνήμη, καὶ ἡ τῶν νοητῶν, οὐκ ἄνευ φαντάσματός ἐστιν) — "memory of *noêmata* is not without a *phantasma*"; memory belongs to the rational part only *per accidens*, and *per se* to the primary sensitive part. Therefore "some other animals have memory, and not only human beings and those beings that have opinion or judgment."

The σκώληξ is given its own entomological gloss via William T. M. Forbes ("The Silkworm of Aristotle," *Classical Philology* 25.1, January 1930, pp. 22–26, fn. 75): the word "is not a 'grub' in general as usually translated, and never a 'worm' (vermis or vermiculus), but it corresponds in a way with the 'earthworm' and 'the maggot-like larvae of the wasps.'" Forbes's positive identification: σκώληξ is "the first stage of the life-history of an insect or other creature which he [Aristotle] did not recognize as produced by birth or hatching from a real egg. Sometimes he actually had an egg in mind (when he refers to it as hard shelled but soft inside), while in other cases it is obviously the first-stage larva." Cross-references: *Historia Animalium* I.5, 489b9–11; *De Gen. Anim.* II.1, 733a1–2 (ὥσπερ δὲ τὸ μὲν ζῷον τέλειον, ὁ σκώληξ καὶ τὸ ᾠὸν ἀτελές, "just as the animal is perfect but the scolex and the egg are imperfect").

Given the philosophical and entomological evidence, Papachristou accepts Adolf Torstrik's 19th-century emendation of DA III.3, 428a10–11: from the manuscript reading "δοκεῖ δ' οὔ, οἷον μύρμηκι ἢ μελίττῃ ἢ σκώληκι" ("it seems not to be the case, as the ant or the bee or the scolex") to "δοκεῖ, οἷον μύρμηκι μὲν ἢ μελίττῃ, σκώληκι δ' οὔ" ("it seems that [*phantasia*] is found in the ant and the bee, but not in the *scolex*"). The emendation is supported by readings in Themistius (*Paraphrasis* 5.3.90.5–8: μύρμηκι μὲν ἴσως καὶ μελίττη… σκώληκι δὲ οὔ, fn. 83) and Sophonias (*Paraphrasis* 55.27–32: μύρμηξι μὲν γὰρ καὶ μελίτταις καὶ τοῖς ὁμοίοις… ἀνάγκη παρεῖναι φαντασίαν… σκώληκες δὲ καὶ μυῖαι καὶ ὅσα ἄλλα ἀτάκτως καὶ ἀορίστως ὁρῶνται κινούμενα, fn. 84) and is attested by Hicks 1907 (pp. 462–463), Wallace 1976 (p. 263), and Ross 1961 (pp. 286–287). Conclusion: ants and bees, intelligent creatures with memory, should be included among animals possessing **sensitive** *phantasia*. The σκώληξ — first-stage larva or egg-like form — does not have *phantasia*.

The section closes with Table 4 (pdf 22): Aristotle's classification of animals, blooded (Ἔναιμα = Vertebrates, *Hist. Anim.* II.15, 505b28–32: man, viviparous quadrupeds, cetacea, birds, oviparous quadrupeds-and-footless-animals, fishes) vs. bloodless (Ἄναιμα = Invertebrates, *De Part. Anim.* IV.5, 678a27–31: hard-shelled, weak-shelled, cephalopoda, insects, zoophytes). The zoophytes and certain molluscs populate the imperfect-animal tier of indefinite *phantasia*; ants and bees, as intelligent insects, belong with the sensitive-*phantasia* tier alongside most of the vertebrates and most invertebrates.

### § III(c). Calculative or Deliberative *Phantasia* (Λογιστική or Βουλευτική Φαντασία) (pdf 23–26, book 41–44)

The third kind. The two canonical Bekker loci: DA III.10, 433b29 — φαντασία δὲ πᾶσα ἢ λογιστικὴ ἢ αἰσθητική — and DA III.11, 434a7 — ἡ δὲ βουλευτικὴ [φαντασία] ἐν τοῖς λογιστικοῖς. Papachristou makes a methodological observation: the epithets λογιστική (calculative) and βουλευτική (deliberative) are equivalent, since the verbs λογίζομαι and βουλεύομαι both mean "to determine, consider."

The defining passage is DA III.11, 434a5–12: "*ἡ μὲν οὖν αἰσθητικὴ φαντασία, ὥσπερ εἴρηται, καὶ ἐν τοῖς ἄλλοις ζῴοις ὑπάρχει, ἡ δὲ βουλευτικὴ ἐν τοῖς λογιστικοῖς (πότερον γὰρ πράξει τόδε ἢ τόδε, λογισμοῦ ἤδη ἐστὶν ἔργον· καὶ ἀνάγκη ἑνὶ μετρεῖν· τὸ μεῖζον γὰρ διώκει. ὥστε δύναται ἓν ἐκ πλειόνων φαντασμάτων ποιεῖν)*" — "Sensitive *phantasia*, as has been said, exists also in the other animals; but deliberative *phantasia* exists in those animals which can calculate; for whether one will do this or that is already a work of calculation; and there must be a single measure (μετρεῖν), for one pursues what is greater. Hence one has the ability to make ONE *phantasma* out of MANY *phantasmata*."

The third kind is found only in animals that possess reason. Sir David Ross (*Aristotle* 1923/1995, p. 319, cited at fn. 91): "the deliberative imagination, the rational imagination… is monopoly of reasoning beings, i.e. of men." But rational beings do not always act according to plan (use of calculative *phantasia*); they can also act according to the awareness of the moment (use of sensitive *phantasia*). The reason Aristotle denies opinion (δόξα) to non-human animals: their desires lack deliberation; only animals with intellect — and therefore language — have the kind of *phantasia* that comes from inference (συλλογισμός).

Calculative *phantasia* appears as an intermediate between sense-perception (αἰσθάνεσθαι) and mind (νοῦς). It involves having and combining several *phantasmata* (mental images) into one. The elaboration, organization, and unification of *phantasmata* are typical characteristics of human beings.

Papachristou then introduces her cognitive-science bridge. The activity of calculative or deliberative *phantasia* involves *phantasmata* with two kinds of content:

- **(a) Propositional content.** DA III.11, 434a7–8: ἡ δὲ βουλευτικὴ ἐν τοῖς λογιστικοῖς (πότερον γὰρ πράξει τόδε ἢ τόδε, λογισμοῦ ἤδη ἐστὶν ἔργον) — the propositional choice between "this or that" is the work of calculation.
- **(b) Pictorial or quasi-pictorial content.** DA III.7, 431b6–8: ὅτε δὲ τοῖς ἐν τῇ ψυχῇ φαντάσμασιν ἢ νοήμασιν ὥσπερ ὁρῶν λογίζεται καὶ βουλεύεται τὰ μέλλοντα πρὸς τὰ παρόντα — "and when by *phantasmata* or *noêmata* in the soul it calculates as if seeing them, and deliberates what is going to happen in the future in relation to the present."

These two Aristotelian remarks are, Papachristou contends, "excellent… with respect to contemporary ideas about mental images." Footnote 94 (pdf 25) draws the parallel: "Two of the most important contemporary theories of mental images or mental imagery are the 'Analog or Pictorial Representation Account' (Kosslyn, Sheppard etc.) and the 'Propositional Representation Account' (Pylyshyn, Fodor etc.). The 'Analog or Pictorial Representation Account' says that visual informations are stored in the brain in an analog or a picture-like (quasi-pictorial) code. The 'Propositional Representation Account' on the contrary argues that visual informations are stored in the brain in a propositional or a word-like code." Citations: Kosslyn, Ball & Reiser 1978 ("Visual Images Preserve Metric Spatial Information," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance* 4: 47–60); Pylyshyn 1981 ("The Imagery Debate: Analogue Media versus Tacit Knowledge," *Psychological Review* 88: 16–45); Block 1981 (*Imagery*, MIT Press). Aristotle's two passages map onto BOTH sides of the contemporary debate.

Table 5 (pdf 26) visualizes the highest tier: Rational Animals → Senses of Taste, Touch, Smell, Sight, Hearing → Sensitive *Phantasia* → Calculative or Deliberative *Phantasia* → *Phantasmata* (mental images either pictorial/quasi-pictorial or propositional content) retained AND combined → Nous.

### § IV. Conclusion (pdf 26–27, book 44–45)

Aristotle's treatment of *phantasia* is a complicated subject. *Phantasia* is a faculty of the soul — the φανταστικόν — placed between sensation (αἴσθησις) and thought (διάνοια). On one hand it depends on sensation, is a kind of affection (πάθος); on the other it is a necessary condition for memory, motion, desire, dreaming, thinking. *Phantasmata* are the products of the function of *phantasia*, resulting from sense-perception (αἰσθάνεσθαι) — mental representations or mental images of sense objects with several functions (remembering, thinking).

The article's thesis is then restated explicitly: "a detailed study of the notion of *phantasia* in *De Anima*, Book III, leads us to the conclusion that the Stageirite philosopher discriminates not two — as it is commonly argued — but **three** kinds or grades of *phantasia*":

- **(a) Indefinite/indeterminate *phantasia* (ἀόριστος φαντασία)** — found in imperfectly developed creatures (zoophytes, molluscs) which have no sense except that of touch; power of formatting diffuse and indefinite *phantasmata*.
- **(b) Sensitive *phantasia* (αἰσθητική φαντασία)** — found in animals that possess more than one sense (normal animals); power of forming more vivid *phantasmata*.
- **(c) Calculative or deliberative *phantasia* (λογιστική or βουλευτική φαντασία)** — the highest development of the faculty of *phantasia*; appears only in human beings, who have the power of thinking and the ability to combine several mental images (*phantasmata*) into one.

A reception coda closes the article (pdf 27). Thomas J. T. Nigel (*Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* 2013) is cited (fn. 95): "the impact on latter thinkers of Aristotle's account of cognition in general, and of imagery and imagination in particular, was enormous, and extended far beyond those who were avowed Aristotelians." Aristotle's treatment of *phantasia* in *De Anima* exerted important influence on Hellenistic philosophy (fn. 96: the Stoic theory of *phantasia*, cf. Papachristou 2009 on Romanes) and Western thought, both Medieval (fn. 97: St. Thomas Aquinas's account of imagination, *imaginatio*) and Modern (Descartes' views on the function of the faculty of imagination). The article closes by pointing forward to the cognitive-science bridge: a deeper conceptual relationship between Aristotle's theory and the current debate over mental images/mental imagery — "another issue that needs further investigation" (cross-reference, fn. 98, to Papachristou's 2006 Greek paper on Aristotelian *phantasmata* and Kosslyn).

## § Comparison with sister readings

### Papachristou and **White 1985** (PHX-02): Thomistic siblings

Papachristou and Kevin White share a disciplinary culture — Catholic-Aristotelian, Thomistic-scholastic — that distinguishes them from the Anglophone analytic mainstream of the cluster (Nussbaum, Caston, Frede, Schofield). The kinship is partly genealogical: both stand within the lineage that runs from Aquinas through Phillipe 1971 (*The Thomist*), Roth 1963 (*New Scholasticism*), and Kocourek's 1946 translation, into the contemporary Greek philological scholarship. But the methods differ. White's Thomism is sensibility, not citation: Aquinas is not named in White's running text, and the Thomistic register appears in such gestures as White's invocation of Augustine's *Confessions* X.8–26 on *memoria* (fn. 67), the careful sequencing of soul-powers, and the willingness to import Latin scholastic terminology (Bonitz, *species rei obiectae* vs. *eam actionem qua rerum imagines animo informamus*). Papachristou's Thomism, by contrast, is explicit citation: Aquinas's *Sentencia libri De anima* Liber II is quoted in full Latin at footnote 57, and Kocourek's English translation at footnote 67. The Corpus Thomisticum is given a URL in the bibliography.

The substantive divergence is sharper. White restricts himself to DA III.3–8 and treats *phantasia* primarily as a preliminary, theoretical-cognitive condition for the analysis of *nous*. The picture-vs-encounter distinction at DA III.3, 427b21–24 is decisive for White, the litmus separating *phantasia* from judgment. Papachristou is silent on the picture-vs-encounter passage; her interpretive center of gravity is elsewhere — at DA III.11, 433b31–434a5 (the imperfect-animals passage), which White treats only obliquely. White's interior-landscape culmination is Thomistic-humanist: *phantasia* as a familiar inner panorama. Papachristou's culmination is taxonomic-developmental: a three-grade hierarchy correlated with three classes of animals. The two readings are not in tension; they could coexist in a single comprehensive interpretation. But they emphasize different dimensions of the Aristotelian *phantasia*.

### Papachristou and **Frede 1992** (PHX-03): Supervenience parallel for indefinite *phantasia*

Dorothea Frede's "Cognitive Role of *Phantasia*" reads *phantasia* as supervenient on sensation: phantastic content is determined by, but irreducible to, sensory content. This formal-analytic apparatus has an interesting parallel in Papachristou's tripartite reading: the criterion that separates indefinite from sensitive *phantasia* — non-retention versus retention of the *motus phantasiae* after the sense apprehension — is functionally a supervenience criterion. In imperfect animals, *phantasia* is so tightly supervenient on the current sense apprehension that it does not survive its absence; in sensitive animals, the supervenience permits temporal extension; in calculative animals, the supervenience permits combination and reconfiguration (ἓν ἐκ πλειόνων φαντασμάτων ποιεῖν).

Where Frede's discussion remains within a binary framework (or within an analytic functionalism that does not require a developmental taxonomy), Papachristou's tripartite scheme reads the same Aristotelian material as a stepwise gradient of supervenient freedom: more retention → more recombination → more freedom from the immediate sense apprehension. The "grades" terminology of the article's title (*Three Kinds or **Grades** of Phantasia*) suggests this developmental reading.

### Papachristou and **Nussbaum 1985** (PHX-01)

Nussbaum's "seeing-as" account of *phantasia* — *phantasia* as the interpretive faculty that supplies the object of desire and motivates action — is a practical-motivational reading. Papachristou is silent on the action-explanation dimension. Her tripartite scheme is taxonomic-developmental, not action-theoretic. The one substantive overlap is the centrality of DA III.10, 433b28–29 (ὀρεκτικὸν δὲ οὐκ ἄνευ φαντασίας): for Nussbaum, this is the keystone of the action-theoretic reading; for Papachristou, it is the operative principle that ties sensitive *phantasia* to animal locomotion and so connects the second tier of her tripartite scheme to the appetitive faculty.

### Papachristou and **Caston 1995** (PHX-04): Why Aristotle needs imagination at THREE levels

Caston's functionalist question — "Why Aristotle Needs Imagination" — receives, on Papachristou's reading, a stratified answer. Aristotle needs *indefinite phantasia* to explain the locomotion of imperfect animals (zoophytes do not have memory but they do move under the immediate impulse of touch-pleasure or touch-displeasure); he needs *sensitive phantasia* to explain the post-sensation behavior of normal animals (the dog continues to seek food after the food is hidden, because the *phantasma* persists); he needs *calculative phantasia* to explain human deliberation, calculation, and the future-directed unification of multiple mental images. The tripartite scheme thus offers a more articulated answer to Caston's question than Caston's own binary framework allows.
