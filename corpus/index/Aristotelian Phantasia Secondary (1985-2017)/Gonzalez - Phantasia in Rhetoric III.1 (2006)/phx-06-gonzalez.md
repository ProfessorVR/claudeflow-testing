## 1. Thesis

In Rhet. III.1, immediately after his terse remark on hypokrisis and the οὐχ ὡς ὀρθῶς clauses of 1404a1–7, Aristotle writes ἀλλ' ἅπαντα φαντασία ταῦτ' ἐστὶ καὶ πρὸς τὸν ἀκροατήν. The English-reading tradition has near-universally rendered φαντασία here pejoratively — Cope's "fancy" or "mental presentation, a mere copy, without reality" (1877); Freese's "mere outward show" (1926); Roberts's "fanciful" (in Ross 1924); Kennedy's "outward show" (1991); Rapp's "reiner Anschein" (2002). Lawrence Green, surveying this history in 2000, remarked that he could not find a modern translation that took the word *phantasia* seriously. González sets out to take it seriously.

His thesis is that *phantasia* at 1404a11 is the same psychological faculty Aristotle theorizes in *De Anima* III.3–8 and uses throughout the corpus: the mediating function between αἴσθησις and the higher cognitive faculties (memory, hope, doxa, νοῦς, ὑπόληψις, βούλευσις). By invoking *phantasia*, Aristotle is not flagging a moral defect of style but signaling that lexis works *psychologically* — by producing in the listener the *phantasmata* that condition emotion, opinion, and judgment. The orator's task is to turn his subject matter into such *phantasmata* as will shape the audience's *doxa* and gain their πίστις.

The argument proceeds through four stages: (S1) summarize the modern consensus and its rationale; (S2) question that consensus on internal-textual grounds; (S3) survey *phantasia* across the Aristotelian oeuvre; (S4) close with the implications for the rhetorical task and a proposed retranslation of 1404a8–11.

## 2. Section-by-section synthesis

### S1 — The modern consensus and its rationale (pp.102–107)

González locates the consensus in three textual moves. First, scholars read lexis at 1404a as inextricably bound to hypokrisis, and hypokrisis as morally suspect (μοχθηρία τῶν ἀκροατῶν at 1404a8, φορτικόν at 1403b36); on the standard reading, *phantasia* inherits this moral taint and means "mere show." Second, Aristotle himself appears to concede that lexis is ἀναγκαῖος only — not pursued for its own sake — and that everything ἔξω τοῦ ἀποδεῖξαι is περίεργα (1404a6–7); this seems to make the bulk of stylistic analysis superficial. Third, the rhetoric/geometry contrast at 1404a12 (διὸ οὐδεὶς οὕτω γεωμετρεῖν διδάσκει) is read as separating honest demonstration (geometry) from showy persuasion (rhetoric).

González accepts none of this. Hypokrisis, he argues, is not a temporary parenthesis that Aristotle closes before turning to lexis-proper at III.2; it is a subordinate division of lexis throughout III.2–11, where Aristotle continues to attend to voice and its impact on ethos and pathos. The implication is structural: if hypokrisis is not morally objectionable at its root, then *phantasia* (the operative principle of lexis-as-hypokrisis) inherits no taint. The full quotation of 1404a1–12, with Ross's Greek and Kennedy's translation lightly modified, anchors his counter-reading.

### S2 — Questioning the consensus (pp.107–113)

González mounts five distinct objections.

(i) **The Platonist pedigree.** The "mere show" reading inherits an anti-rhetorical animus from the *Gorgias* (463d1–2). Aristotle's via media is more pragmatic: rhetoric addresses the polis's need to build consensus and manage dissent, and that need entails logos, ethos, and pathos together — making rhetoric potentially corrupt but also constitutively legitimate.

(ii) **Mokhthēria and phortikon are audience-dependent, not lexis-inherent.** Drawing the Poetics 26 parallel (1461b27–29, 1462a2–6), González argues that the φορτική label tracks the performer-audience interaction rather than the practice itself. Mokhthēria τῶν ἀκροατῶν names a fact about audiences, not lexis. The Pol. 8.6 1340b20ff. parallel on mousikē's φορτικότης works the same way: a θεατὴς φορτικός corrupts the τεχνῖται who ply their trade for him. The note on καλῶς ὑπολαμβανόμενον (1403b36) is closely argued: Cope's "rightly so considered" is challenged with Vettori's Renaissance "recte ponderatum" ("when properly understood"); LSJ s.v. ὑπολαμβάνω III.1 permits the softer reading.

(iii) **πρὸς δόξαν is reputation-management, not ostentation.** The Rhet. 1365b1–8 catalogue — what one would not choose were one to escape notice (doing-good vs. faring-well, being-just vs. seeming-just, being-healthy vs. seeming-healthy) — defines πρὸς δόξαν as a matter of *social appearance and reputation*, not "ostentation." Cope's slide from πρὸς δόξαν to "ostentatious and hollow" overtranslates. The Eudemian Ethics 1215a29–30 parallel (φορτικὰς τέχνας τὰς πρὸς δόξαν πραγματευομένας μόνον) is properly rendered "pursued only for reputation" (Rackham LCL), not "merely for outward show." A survey of doxa across the Rhetoric (1360b22, 1362b20, 1367a17, 1368b23, 1371a16, 1388a2–7, 1404a25, 1372b21–22) confirms the dominant sense as "opinion / reputation."

(iv) **διδασκαλία as demonstrative practice; the geometer/rhetorician contrast is about directionality, not honesty.** Aristotle at 1404a9 says lexis has a small necessary place ἐν πάσῃ διδασκαλίᾳ. González reads διδασκαλία as "demonstrative practice" (matching its Poetics 1456b5 use, and Plato's pairing of διδασκαλικαὶ τέχναι with rhetoric at *Gorgias* 453d7–454e2). The contrast διὸ οὐδεὶς οὕτω γεωμετρεῖν διδάσκει is then not "geometry is honest, rhetoric is showy" but "geometric demonstration is πρὸς αὑτόν, rhetorical demonstration is πρὸς τὸν ἀκροατήν." *Phantasia* operates in geometry too — *De Memoria* 449b30–450a1: νοεῖν οὐκ ἐστιν ἄνευ φαντάσματος; *De Mem.* 450a4–5: νοεῖ τίθεται πρὸ ὀμμάτων ποσόν.

(v) **The καί at 1404a11 is adverbial-epexegetic, not conjunctive.** González's textual-critical sub-thesis (n.42): the καί in ἅπαντα φαντασία ταῦτ' ἐστὶ καὶ πρὸς τὸν ἀκροατήν marks the directionality of *phantasia* toward the hearer ("I mean *phantasia* DIRECTED to the hearer"), not a conjunction with a separate predicate ("and besides directed to the hearer"). Editors who omit the comma before καί (Bekker, Spengel, Roemer, Kassel, Cope, Sandys) implicitly support González; those who insert it (Ross, Dufour-Wartelle) implicitly support the consensus reading.

### S3 — *Phantasia* in Aristotle's oeuvre (pp.113–122)

This is the article's centerpiece and its longest section. González traces the "mere show" reading philologically. The germ is Freudenthal 1863:17–18, who proposed it to explain Aristotle's *καθάπερ μεταφορά* clause at *DA* 428a1–4. Nussbaum 1978:254 popularized it. Wedin 1988:68 acknowledges that *Rhet.* 1404a11 is the SOLE Aristotelian passage adduced for the Freudenthal sense — the textual evidence is one-passage-circular. Wedin 1988:69–70 supplies a better solution to the DA 428a1–4 clause that does not require importing "ostentation" into the noun.

**The Sophistical Refutations parallel** — the signature philological move of the article. Halliwell 1993:59 n.16 had glossed *phantasia* at SE 4 165b25 as "merely appearance" and treated this as a precedent for non-psychological *phantasia* at Rhet. 1404a11. González inverts the inference. SE 165b24–27 lists six sources of false-syllogism *phantasia*: ὁμωνυμία, ἀμφιβολία, σύνθεσις, διαίρεσις, προσῳδία, σχῆμα λέξεως. All of these are *psychological* *phantasia* phenomena — fallacies that arise from how utterances are aurally or visually perceived and processed by νοῦς. The treatise's opening at 164a20–21 (οἱ φαινόμενοι ἔλεγχοι) and 164a23–24 (συλλογισμοί, οἳ δ' οὐκ ὄντες δοκοῦσι) makes the topic explicit: fallacy as *phantasia*. Aristotle's own parallel at SE 164b27 — those who view things from a distance reason and refute falsely — connects directly to *DA* 428b17–22 (perception of ἴδια vs. αἰσθητά κατὰ συμβεβηκός) and *DA* 430b29–30 (sight of proper objects always true; immaterial objects parallel). The SE *phantasia* is therefore psychological, fallible, and distance-vulnerable — NOT "mere show." Rhet. III.1's *phantasia* is not Sophistic deception precisely because it is the *same* psychological faculty operating in a legitimate-rhetorical mode rather than a fallacious-dialectical one. The rhetorical *phantasia* is detached from sophistic deception so long as it remains tethered to ethical and dialectical grounding.

The other LSJ-cited candidate for an "ostentation" *phantasia*, [Hippocrates] *Decorum* 7, González disposes of via Valdés 1992:304 — a detailed linguistic study that dates *Decorum* "with a fair degree of certainty" to the second century AD or later, well after Aristotle. Hdt. 7.10ε's φαντάζεσθαι (Artabanos warning Xerxes about prominent animals struck by thunderbolts) is verbal, not nominal, and the "arrogant display" coloring is contextual rather than lexicalized. Footnote 57 then mounts a rigorous methodological objection to extrapolating from verb to noun: Martínez Hernández 1997:196, Coseriu-Geckeler 1981:56–59, Geckeler 1976:158–60, and Gutiérrez Ordóñez 1989:104–5 are marshaled to insist on the distinction between lexical fields and lexical spheres — even when a verb and its cognate noun share a diachronic meaning, synchronic identity cannot be assumed.

González then maps the positive Aristotelian *phantasia*. *DA* 427b14–16: *phantasia* is distinct from αἴσθησις and διάνοια but never occurs without αἴσθησις; ὑπόληψις never occurs without *phantasia*. *DA* 433b27–30: animal motion presupposes ὄρεξις, which presupposes *phantasia*, which divides into λογιστική and αἰσθητική. *DA* 434a5–10: the βουλευτικὴ *phantasia* in rational animals combines πλείω *phantasmata* into one course of action by measuring against the greater good — the conceptual bridge to rhetorical practice, since the orator artfully crafts *phantasmata* that prompt the audience's deliberative combination toward the desired κρίσις. *De motu animalium* 702a17–19 gives the causal chain διάνοια/αἴσθησις → *phantasia* → ὄρεξις → bodily πάθη → motion (note: the πάθη of De motu are *bodily* — chillings, heatings — not the psychic πάθη of Rhetoric II, which precede desire and motivate judgment). *De motu* 700b17–21: *phantasia* and αἴσθησις occupy the same place as νοῦς because all are κριτικά.

NE 1102a27–b31 supplies the soul's division — λόγον ἔχον and ἄλογον; the alogon further divided into the φυτικόν and the ἐπιθυμητικόν that "somehow shares in logos." This grounds Aristotle's distinctive placement of desire in the sphere of logos and so its openness to rhetorical persuasion. Rhet. 1370a18–27's division of ἐπιθυμία into ἄλογος and μετὰ λόγου — the latter ἐκ τοῦ πασθῆναι, "they often long to see and acquire when they have heard and been persuaded" — is for González the textual lock between psychological *phantasia* and rhetorical practice. He reads ἀκούσαντες-πεισθέντες as conceptually subordinated (not coordinated): rhetorical persuasion is preeminently oral-aural, working via heard *phantasmata*.

### S4 — *Phantasia* in the *Rhetoric* (pp.122–128)

González catalogues every *phantasia* occurrence in the Rhetoric and shows their psychological coherence. The working definition at 1370a28–30 — φαντασία ἐστὶν αἴσθησίς τις ἀσθενής, connected with memory and hope — frames the survey. The enclitic τις, he notes, signals contextual approximation rather than ontological reduction; the philosophical sophistication present elsewhere is here simplified for the rhetorical argument. The αἴσθησις-side connection runs through *DA* II.6's incidental-perception (418a20–24): we "see the son of Diares" — at one level a sensory datum (whiteness), at another a recognition that calls for processing memory and common sensibles. *Phantasia* governs this perception-as-realization.

The Rhetoric's other *phantasia* loci: 1370b33 (νίκη producing *phantasia* of ὑπεροχή); 1371a9 (honor's pleasure as *phantasia* τοῦ σπουδαῖος εἶναι); 1371a19 (being loved as *phantasia* of being-good-by-nature); 1378b10 (dwelling-upon-revenge); 1382a21 (φόβος as *phantasia* of future evil); 1383a17 (courage as *phantasia* of safety); 1384a22 (αἰσχύνη as *phantasia* of loss of reputation). In none of these is the emphasis on unreality; the focus is the cognitive engagement with a *phantasma*. Emotions are *not* irrational affections — they are cognitive responses to *phantasmata*, and *that* is why lexis can produce and modulate them.

The decisive theoretical move: *DA* 428a20–24 distinguishes *phantasia* from doxa — *phantasia* does NOT necessarily carry πίστις. Yet the rhetorical *phantasia* is treated throughout the Rhetoric as if it carries πίστις (emotions move; we act on them). González's elegant resolution: in the Rhetoric, Aristotle *elides* the possibility of *phantasia*-without-pistis because the rhetorical goal is precisely *phantasia*-WITH-pistis. The orator works to convert the audience's *phantasia* into pistis-bearing doxa. This dissolves Nussbaum 1996:321 n.16's puzzle about "emotion without belief" in Rhet. II: there is no genuine such emotion in the Rhetoric; the Rhetoric is talking about *phantasia*-with-pistis throughout.

The closing pages assemble the rhetorical economy. Rhet. 1408a16–19 inventories style-registers (angry style for hubris, indignant/reticent for impious matters, admiring for praiseworthy, humble for pitiable); these depend on intonation, loudness, prose-rhythm choices (1408b4–7), and a fitting countenance that does not raise the suspicion of speciousness. Rhet. 1408a19–24: the proper lexis makes the matter credible because the audience feels-along-with the speaker; the mind draws a false inference of the truth of what is said even when nothing is said. Rhet. 1386a29–34: pity is evoked by making the evil appear close (ἐγγὺς ποιοῦσι φαίνεσθαι) through gestures (σχήμασιν), cries (φωναῖς), display of feeling, and hypokrisis generally. Poetics 17 1455a22–32 supplies the poetic parallel: the poet should "put before the eyes" and "work himself into grief with the action" — phantasma-construction for poetic ends, mirroring the rhetorical economy.

The closing thesis: the orator turns his own *phantasia* (his internal [re]presentations) into sensory *phantasmata* (lexis + σχήματα + φωνή); these become the hearers' sensory *phantasmata*; these become the hearers' noetic *phantasia*; this, supported by lexis-induced pathos, comes to carry pistis and so becomes the hearers' own doxa. *Phantasia* therefore names not only the soul's representational faculty but the entire rhetorical economy of phantasma-construction.

González closes by proposing a retranslation of Rhet. 1404a8–11 (n.90): lexis has a small but necessary place in every demonstrative discipline; speaking one way rather than another does make some difference to clarity, but not a large one — and all of this is a matter of how things will appear to the listener (paraphrase; the original retranslation runs across his n.90 with the additional qualifying gloss that lexis's importance should not be overstated). The οὐ μέντοι τοσοῦτον is not dismissive but a *realistic estimate* of lexis's promise: phantasma-mediated rhetorical demonstration is genuine demonstration, but its conclusions, unlike those of geometry, lack logical inevitability and can be deconstructed by an opponent.

## 3. The Rhet. III.1 1404a11 crux: textual issues

The textual stakes are unusually tight for a 33-page philological article. González works closely with Ross 1959 (OCT) and Kassel 1976, noting their differences at 1404a2 (οὐχ ὡς ὀρθῶς vs. οὐκ ὀρθῶς), 1404a4 (ζητεῖν vs. ζητεῖ; μηδὲν πλέον vs. μηδὲν πλείω; ἢ ὥστε vs. ἢ ὡς), and 1403b35 (transmitted πολιτειῶν vs. Spengel's conjecture, defended by Lossau 1971). At 1404a11 itself the question is not emendation but punctuation: should there be a comma before καί? Bekker, Spengel, Roemer, Kassel, Cope, and Sandys omit it; Ross and Dufour-Wartelle insert it. González argues the omission supports an adverbial-epexegetic reading of καί ("I mean *phantasia* directed to the hearer"), the insertion supports the conjunctive ("phantasia, AND directed to the hearer"). The English-translation tradition (Cope, Freese, Roberts, Kennedy, Rapp) implicitly reads conjunctively and so requires an independent predicate for ἅπαντα φαντασία ταῦτ' ἐστὶ — hence "mere show." González's adverbial-epexegetic καί removes the need for that independent predicate: the predicate is *phantasia*, qualified by directionality (πρὸς τὸν ἀκροατήν, as distinct from πρὸς αὑτόν in geometric demonstration).

The other textual nuance is the broader anaphoric subject of phortikon at 1403b36 (footnote 13): González argues that the moment Aristotle pivots from hypokrisis to τὸ περὶ τὴν λέξιν (1403b36), the syntactic agreement of phortikon and ὑπολαμβανόμενον (1403b36–1404a1) is best read with τὸ περὶ τὴν λέξιν as the proper subject, confirmed by the τὸ μὲν οὖν τῆς λέξεως resumption at 1404a8. Hypokrisis is a subordinate matter throughout.

## 4. The Sophistical Refutations parallel (164a20–24, 165b24–27)

The article's most distinctive philological move deserves separate attention. The full SE 165b24–27 catalogue runs: ἔστι δὲ τὰ μὲν παρὰ τὴν λέξιν ἐμποιοῦντα τὴν φαντασίαν ἓξ τὸν ἀριθμόν. ταῦτα δ' ἐστὶν ὁμωνυμία, ἀμφιβολία, σύνθεσις, διαίρεσις, προσῳδία, σχῆμα λέξεως. The vocabulary is suggestive: παρὰ τὴν λέξιν (alongside / through lexis), ἐμποιοῦντα τὴν φαντασίαν (producing the *phantasia*), σχῆμα λέξεως (figure of speech) — all of which echo the Rhet. III.1 territory of lexis-producing-*phantasia*. The SE phantasia is unequivocally psychological: false syllogisms appear true because of psychological mechanisms (ambiguity-of-term, ambiguity-of-syntax, wrong syntactic disjunction/conjunction, accentuation errors, similarity-of-termination) that operate on the audience's perceptual-cognitive processing of utterances.

If, González argues, the SE *phantasia* is psychological — not "mere appearance" but the FACULTY whose fallible operations produce sophistic illusion — then *a fortiori* the Rhet. III.1 *phantasia*, which operates in a legitimate-rhetorical mode, must also be psychological. The difference between Rhetoric and Sophistical Refutations is not that the former trades in "show" and the latter in psychology; the difference is that the Rhetoric integrates *phantasia* into a legitimate civic-persuasive economy (logos / ethos / pathos), while the SE flags *phantasia* as the locus of dialectical fallibility. The SAME faculty is at work; what differs is its alignment with truth, ethics, and dialectical grounding. The rhetorical *phantasia* "still belongs to legitimate persuasion as long as it isn't sophistically detached from ethical/dialectical grounding" — this is the through-line of González's reading.

A secondary observation: the SE/DA parallel via "those who view things from a distance" (SE 164b27 ↔ DA 428b17–22) confirms that *phantasia* in BOTH treatises is the same faculty — the distance-vulnerability of perception that produces both the sophist's fallacious appearances and the orator's legitimate *phantasmata*.

## 5. Corrections to Kennedy and the English-reading tradition

González's translation-history work is meticulous. The chief targets:

- **Cope 1877**: "fancy," "the mental presentation, a mere copy, without reality." The strongest pejorative reading; cross-referenced to Cope's own EE 1215a29–30 gloss "ostentatious and hollow," which González rejects (the Rackham LCL renders Eudemian Ethics here as "pursued only for reputation").
- **Freese 1926** (LCL): "mere outward show." Standard English-language entry in the Loeb tradition.
- **Roberts in Ross 1924**: "fanciful." A more mannered rendering but in the same family.
- **Kennedy 1991**: "outward show." The standard contemporary translation; González uses Kennedy as base translation and amends specifically at 1404a11.
- **Rapp 2002**: "reiner Anschein." The standard German rendering, in the same pejorative family.
- **Jebb in Sandys 1909**: "imagination." More cautious; preserves room for the psychological reading.
- **Tovar 1953** (Spanish): "apariencia." Also more cautious.

González notes Baldwin 1924 (the rare American dissenter): "all these things [i.e. of diction and delivery] being means of suggesting images and gauged to the hearer" (approved by Meijering 1987:22). And Labarrière 1994:238 (cited verbatim by González at p.99 n.1): "la phantasia de la Rhétorique 3.1.6, 04a11 ne saurait s'entendre comme la présentation mentale, une simple copie, sans réalité. Bien au contraire, la phantasia ici en question renvoie à ce en fonction de quoi quelque chose apparaît à X ou Y comme ceci ou cela." The dissenters before González (Baldwin 1924; Labarrière 1994; arguably Jebb in Sandys 1909) are isolated and unsystematically defended; González gives the dissenting position its first sustained philological scaffolding.

The proposed retranslation of 1404a8–11 (offered at n.90, p.128) renders lexis as having a small but necessary place in every demonstrative discipline, with speech-choice making some difference to clarity, though a difference whose importance should not be overstated — and registers the closing clause as "but all this is a matter of how things will appear to the listener" (paraphrase of González's n.90 rendering).

## 7. Cluster comparison

### González and O'Gorman 2005 (PHX-05)

The two papers are the cluster's rhetoric-side pair, both treating *phantasia* in Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, both published within a year of each other (Phil&Rhet 38.1 in 2005; TAPA 136 in Spring 2006), and (notably) without cross-citation in either direction — plausibly because they were composed in parallel. They share three foundational moves: (a) they both reject the Sorabji/Cope/Kennedy image-and-show consensus; (b) they both endorse the Nussbaum-broadening of *phantasia* from "image" to "appearance"; (c) they both treat the lexis-*phantasia* conjunction at Rhet. III.1 1404a as the textual lock between Aristotle's psychology and his rhetorical theory.

They differ in scope and strategy. González focuses *tightly* on the single passage 1404a11 and rebuilds the philological-lexicographical case for a psychological reading; the rest of the Rhetoric is mobilized only insofar as it supports that single-passage reading. O'Gorman ranges *widely* across Rhetoric I.3 (theōros), I.9 (epideictic-amplification, megethos), II (emotions-as-phantasiai), and III (lexis-as-phantasia, pro ommatōn poiein), and argues for *phantasia*'s rhetorical home in the EPIDEICTIC function — what he calls the "phantasmatic function of public discourse." González's claim is philological: *phantasia* at 1404a11 IS the psychological faculty. O'Gorman's claim is rhetorical-theoretical: *phantasia* in the Rhetoric is the operative principle of amplification (auxesis) via megethos, megaloprepeia, megalopsychia. The two papers are complementary: González supplies the textual lock; O'Gorman supplies the wide-angle rhetorical economy.

A subtler difference is their treatment of Nussbaum. O'Gorman endorses Nussbaum 1978 at face value as a corrective to the strict image-theory (Sorabji 1972). González, by contrast, traces a problematic Freudenthal-Nussbaum genealogy: Nussbaum 1978:254 is the modern popularizer of Freudenthal's 1863 "mere show" reading; her motive was to handle the *DA* 428a1–4 *καθάπερ μεταφορά* clause, and Wedin 1988:69–70 supplies a better solution that does not require importing "ostentation." González is therefore *more critical* of Nussbaum than O'Gorman, while still using her *De motu* translation at multiple points.

### González and Hawhee 2011 (PHX-07)

Hawhee, writing five years after González, explicitly registers him in her Note 16 as "an important exception" — the corrective to the Kennedy-mediated English-reading tradition. Hawhee's "rhetorical vision" thesis extends the cluster's rhetoric-side trajectory: where González corrects the philology and O'Gorman maps the epideictic function, Hawhee generalizes *phantasia* across Rhet. I/II/III as a unified theory of rhetorical visualization. Hawhee inherits the González/O'Gorman corrective and builds it into a positive theory of vision-in-rhetoric.

### González and Nussbaum 1985 (PHX-01)

Nussbaum 1985's "Role of *Phantasia* in Aristotle's Explanation of Action" supplied the philosophical-psychological framework that all later cluster work inherits. González inherits the framework but flags two specific Nussbaum positions for correction: (a) Nussbaum 1978's popularization of Freudenthal at 1404a11, and (b) Nussbaum 1996's claim that *phantasia* at Rh. II.5 is "simply" the verbal noun of *phainesthai* with no technical psychological framework. González disputes both — the former on philological-historical grounds; the latter via the *phantasia*-with-pistis resolution that explains apparent "emotion without belief" as *phantasia* operating with rhetorical-pistis.

### González and Frede 1992 (PHX-03)

Frede's supervenience thesis (*phantasia* as supervenient on αἴσθησις rather than a separate faculty) is broadly compatible with González's mediating-faculty reading but operates at a different analytical level. González cites Frede courteously (p.114 n.48 on whether Aristotle held a consistent view across works; p.115 n.52 on non-visual *phantasmata*) but does not centrally engage. The cluster-positioning is "secondary ally."

### González and White 1985 / Caston 1995 (PHX-02 / PHX-04)

Neither is cited by González. This is a notable cluster-citation gap, especially given the topical proximity of White (DA III.3–8 *phantasia*) and Caston (the appearance/truth dichotomy that González treats at SE 165b25). The gap is worth flagging for the cluster's citation-network analysis: González works almost exclusively from the philological-Aristotelian tradition (Cope, Spengel, Ross, Kassel, Vettori, Freudenthal, Wedin, Schofield, Watson, Labarrière, Halliwell, Fortenbaugh, Nussbaum, Frede) and from rhetorical-textual scholarship (Kennedy, Rapp, Grimaldi, Striker, Brinton), without engaging the analytic-philosophy-of-mind branch that White and Caston represent.

## 8. Cross-pipeline bridges

(i) **Aristotle pipeline**. Every Bekker locus González cites — Rhetoric, *DA*, *De Mem.*, *De Motu An.*, NE, EE, *Poet.*, *SE*, *Top.*, *Post. An.*, *Cat.*, *De Int.*, *De Insomn.*, *GA*, *Pol.*, *Metaph.* — feeds the Aristotelian KU map directly. The cumulative Bekker inventory (≥50 distinct loci in a single article) makes González the cluster's heaviest single-author Aristotle-loci contributor.

(ii) **Heidegger / BCAP (GA 18, 1924 Marburg lectures on Aristotle's *Rhetoric*)**. Heidegger reads *phantasia* in DA + Rhet. in tandem and treats the rhetorical-emotional sphere as a primordial site of being-with-others. González's psychological-mediation reading mirrors Heidegger's psychological-existential reading at structural level — both insist on the integration of *Rhet.* and *DA* and both reject the "mere show" reading. The bridge is structural rather than direct (González does not cite Heidegger, and Heidegger is not engaged in the Phantasia cluster generally).

(iii) **HR-2005 / Dalton dissertation chapters on rhetorical *phantasia***. González's psychological-mediation reading is foundational for any chapter treating Rhet. III.1 1404a11. The cluster-citation gap with White / Caston (PHX-02 / PHX-04) is itself a dissertation-relevant observation: it signals which side of the cluster's debate González is positioned within (philological-rhetorical, not analytic-philosophy-of-mind).

(iv) **Uncomfortable Situations (Gross 2017)** and the situated-emotion tradition. González's *phantasia*-with-pistis resolution is, in effect, a defense of cognitive emotion-theory against the residual Nussbaum-1996 reading. The doxa-emotion-judgment circuit González reconstructs is the textual-Aristotelian backbone of any situated-emotion theory that wants Aristotelian credentials.

(v) **Lanham-style / writing pipeline**. González's article is itself a model of dense philological prose: extensive Greek apparatus, Renaissance-and-modern editorial dialogue, multi-language translation history (English, French, German, Spanish, Italian), and careful textual-critical attention to punctuation and emendation. As a style-profile reference, it sits at the maximum-formality end of the Phantasia cluster.

## 9. Closing observation

González's article does what the cluster has been (until 2006) unable to do for the *Rhetoric*'s lexis-*phantasia* conjunction: it gives the dissenting position — Baldwin 1924, Labarrière 1994, and a chain of Renaissance commentators (Vettori 1579, Robortello 1548, Riccobono 1588, Barbaro & Barbaro 1544) — a sustained, peer-reviewed, philologically rigorous defense. The cumulative effect is that after González 2006, the Kennedy / Cope / Freese / Rapp / Roberts "outward show / fanciful / mere appearance / reiner Anschein" consensus is no longer the unmarked default. Hawhee 2011's Note 16 acknowledgment registers the shift. The cluster's later work (Papachristou 2013 on the DA-side; Bowin 2017 on *De Memoria* and time) can therefore proceed on a *phantasia* whose Rhetoric-side translation is no longer pejorative-by-default.

The single most distinctive contribution of the article is the *Sophistical Refutations* parallel (SE 164a20–24 + 165b24–27 in dialogue with *DA* 428b17–22 and 430b29–30): it rescues *phantasia* from "mere show" by showing that the SAME psychological faculty — fallible, distance-vulnerable, lexis-mediated — operates in both fallacious-dialectical and legitimate-rhetorical modes. The Rhetoric's *phantasia* is not Sophistic deception precisely because, when properly used, it is not detached from the ethical and dialectical grounding that distinguishes rhetoric from sophistry.
