# Scholarly Evolution of the Aristotelian *Phantasia* Debate, 1985–2017

This narrative traces the 32-year trajectory of the eight-unit Aristotelian *Phantasia* Secondary cluster. The structural data — 41 concept nodes, 22 readings, 12 cross-article debates, 11 contested readings, 31 directed citation edges, and 27 secondary figures across six camps — orient the chronology, but the spine of the story is provided by the publication metadata of each unit and the citation patterns that connect them. The cluster's center of gravity is set in 1985, consolidated 1992–1996, contested rhetorically 2005–2011, and reopened from a Thomistic flank in 2013. None of the cluster's load-bearing debates (D1, D2, D4, D5) is settled at the cluster's terminus; the trajectory clarifies their structure rather than resolving them.

## § 1985 — Foundational year: independent simultaneous foundations

The cluster opens with two essays that appeared in the same calendar year on opposite sides of the Atlantic and in incompatible scholarly cultures. **Martha C. Nussbaum's "The Role of Phantasia in Aristotle's Explanation of Action"** (Essay 5 of *Aristotle's De Motu Animalium: Text with Translation, Commentary, and Interpretive Essays*, Princeton University Press, 2nd ed. 1985, pp. 221–269) is the cluster's foundational document. Nussbaum was at Brown when the essay's host volume issued; the body essay spans pp. 221–269 of the Princeton volume. Her signature thesis — *phantasia*-as-interpretation — is the cluster's universal anchor (PHX-01 Reading R1: "scope as wide as that of *phainomena*"). Two textual moves drive it: the rereading of DA III.3 428a1–4 *kata metaphoran* as bracketing only the "mere show" colloquial sense (R3), and the inferential reading of *phantasia bouleutikē* at DA III.11 434a7–10 (R5) as deliberative reasoning rather than image-collage. Her keystone passage is DA III.8 432a3–10 ("the soul never thinks without a *phantasma*"; PHX-01 R6, the symbolic-vehicle thesis).

In the same year, **Kevin White's "The Meaning of *Phantasia* in Aristotle's *De Anima* III, 3–8"** appeared in *Dialogue: Canadian Philosophical Review* XXIV (1985), pp. 483–505. White, then at the University of Ottawa, worked within Thomistic-philological close-reading (with explicit footnote 11 citations to the phenomenological tradition). His signature contribution is the picture-vs-encounter distinction anchored at DA III.3 427b21–24 (PHX-02 R2): *phantasia* preserves an interior panorama for *nous*, detached from immediate affect — a "preparation for *noein*" thesis (R7) and a temporal-mediator account (R9).

The cluster's citation network confirms the two essays were independent productions. White cites Nussbaum's 1978 first edition of *De Motu Animalium* (the essay-host volume), but the 1985 second-edition essay itself does not enter his bibliography; conversely, Nussbaum's apparatus shows no engagement with the *Dialogue* paper. The disciplinary cultures explain the silence: *Princeton* analytic-anglophone vs. *Dialogue* Thomistic-Canadian, Owen-school ancient ethics vs. Catholic-Aristotelian commentary. The citation-network synthesis records this as the cluster's first structural gap (Gap 1's prefiguration). What the two essays jointly establish, in mutual obliviousness, is the cluster's first debate axis (D1 — interpretation vs. image-residue) and its first contested reading (CR-1 — Nussbaum-thesis vs. picture-residue). 1985 fixes the two interpretive poles that the next 28 years will negotiate.

## § 1992 — Consolidation in the field-defining anthology

Seven years later, **Dorothea Frede's "The Cognitive Role of *Phantasia* in Aristotle"** appeared as chapter 15 of Nussbaum and Rorty's *Essays on Aristotle's De Anima* (Clarendon, 1992, pp. 279–295). Frede, then at the University of Hamburg, advanced the cluster's supervenience thesis (PHX-03 R1): *phantasia* is not a separate faculty, only a phenomenon parasitic on *aisthesis*. Frede's argument runs through a two-functions account (synthesis of the manifold + cognitive bridging; R11) and the strict *aisthetikon*/*noēton* dualism against Modrak (R12) and Schofield (R13). Her supervenience claim ignites debate D2 (separate faculty vs. supervenient capacity); her denial of intellectual memory at PHX-03 C7 launches D9.

Two facts about 1992 are decisive for the cluster's evolution. First, the *Essays on Aristotle's De Anima* volume becomes the cluster's center-of-gravity anthology — the venue every subsequent analytic-DA author cites. Second, Frede chooses not to engage White 1985 (citation-network Gap 1, "the most consequential omission"). Frede and White produced substantively convergent supervenience-type readings in mutual independence; the *Phronesis*/Cambridge-analytic vs. *Dialogue*/Thomistic venue divide prevents cross-pollination. The analytic phantasia-as-supervenience tradition (Frede→Wedin→Caston-adjacent) accordingly absorbs only Frede's articulation, never White's parallel Thomistic-philological case.

Frede is also the cluster's first author to invoke Kant explicitly as a structural analogue — the secondary-literature network records two Frede→Kant references (A 190ff for synthesis of the manifold; B 154 for Gestalt). This bridge to modern philosophy of mind, modest in 1992, becomes load-bearing for Caston three years later.

## § 1995–1996 — Analytic-philosophy-of-mind consolidation

**Victor Caston's "Why Aristotle Needs Imagination"** was accepted at *Phronesis* in July 1995 and published in volume XLI/1 (1996), pp. 20–55. Caston, then at Brown, advanced the cluster's most theoretically articulated analytic argument: *phantasia* is introduced into the *De Anima* to solve the problem of error without conceding Protagorean antirealism (PHX-04 R1; cluster debate D2's positive pole; CR-6). The argument proceeds through numbered theses (1)/(1ʹ)/(2)/(3)/(3a)/(3b)/(4)/(5)/(6), a causal-powers content account (PHX-04 R2/R3), the rendering of DA 433a31's *einai*-formula as a non-Leibnizian identity criterion (his fn. 51, anchoring debate D12), and a non-propositional intentionality reading (R16). The salamander/whiskey/dragon counterexamples discipline the content-fixing apparatus.

The citation pattern is diagnostic. Caston engages Nussbaum 1978 (the *De Motu* monograph), Schofield 1978 (his polemic target), Modrak 1986/1987 (foil), and Wedin 1988 (Aristotle on intellect). He does not cite **White 1985** (citation-network Gap 2: "Caston's published apparatus carries no trace of White anywhere") and does not cite **Nussbaum 1985 essay** (Gap 3: "Caston cites only Nussbaum 1978 *De Motu*, not the 1985 expanded essay"). The 1985-essay omission is more substantive than disciplinary culture alone explains; the citation-network synthesis suggests Caston's analytic register cannot easily absorb Nussbaum's "envisaging the good" idiom. Caston accordingly completes an analytic-philosophy-of-mind triangulation (Nussbaum 1978 + Frede 1992 + Caston 1995/96) that never engages the Thomistic line.

By the end of 1996 the cluster has reached its first consolidation: a four-author analytic conversation (Nussbaum, Frede, Caston) and a dormant Thomistic flank (White). Six of the eleven cluster contested readings are now in play; the four load-bearing debates (D1, D2, D4, D5) are all activated, though D4 (lexis-narrow vs. epideictic-broad rhetorical home) and D5 ("mere show" pejorative vs. legitimate rhetorical function) await their primary disputants.

## § 2005–2006 — The rhetorical turn

The cluster's second wave opens with a sharp disciplinary shift. **Ned O'Gorman's "Aristotle's *Phantasia* in the *Rhetoric*: Lexis, Appearance, and the Epideictic Function of Discourse"** appeared in *Philosophy and Rhetoric* 38.1 (2005), pp. 16–40, from the Penn State Department of Communication Arts and Sciences. O'Gorman's signature thesis (PHX-05 R17/R18) is that *phantasia*'s rhetorical home is not Book III's narrow *lexis* topic but the epideictic function in Rhetoric I, anchored in *megethos*/*auxēsis*/*hyperochē* (Rhet. I.3, I.9; cluster concept PHX-C-022) and extended into Rhet. III.10–11's *pro ommatōn poiein*. Farrell 1995 is the principal contemporary scaffolding. The thesis opens debate D4 (lexis-narrow vs. epideictic-broad) and contested reading CR-9 (*megethos*/amplification as *phantasia*-anchored epideictic concept).

**José M. González's "The Meaning and Function of *Phantasia* in Aristotle's *Rhetoric* III.1"** followed in *Transactions of the American Philological Association* 136.1 (Spring 2006): 99–131 (the byline lists the University of Oregon at time of publication; González subsequently moved to Duke Classics). González's signature argument (PHX-06 R19) is a philological refutation of the Cope/Freese/Kennedy translation tradition that renders *phantasia* at Rhet. III.1 1404a11 as "mere show." His load-bearing evidence is the *Sophistical Refutations* 164a20–24 parallel and the adverbial *kai* at 1404a11 (PHX-06 C3). González names the target genealogy "the Freudenthal–Nussbaum reading" (PHX-06 C18) — a striking move, since the same article uses Nussbaum 1978 as its working *De Motu* translation at 700b17–21. The dual edge (foil + tool) is the cluster's clearest case of polemic-and-dependence: González polemically opposes Nussbaum on "mere show" while methodologically adopting her *De Motu* edition. Debate D5 (mere-show pejorative vs. legitimate rhetorical function) reaches its sharpest articulation here, and contested reading CR-7 (Rhet. III.1 1404a11 — psychological faculty or "mere show"?) becomes the cluster's cleanest case of explicit cross-cluster contestation.

The rhetorical-turn citation pattern is asymmetric. O'Gorman cites Nussbaum 1978 heavily, Frede 1992 (his most-cited cluster sibling), White 1985 (n. 6), and Caston 1996 (n. 6). González cites Nussbaum 1978/1996 and Frede 1992 but neither White, Caston, nor O'Gorman (Gaps 4, 5, 6 — the *Dialogue* venue gap, the analytic-vs.-philology discipline gap, and a publication-timing gap). The rhetoric subcluster reads the analytic subcluster systematically; the analytic subcluster does not return the citation traffic. This is the citation-network synthesis's "rhetoric→philosophy traffic asymmetry" — a structural feature, not an artifact.

## § 2011 — Synthesis essay

**Debra Hawhee's "Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes: Toward a Theory of Rhetorical Vision"** appeared in *Advances in the History of Rhetoric* 14.2 (2011), pp. 139–165, from Penn State's English/Rhetoric program. Hawhee threads phantasia through all three books of the *Rhetoric*: Book I (genres), Book II (the *pathē* as *phantasiai* of pleasure/pain), and Book III (*lexis*). Her signature move (PHX-07 R3, T1) is the "grafted interpretation" — an explicit modification of Nussbaum 1985 at the article's p. 152 — that locates rhetorical *phantasia* at or near the level of direct sensory perception rather than at the level of "seeing-as-understanding." Kennedy 2007's scare-quoted "bringing-before-the-eyes" is the polemical foil (PHX-07 T2). The grafting clause opens debate D6 (phantasia grafted onto perception vs. pure interpretation), the only intra-Nussbaum-camp dispute in the cluster.

Hawhee is the cluster's unique citation-omnivore. The citation-network's hub-score table records her outbound score at 6 — the only systematic cross-reader. She cites all six prior PHX siblings: Nussbaum 1985 at six different pages, White 1985 (n. 12, Galenic genealogy), Frede 1992 (the cluster's secondary hub for her), Caston 1996, O'Gorman 2005 (twice), and González 2006. Her institutional position at the intersection of classical rhetoric and ancient philosophy explains the bibliographic reach. Hawhee 2011 functions as the cluster's late-stage synthesizer: her grafting clause becomes the cluster's reconciliation move between Nussbaum's interpretivism (Axis 1's interpretive pole) and the White/Frede presentational reading. Among the contested readings, Hawhee uniquely inverts White on CR-5: where White makes DA 427b21–24 the locus of detachment, Hawhee makes the *same Bekker line* the locus of pre-judgmental sensory engagement — the cluster's clearest case of one passage anchoring incompatible programmatic readings.

## § 2013 — Thomistic-philological revisitation

**Christina S. Papachristou's "Three Kinds or Grades of *Phantasia* in Aristotle's *De Anima*"** appeared in the *Journal of Ancient Philosophy* 7.1 (2013), pp. 19–48 (FFLCH/USP, São Paulo), from the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki's Department of Philosophy. Her signature contribution (PHX-08 R1) is the tripartite *phantasia* taxonomy — *phantasia indeterminata* / *determinata* / deliberative — corresponding to animal classes and legitimated by Aquinas's *Sentencia libri De anima* II (fn. 57). Five tables systematize the scheme. The textual lever is Torstrik's emendation at DA 428a8–11 (separating ants/bees from *scolex*) and a retention-of-*phantasmata* criterion derived from Aquinas. Papachristou opens debate D3 (unitary vs. tripartite taxonomy) — the cluster's only sustained challenge to the modern consensus on faculty-individuation — and reactivates debates D10 (Thomistic vs. analytic-Anglophone reception) and D11 (Aquinas-reception as legitimate philological method, contested reading CR-11).

The citation pattern shows the dormant Thomistic flank reopening. Papachristou's four cluster citations all occur at a single footnote (fn. 34, PDF p. 12) listing Nussbaum, White, Frede, and Caston as "indicative readings." She does not cite O'Gorman, González, or Hawhee — citation-network Gaps 7–9, "the strongest in the cluster." The triple gap demonstrates the analytic-DA / rhetorical-Rhetoric split: even by 2013, the two subclusters do not exchange citations across the boundary in this direction. Papachristou also imports a distinctive philosophy-of-mind/cognitive-science bridge — the Kosslyn-vs.-Pylyshyn imagery debate at fn. 94 — declaring her own prior 2006 work. Her thesis revives the line that ran from White 1985: where White's storehouse-of-forms remained a single-author position for 28 years, Papachristou reactivates it as a Thomistic-philological program, making it the cluster's only minority report that survives into the closing decade.

## § Closing analysis

Four trajectories organize the cluster's 32-year evolution.

**(1) The Nussbaum gravitational center.** The citation-network's hub-score table records Nussbaum 1985 as the cluster's unique universal hub (inbound 7, outbound 0). Seven of eight subsequent articles cite Nussbaum 1978 or 1985 directly, and six of the eleven contested readings (CR-1, CR-2, CR-3, CR-4, CR-5, CR-7) are framed against or with her. No other author functions thus: Frede is the secondary hub at 4 inbound, but only the rhetoric subcluster engages her at scale. Nussbaum's centrality is structurally explained by three facts the citation-network synthesis foregrounds: her status as the "modern popularizer" of the Freudenthal "mere show" reading (named by González); her *De Motu* edition's institutional dominance (adopted even by polemical opponents); and her action-theory thesis, which sits at the intersection of philosophy of mind, ancient ethics, and rhetoric.

**(2) The Thomistic shadow line.** White 1985 → Papachristou 2013 forms a parallel philological tradition that the analytic mainstream never engages substantively. White's three inbound edges (O'Gorman n. 6, Hawhee n. 12, Papachristou fn. 34) are characterized in the citation-network synthesis as "gestural rather than argumentative." The shadow line nevertheless produces the cluster's only sustained taxonomic challenge (D3, CR-11). Six of the eight authors treat medieval-reception evidence as inadmissible; two treat it as decisive — and the two come from a single Thomistic-philosophical formation (Ottawa, Thessaloniki).

**(3) The rhetoric→philosophy citation asymmetry.** The cluster's three rhetoric-side units (O'Gorman, González, Hawhee) read the five philosophy-side units systematically — Hawhee citing all six prior siblings. The reverse flow is near-zero: only Papachristou's gestural fn. 34 acknowledges the rhetoric subcluster, and only by listing rhetoric-adjacent figures (Nussbaum's *De Motu*) rather than the rhetoric papers themselves. The asymmetry diagnoses debate D11 (silent disagreement: citation gaps) as a structural disciplinary feature, not contingent oversight.

**(4) The universal Bekker canon.** Across all eight articles, two passages are cited by every author: DA III.3 (427b14–429a9, the *phantasia*-as-*kinēsis-by-aisthēsis* definition) and *De Memoria* 450a (the residual-motion mnemonic doctrine). DA 427b21–24, 428a1–4, 433a31, 434a7–10, and Rhet. III.1 1404a11 anchor opposing readings for different authors. The cluster's debates are not over which passages matter but over what those passages mean — the diagnostic the contested-readings synthesis records in its closing remark.

**The unresolved question.** The cluster's terminus has clarified its central question without settling it: is *phantasia* primarily a perceptual capacity (Frede / Caston / White / Papachristou, each in a distinct mode — supervenient, intentional, preparatory, tripartite) or primarily an interpretive-deliberative capacity (Nussbaum / Hawhee, with Hawhee's grafting clause as partial reconciliation)? The rhetoric subcluster's contribution (O'Gorman, González) sits orthogonal to this axis: it asks not what *phantasia* is in the soul but where in the *Rhetoric* it does its work. Across 32 years, eight units, two disciplinary cultures, and twelve canonical debates, the cluster has produced a stable cartography of the question without producing a verdict — exactly the diagnostic the debate-map records in its closing line.
