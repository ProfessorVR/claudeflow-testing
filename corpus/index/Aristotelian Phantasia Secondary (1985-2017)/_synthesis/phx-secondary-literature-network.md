# Key Figures Secondary-Literature Network: Aristotelian Phantasia Cluster

**Scope**: 27 non-cluster modern scholars cited across the 8 cluster units. Tier-A (14): cited by ≥3 cluster units. Tier-B (13): cited by 1-2 units. Within-cluster authors are tracked separately in `citation-network.md`.

**Tied for most-cited**: Freudenthal (6 units) and Schofield (6 units) — the 19th-century philological origin and the 1978 Anglophone re-founding of phantasia scholarship anchor the entire cluster's bibliographic backbone.

## Camp 1: 19th-century German Philological Foundations

**Jakob Freudenthal (SL-001)** is the genealogical taproot of the cluster. His 1863 *Ueber den Begriff des Wortes ΦΑΝΤΑΣΙΑ bei Aristoteles* defines the philological grammar that González later credits — and contests — as the "mere show" reading of phantasia at *DA* 428a1-4. Six of eight cluster authors cite Freudenthal directly: Nussbaum at pp. 30, 59; White at fn. 92; Frede at PDF 8 (book 291, fn. 41) approvingly for the *Verallgemeinerung* gloss; Caston gently correcting at fn. 9 on the extension of phantasia; González at p. 113 as the source Nussbaum popularized; Papachristou as "indicative" foundational reading. Only O'Gorman and Hawhee bypass Freudenthal — both for rhetorical-tradition reasons, not philological ignorance.

The Freudenthal afterlife is double-edged. The 1863 monograph supplied the lexical-philological vocabulary that the entire 20th-century scholarship inherits; but Freudenthal's restriction of phantasia ("not held by every animal") is precisely the claim Caston needs to overturn for his animal-intentionality thesis. The cluster's polemical center of gravity sits between Freudenthal's 1863 lexicon and Schofield's 1978 family-concept reframing.

## Camp 2: The Anglophone Aristotle Revival (Cambridge / Oxford)

**Malcolm Schofield (SL-002)** is the polemical fulcrum. His 1978 essay "Aristotle on the Imagination" (in Lloyd & Owen's *Aristotle on Mind and the Senses*, reprinted in the 1979 *Articles on Aristotle* vol. 4 and again in the 1992 Nussbaum-Rorty *Essays on Aristotle's De Anima*) gives the "loose-knit family concept" and "sceptical cautious *phainetai*" readings that Caston explicitly contests at fn. 70, Frede rejects as "one-sided," and Papachristou lists as foundational. Six of eight cluster units cite Schofield — tied with Freudenthal for most-cited. The 1992 reprinting in the Frede volume re-activates Schofield for the 1990s-2000s cohort (O'Gorman, González, Hawhee).

**Myles Burnyeat (SL-014)** sits in the same Cambridge-Oxford ancient-philosophy milieu but with a different center of gravity: Plato. Caston relies on Burnyeat's 1990 *Theaetetus of Plato* for the Platonic Secret Doctrine background (fn. 22) and acknowledges him in closing. Frede cites Burnyeat 1981 supportively on "intellectual habituation" of first principles. Two-unit Tier-B citation, but with disproportionate philosophical weight: Burnyeat is the cluster's indirect gateway to the Plato-Theaetetus parentage of phantasia-as-appearance.

**G. E. L. Owen (SL-022)** appears more by methodological inheritance than direct engagement. Nussbaum's interlocutor entry identifies Owen as her thesis supervisor at Harvard and Oxford; her phantasia-in-action argument explicitly deploys Owen's "tithenai ta phainomena" methodology. Caston invokes the same Owen 1961 essay at fn. 42. Two-unit Tier-B citation, but the genealogical influence reaches further: every Anglophone Aristotelian in the cluster's 1985-1996 wave (Nussbaum, Schofield, Frede, Caston) was trained in the Owen-Ackrill methodological tradition.

**Charles Kahn (SL-023)**, despite appearing in only one unit's bibliography (Frede), is the proximate ally for Frede's anti-Modrak case: Frede explicitly aligns with Kahn on confining unity-of-consciousness to the inner sense rather than extending it to intellect.

**S. Cashdollar (SL-024)** is the cluster's main authority on incidental perception. Caston cites approvingly at fn. 48 against Modrak and Wedin; Frede engages on perceptive predication and habit. The cluster's analytic DA reading on incidental perception runs through Cashdollar 1973.

## Camp 3: Analytic Philosophy of Mind Applied to Ancient Psychology

The polemical heart of the cluster is the analytic phantasia debate of 1986-1996, played out among four non-cluster figures:

**Michael Wedin (SL-003)** publishes *Mind and Imagination in Aristotle* (1988), the cluster's most technically refined analytic phantasia reading. Frede declares Wedin convergent ("similar results but much more extensive and technically refined") and explicitly declines to engage Wedin's arguments because they would exceed her essay's limits — a striking gesture that effectively franchises the technical work to Wedin. Caston dissents at fn. 49 on the narrow point that Wedin makes phantasia a necessary condition of sensation of proper perceptibles; González treats Wedin as key-ally for the visual-imaging reading. Five of eight cluster units cite Wedin; tied with W. D. Ross for second-most-cited overall.

**Deborah K. W. Modrak (SL-004)** publishes 'ΦΑΝΤΑΣΙΑ Reconsidered' (1986) and *Aristotle: The Power of Perception* (Chicago 1987). She is the cluster's principal polemical foil: Caston disagrees at fns. 7, 39, 48, 55; Frede disagrees at fns. 5, 10, 42, 44, 48. The disagreement is over (a) phantasia's "belief-like character" and propositional content (Caston rejects); (b) whether Aristotle offers a theory of sensation that explains intersubstitutivity failures (Frede rejects); (c) Modrak's unity-of-consciousness reading that downplays Aristotle's dualism (Frede rejects). Hawhee pairs Modrak with Caston at p. 141 on the ill fit of the term "imagination." Papachristou cites Modrak neutrally at fn. 34. Four-unit Tier-A citation, all of them substantive engagements.

**W. D. Ross (SL-005)**, the canonical mid-century De Anima editor and Aristotle commentator, is cited by five units. Ross 1961 supplies the standard text and a "collage-of-pictures" reading at *DA* 434a7-10 that Nussbaum explicitly contests. Frede inverts Ross's verdict that phantasia is a "disability." For the 1985-1996 cohort Ross is canonical; later units (Hawhee, Papachristou) cite Ross 1995 reissue with Ackrill introduction.

**Joyce Engmann (SL-008)**, a less prominent figure than Modrak or Wedin, nonetheless reaches Tier-A (3 units): her 1976 *Journal of the History of Philosophy* essay 'Imagination and Truth in Aristotle' is cited by White at fn. 92, Caston approvingly at fn. 67 as antecedent of the broader (non-propositional) falsity reading, and Papachristou as indicative reading.

## Camp 4: Classical Philology and the Aristotelian Commentary Tradition

**R. D. Hicks (SL-012)**'s 1907 Cambridge *De anima* edition is the cluster's standard philological reference. Nussbaum and Caston both engage Hicks on individual passages (Caston fn. 8 on the "kataopsetai" note; fn. 13 on punctuation); Nussbaum rejects Hicks's voice-image reading. Papachristou cites the Arno 1976 reprint.

**D. W. Hamlyn (SL-013)**'s 1968 Clarendon *Aristotle's De Anima Books II and III* supplies the standard Anglophone translation. Frede notes Hamlyn as "cryptic" on plurality of senses and number; Nussbaum engages on 431a8 and 431b17; González cites for *Rhetoric* / *DA* cross-references. The Clarendon Hamlyn is the analytic-DA cohort's working translation.

**D. A. Rees (SL-007)**, in Anton & Kustas (Albany 1971), supplies the binary propositional/imagistic gloss that Nussbaum, Caston, White, and Papachristou all cite — usually as a foil, never as ally. The 1971 Anton-Kustas volume is one of the cluster's standard literature-review repositories.

**K. Lycos (SL-009)** appears in three units (Caston, Nussbaum, White) for his 1964 *Mind* essay on Aristotelian/Platonic phantasia continuity. Caston engages most substantively at fn. 54.

**Gerard Watson (SL-006)** straddles philology and philosophy: his 1982 *Classical Quarterly* essay defends the coherence of *DA* III.3 by pointing to its anti-Platonic stance, and his 1988 Galway monograph *Phantasia in Classical Thought* + 1994 ANRW chapter are the cluster's standard reference for post-Aristotelian phantasia history (Caston fn. 2, Hawhee, González). Four-unit Tier-A status reflects Watson's bridging position between philology and philosophy.

**Richard Sorabji (SL-011)**, founder of the King's College Ancient Commentators on Aristotle project, is cited by Nussbaum, Frede, and O'Gorman. Sorabji's 1972 *Aristotle on Memory* is the image-theorist par excellence: Nussbaum's phantasia-in-action thesis is partly positioned against Sorabji's mnemonic image-theory. O'Gorman's stance is the most distinctive: he "corrects" Sorabji on the memory connection in the rhetorical context.

**Jean-Louis Labarrière (SL-010)** is the cluster's main francophone interlocutor. His 1984 *Phronesis* essay on animal vs. human phantasia is cited by White, Caston (background), and González (key-ally and rare-dissenter). Three-unit Tier-A status, but the engagement is uneven — Caston gestural, González substantive.

## Camp 5: Classical Rhetoric Tradition

**George A. Kennedy (SL-015)** is the indispensable Aristotle Rhetoric translator. O'Gorman and Hawhee both deploy Kennedy's 1991 *On Rhetoric* translation as their working text; Hawhee adds Kennedy 1996 'Reworking Aristotle's Rhetoric.' Two-unit Tier-B citation, but Kennedy is functionally a primary reference, not a secondary scholar — the rhetoric subcluster's translation infrastructure.

**William W. Fortenbaugh (SL-016)** is the cluster's authority on Aristotle's *pathē* in the Rhetoric. His *Aristotle on Emotion* (1975; 2nd ed. 2002), 'Aristotle's Platonic Attitude Toward Delivery' (1986), and 'Persuasion through Character' (1996) are cited by O'Gorman, González, and Hawhee — all three rhetoric-subcluster authors. Three-unit Tier-A status. Fortenbaugh provides the emotion-theory backbone that lets the rhetoric subcluster connect phantasia to pathos and to the audience-effect apparatus of the *Rhetoric*.

**David Konstan (SL-017)** complements Fortenbaugh. González cites Konstan's *Emotions in Context* (1996); Hawhee cites *The Emotions of the Ancient Greeks* (2007). Konstan is the Brown-classics emotion-theorist whose work is the rhetoric subcluster's principal interlocutor for Aristotelian-affective-cognition outside the analytic DA literature.

## Camp 6: History of Ideas and Continental Phenomenology

**H. A. Wolfson (SL-018)**'s 1935 'The Internal Senses' in *Harvard Theological Review* 28 is cited by White (fn. 4) and Hawhee — both for the post-Aristotelian Galenic-Latin-Arabic-Hebrew transmission of the inner-senses doctrine. Two-unit Tier-B citation; the cluster's only systematic reference to the medieval transmission history.

**M. W. Bundy (SL-019)** is the 1927 *Theory of Imagination in Classical and Medieval Thought*; cited by White and Caston as standard background reference for the longue-durée imagination tradition.

**Kenneth Turnbull (SL-020)** is cited as background by O'Gorman (visual-reading camp at n. 6) and Papachristou (indicative reading at fn. 34).

**Cornelius Castoriadis (SL-025)** is the cluster's main bridge to post-phenomenological imagination theory. Papachristou invokes Castoriadis's *The Imaginary Institution of Society* (1975, MIT Press translation 1998) as the continental imaginary-tradition framing of Aristotelian phantasia.

**Martin Heidegger (SL-026)** appears explicitly in only one unit's apparatus — White at fn. 11 — as part of the modern continental phantasia genealogy (Leibniz, Kant, Hegel, Husserl, Heidegger). White's invocation is gestural via Spiegelberg's *Phenomenological Movement*. The Heidegger node is nonetheless a cross-pipeline anchor: the dissertation's broader Heidegger GA 18 reading of *pathos* as phantasia-correlate hooks here.

**Immanuel Kant (SL-027)** appears in Frede only but with disproportionate philosophical weight: Frede invokes Kant explicitly at two crucial junctures — synthesis of manifold (§II, A 190ff = looking up/down a house) and Gestalt requirement for thought (§III, B 154 = cannot think a line without drawing one). The cluster's most prominent post-Aristotelian philosophical parallel.

## Summary Pattern

The cluster's secondary-literature network shows three structural features:

1. **Two-tier hub structure mirrors the cluster's two intellectual centers**. Freudenthal and Schofield (6 units each, tied) anchor the bibliographic backbone. Wedin, Ross (5 each), Modrak, Rees, Watson (4 each) form the second tier. All seven are analytic-DA references except Watson, who straddles philology and philosophy.

2. **The rhetoric subcluster reads more widely than the analytic subcluster reads it back**. Rhetoric authors (O'Gorman, González, Hawhee) systematically cite the analytic DA references (Schofield, Wedin, Modrak, Frede, Caston). The reverse traffic — analytic-DA authors citing rhetoric-tradition secondary scholars (Kennedy, Fortenbaugh, Konstan) — is zero across the entire cluster.

3. **Continental phenomenology and Thomism are present but marginal**. Heidegger, Castoriadis, Wolfson, and Bundy appear only in one or two units; the cluster is overwhelmingly an analytic-anglophone and classical-philological project. The Heidegger node (White only) and the Castoriadis node (Papachristou only) are the cluster's main connective tissue to the dissertation's broader phenomenological-imaginary commitments, but they do not propagate through the citation network.
