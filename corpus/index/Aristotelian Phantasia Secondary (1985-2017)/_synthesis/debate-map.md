# Debate Map — Aristotelian Phantasia Secondary (1985-2017)

This document narrates the twelve canonical cross-article debates distilled from the cluster's 46 raw within-unit tensions. The cluster spans eight monographs and journal essays in three subfields — analytic philosophy of mind (Nussbaum 1985; Frede 1992; Caston 1996), Anglophone rhetorical theory (O'Gorman 2005; Gonzalez 2006; Hawhee 2011), classical philology (White 1985), and Thomistic-influenced commentary (Papachristou 2013) — and their debates accordingly cross disciplinary boundaries that the authors themselves rarely make explicit.

## D1. Interpretation vs. image-residue phantasia

The cluster's most central and recurring debate. Nussbaum's signature 1985 thesis is that phantasia is the interpretive aspect of perception, the faculty that supplies "seeing-as" content; the pictorial connotation ("places before the eyes," decaying kineseis) is peripheral. O'Gorman and Gonzalez extend this interpretive reading into the rhetorical register. Against this, White, Frede, and Papachristou foreground phantasia's image-residue character — White by reading DA III.3 429a1ff's closing paragraph as theoretically primary; Frede by emphasizing the "mere after-images often false" passage of 428a11-16; Papachristou by leaning hard on pro ommaton poiesthai at 427b18-19. The two readings are not strictly contradictory — both sides concede textual mixture — but they pull interpretation in opposite directions on every downstream question (the role of phantasia in cognition, its rhetorical legitimacy, its relation to memory and dreams).

## D2. Separate faculty vs. supervenient capacity

Caston 1996's central polemic, directed against Frede's "minimal account": is phantasia a power of the soul in its own right, or does it supervene on aisthesis without faculty-status? Caston argues for own-capacity via Aristotle's "same in number, different in being" formula at DA III.9 432a31-b1 + De Insomniis 1 459a16-18; he reads the einai-clause as a non-Leibnizian identity criterion (his fn. 51) that permits one underlying power to bear genuinely distinct aspects. Frede, by contrast, treats supervenience as theoretically sufficient — phantasia performs synthesis and bridging without being upgraded ontologically. Nussbaum and Papachristou tacitly side with Caston by treating phantasia as a faculty with its own work; only Frede holds the supervenience line.

## D3. Unitary vs. tripartite taxonomy

DA III.10 433b29 says "all phantasia is either calculative (logistike) or sensitive (aisthetike)." Most of the cluster reads this as a binary or even monistic taxonomy (Frede explicitly defends a single causal account; Caston needs a unified faculty for his intentionality story). Papachristou's 2013 minority report argues that DA III.11 433b31-434a5's hesitations about imperfect animals, together with the Themistius/Philoponus/Aquinas commentary tradition, require a third sub-sensitive tier of "indefinite" phantasia. The binary remains but recontextualizes as the upper level of a tripartite scheme. This is the cluster's only sustained challenge to the modern consensus on faculty-individuation.

## D4. Lexis-narrow vs. epideictic-broad rhetorical home

For most twentieth-century commentary, phantasia in the Rhetoric is a Book-III lexis topic — bracketed off as the verbal "outward show" supplement to argument proper. O'Gorman and Gonzalez systematically dismantle this. O'Gorman relocates phantasia's rhetorical home in epideictic, anchored in megethos / auxesis (Rhet. I.3, I.9); Gonzalez relocates it in the psychological-emotional fabric of Rhetoric II (emotions defined as phantasiai of pleasure/pain). Hawhee threads both books together. The philosophy-side authors (Frede, Caston) simply assume the Book-III default; the rhetoric-side authors collectively dispute it. This is the cluster's signature interdisciplinary axis.

## D5. Mere-show pejorative vs. legitimate rhetorical function

A philological micro-question with disproportionate stakes. Freudenthal 1863 inferred from Herodotus 7.10e's phantazesthai that phantasia at Rhet. III.1 1404a11 means "ostentation / mere show." Cope, Kennedy, Halliwell, and through them most editorial traditions adopted this reading; Frede preserves it by silence (her cluster-late article does not engage the rhetorical passages). Gonzalez 2006 is the most sustained refutation: the noun phantasia in Aristotle's 100+ uses never elsewhere means "ostentation"; Freudenthal's verb-to-noun extrapolation lacks synchronic-lexical warrant. O'Gorman, Hawhee, and (already in 1978) Nussbaum side with Gonzalez. The debate is local in evidence but global in implication: it decides whether rhetorical phantasia is constitutively suspect or constitutively legitimate.

## D6. Phantasia grafted onto perception vs. pure interpretation

Hawhee's 2011 explicit modification of Nussbaum 1985. Hawhee accepts the interpretivist drift but insists that rhetorical phantasia operates at or near the level of direct sensory perception — words "graft" phantasia onto perception non-metaphorically rather than at the level of "seeing-as-understanding." The debate is internal to the pro-Nussbaum camp; it sharpens the interpretivist thesis rather than overturning it. Kennedy 2007's scare-quoted "bringing-before-the-eyes" is Hawhee's target rather than Nussbaum.

## D7. DA III.3 428a1-4 *kata metaphoran*

A textual question that allows two grammatical readings. The standard reading (Hicks, Ross, Rodier) takes the bracketed transferred-sense as the broad phainetai usage Aristotle is excluding from the official noun-account. Nussbaum's 1978 rereading takes the bracketed sense to be "mere show" — Aristotle is bracketing colloquial usage to keep the technical noun-account clean. Gonzalez 2006 extends Nussbaum's rereading to discipline the 1404a11 question. White and Frede stay with the standard reading by default. Aristotle's own silence about which transferred sense he means leaves the question genuinely open.

## D8. Deliberative phantasia at DA III.11 434a7-10

Whether the deliberative-phantasia (phantasia bouleutike) passage at III.11 434a7-10 carries cognitive weight. Nussbaum and Papachristou treat it as robust evidence for phantasia's role in practical reasoning — the single-pictured measure of combined ends that motivates action. White (in his footnote 80) and Frede (by methodological confinement to III.3-8 and III.9 dualism-arguments) treat the III.11 passage as marginal or textually unstable. Ross-tradition commentary tends to side with White; the action-theoretic readers side with Nussbaum.

## D9. Intellectual memory Yes vs. No

Frede holds that there is no intellectual memory in Aristotle: memory and dreams stay entirely on the sensible side; phantasia is the bridge but is CONFINED to sensible content; this preserves Aristotelian dualism (DA 431b20-432a2 sharply, softened only at 432a3ff). Nussbaum, White, and Papachristou tend toward the continuity reading — phantasia carries intellectual purchase via standardized pictures (DA III.7 431b6-8) and the APo II.19 routed-army ascent. The debate is less explicit than D1-D5 but turns on whether phantasia is the floor of intellection or only its sensible adjunct.

## D10. Thomistic vs. analytic-Anglophone reception

A meta-debate visible mostly in citation profiles. Papachristou (and to a lesser extent White) treat the late-ancient and Thomistic commentary tradition (Themistius, Philoponus, Aquinas's Sentencia libri De anima) as governing authorities for reading DA III's psychology. Nussbaum, Frede, and Caston work in the analytic-Anglophone idiom that began with Schofield 1978 — Sorabji, Modrak, Wedin, Nussbaum 1978 are the canonical interlocutors; Aquinas appears only sporadically. The cluster's only place to see this disagreement directly is its citation patterns.

## D11. Silent disagreement: citation gaps

A debate not stated as a tension by any single author, but visible across the cluster: omissions can function as silent disagreement. Caston 1996 nowhere engages Nussbaum's 1985 essay (though he engages her 1978 monograph); Gonzalez 2006 does not cite White 1985, Caston 1996, or O'Gorman 2005; Hawhee 2011 does not cite Frede; Papachristou 2013 does not engage Caston 1996. Pole A reads these omissions as scholarly judgments (engagement-by-omission); pole B reads them as benign disciplinary boundaries — analytic philosophy of mind, rhetoric, philology, and Thomistic commentary read different bibliographies. The debate is meta-bibliographic: it asks whether the cluster is a unified scholarly conversation or a set of parallel disciplinary tracks that happen to share a Greek term.

## D12. Phantasia/aisthesis "one in number, different in being"

The famous DA 433a31 / De Insomniis 1 459a16-18 formula is parsed in (at least) two ways. Nussbaum prefers the two-description / arete-dikaiosune reading: ONE activity under TWO descriptions of one power. White and Caston prefer the two-activity / road-up-road-down model: phantasia is a SECONDARY motion arising from the PRIMARY motion of aisthesis. Caston's contribution is to render the einai-formula as a non-Leibnizian identity criterion (fn. 51) — same-yet-distinct as a paraphrase-resistant logical primitive. Both Nussbaum and White concede the evidence is mixed; Caston's logical-primitive move is the most theoretically articulated resolution available in the cluster.

These twelve debates organize 46 within-unit tensions across eight units. D1, D2, D4, and D5 are the cluster's load-bearing axes (centrality tier 1); D3, D6, D7, D8, D12 are second-tier interpretive disputes; D9, D10, D11 are background or meta-bibliographic. No debate is resolved at the cluster level; the synthesis records the cartography, not the verdict.
