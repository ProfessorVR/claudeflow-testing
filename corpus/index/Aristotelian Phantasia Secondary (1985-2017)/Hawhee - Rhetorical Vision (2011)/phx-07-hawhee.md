# PHX-07: Debra Hawhee, "Looking Into Aristotle's Eyes: Toward a Theory of Rhetorical Vision" (2011)

*Advances in the History of Rhetoric* 14.2 (2011): 139–165. Pennsylvania State University, Department of English. DOI 10.1080/15362426.2011.613288.

## § Overview and the "rhetorical vision" thesis

Hawhee's essay is the most ambitious synthetic statement of the rhetoric-cluster's reading of Aristotelian *phantasia*. Where Nussbaum 1985 had used *phantasia* to repair Aristotle's theory of action, where White 1985 had retrieved its meaning in *De Anima* III.3–8, where Caston 1996 had defended its imaginative-yet-non-imagistic character, and where O'Gorman 2005 and González 2006 had inserted *phantasia* into rhetorical theory through the chapters on epideictic and *lexis* respectively, Hawhee proposes a more comprehensive thesis: that "rhetorical vision" — the capacity by which words form, alter, and conjure visual perception in audiences — is the throughline that unifies all three books of the *Rhetoric* and binds them to the *De Anima* and the *Parva Naturalia* (p.140). The opening epigraphs make the program plain: Sophocles' blind Oedipus assures the Stranger that "in everything I say there will be vision," and Aristotle in *De Anima* 428a.15–16 affirms that visions appear to us even when our eyes are shut. The first sentences of the article (p.139) state Hawhee's program of culling a theory of rhetorical vision from "the cluster of terms" that bears on Aristotle's theory of visual style — *phantasia*, *pro ommatōn poiein*, *energeia*, *enargeia*, *megethos*, *hyperochē*, and *asteia* among them. Rhetorical vision, she insists at p.140, "is not merely a passing notion in Aristotle's *Rhetoric*"; it "lends unity to the treatise's famously various parts." Yet the construct does more than house an academic-historical claim. Hawhee programmatically distinguishes "rhetorical vision" from the now-familiar "visual rhetoric" of Finnegan, Hariman & Lucaites, Blakesley & Brooke, and Kimble. Visual rhetoric, she says, concerns "the rhetorical work of the visual," that is, the rhetorical force of plastic, photographic, televisual, and other visual media; rhetorical vision concerns "the complex ways that words — oral or written — form perception" (p.140). Through rhetorical vision, "words come to life." Crucially, Hawhee rejects the reading (associated with Kennedy 2007, 222, and signaled by his use of scare quotes around "bringing-before-the-eyes") that *phantasia* and pro ommatōn poiein operate only on the level of cognitive metaphor — as a placeholder for understanding ("Ah, now I see what you mean," p.141). Such a reading, she argues, severs Aristotelian rhetoric from the animistic, sense-related treatises elsewhere in the corpus. The non-metaphorical alternative she proposes claims that rhetorical vision occurs "very near and sometimes at the level of direct sensory perception" (p.141). The animating bet of the essay is that this stronger claim helps "enliven rhetorical theory and criticism."

## § Section-by-section synthesis

After the untitled introduction (pp.139–141), the article unfolds in five sections. **(1) *Phantasia and Rhetoric* (pp.141–144)** surveys the scholarship — Schofield, Watson, Caston, Modrak, Nussbaum, Frede, O'Gorman, Kennerly — and isolates two features for emphasis: *phantasia*'s status as a *dunamis* distinct from *aisthēsis*, opinion, knowledge, and intelligence (DA 428a.1–8); and its association with vision happening at some "remove," "fuzziness," or limit-condition (bronze mirrors, distant figures, after-images, dreams). Hawhee threads in DA 2.8.420b.29–33's account of voice as "sound with *phantasiai*" to tie *phantasia* to meaning-making (*sēmantikos*), so that language-and-phantasia have an Aristotelian theoretical pedigree, not just a Rhetorical convenience (p.143). **(2) *Phantasia and the* Rhetoric (pp.144–149)** moves into Book I, beginning with the famous I.11.1370a.28–29 line that *phantasia* is a kind of weak perception (*aisthēsis tis asthenēs*), but resisting its standard ahistorical use as an essence-definition. Hawhee instead nests it in the pleasure-and-desire context of I.11 (1369b.18–20, 1370a.25–27, 1370a.27–31, 1370b.7, 1370b.25–26, 1370b.27, 1370b.33–34, 1371a.18–20) and follows the chain through I.12's account of reputation, witnesses, babies and small animals (1371a.8–10, 13–16), and I.14's "heaping up" of wrongs (1375a.8–11). She introduces *phantasia bouleutikē* (deliberative phantasia) by way of DA 3.11.434a.6–11 and 3.7.431b.6–8, with Wolfson 1935 supplying the older language of a "compositive sort of imagination." **(3) *Dispositional Vision:* Phantasia *and the* Pathē (pp.149–152)** crosses into Book II by way of the *echontes/echein* (disposed/being-disposed) language of 2.1.1377b.20–1378a.1, identifying the verb *phainein* (to show, disclose, bring to light, appear) and its many forms as the dominant lexical signature of Book II. Anger at 2.2.1378a.30–32 is defined via *phainomenēs/n* (the slight must appear); shame at 2.6.1384a.21–24 *is phantasia* "about loss of reputation"; the proverb "shame is in the eyes" (2.6.1384a.33–35) and the *exaggeltikous*/broadcaster talk (2.6.5–8) confirm shame's irreducibly visual mode of social transmission. **(4) *Rhetorical Vision through* Lexis (pp.152–155)** moves into Book III, beginning with Licymnius and Bryson (3.2.1405b.5–7 and ff.), passing through urbanity / *asteia* at 3.10.1410b.33–35 (the canonical *pro ommatōn poiein* line), arriving at 3.11.1411b.22–23's "I call those things 'before-the-eyes' that signify things engaged in activity," and finishing with the Homeric examples at 3.11.1411b.26–32, 1411b.33–1412a.5 and 1412a.9–10, anchored by *Metaph.* 9.5.1048a.32–34 ("Energeia means the presence of the thing"). She closes the section with Moran 1996 and a long quoted excerpt from Burke's *Rhetoric of Motives* on "placing before our very eyes." **(5) *Rhetorical Vision in Demosthenes' "Epitaphios"* (pp.155–159)** moves from theory to a performative case study of the 338 BCE Athenian funeral oration delivered after Chaironeia. The brief **Conclusion (pp.159–160)** condenses the article's wager: words have the capacity to bring energetic images before the eyes, overtake what is already before the eyes, and so move audiences into deliberative *phantasia* leading to belief and decision; and the wager travels gracefully toward contemporary neuroscience (Kosslyn & Thompson 2003; Prasad, Thomas, & Aguirre 2008).

## § *Phantasia* in *Rhetoric* Book I — deliberative, judicial, and epideictic genres

Hawhee's reading of Book I is the most original section of the article because it draws the most from previously underused material. The famous "weak perception" line at 1.11.1370a.28–29 is treated not as a definition but as an embedded clause inside Aristotle's argument that pleasure depends on perceiving (*aisthanesthai*) "a certain emotion" (*tinos pathous*), and that *phantasia* of remembered or hoped pleasures yields pleasure now (1370a.27–31). The chapter, Hawhee emphasizes, is the one immediately preceding the judicial chapters — that is, the rhetorical context is judicial argument about wrongdoing, and the phenomenological context is the desire to *see and possess* (*theasthai kai ktēsasthai*, 1370a.25–27 in Kennedy's translation) the pleasures one has been told about (p.145). The Iliad and Odyssey lines that Aristotle cites at 1.11.1370b.25–26 about Odysseus and the mourners "raising sweet longing of tears" (1370b.27) prove for Hawhee that *phantasia* and *pathē* mingle "simultaneously, forming a complex and at times compound set of competing but often complementary visions, thereby intensifying the pathē overall" (p.146). The *phantasia* of victory (1370b.33–34) and of friendship's perceived presence of the good (1371a.18–20) confirm a structural feature: *phantasia* lets desire run forward in time. The Freese-translated *De Memoria* signet-ring passage (1.450a.26–450b.1) — "the stimulus produced impresses a sort of likeness of the percept" — supplies the physical-materialist substrate. Chapter 12 carries the analysis from *pathos* into *ēthos*: honor and reputation depend on "each person's *phantasia* that he has the qualities of an important person" (1371a.8–10), and so the reputation of speakers and witnesses heightens the seeing. Babies and small animals (1371a.13–16) introduce a limit case to which Aristotle returns in Book II.6 — those who can see but not say. Chapter 14 supplies the "heaping up" (*huperochē*) of wrongs at 1.14.1375a.8–11: piling many actions on one criminal generates near-visual density. Hawhee then expands *huperochē* etymologically (p.148): *huper-* (excessive, above) plus *-ochē* (support, prop), so the word itself names something like excessive support, the prop of a pile. With reference to language it shifts toward periphrasis or prolixity (LSJ); in rhetorical context, superiority is captured through excess, a "heaping up" of active descriptions. From here Hawhee reaches her crucial Book-I move: she introduces *phantasia bouleutikē* by way of DA 3.11.434a.6–11 ("This implies the ability to combine several images [*pleionōn phantasmatōn*] into one") and DA 3.7.431b.6–8 ("by means of the images or thoughts in the soul, just as if it were seeing, it [the soul] calculates"). Nussbaum 1985, 264 is cited approvingly: "deliberative *phantasia* can also involve picturing, or at least some sort of envisaging." Hawhee's deliberative *phantasia* "encompasses all three branches of rhetoric — deliberative, judicial, epideictic — and is almost certainly the faculty at work that enables both rhetor and audience to process the 'heaping up' of wrongs, thereby forming a composite sketch of an alleged criminal's — or a revered person's — actions" (pp.148–149). The link to *auxēsis* at 1.9.1368a.25–30 (epideictic "clothes the actions with greatness [*megethos*] and beauty") and the astronomic-Homeric use of *megethos* (stellar magnitudes, loftiness) seal Hawhee's reading of Book I: *phantasia* "traffics in desire, belief, and action, sharing crucial patterns with rhetoric" (p.149).

## § *Phantasia* in *Rhetoric* Book II — *pathos* read through vision

Book II is the book in which, on Hawhee's account, the *phantasia*-*pathos* knot tightens to the point of being inseparable. She begins by reading the opening gambit of Book II (2.1.1377b.20–1378a.1) as a stack of "dispositions on dispositions, *echontes* on *echontes*, a meeting of appearances" (p.150). The verb *phainesthai* in the line about how the speaker should "seem to be" (*phainesthai*) a certain kind of person and the hearer should be "disposed" (*echein*) is read as a verb-cluster: *phainein* (to show, disclose, bring to light, appear) is the lexical root of *phantasia*. Book II is "replete" (p.150) with these forms. Anger at 2.2.1378a.30–32 is then dispatched. The "famous definition" — desire, accompanied by mental and physical distress, for apparent retaliation because of an apparent slight — turns twice on *phainomenēs/n*. Hawhee endorses Nussbaum 1985's interpretation of *phantasia* here ("perception-as-interpretation"): "the slight and the desired retaliation need to appear as such in order for anger to be present" (p.150). The "strong sense of appearance and the relative weakness of the word *apparent* here" explains why Kennedy in his first edition rendered *phainomenēs* as "conspicuous"; Note 21 mediates a small philological debate by way of Harris 1997. From anger Hawhee moves to shame at 2.6.1384a.21–24. Shame "is *phantasia* about a loss of reputation and for its own sake, not for its results" — and the proverb "shame is in the eyes" (1384a.33–35) and the *exaggeltikous*/broadcaster passage (1384b.5–8) seal her interpretive bet. Shame is irreducibly social (Gross 2006, 41) and irreducibly visual: "shame is transmitted socially through the eyes of others into the faculty of *phantasia* — perceptions layer onto perceptions, dispositions on dispositions, shameful actions on actions" (p.151). The "in both cases they are 'in' their eyes" (1384a.34–35) reading literalizes the visual transmission. And the babies-and-small-animals figure returns at 1384b.22–24 as the limit case: there is no shame felt before those who lack the capacity to speak. Book II's payoff for Hawhee is the second sentence of p.152: "In book II, then, *phantasia* moves closer to the way Nussbaum (1985) figures it — as interpretation, but it is interpretation that is grafted onto direct perception: rhetorical vision depends upon both simultaneously." The grafting clause is Hawhee's signature modification.

## § *Phantasia* in *Rhetoric* Book III — *pro ommatōn poiein*, metaphor, *megethos*, *energeia*

Book III is the book Hawhee reads as the synthesis: it is where everything falls together, where a cluster of related visual terms is introduced and explained, and where rhetorical vision takes shape (p.152). She begins with the sophists Licymnius and Bryson at the opening of 3.2 (1405b.5–7 and ff.), pointing out that Aristotle refutes Bryson's substitutability thesis by appealing to the words' capacity to "make the thing appear before the eyes" (*poieinto pragma pro ommatōn*) — that is, beauty in words lies in their sensuous appeal, in sound (*phōnē*), in meaning (*dunamei*), in visualization (*opsei*), or in some other form of sense perception (*aisthēsei*). The Homer example — calling the dawn "rosy-fingered" rather than "red-fingered" — locates aesthetic difference in sensory specificity. She then jumps to 3.10 and the discussion of urbanity / *asteia*, where pro ommatōn poiein receives its canonical line: "things should be seen as being done rather than as going to be done" (3.10.1410b.33–35). Hawhee underscores that the change is not merely from future to present tense but the "animated gathering of both future and past images into the now by rendering them lively, vivid, and kinetic" (p.153). Iphicrates' "my path of words is through the midst of Chares' actions" (3.10.1411b.1–3) yields a metaphor of cutting a path through actions and so making the pile of them visible. Isocrates' description of the Persians "in every way practicing lowly thinking" (*Panegyricus* 11–12) introduces *auxein* — a kind of visual accumulation reaching back to the Book-I *huperochē*. Then 3.11.1411b.22–23: "I call those things 'before-the-eyes' that signify things engaged in activity." The word for activity is *energeia*. Hawhee draws out the contrast at 3.11.1411b.26–32 between "foursquare" (a geometric, flat metaphor without *energeia*) and "free-ranging animal" / "Greeks darting forward on their feet" (where darting is actualization and metaphor "for he means 'quickly'"). The Homeric examples at 3.11.1411b.33–1412a.5 (rolling stone, flying arrow, longing spear) culminate in 3.11.1412a.9–10's "He makes everything move and live, and *energeia* is motion." She closes the lexical analysis by anchoring *energeia* in *Metaphysics* 9.5.1048a.32–34: "Energeia means the presence of the thing." The conclusion of the section makes *lexis* the lifeblood of rhetorical vision: bringing-before-the-eyes and *energeia* are the leading stylistic strategies for tapping *phantasia* and stirring the *pathē*. Hawhee then turns to Moran 1996, who reads *energeia* as occurring "as much in the audience as it does in or around the metaphorical subject" (Moran 1996, 393), and who closes by quoting Kenneth Burke from *A Rhetoric of Motives* (1950/1969, 86–87) on the difference between an abstract term and a "concrete image designed to stand for this idea, and to 'place it before our very eyes.'" The Burke passage emphasizes that such an image will "contain a whole bundle of principles." For Hawhee this dovetails with deliberative *phantasia*: each composite image is a bundle, and audiences are engaged in their own *phantasia*-work; invention spreads from rhetor to hearer.

## § The Nussbaum 1985 citation at p.152 — what Hawhee takes and what she adds

The most-cited interlocutor in the rhetoric cluster is Nussbaum, and Hawhee's most-quoted line in subsequent work is her grafting clause at p.152: "*phantasia* moves closer to the way Nussbaum (1985) figures it — as interpretation, but it is interpretation that is grafted onto direct perception: rhetorical vision depends upon both simultaneously." The line has two halves. The first half affirms Nussbaum's interpretivist reading — *phantasia* as the faculty by which an animal sees an x as a y — and applies it specifically to Book II. The second half corrects (or extends) Nussbaum: interpretation does not float free of perception, it is grafted onto it. This second clause does several pieces of work. It rescues the imagist insight (Modrak, Watson, in part) without conceding the field; it lines up with Hawhee's "fuzzy" / distance-dependent reading from p.143; it makes rhetorical vision a hybrid concept; and it dovetails with Hawhee's *pro ommatōn poiein* analysis, where Aristotle's "I call those things before-the-eyes that signify things engaged in activity" is, after all, both an interpretive *and* a perceptual claim. Two further citations of Nussbaum surface elsewhere. At p.148, in the deliberative-*phantasia* discussion, Hawhee notes: "According to Nussbaum, who otherwise argues against a purely imagistic reading of *phantasia*, this passage suggests on the contrary some sort of envisaging" (Nussbaum 1985, 264). At p.142, Nussbaum 1985, 265 is cited for the thesis that *phantasia* "ties abstract thought to concrete perceptible objects or situations." Note 12 is candid: "Martha Nussbaum (1985) and Victor Caston (1996) would probably disagree with my wish to stress 'image' here; Nussbaum argues that images have been *over*stressed in contemporary interpretations of *phantasia*, and she opts instead to emphasize its ties to the verb *phainesthai*, 'to appear'; Caston follows a similar logic linking Aristotle's *phantasia* to Plato (21n3). Most other scholars discussed in this essay, however, foreground images rather strenuously." This is the cleanest map in the cluster of Hawhee's position relative to Nussbaum: alignment in Book II, disagreement on stress, mutual gain in rhetoric.

## § Hawhee's monograph trajectory — from *Bodily Arts* (2004) to *Moving Bodies* (2009) to *Rhetoric in Tooth and Claw* (2017)

The article is built on Hawhee's prior and subsequent monographs. *Bodily Arts: Rhetoric and Athletics in Ancient Greece* (Texas, 2004) is cited at Note 18 for her prior treatment of "the antagonistic relationship between saying and seeing in the ancient world"; the 2011 article reverses ground to argue that saying and seeing also work in tandem. *Moving Bodies: Kenneth Burke at the Edges of Language* (South Carolina, 2009) is not cited by name in the article but is everywhere implicit: Burke's "place it before our very eyes" passage from *A Rhetoric of Motives* is the centerpiece of pp.154–155, and Hawhee's Moran-mediated framing of Burke as audience-locating *energeia* is the natural Burke-Aristotle bridge that *Moving Bodies* had explored at length. The Burke citation also signals Hawhee's longer commitment to a rhetorical theory that crosses verbal-and-bodily registers. The 2010 collaboration with Cory Holding on Priestley and Austin (Note 10) — "Case Studies in Material Rhetoric: Joseph Priestley and Gilbert Austin," *Rhetorica* 28(3) — pushes rhetorical vision into the eighteenth- and early-nineteenth-century elocutionary tradition. Hawhee's later monograph, *Rhetoric in Tooth and Claw: Animals, Language, Sensation* (Chicago, 2017), is the natural sequel: the 2011 essay's recurring attention to animals and babies as limit-cases for *phantasia* (1.12.1371a.13–16, 2.6.1384b.22–24) anticipates the 2017 book's program of decentering the human in rhetorical theory. The article therefore stands at the hinge of Hawhee's career: it consolidates the 2004 monograph's interest in bodies, develops the 2009 monograph's Burkean inheritance, and seeds the 2017 monograph's animal turn.

## § Comparison with rhetoric-cluster siblings — O'Gorman 2005 and González 2006

Hawhee's most direct sister-essay in the cluster is O'Gorman 2005, "Aristotle's *Phantasia* in the *Rhetoric*: Lexis, Appearance, and the Epideictic Function of Discourse," *Philosophy and Rhetoric* 38(1): 16–40. Hawhee explicitly thanks Ned O'Gorman in the article's acknowledgment (p.139), declares O'Gorman's piece "the most thorough and head-on examination of *phantasia* in the context of epideictic discourse" (p.144), and Note 20 says: "My reading of *huperochē* as a rhetorical strategy complements O'Gorman's reading of epideictic in terms of sight and scale (2005, 30) rather than restricting it to belief and value. Considering *phantasia* as the link between rhetoric and belief, *huperochē* as a kind of piling up of images good or bad helps to make the link between magnitude and value." Hawhee both extends O'Gorman beyond epideictic (into deliberative and judicial via Book I) and supplies the *huperochē* concept-cluster O'Gorman did not develop. The second sibling is González 2006, "The Meaning and Function of *Phantasia* in Aristotle's *Rhetoric* III.1," *TAPA* 136: 99–131. Hawhee cites González at Note 16 as "an important exception" in scholarship that takes up *phantasia* in Book III specifically, and signals that her own Book-III discussion will engage González. González's specific home in *Rhetoric* III.1 (on *hypocrisis* and the rise of written prose) is narrower than Hawhee's three-chapter (3.2 / 3.10 / 3.11) sweep, so the two essays are complementary rather than competing. With Caston 1996, Hawhee shares the rejection of "imagination" as a translation but stresses image more than Caston (Note 12). With Frede 1992, she shares the "after-image" valence (via O'Gorman). With Nussbaum 1985 she shares the interpretivist core but grafts it onto perception. With White 1985 she shares the Galenic-skepticism note (Note 12). The cluster therefore reads as: White and Schofield clear away post-Aristotelian "imagination"; Nussbaum installs interpretivism; Caston salvages a non-imagistic imagination; Frede works out the cognitive role; O'Gorman pushes the construct into epideictic; González hammers Book III.1; Hawhee then synthesizes the whole into rhetorical vision and adds the *pro ommatōn poiein* / *energeia* / *huperochē* lexical map. The two parallel rhetoric-cluster essays running concurrently with this analysis (Frede 1992 and O'Gorman 2005) appear, on the references-list verification above, exactly where the cluster master plan would predict.

## § Cross-pipeline bridges — Aristotle pipeline, HR-01-GROSS rhetorical ontology, Uncomfortable Situations situated emotion

Hawhee's 2011 article runs cleanly into three of the corpus's adjacent pipelines. **(1) Aristotle pipeline.** The Bekker-anchored locus map in *phx-07-hawhee.json* — Rhet. 1.9 / 1.10 / 1.11 / 1.12 / 1.14 / 2.1 / 2.2 / 2.6 / 3.2 / 3.10 / 3.11, plus DA 2.8, 3.3, 3.7, 3.11, plus *De Memoria* 1.450a, plus Poet. 17.1.1455a, plus Metaph. 9.5.1048a, plus Metaph. A.1.981a — is a near-complete map of the Aristotelian *phantasia* substrate, and the Bekker numbers can be passed directly to the Aristotle pipeline as canonical anchors for KU edges. **(2) HR-01-GROSS (rhetorical ontology of emotion).** Daniel Gross 2006, *The Secret History of Emotion: From Aristotle to Modern Brain Science* (Chicago), is cited at p.151 ("'shame as Aristotle describes it is irreducibly social'"). Hawhee's reading — "I would add that this is because it is also irreducibly visual in that it must be visible: shame is transmitted socially through the eyes of others into the faculty of *phantasia*" — is a direct extension of the HR-01-GROSS thesis that emotion is constituted in social interaction rather than residing in the interior of the subject. The bridge is bidirectional: HR-01-GROSS supplies the social ontology of emotion; PHX-07 supplies the visual phenomenology. **(3) Uncomfortable Situations / situated emotion.** Konstan 2007's *Emotions of the Ancient Greeks* (Note 22) — "classical view of *pathē* as arising primarily in and from social interactions" — anchors the situated-emotion subcluster. The shame and anger analyses in pp.150–151 are the most usable Aristotelian materials for that pipeline. **(4) Burke pipeline / Moving Bodies (Hawhee 2009).** The Burke / Moran 1996 nexus at pp.154–155 is the natural anchor for any KU graph that connects Aristotelian *phantasia* to twentieth-century rhetorical theory. **(5) Neurorhetoric / cognitive-science extension.** Kosslyn & Thompson 2003, Prasad et al. 2008, Cohen et al. 2002, Slobin 2008, and Mildner 2008 open an explicit empirical-extension lane that the cluster's master plan can route as a separate downstream pipeline.

## § Coda — what the article changes

After Hawhee 2011, three things change for the cluster. First, *phantasia* in the *Rhetoric* can no longer be read as a marginal Book-III-3.11 add-on; it threads through all three books and ties the *Rhetoric* to *De Anima* and *Parva Naturalia*. Second, the "metaphorical seeing" reading of *pro ommatōn poiein* — Kennedy's default — is no longer the only available reading; a non-metaphorical reading, in which *phantasia* operates at or near direct sense perception, is on the table. Third, the cluster's interpretive map can now be read as a four-corner constellation: Modrak (imagist), Nussbaum (interpretivist), Caston (non-imagistic imaginative), Hawhee (grafted hybrid in the service of rhetorical vision). Hawhee's article is the most-cited rhetoric-cluster piece in subsequent scholarship for these three reasons — programmatic scope, performative case study, and the bridge to neuroscience that gives the construct a contemporary handle.
