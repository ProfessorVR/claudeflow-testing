## § Overview and the Supervenience Thesis

Frede's seventeen-page essay is the shortest in the cluster and the most polemically directed of the three pre-Caston pieces. Within the field-defining Nussbaum-Rorty volume that helped consolidate the Anglo-Aristotelian revival in philosophy of mind, Frede stakes out a deliberately deflationary position. Her signature thesis, set out at book p. 286, is that *phantasia* is not a separate faculty of the soul but a phenomenon that supervenes on sense-perception. There is, on her reading, no faculty in charge of images as such; *phantasia* is the residual, derivative dimension of *aisthesis* that lingers and decays after the sense-object has departed. When the quality or truth-status of an image becomes doubtful, one cannot consult an independent imagination-faculty for verification; one can only return to sense-experience itself.

This thesis structures Frede's treatment. She announces a "minimal account" (p. 280) confined to two cognitive functions: (1) *phantasia* as synthesizer/retainer of sense-perceptions across time and across the senses, and (2) *phantasia* as the bridge between sensible particulars and intellect in both practical and theoretical reasoning. The minimalism is pointed: Frede refuses to upgrade *phantasia* into a separate faculty even while granting it cognitive work, and refuses to dissolve Aristotle's strict aisthesis/noeton dichotomy into a unified consciousness even while granting mediation. Her polemical edge targets Deborah Modrak (1986, 1987a), whose unified-phantasia and unified-consciousness readings she dismantles, and secondarily Malcolm Schofield (1979), whose "loose-knit family concept" verdict she contests with a unified causal account.

## § Section-by-Section Synthesis

Frede organizes the essay into three Roman-numeral sections.

**Section I, "Problems with a Unified Concept of *Phantasia*"** (pp. 279–282), diagnoses the translational and exegetical difficulties of the term. *Phantasia* does "triple duty" designating capacity, activity, and product — a triplicity not in itself unusual (English "sight" carries the same range without confusion), but troublesome because no single English word covers all three. Aristotle himself shifts between *phantasia*, *phantasma* (the product), and *phantastikon* (the capacity, DA 432a31). The active sense *eidolopoiein* (DA 427b18–20), in which we create appearances at will, is ruled out at 428a2 as *kata metaphoran*: it never recurs in De Anima, fails the soon-to-come definition of *phantasia* as motion caused by perception, and does not suit the cognitive ascription Aristotle wants to make. Frede settles on the passive/medial sense — "appearance" in a wide sense — as the central meaning. She then surveys what she calls the "untidy-genius syndrome" of DA III.3: repetitions and inconsistencies abound; the definition itself is postponed to the chapter's last section (428b10ff); criteria first introduced to distinguish perception, thought, and *phantasia* are circumstantial and soon modified or contradicted. Sense-perception is first said to be always true (with *phantasia* usually false, 428a11) and then only "specific" perceptions are nearly always true while perceptions of common and accidental objects can fail (428b18ff). *Phantasia* is first separated from sense-perception and thought (427b14), then subsumed under *noein* as the counterpart of *hupolepsis* (427b27). It is first "up to us," then not (dreams aren't); first opposed to truth-functional cognition, then itself capable of truth or falsity.

What surprises Frede more than the inconsistencies is Aristotle's methodological reversal: in III.3 he spends time pointing out what perception, *phantasia*, and thought have in common, rather than distinguishing them by their specific objects as his book-II methodology (415a20; 418a7ff; EN 1139a8–11) would dictate. The explanation, on Frede's reading, is that Aristotle wants to lead the reader to agree that *phantasia* must differ from sense-perception precisely because his final definition does not give it a separate faculty or separate objects. The definition is: *phantasia* is a motion (*kinesis*) that does not happen without sense-perception but comes to be as the result of sense-perception's active operation, and is *like* perception (DA 428b11–15; 429a1; *Somn. Vig.* 459a17). Frede's gloss: *phantasia* has no faculty of its own; it is "parasitic" on sense-perception. The truth-value of phantasiai tracks the truth-conditions of their causally underlying perceptions: phantasiai following perceptions of the proper sensible (*idion*) are true as long as the perception lasts but can falsify once the perception is over; phantasiai following perceptions of common and accidental objects can be false even alongside the perception (428b25–30). On this account, phantasiai look like mere epiphenomena, lingering after-images.

**Section II, "*Phantasia* as Synthesizer"** (pp. 282–286), develops the first cognitive function. Perception (DA II.12, 424a17: the sense-organ receives perceptible form without matter, as wax takes the seal without iron) is narrowly confined to its present object (417a24–8; 425b6ff; APo. 87b28–39); Aristotle has no panoramic field of vision. When one's eyes glide over the books on a shelf, there is only piecemeal vision of this or that coloured object; the overall impression of all the books (including those behind one's back) is already a *phantasia* — a synthesis of what one perceives now and what one perceived a moment ago. Frede invokes Kant's house example (*Critique of Pure Reason* A 190ff): the synthesis-of-the-manifold motif is Kantian. The "common sense" (*koine aisthesis*, DA 426b17–29) might seem the natural locus, but Frede follows Kahn (1966) in denying it is a faculty above the senses: it is only their *centre*. What lingers when the eye averts is already a *phantasia*. This explains 425b23 ("perceptions and imaginations") and 428b27 (we have phantasiai while perception is still in operation). The simultaneity is necessary both for causal continuity (else an unbridgeable gap between perception and after-image) and for the coherence of perception itself.

## § The Decay and Persistence of *Phantasmata*

Phantasiai, once separated from their origin, take on a life of their own. They linger (*to emmenein*, DA 429a4; *Insomn.* 460b1–3), change in quality and truth-value, and decay (*Somn. Vig.* 459b12ff: after-images of the sun pass from yellow to crimson to purple to black). Aristotle explains image-reoccurrence by reference to continuing motion in inner sense and sense-organs, motion sometimes unperceived because concentration is elsewhere (459b9) — Frede infers, strikingly, that we are always full of such unperceived motions, a sub-threshold reservoir of latent imagery. Memory is distinguished from this drift: per *De Memoria*, memory is remembering a past experience *qua* past, with the associative *prosaisthanesthai hoti proteron* (450a21ff). Phantasmata need not correspond precisely to their object (*Insomn.* 460b6) and are not confined to vision (we have phantasmata from hearing, smell, touch, taste, though no verb covers them all). Severed from origins, phantasiai give us coherent pictures transcending immediate perception, allow change-over-time, but with no faculty keeping them in order they drift, dream, delude. This is precisely why Aristotle declines to make *phantastike* a separate faculty.

## § "Incorrigible" Appearances: the Size of the Sun

A specific case sharpens the limits of *phantasia*'s self-correction. The sun appears a foot wide; it is in fact enormous. *Insomn.* 458b28 records the resistance: even when persons are in excellent health and know the facts of the case perfectly well, the sun nevertheless appears to them to be a foot wide. The appearance is incorrigible. In such cases, where sense itself cannot decide, reason must find other means (DA 428b3). The example carries two pieces of Frede's argument: (a) phantasiai can be "recalcitrant" — they resist information from reason; they are "phenomena in their own right," not mere passive substrates that the intellect can simply override; (b) the size-comparison this example invites (comparing the sun with tree-tops or chimney-pots) is itself a kind of comparative seeing, a phantasia-function that estimates dimensions where bare perception cannot. The same point clarifies *De Sensu* 448a13 (*all' ou phainetai hosa estin*): one sees the sun or a four-cubit rod but not how large they are; estimating size is what *phantasia* contributes as a kind of standardized vision spanning beyond the immediate sense-report. *Phantasia* is in this respect the counterpart of *doxa* (*Insomn.* 462a1: *ou monon phaneitai alla kai dokei*), giving us a sensory representation of states of affairs that goes beyond the simultaneous reports of the senses.

## § *Phantasia* and the Denial of Intellectual Memory

Section III ("Thought and the Objects of Sense-Perception," pp. 287–295) takes up *phantasia*'s second function: bridging the intellect to sensible objects. Aristotle's initial sharp dichotomy at DA 431b20–432a2 — intellect (*nous*) receives intelligible forms without matter; sense-perception receives perceptible forms — is later softened at 432a3ff: we only get to know intelligibles through sensibles. *Phantasia* establishes the connection. The need is twofold.

For **practical reasoning**, action requires envisaging something as good or bad, to be pursued or avoided. The necessary condition is that the soul possess certain phantasmata (DA 431a14–17): the image of a future good or bad (433b12, 28). Frede's example, vivid and modern: her geometric mind tells her she can pass along any path wide enough for her two feet; but the depiction of walking over a plank stretched between the towers of the World Trade Center tells her this is an absolute *pheukton*, a thing to avoid. Geometric reasoning alone does not stir us; the image does. Aristotle himself puts it: thinking of something terrible alone does not move us; we have to envisage the phenomenon itself (DA 432b29). The beacon-example: when we see fire and that it moves, we realize the enemy is there (perception and calculation do the job); but the soul also calculates through images and thoughts "as if seeing" (*hosper horon*, 431b7), deliberating about the future from the present. Aristotle distinguishes a *bouleutike* or *logistike phantasia* (433b29, 434a5–7) — calculative/deliberative imagination in human decision-making — from non-rational *phantasia* shared with animals; even worms have *phantasia* "indefinitely," needing some notion of an aim in their food-search (433b31ff).

For **theoretical reasoning**, *phantasia* is no less indispensable. Aristotle concedes at 432a3–9 that intelligible objects of all thought, even in the abstract sciences, are contained in sensible objects (*ta te en aphairesei legomena kai hosa ton aistheton hexeis kai pathe*). The general conclusion is that no one could get to know or understand things without sense-perception (*oute me aisthanomenos methen outhen an mathoi oude xunheie*). More importantly, one cannot even contemplate (*theorein*) without an image: *hotan te theorei, ananke hama phantasma ti theorein* (432a8–9). Images, "like sense-perceptions but without (sensible) matter," provide the substrate of all thought; reason "thinks the forms in the images" (431b2). Frede's Kantian comparison (B 154): like Kant, Aristotle wants to say that we cannot think of a line without drawing one in our mind. Even when Aristotle denies the identity of objects of thought and objects of imagination (432a12–14), he still insists that whenever we think of the form of something we have something like a *Gestalt* of it in mind.

Frede does not turn Aristotle into a *Gestalt* psychologist, but the basic idea must be that comprehension of formal definition alone is insufficient. Thinking of a house: it is not enough to think "a shelter against destruction"; of a circle: not enough as "the locus of points equidistant from a centre." Without *Gestalt* we could not recognize an exemplar when we met one, nor perform geometric constructions, which depend on seeing relevant relationships. Phantasmata are flexible (sense-perceptions are not); they can be enriched by repeated observations; they supply the "standardized picture" needed for generalization. Their inaccuracy is, paradoxically, their advantage (cf. Freudenthal 1863: phantasmata as "*Verallgemeinerung durch Denkthätigkeit*," generalization through thinking-activity). The leopard-spots example: the zoologist studying leopards needs not just the view of this or that leopard but a generalized image of their spottedness before he can do the more abstract business of his science. Phantasmata also figure in decision-making, where Aristotle mentions a "merger" of different phantasiai into one image allowing comparison of the relative goodness of possible ends (434a9), and in science, as the substrate of *epagoge*. Frede reads *APo*. II.19 — the *locus classicus* on the ascent from sense-perception to *nous* of first principles — as implicitly governed by phantasia: only retained perceptions (animals possessing *mone*) yield memory and experience (99b36–100b9); the *aisthesis* that "produces the universal" (100b4–5 *kai gar he aisthesis houto to katholou empoiei*) is *aisthesis* in the wider sense, i.e. phantasia.

A central Fredean move here, against Modrak, is the **denial that Aristotle admits intellectual memory**. Aristotle locates memory exclusively in the sensitive faculty. Knowledge is "remembered" only accidentally (*Mem.* 450a12–14): we remember *when* and *how* we first learned Pythagoras' theorem, not the theorem itself. The same exclusion governs dreams: though Aristotle grants that we have opinions in dreams (*Insomn.* 458b10), he attributes dreams exclusively to the *aisthetikon* "in so far as it is *phantastikon*" (459a8). This leaves the status of opinions in dreams unexplained, but it signals Aristotle's commitment: psychic phenomena must be assigned either to the sensible domain or the intelligible domain; no real fusion of the two domains was conceivable to him. Memory and dream both belong, in the end, to the sensible side. The decision is not casual; it is structural for Aristotle's philosophy of mind.

## § Comparison with Nussbaum 1985 and White 1985

Frede positions herself centrally between the cluster's two 1985 forerunners (PHX-01 Nussbaum, "The Role of *Phantasia* in Aristotle's Explanation of Action," and PHX-02 White, "The Meaning of *Phantasia* in Aristotle's *De Anima*, III, 3–8"), though her engagement with each is asymmetric.

With **Nussbaum (PHX-01)**, Frede is explicit and critical. She cites Nussbaum's 1978 *De Motu Animalium* repeatedly (footnotes 5, 23, 34) and accuses her of broadening *phantasia* to encompass all phenomena admitting interpretation in the wider sense ("envisaging the good"; Nussbaum 1978 p. 263) — a broadening that, on Frede's reading, severs phantasia's ties to envisaging-with-perception and fails to explain why Aristotle assigns *phantasia* to *aisthesis* at all. Frede's counter-thesis — *phantasia* as supervenient phenomenon, parasitic on perception, with no faculty of its own — is a corrective. (Frede's 1978 citation captures most of Nussbaum's 1985 PHX-01 essay, since the 1985 piece is the 2nd-edition essay in *De Motu* and substantively overlaps.) The relation is **contests-reading-of**: Frede preserves phantasia's cognitive load (synthesizer, bridge) but resists Nussbaum's expansive de-perceptualization.

With **White (PHX-02)**, Frede has no engagement. White 1985 is not in her bibliography. The non-citation is informative: White appeared in *Dialogue: Canadian Philosophical Review* the same year Nussbaum-Rorty was assembling drafts, and the two essays were produced in mutual independence in different scholarly networks (White in Thomistic close-reading; Frede in analytic philosophy of mind). Substantively, Frede and White **converge** on the supervenience thesis: both deny *phantasia* is a separate faculty; both emphasize its derivative status on *aisthesis*; both invoke the *De Insomniis* formula (*phantastikon* same as *aisthetikon* but different in being); both stress the *kinesis-hypo-aistheseos* definition (428b10–11); both treat *phantasia* as preserving sensible forms after the object's departure. They **diverge** in framework: White's Thomistic-humanist trajectory culminates in *phantasia* as a "familiar interior landscape" orienting man in the cosmos; Frede's analytic framework culminates in anti-Modrak polemic and defence of Aristotelian dualism. White reads *phantasia* teleologically toward *nous*; Frede reads it functionally as a bounded sensory link.

The **cluster's triangle**: Nussbaum (1985, expansive practical-rational), White (1985, Thomistic-derivative reading of *phantasia* as preserved sensible forms in service of *nous*), Frede (1992, minimal supervenience reading with two cognitive functions and dualist boundary). Frede contests Nussbaum explicitly and converges with White independently; the 1985–1992 trajectory shows independent convergence on supervenience from two methodological starting-points, with Nussbaum as the dissenting target.

A note on the deeper polemic: Frede's primary antagonist is **Deborah Modrak** (1986, 1987a), whose unified-phantasia and unified-consciousness readings dominate her footnotes (5, 10, 42, 44, 48). Modrak is not in the PHX-01–06 selection but frames Frede's intervention; Frede's dualism-defense is calibrated against Modrak's fusionism. Frede also acknowledges **Wedin (1988)** in her closing footnote as reaching similar but more technically refined results. The four-way cluster-adjacent debate of the early 1990s — Nussbaum (expansive), Modrak (fusionist), Wedin (technical reconstructor), Frede (deflationary dualist) — sets the terrain Caston (PHX-04) will recalibrate.

## § Cross-Pipeline Bridges

(1) **Heidegger GA 18 / *pathos*.** Frede's incorrigible-sun example and her insistence that phantasiai are phenomena in their own right resisting rational correction connect to Heidegger's reading of *pathos* and *Befindlichkeit* as primordial disclosures not reducible to belief. (2) **A₃ *phantasma* / resonant *kinēsis*.** Frede's reading of *phantasia* as a kinēsis with a residual life after perception ceases aligns with the phantasma-as-resonant-motion thematic; her *Somn. Vig.* 459b5ff reference (we are always full of unperceived motions) supplies an Aristotelian warrant for sub-threshold resonance. (3) **BCAP and active-intellect.** Frede's preservation of Aristotelian dualism — and her argument that Aristotle declines fusion to protect the link between *nous* and the divine pure active mind (DA 408b18–29; Metaph. 983a6–7) — supplies a load-bearing premise for any active-intellect reading preserving transcendence. (4) ***Aisthesis* / inner-sense (Kahn, Caston).** Frede's confinement of any Aristotelian unity-of-consciousness to the inner sense (with Kahn 1966) marks a boundary condition Caston (PHX-04, 1995) will revisit.
