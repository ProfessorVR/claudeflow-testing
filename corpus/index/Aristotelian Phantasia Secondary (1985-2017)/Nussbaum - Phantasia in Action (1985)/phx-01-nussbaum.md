## § Overview and Nussbaum's Interpretive Thesis

Nussbaum's 1985 essay, originally drafted as part of her 1975 Harvard PhD thesis under G. E. L. Owen and incorporated as Essay 5 of her landmark *De Motu Animalium* edition (Princeton 1978; 2nd ed. 1985), is the founding text of the modern Anglophone debate over *phantasia* in Aristotle. Every later phantasia-cluster author engaged in the present index — Stephen White (1985), Dorothea Frede (1992), Victor Caston (1996, 1998), Daniel O'Gorman (2005), Francisco Gonzalez, Debra Hawhee, and Eleni Papachristou — argues in conversation with the interpretive program Nussbaum lays down here. Its 49-page argument constitutes the cluster's longest single article and its principal axis of dispute.

The interpretive thesis, stripped of its lexical and exegetical scaffolding, is this. *Phantasia* in Aristotle is not (primarily) the faculty of internal image-having. It is **the interpretive aspect of perception** — the faculty in virtue of which an animal sees an object *as* an object of a certain sort, where this object-*as-F* awareness is the formal content that can move the animal to act. To say that *phainetai F* to X is to say that X sees the thing *as F*. There is no innocent eye in perception: reception and interpretation are thoroughly interdependent. *Phantasia* is, by approximate analogy with Wittgenstein's and Ishiguro's "seeing-as," the cognitive activity that supplies the *qua*-structure of object-awareness on which both desire and discursive thought subsequently operate.

The essay's argumentative shape is dialectical-philological. Nussbaum (i) diagnoses the one-sidedness of prior scholarship, which has privileged the few "decaying-sense" passages of *DA* III.3 and the *Parva Naturalia* over the broader role *phantasia* plays in *DA* III.9-11 and *De Motu Animalium*; (ii) mounts a multi-part attack on the "image-theory" attributed to Aristotle by Ross, Hicks, Hamlyn, Rees, Freudenthal, and Sorabji, drawing on Wittgenstein, Sartre, Ryle, Dennett, Goodman, and Ishiguro; (iii) reconstructs Aristotle's positive theory from the lexical anchor of *phainesthai* and from the action-passages of *MA* and *DA* III, with the *DA* II.12 wax/signet analysis of *aisthesis* as the key contrastive backdrop; and (iv) closes with the strong epistemic claim of *DA* III.8 432a3-10 that all thought — practical and theoretical — is accompanied by some *phantasma*-vehicle. The closing aphorism crystallizes the program: "Learning to perceive, as Aristotle tells us, is learning to make distinctions" (p.269 / PDF 59).

## § Section-by-Section Synthesis

### Introduction (PDF 11-21 / book 221-231)

The essay opens, after an epigraph from Henry James's *The Portrait of a Lady* spoken by Isabel Archer, with a diagnosis: studies of Aristotle's theory of *phantasia* have been one-sided. Critics — Nussbaum cites Freudenthal's famous declaration that the action passages do not deserve full treatment — have used *DA* III.3 and the *Parva Naturalia* as their privileged texts, leaving the action-explanatory function of *phantasia* in *DA* III.9-11 and *MA* 6-8 as parasitic and of lesser philosophical interest (p.221 / PDF 11). Yet the textual evidence shows that *phantasia* and *orexis* are jointly necessary for animal motion (*DA* 432b15), that desire requires *phantasia* (*DA* 433b26-30), and that *phantasia* "prepares" the desire before desire can issue in action (*MA* 8, 702a18 ff.). To privilege the few decaying-sense passages over these is methodologically unsound.

A second prevalent assumption is targeted: that *phantasmata* in Aristotle are always mental images representing by similarity, that thinking always requires such internal objects as raw material. Nussbaum names the dominant statements of this view in Sorabji's *Aristotle on Memory*, in Hamlyn's note on *DA* 431a8, and in Hicks's notes on 425a11 and 433b25 (p.222 / PDF 12). She formulates the position as two basic claims: (1) imagination always involves images that represent in virtue of similarity, and (2) imagination always involves the having of an image plus its inspection (p.224 / PDF 14).

Nussbaum then mounts a four-part attack on the image view, drawing heavily on Nelson Goodman's *Languages of Art*. (i) The location-objection to images is dismissed as no worse than the analogous problem about the location of sensations under physicalism (p.225 / PDF 15). (ii) The similarity criterion for representation is exposed as false: an object closely resembles, but does not represent, a copy of itself; reference is secured by convention not similarity; Picasso's reply concerning his portrait of Gertrude Stein ("No matter; it will," p.227 / PDF 17) anchors the anti-similarity thesis. (iii) The image-theory cannot accommodate fictional and null-denoting imaginings without ad hoc supplementation (p.228 / PDF 18). (iv) The image-theory cannot do justice to the auditory, olfactory, and tactile imagining it persistently ignores, and even where pictorial imagining occurs the image is rarely "dense" in Goodman's sense — Sartre's claim that imagining yields only what one intentionally puts in is too rigid as a universal claim but illuminating for the typical case (pp.228-229 / PDF 18-19).

The constructive turn introduces Wittgenstein's "seeing an aspect" and Ishiguro's elaboration of imagining as a species of seeing-as (p.230 / PDF 20). What *phainetai F* to someone is, after all, what is *seen by him as F*; his *phainomenon agathon* is his view of the good (with Owen's "*Tithenai ta phainomena*" cited as the methodological anchor). Aristotle's interest is in how things in the world appear to sentient beings, and this very general interest underwrites his more specialized treatments of envisaging and of motivational awareness alike (p.231 / PDF 21). The program: find images only where there is concrete evidence of their presence.

### §1 *Phantasia* in the accounts of action (PDF 22-30 / book 232-240)

The first interpretive section surveys the action passages systematically. *MA* 6 opens by listing *phantasia* among the cognitive faculties relevant to motion-explanation: *phantasia*, *aisthesis*, and *nous*/*dianoia* are the *kritika* (distinction-making faculties), set against the *orektika* (desiderative faculties). The text at *MA* 700b17 ff. (PDF 22) presents these as a disjunction: any one of the three *kritika*, paired with *orexis*, can produce motion. *MA* 701a29-32 confirms this with the famous drink-syllogism: "'Here's drink,' says sense-perception or *phantasia* or thought." On this minimal reading, *phantasia* is simply one option among three for presenting the perceived or thought object.

But further on in the same discussion, at *MA* 702a18 ff., Aristotle offers a more careful and ordered account: "the affections suitably prepare the organic parts, desire the affections, and *phantasia* the desire; and *phantasia* comes about either through thought or through sense-perception" (p.233 / PDF 23). On this revised account *phantasia* has an umbrella role: it must "prepare" desire in every case, whether actual perceiving is going on or not. The parallel *DA* passages confirm this elevated role: 432b15 (motion always with both *phantasia* and *orexis*), 433a20 (when *phantasia* imparts motion it does so with *orexis*), 433b26-30 (no desire without *phantasia*; all *phantasia* is connected either with thinking or with perception).

*DA* III.11 introduces the two species of *phantasia*: the *bouleutike* (deliberative/calculative), peculiar to rational creatures, and the perceptual, shared with non-rational animals. The "incomplete creatures" of III.11 — animals who move but lack developed reason — are admitted to possess some indistinct *phantasia* because they do, in fact, move (p.234 / PDF 24).

A perplexing puzzle now emerges: at *DA* 433a31 Aristotle says the *einai* of *phantasia* is different from the *einai* of any other faculty, but *phantasia* may still be "the same as" some other faculty; the *De Insomniis* 459a15 specifies that it is the same as the faculty of *aisthesis* but different in *einai*. Nussbaum analyzes the *einai*-formula by aggregating Aristotle's analogous usages: *Metaph.* XI.10 1066a33 (the road up and the road down are one but different in *einai*); *DA* 424a25 and 425b25 on the actuality of *aisthesis* and its object; *Mem.* 450b21 (the painted animal qua likeness vs. qua animal); *EN* V 1130a12 (*arete* is the disposition *simpliciter*, *dikaiosune* its name in its outward-facing aspect); *EN* VI 1143b23 ff. (*phronesis* and *politike* — same *hexis* under different aspects). She concludes for a two-description reading: same capacity (*dunamis*), two aspects. The fact that Aristotle uses the capacity-names *aisthetikon* and *phantastikon* (not the activity-names *aisthesis* and *phantasia*) supports this — Aristotle says two *capacities* are the same, not that two activities derive from one capacity (p.235 / PDF 25).

Nussbaum then assembles the evidence that thinking too involves *phantasia*. *DA* I.1 403a3-10 (PDF 27) treats thinking as the only candidate activity peculiar to the soul, but immediately concedes that if it is some kind of *phantasia* or *not without phantasia*, it cannot occur without body. The *MA* passages 701a5-7, 701a34-36, 701b17-21 (PDF 28) link *noesis* and *phantasia* in physiological-alteration accounts. *DA* III.7 431a14-17 gives the canonical statement: "To the thinking soul, *phantasmata* serve as *aisthemata*; and when it asserts or denies good or bad, it avoids or pursues it. Hence the soul never thinks without a *phantasma*" (p.239 / PDF 29). *DA* 431b2 ff. continues: the thinking creature thinks the forms in the *phantasmata*. *DA* III.8 432a3-10 (PDF 30) generalizes: "the objects of thought are in the perceptible forms... when one reflects, one must reflect simultaneously with a *phantasma*. For *phantasmata* are like *aisthemata*, only without matter." This last lemma is the linchpin for everything that follows.

### §2 The use and range of *phantasia* (PDF 31-44 / book 241-254)

The second section is a lexical-historical archaeology of the *phantasia*-family. Beginning from the earliest attestations, Nussbaum shows that *phantasma* and the verb *phantazesthai* function in pre-Aristotelian Greek primarily as relatives of *phainesthai* — meaning "what appears" — not as technical terms for representational images. The standard image-words are *eikon*, *eidolon*, and *enupnion* (p.241 / PDF 31).

A series of Platonic passages illustrates: *Rep.* 596e-599a contrasts *phainomena ouk onta* with *phantasmata ouk onta*, the image being called not *phantasma* but *eidolon* (601b); *Parm.* 166a4 has *phantasma* meaning "appearance" parallel to *doxa* relative to *doxazetai*; *Theaet.* 152b-c uses *phantasia* in its earliest extant Platonic occurrence to mean "appearing" (correlated to *phainetai* and *aisthesis*); *Soph.* 235d-236b explicitly contrasts the *eikastike techne* (which makes accurate likenesses) with the *phantastike* (which produces what *phainetai* but is not *eoike*); *Symp.* 211a uses *phantazesthai* non-sensorily of the Form of Beauty's appearing; *Phileb.* 38c uses *phantazomenon* equivalently to *phainetai*; *Phileb.* 34a-b gives a decaying-sense account of memory using *kinesis* and *soteria aistheseos*, not *phantasia* (pp.242-243 / PDF 32-33). Apparition-uses occur (Aeschylus *Agam.* 1500, *Septem* 710, Eur. *Hec.* 54, 94, 390, Hdt. 4.124, Pl. *Phd.* 81d, *Rep.* 380d, 382c — the earliest known use of *phantasia*) but do not lexicalize *phantasma* as image (p.244 / PDF 34).

Aristotle's own broad usage is then surveyed. *EN* III 1114a32: all men strive for the *phainomenon agathon*; no one controls the *phantasia* — the way the end appears to one depends on what sort of person one is. *EE* 1235b25-29: pleasure is desired as *phainomenon agathon*; *phantasia* and *doxa* are not in the same part of the soul. *Insomn.* 460b19: the sun *phainetai* a foot wide. *DA* 402b22-24: "when we are able to give an account of the incidental attributes *kata ten phantasian*..." (p.245 / PDF 35).

A characteristic Nussbaumian move follows. Bonitz, in his *Index Aristotelicus*, distinguishes multiple senses of *phantasia* in Aristotle; Nussbaum unifies. *Phantasia* for Aristotle is as wide-ranging as the *phainomena*, which (per Owen) embraces far more than perception-data. The animal who *phantasizes* does not just perceive an object; he perceives it *as* a thing of a certain sort, a thing that can become an object of pursuit or avoidance (p.246 / PDF 36).

Nussbaum then concedes the imagistic occurrences honestly. The *PN* memory and dream accounts use likeness-language: *Mem.* 450a25-30 (*zographema*), 451a15 (*phantasma* as memory-image regarded as a likeness), *Insomn.* 461b29 ff. (dream-image likened to cloud-form residue of *aisthema*) (PDF 39). She argues that even here the imagistic commitment is constrained. (i) *Mem.* 450a25-30 says the image regarded in isolation from what it denotes is either a *theorema* or a *phantasma* — the *theorema* is unlikely to be pictorial. (ii) At 451a1 a *noema* is contrasted with a *mnemoneuma* (thought regarded as likeness). (iii) No informational density is implied. (iv) *MA* 702a5 already revises the *Mem.* theory: memory is awareness of the pleasant and painful things as *eidola* — shadows, things no longer extant — bypassing imaging entirely (PDF 41).

The crux of Section 2 is the rereading of *DA* 428a1-4 — the controversial "*kata metaphoran*" clause at the head of *DA* III.3: "If *phantasia* is that in virtue of which some *phantasma* comes-to-be for us, and not what we call *phantasia* using a transferred sense (*kata metaphoran*), it is one of those capacities or dispositions in virtue of which we make distinctions, and tell truth or falsehood" (p.252 / PDF 42). Standardly read as Aristotle's official restriction to image-cases, Nussbaum demurs on three grounds. First, *phantasma* need not mean pictorial image. Second, in the immediately ensuing discussion (428a7, a14, b2-3) and elsewhere in *Insomn.*, Aristotle continues to play on the *phainetai*-connection — the supposedly excluded broad usage is in fact used. Third — the decisive philological point — Aristotle's use of "*kata metaphoran*" elsewhere consistently marks a *transferred or extended sense* away from ordinary literal usage, not a popular usage from which the technical sense is being distinguished. The catalog: musical *oxu* and *baru* of pitch (*DA* 420a29); mathematical *dunamis* (*Metaph.* 1019b33); loose *andreios* (*EN* 1115a15); horns *kata metaphoran* (*HA* 500a3, *PA* 662b25) (p.253 / PDF 43).

The constructive alternative: there *is* a usage of *phantasia* that would much more plausibly be described as transferred — *phantasia* meaning "(mere) show, pomp, ostentatiousness," attested first as *phantazesthai* in Herodotus VII.10.5, frequent in Polybius, and at least once in Aristotle himself in the *Rhetoric*: "But all these (sc. points of diction) are *phantasia*, and aimed at the audience" (*Rhet.* 1404a11, p.254 / PDF 44). Aristotle's 428a1-4 is thus the bracketing of the ostentation-sense, not the bracketing of *phainetai*-uses.

### §3 *Phantasia* and *aisthesis* (PDF 45-50 / book 255-260)

Section 3 develops the positive constructive thesis. The starting question: why does Aristotle in the action contexts say *aisthesis* alone is insufficient — that it must "prepare" a *phantasia* which in turn prepares the desire? The answer is sought in Aristotle's theory of *aisthesis* itself, particularly *DA* II.12.

*DA* II.12 424a18-19 describes *aisthesis* as "what receives perceptible forms without the matter," compared to wax receiving the impress of a metal signet-ring without receiving the metal itself. Plants (424a33 ff.) are contrasted: they are only affected when they absorb the matter of what affects them. *Aisthesis* on this picture plays an entirely passive role: receiving but not selecting. Crucially, what *aisthesis* receives are sensible *qualities*, not substances under sortal descriptions: "*aisthesis* is affected by the things that have color or taste or sound, but not insofar as they are what each of them is spoken of as being, but insofar as they possess a certain quality" (424a21 ff., p.258 / PDF 48).

As Hicks himself notes (here Nussbaum turns Hicks against the image-theorists), when we perceive a rose by sight, it is not the rose qua rose, but the rose qua white that acts upon our sight. But to be moved to action an animal must become aware of something qua what-it-is-called: he must see the man as a man, not just as pale. The forms presented by *phantasia* — the "forms of the pleasant or painful" (*MA* 701b21, 703b19-20) — are exactly such object-as-F sortal contents. This is what *DA* 432a9-10 means by saying *phantasmata* are like *aisthemata* "only without matter": *aisthesis* takes the material impress of a particular, *phantasia* sees its form, *qua* belonging to a certain class (p.257 n.52 / PDF 47). With Strawson and Wiggins on sortal vs. characterizing universals quietly in the background, Nussbaum constructs Aristotle as the proto-theorist of *qua*-perception.

The pivotal text of Section 3, however, is *DA* II.8 420b31 ff. — Aristotle's account of voice. "That which does the striking must be a living creature, and must be with some *phantasia*; for voice (*phone*) is a noise that is indicative (*semantikos*) of something" (PDF 49). Hicks predictably reads this as requiring an internal image alongside the perception. Nussbaum rejects the reading as bizarre — voice would then be *semantikos* not of the world but of the mental picture. Her alternative: an animal's utterance conveys something only if it is *about* something — only if the animal is aware of something in the world *as F* in virtue of the faculty of *phantasia*. *Phantasia* is thus the faculty of intentional content, of object-directed semantic awareness. The *De Interpretatione* I claim that vocal sounds are *sumbola* of *pathemata*, and that the *pathemata* are *homoiomata* of actual states, is glossed accordingly: the talk of likeness pertains to the passivity of *aisthesis*, not to *phantasia* (p.260 n.60 / PDF 50).

Section 3 closes with the load-bearing formula: the claim that *aisthesis* and *phantasia* are "the same faculty" amounts to the contention that reception and interpretation are not separable but thoroughly interdependent. **"There is no receptive 'innocent eye' in perception"** (p.261 / PDF 51). How something *phainetai* to me is bound up with my past, my prejudices, and my needs. *Aisthesis* presents uninterpreted colors and sounds at the quality-level; *phantasia* is required for object-perception, for the *qua*-structure that makes the world articulable for action.

### §4 *Phantasia* and *orexis* (PDF 51-54 / book 261-264)

Section 4 specifies the desire-side of the picture. *Phantasia* presents the animal with awareness of an object that may prove desirable; it can serve both to present the absent goal initially (the thirsty agent's *phantasia* of drink) and, later, to specify the present situation as the desired thing (the water-fountain seen as drink). The practical-delusion case: "Poor dog, he saw that as water (or as drink) when it was really ammonia solution." The role of *phantasia* in the practical case parallels its role in the non-practical (the deceived perceiver who sees the sun as a foot wide); the difference lies only in the role played by desire (PDF 51).

Nussbaum resists treating *phantasia* as a behavioral disposition. It is a psychological process for which a token-physiological description is in principle available, so the *phantasia* could in principle be specified even where no desire (and no behavior) followed. Hence *phantasiai* can be used to *explain* action, not merely redescribe it (p.262 / PDF 52). She marks the standard distinction-from-*doxa*: *DA* 428a21-23 denies animals *doxa* because they lack *pistis* (conviction grounded in *logos* and experience).

The deliberative species of *phantasia* is then analyzed. *DA* III.11 433b28 distinguishes the deliberative/calculative from the perceptual; 434a6 confirms the perceptual species belongs to other animals as well. The crucial passage 434a7-10 (p.263 / PDF 53): "For whether he will do this or this is already the job of reasoning. And he must measure using a single criterion; for he seeks the greater good. So he is able to make one *phantasma* out of many."

Ross reads this collage-of-pictures style: the deliberator combines elements from different mental pictures to construct a composite picture. Nussbaum rejects the reading: it implies that whatever criterion is selected (goodness, beauty) must be susceptible of pictorial representation — palpably false for goodness. Her positive interpretation: the rational creature, unlike the animal who follows one *phantasia* after another in sequence, can (i) see consequences as following from an action (joining two appearings into a unified one), and (ii) measure consequence-sets against a single criterion of desirability. The discrete considerations "this or that" become the unity "this rather than that." This is the substrate of *phantasia ek syllogismou* (*DA* 434a11) — *phantasia* "from inference" — which only humans possess (p.264 / PDF 54). The cloak-example from *MA* 7 ("I want X but to have X I must get Y first") exemplifies the chain-reasoning version.

Section 4 concedes that *DA* III.7 431b6-8 does admit picturing language ("when one calculates, using the *phantasmata* or *noemata* in the soul, as if one saw the things, and plans for the future in view of the present"). But the inclusion of *noemata* alongside *phantasmata* shows Aristotle is not committing himself to picture-like internal objects across the board. Deliberation may often involve some sort of envisaging; it need not be reducible to picturing.

### §5 *Phantasia* and thinking (PDF 55-59 / book 265-269)

The final section confronts the strongest version of Aristotle's claim: *DA* III.8's blanket assertion that *all* thought — theoretical as well as practical — is accompanied by some activity of *phantasia*. The passage 432a3-10 has already been quoted in §1; here Nussbaum gives it sustained interpretation.

The reading: there is no thinking that is simply abstract. Every thought has some concrete symbolic vehicle — an example, a logical formalization, a token act of saying-the-words to oneself. One does not simply think of the Principle of Non-Contradiction in abstract purity; one symbolizes it via some imagining-an-instance, some formal notation, or some episode of saying. This is, Nussbaum argues, empirically true insofar as it goes; it might be defeated by the discovery of beings who think in purely abstract fashion. "God would not require *phantasia*" (p.266 / PDF 56).

*Mem.* 449b31 ff., the triangle example, illustrates the typical case: thinking the mathematical abstraction Triangle is accompanied by envisaging a particular triangle of definite size — "unnecessary information" from the abstract standpoint, but functionally inseparable from the act of thinking.

Two worries are voiced. First, the triangle example slips into picturing-as-similarity language (Aristotle says we "place before our eyes" a triangle of definite size) — suggesting Aristotle's exposition flirts with the very image-theory Nussbaum has been resisting. The defense: Aristotle is at most taking picturing as his central case, not as exhaustive; his trouble at the end of III.8 in distinguishing *phantasmata* from *prota noemata* (the *asuntheta* of *Metaph.* IX.10) suggests he is not deliberately restricting the account to the pictorial (p.267 / PDF 57). Second, the overcomplexity worry: if my judgment that the sun is large must itself be accompanied by *phantasia* (to be physiologically realized and motion-causally efficacious), we end up multiplying *phantasiai* per cognitive episode. The functionalist solution — making thinking a functional state of matter — is blocked by *DA* III.5's commitment to *nous* as separable (the worry connects to *De Interpretatione* 9 on freedom and determinism, p.267 n.69 / PDF 58).

The closing synthesis (PDF 58-59) is the essay's most quoted: "The basic insight underlying the theory is the important one that perceptual reception is inseparable from interpretation — that we cannot explain an animal's perception of something as a distinct feature in his environment with reference only to a process of receiving or imprinting, but must refer also to his interpretive activity, the activity in virtue of which the scene appears a certain way to him." As in scientific method the philosopher must begin with and return to the *phainomena* (not to "pure" or "raw" data), so in the explanation of action he must begin not with received unprocessed sensory material but with the way the world looks to the particular creature, what it sees things as. The same theory must explain practical and non-practical cases, because these differ only in the role of desire; the cognitive substrate is identical. "Learning to perceive, as Aristotle tells us, is learning to make distinctions" (*Metaph.* I.1 980b26-27, *Phys.* 184a24). Footnote 71 closes with an acknowledgement of Malcolm Schofield's parallel paper "Aristotle on the Imagination" — composed concurrently, reaching different conclusions, discussing different passages.

## § The *Phantasia*-as-Interpretation Argument

The interpretive thesis Nussbaum advances admits of several formulations operating at different argumentative levels, and it is worth distinguishing them.

**Lexical version.** *Phantasia* is etymologically the abstract noun corresponding to *phainesthai*. *Phantasma* is its verbal noun: "that which appears." Across pre-Aristotelian and Aristotelian usage these words function as relatives of the appearing-verb. To read "mental image" into them whenever they occur is to import a technical theory unsupported by ordinary lexicography. The image-theorist must defend a substantial extension of the ordinary lexical sense.

**Methodological version.** Aristotle's procedure throughout (per Owen's "*Tithenai ta phainomena*") is to begin from the *phainomena* — things as they appear to sentient observers — and to explain by reference to these, not to some unobservable underlying given. *Phantasia*, the faculty of *phainomena*-having, is therefore the cognitive locus of all explanatorily basic content. Action-theory and perception-theory must both begin here.

**Cognitive-architectural version.** *Aisthesis*, on Aristotle's *DA* II.12 account, is structurally passive — wax to the world's signet-ring. Its proper object is sensible quality, not substance. But action requires the agent to see his object *qua* what-it-is, *qua* a thing of a certain class; the rose *qua* rose, the drink *qua* drink, the man *qua* man. This sortal/categorial structuring cannot come from *aisthesis* on its own analysis. *Phantasia* supplies it. The "form without matter" of *DA* 432a9-10 is the formal sortal content that *phantasia* abstracts from the qualitative impress.

**Semantic version.** *Voice* (*phone*) is *semantikos*, meaning-bearing, only when issuing from a creature with *phantasia* (*DA* II.8 420b31 ff.). What makes utterance meaningful is not that the utterer is imaging but that he is *aware-of-something-as-F* — that his awareness has intentional content directable at an object under a description. *Phantasia* is the faculty of intentional content.

**Modal version.** Reception and interpretation are not separable in perceptual experience. There is no innocent eye, no uninterpreted given that could be the explanatory starting point for action. How something *phainetai* to one is bound up with one's past, prejudices, and needs. *Phantasia* is the term naming this interpretive permeation of perception by the agent's character, history, and orientation.

**Practical version.** What moves an animal to act is not the bare object but the object presented to it under a desire-relevant description. *Phantasia* is the faculty of such desire-relevant presentation: it presents *the pleasant* or *the painful*, *the to-be-pursued* or *the to-be-avoided* (per *MA* 702a18 ff. + *DA* 431b2). Practical motivation requires the object-*as-desirable*, which only *phantasia* (not *aisthesis* alone) can supply.

These versions are mutually reinforcing rather than disjoint. The interpretive thesis is, in effect, that all five describe one and the same Aristotelian innovation: the recognition that the *qua*-structure of perceived content is what makes perception cognitively productive, motivationally efficacious, and semantically articulable.

## § The Key Aristotle Loci

Nussbaum's evidential base is unusually broad. The principal *DA* loci: III.3 (the canonical chapter she destabilizes, esp. 428a1-4); III.7 431a14-17 (the *phantasmata*-as-*aisthemata* lemma); III.7 431b2 (the *horistai* clause); III.8 432a3-10 (the load-bearing "phantasmata like aisthemata only without matter" passage); III.9-11 (the action chapters); III.10 433a31 (same in capacity, different in *einai*); III.11 433b28-30 (the two species); 434a7-10 (the "one out of many" deliberative passage); 432b15 (motion always with phantasia + orexis); 433b26-30 (no desire without phantasia); II.8 420b31 ff. (the voice passage — Nussbaum's strongest single piece of constructive evidence); II.12 424a18-19 (the wax/signet analysis of aisthesis as receiving form without matter); II.12 424a21 ff. (sense-objects as qualities, not substances).

The *MA* loci: 6, 700b17 ff. (the *kritika* catalog); 7, 701a29-32 (the drink syllogism); 7, 701a33-35 (heating/chilling follows pursuit/avoidance phantasia); 7, 701b17-21 (noesis and phantasia have the power of actual things); 8, 702a5 (memory as awareness of eidola/shadows); 8, 702a18 ff. ## § The *Phantasmata* + Thinking Passage (*DA* III.8 432a3-10)

This passage is the keystone of Nussbaum's case for the umbrella role of *phantasia* in cognition, and her interpretation deserves separate treatment.

The text (in her translation, p.240 / PDF 30): "Since there is nothing that has separate existence, it seems, apart from perceptible magnitudes, the objects of thought are in the perceptible forms... and for this reason unless one perceived things one would not learn or understand anything; and when one reflects, one must reflect simultaneously with a *phantasma*. For *phantasmata* are like *aisthemata*, only without matter."

Three glosses operate on the passage. First, the *anti-Platonic* gloss: there are no separately existing intelligibles, so cognitive contents must be embedded in (drawn from, accompanied by, articulated through) perceptible forms. Second, the *empirical-developmental* gloss: a creature that never perceived would never learn — the *APo* I.18 thesis about induction from particulars is in play. Third, and decisive for Nussbaum, the *constitutive* gloss: every act of reflection is simultaneous with a *phantasma*. The *phantasma* is not merely a causal antecedent of thought but its concurrent accompaniment, its concrete symbolic vehicle.

The crux phrase is "without matter" (*aneu hyles*). Nussbaum (p.257 n.52 / PDF 47) reads this not as "non-material" in some ontological sense but as "without the passive impress-matter that *aisthesis* receives" — without the wax-stamp side of perception. The *phantasma* is the form (*eidos*) of what *aisthesis* received, abstracted from the impression-mechanism, available for sortal cognition. The *phantasma* is what makes the perceived form available as content under a description: form-without-matter = the *qua*-structure freed from the impress.

The cognitive consequence: every act of thinking is an act of operating on form-with-its-qua-structure. The triangle example of *Mem.* 449b31 ff. illustrates: the thought of Triangle (abstract) is accompanied by some envisaging-of-a-particular-triangle (the *phantasma*); the *phantasma* supplies the concrete *qua*-structured vehicle through which the abstract thought is articulated.

Nussbaum reads this as a deep insight rather than a defective theory. Empirical work since on "imageless thinking," symbol-grounding, and embodied cognition has tended to vindicate Aristotle: pure abstract thought without concrete symbolic vehicle is at best an idealization, more likely a fiction. "God would not require *phantasia*" — but no embodied cognizer can do without it.

## § Tensions Within the Essay

(i) Nussbaum admits that *DA* III.3 itself contains both the broad interpretation-friendly material and the closing decaying-sense paragraph. Her solution — let the broad scope govern the chapter and treat the closing paragraph as physiological hypothesis — is not without strain.

(ii) The two-description reading of phantasia/aisthesis identity (on the arete/dikaiosune model) is offered as preferable to the road-up/road-down model, but Nussbaum admits the textual evidence is mixed. *DA* II.5's more active *aisthesis* and the *aisthetikon* vs. *phantastikon* terminology pull in different directions.

(iii) The triangle example at *Mem.* 449b31 is pictorial in a way that strains Nussbaum's general anti-image program. She concedes Aristotle's exposition uses picture-language but argues against making it theoretically central.

(iv) The "extra phantasia" worry — that judgments opposing sensory *phantasia* must themselves be accompanied by *phantasia* — generates a regress that Aristotle never fully resolves. Nussbaum locates the block in *DA* III.5's commitment to separable *nous*.

(v) Aristotle is inconsistent about phantasia-possession across the animal kingdom: *DA* 425a10 ff. tentatively denies it to the worm; III.11 extends it to all self-movers; 415a10 ff. allows some *phtharta* to lack it. Nussbaum notes the inconsistency without resolving it.

(vi) The 428a1-4 *kata metaphoran* re-reading, while philologically defensible, asks the reader to override the dominant tradition of commentary. Nussbaum's case is strong but not decisive.

## § Anticipated Cross-Cluster Citations

Nussbaum's essay is structurally upstream of the entire post-1985 Anglophone phantasia debate, and its citation profile across the cluster can be projected with some confidence.

**Stephen White (1985, contemporaneous).** The only contemporaneous cluster author. White and Nussbaum work the same passages without direct cross-citation possible at composition; White's published version may add brief reference. Nussbaum's footnote 71 acknowledges Malcolm Schofield's parallel "Aristotle on the Imagination" (1978/1985) — the methodologically closest competitor.

**Dorothea Frede (1992, "The Cognitive Role of Phantasia in Aristotle").** Expected to cite Nussbaum centrally — particularly the interpretive thesis and the *DA* 428a1-4 rereading — while qualifying. Frede tends to preserve more of the *phantasma*-as-presentational-content tradition than Nussbaum allows. She will likely contest Nussbaum's broad-scope reading and reinstate a more focused conception.

**Victor Caston (1996 "Why Aristotle Needs Imagination," 1998 "Aristotle and the Problem of Intentionality").** Caston will cite Nussbaum on the interpretive thesis but defend a more imagistic-presentational account. The voice-passage (*DA* II.8) and the *aisthesis*/*phantasia* identity question (433a31) will be central battlegrounds. Caston is sympathetic to the intentional-content framing but resists Nussbaum's full assimilation to seeing-as.

**Daniel O'Gorman (2005, in rhetorical-studies appropriation).** Will cite Nussbaum as the standard authority for the anti-image reading and use the interpretive thesis to underwrite a rhetorical conception of *phantasia* as audience-directed sortal cognition. The *Rhet.* 1404a11 passage (whose significance Nussbaum identified) will likely feature.

**Francisco Gonzalez (Aristotle on phantasia / dialogue with Heidegger).** Will engage Nussbaum on the *DA* III.3 hermeneutic question, the *Mem.* readings, and the relation of *phantasia* to the temporal modalities. Gonzalez's hermeneutic-phenomenological commitments dispose him to extend Nussbaum's interpretive thesis but to thicken it with a more existential account of disclosure.

**Debra Hawhee (Bodily Arts; Moving Bodies).** Will cite Nussbaum as the foundational authority for reading Aristotelian *phantasia* as embodied and animal-cognitive rather than as detached image-having. Hawhee's rhetorical-pedagogical applications draw directly on Nussbaum's animal/incomplete-creature material and on the *Rhet.* 1404a11 passage.

**Eleni Papachristou (forthcoming/recent).** Will cite Nussbaum as the canonical interpretive starting point and engage especially the *MA* passages and the deliberative *phantasia* analysis.

## § Cross-Pipeline Bridges

**Aristotle pipeline (corpus/index/Aristotle - Complete Works).** Nussbaum's mapping of phantasia across DA, MA, PN, NE, Rhet, Metaph, De Int, Cat, Phys, APo, HA, PA effectively constitutes a full-corpus thematic concordance. Every Bekker citation in her index is a candidate primary-source anchor for the Aristotle pipeline. Particular bridges: (i) the DA III.7-8 phantasma-thinking nexus; (ii) the MA 702a18 ff. preparation-of-desire mechanism; (iii) the DA II.8 420b31 voice-semantikos passage; (iv) the EN III.4-5 *phainomenon agathon* — character ethics nexus; (v) the *Rhet.* 1404a11 / 1370b32 / 1371b19 rhetorical *phantasia* triplet.

**Heidegger BCAP GA 18 (corpus/index/Heidegger - Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy).** Heidegger's 1924 Marburg course reads *DA* II-III as a rhetorical-ontological text. His phantasia-treatment in §§14-22 (where it is read alongside *pathos*, *aisthesis*, *hexis*, *logos*) operates with a phenomenological-disclosive conception that converges substantially with Nussbaum's interpretive thesis but diverges in its temporal and ontological commitments. Heidegger reads *phantasia* as the disclosure-of-being-of-the-living, where Nussbaum reads it as the *qua*-structure of perceptual content. The Heidegger / Nussbaum comparison is one of the cluster's most productive cross-pipeline axes. Key shared loci: *DA* III.3, III.7-8, II.8 (voice as *semantikos*), *Rhet.* 1404a (rhetorical *phantasia*).

**Dalton dissertation phantasia chapters.** The dissertation's phantasia material (the A-chain *phantasma* sections, A₃-A₄ Phantasma/resonant kinesis, the renumbered chain) is anchored in this interpretive triangle: Aristotle primary loci ↔ Heidegger BCAP ↔ Nussbaum + post-1985 secondary. Nussbaum's identification of *phantasia* as the interpretive-formal *qua*-structure of perception supplies the dissertation's analytic backbone; Heidegger's disclosive temporalization supplies the existential-ontological depth; the rhetorical-ontology pipeline reads them together as offering a unified theory of attuned bodily cognition.
