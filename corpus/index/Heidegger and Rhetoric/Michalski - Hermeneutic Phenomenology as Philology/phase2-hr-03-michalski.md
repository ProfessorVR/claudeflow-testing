## 1. Unit Metadata

**Thesis (one sentence)**: Heidegger's SS 1924 lecture course is methodologically philological in a rigorously academic sense — its conceptual centeredness, text-centered Greek reading, and rejection of presuppositionless philosophy together constitute a hermeneutic-phenomenological philology that exceeds the horizon of classical philology and finds its prefiguration in Aristotle's *Rhetoric* as the hermeneutic of concrete *Dasein*.

**Methodological weight**: Michalski is the editor of GA 18. His framing of Heidegger's procedure carries authoritative weight on what counts as a legitimate reading of the lectures' textual operations. Crucially, he distinguishes the manuscript wording (GA 18 333) from the student-notes wording (GA 18 4) at the load-bearing point — the philology-formula itself (note 18). The full Heideggerian formula reads only in the manuscript: *Leidenschaft der Erkenntnis des Ausgesprochenen und des Sichaussprechens* — "passion for the knowledge of that which has been expressed and of its self-expression."

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### Opening (book pp. 65–67)

Michalski frames the surprise: Heidegger's SS 1924 lecture course "Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy" is announced as a course on philology, not on philosophy or its history (note 1 = GA 18 4, 333). To understand this, two points of intellectual history are introduced.

(1) Heidegger's prior multi-year project of laying bare the primordial concern of Aristotelian thinking via *Destruktion* of the philosophical-theological systemicity and conceptuality layered upon it through the philosophical tradition — particularly Aquinas, Thomism, and Catholic theology. In this destructive project Heidegger could find more solidarity with Bultmann (striving in a parallel fashion to release the authentic *kerygma* of the New Testament from the distorting conceptuality of Greek philosophy) than with his more systematically-disposed philosophical colleagues Paul Natorp and Nicolai Hartmann.

(2) The Marburg 1920s philological cooperation — strict cooperation between philosophy, theology, and philology departments, with numerous reading circles attested by Hans-Georg Gadamer's published recollections (note 3 = *Philosophische Lehrjahre* 14ff). One year before SS 1924, Werner Jaeger had published an extremely significant contribution to Aristotle scholarship — his attempted reconstruction of the historical development of Aristotelian philosophy. Jaeger placed philosophers in the position of having to argue philologically as well as philosophically. Gadamer's testimony in his vita (note 4, quoted in Grondin's biography 139) is instructive: Jaeger's book brought home to Gadamer "as a philosopher, the need for a thorough training in philology... Philosophical and philological questions cannot be posed independently of each other."

The opening then sets up the chapter's two-part structure. §I will examine whether Heidegger's claim to do philology is confirmed. The decisive matter is not laid to rest by §I, however, since (first) Heidegger does not actually say anything about *joining* philosophy with philology but rather says he wishes to *replace* philosophy with philology, and (second) he gives a hypothetical formulation: "If philology were to denote 'passion for the knowledge of that which has been expressed and of its self-expression' [*Leidenschaft der Erkenntnis des Ausgesprochenen und des Sichaussprechens*], then his aim and his procedure would be purely philological" (book p. 66, citing GA 18 4, 333). Heidegger does not employ a vague concept of philology nor an established academic concept; he determines the concept in terms of a fundamental way of knowing — one rather well suited for disputing the privileged rank of what is traditionally called philosophy. The second part of the exposition will pursue this. The lecture course's parallel occasional usage of *hermeneutics* signals that SS 1924 belongs to Heidegger's recasting of Husserl's reflexive phenomenology as hermeneutic phenomenology. We are faced with the task of grasping philology as a moment in the hermeneuticizing of phenomenology, while also inquiring into philology's relation to Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, dealt with in the lectures under the heading "hermeneutic of *Dasein*."

### §I. HEIDEGGER AS CLASSICAL PHILOLOGIST (book pp. 67–72)

A philological audit of Heidegger's procedure. Surveying Heidegger's sources and aids, one finds him relying almost exclusively on the texts of Aristotle themselves; the history of philosophical Aristotelianism remains "so to speak, bracketed." Where Heidegger does refer to Porphyry, Boethius, Kant, and Hegel (in connection with the question of the essence of the concept and its definition), he does so only to show that in their scholastic logic a fundamental possibility of speaking has degenerated into mere thought-technique, recoverable only by return to the Aristotelian determination of *logos as horismos as logos ousias* (GA 18 9f, 15f, 335f). Heidegger's reference to the Stoics, church fathers, Aquinas, and Luther on the affects (*Wirkungsgeschichte*) does not imply that understanding of Aristotle is to be augmented by such study; on the contrary, it seeks to show that Aristotle's *pathē*-doctrine exerts tremendous influence and represents a necessary basis for those discussions (GA 18 177f).

Two apparent exceptions — Themistius/Simplicius and Nietzsche — are introduced "not so much as philosophers but as philologists." Themistius and Simplicius are used for their commentaries on *Physics* III.1–3 (GA 18 319, 369, 376, 381, 386, 394), from which one may conclude that Heidegger carefully worked through at least the parts dealing with chapters 1–3 of the third book of the *Physics*. From Nietzsche there is simply the *aperçu* that Greek is the "most speakable of all languages," to which Heidegger adds "In the final analysis he must have known what it is to be Greek" [*was das Griechentum ist*] (GA 18 109). It is worth noting that Heidegger had undertaken a reading of Nietzsche's lecture on the "History of Greek Eloquence" in volume 2 of *Philologica*.

What emerges: apart from the texts of Aristotle himself, no philosophical authorities whatsoever are admitted as interpretive aids; whereas at least a few philological authorities are. Most prominent is Werner Jaeger, to whom Heidegger returns repeatedly throughout the course. Jaeger's book on Aristotle is the only text Heidegger recommends to students as secondary reading. Admittedly, Heidegger lets it be known that this book addresses precisely what he himself proposes *not* to deal with: Aristotle's personality, philosophy as a system, and his development (note 5 = GA 18 5, 333f). What cannot fail to be noticed is the switching of labels: "Jaeger, the classical philologist, gives us philosophy; Heidegger, the philosopher, gives us philology." Heidegger accepts certain isolated conclusions of Jaeger's research — concerning literary character, relative chronology, and authenticity of writings in the Aristotelian corpus (GA 18 4f, 37, 107, 239, 323) — but speaks disapprovingly with regard to Jaeger's developmental-historical reconstruction project: "It is my conviction that this effort is altogether hopeless" (GA 18 299). Heidegger saw himself quite seriously challenged by this effort, which is why he gave such a rigorous examination in the subject to his student Gadamer (note 6).

Other philologists used: Hermann Diels (source for *exoterikoi logoi* as nonscientific discourse founded on prevailing opinions; etymology of *entelecheia*; GA 18 107, 296, 367f); Ludwig Spengel (conjecture of pre-Aristotelian authorship for *Rhetorica ad Alexandrum*); Hermann Bonitz (*Index Aristotelicus* providing reference for use of *ousia*; GA 18 113f, 345). Hence, in lexicology, etymology, and authenticity criticism, Heidegger places himself under the care of recognized scholars.

For the rest of it, however, so exclusively does Aristotle's text govern Heidegger's interpretation that Luther's principle of *sola scriptura* seems to find application: Aristotle *sui ipsius interpres*. Heidegger makes it clear in the very first hour that his approach to Aristotle will be concept-centered, and to that end will also be text-centered and reading-centered: the goal is "the understanding of some fundamental concepts of Aristotelian philosophy," to be gained "by way of the study of the *text* of the Aristotelian treatises" (GA 18 3, 5). According to the manuscript, this way of gaining understanding is at the same time hearing-centered: the course should yield instructions for "*listening* to what Aristotle has to say" (GA 18 333). It may be inferred from these notions of reading and listening that two separate principles of textual interpretation are asserted: (1) As reader, the interpreter must show that interpretation "stands in the text itself" and is not "fabricated," that it "has its ground" in the text (GA 18 216, 237). (2) As listener, the interpreter must go beyond the text to what is genuinely said and meant, such that interpretation in this sense "is nothing else than emphasizing what is not there" (GA 18 66). These do not contradict but coincide in concrete activity, made plain by the fact that both play equal part in Heidegger's interpretation of *zoē* as being-in-the-world (note 7 = GA 18 234f, where Heidegger admits the interpretation might initially be held to be "read into" Aristotle and defends it from *De partibus animalium*).

Text-centeredness implies extensive citation of the Greek wording, with reliance on critical scholarly editions. As a rule, Heidegger does not cite from German translations but gives his own translation/paraphrase, often formulated at the podium. The rough, imprecise practice makes evident that "the character of translation is in principle experimental, provisional, and always incomplete," and rules out the erroneous notion that a translation could ever replace the Greek text. Whenever Heidegger notices a mistake — or has it pointed out by his attentive audience, "astonishingly strong in Greek" — he takes deliberate pains to set things right (GA 18 222, 226f). Range of texts: Heidegger occupies a middle ground, between fickle leaping and immanent preoccupation with a single text, guided by his initial question concerning the rootedness or autochthony of conceptuality (*Bodenständigkeit der Begrifflichkeit*).

The concept-centered procedure pays close attention to prephilosophical, nonphilosophical (natural) language usage of the Greeks, most emphatically in the case of the fundamental concept of *ousia* (GA 18 12). The reflection on *metapherein*, "transference," explicitly raised by Aristotle himself, moves along the same lines: a meaning is borrowed from the "first and primordial" addressing of something and transferred to a new way of addressing a thing — a process whereby the borrowed meaning itself can be seen as such (GA 18 83). Heidegger takes this event into account whenever it lends itself to investigation of the "first and primordial" meaning of a word. He makes the translation of *soma* as "body" more precise by pointing out that corporeality in this case does not mean stuffiness (*Stofflichkeit*) or materiality (*Materialität*), but rather that strange obtrusiveness of a being detected in later usage (GA 18 28, 347).

Particularly when translating fundamental concepts, Heidegger is even less confident in standard German translations: those common German translations are either given more precise meanings or are explicitly rejected. *Hekaston*: "This is not 'each' [*jedes*] or even 'single' and 'individual.' *Hekaston* means 'what in each case obtains' [*Jeweiliges*] insofar as I linger with it and thereby see it from a certain distance" (GA 18 32). Other examples: *telos* — not "goal" (*Ziel*) or "purpose" (*Zweck*), but "end" (*Ende*) in the sense of completion (note 11 = GA 18 39); *ousia* — not "essence" (*Wesen*), but "existence" (*Dasein*) in the sense of being available (GA 18 25, 40); *aisthēsis* — not "sensation" (*Empfindung*), but "attending to" (*Vernehmen*) the world in the sense of having-it-there (GA 18 52); *energeia* — not "reality" (*Wirklichkeit*), but "being-at-work" (*Im-Werke-Sein*) in the sense of a distinctive "how" of being (*ausgezeichneten Wie des Seins*). Note 12 (GA 18 70): "If our expression 'reality' [*Wirklichkeit*] were not so well worn, it would be an excellent translation" — here over-familiarity (*Abgegriffenheit*) of a word is the decisive criterion for rejecting a translation. Etymology too: the Aristotelian neologism *entelecheia*, derived from *enteles* and *echein*. Heidegger rejects "Possession of completeness" [*Besitz der Vollkommenheit*] as not conveying the ontologically fundamental meaning, substituting "holding-itself-in-completion" [*Sich-im-Fertigsein-Halten*] (note 13 = GA 18 368).

The verdict (book p. 72): Heidegger may be said to engage in philology in a rigorously academic sense — he does not work in the company of a single philosopher, he does enlist the aid of a number of philologists, several of whom have through application of genuinely philological methods reached conclusions relevant for Heidegger's own interpretation, and have placed the original text and its concepts at the center of their concerns. At the same time, Heidegger has not entirely become a classical philologist: in a negative sense, he fails to take research literature into account to any extensive degree, nor does he apply consistently and self-sufficiently the specialized philological methods then current. In a positive sense, his conceptual discussions, carried out in close connection with the concrete texts (so strenuously does Heidegger reject the aim of reconstructing a philosophical system), actually turn out to order these concepts — by virtue of the guiding question of the autochthony of conceptuality — into a systematic whole that **exceeds the horizon of classical philology**.

### §II. PHILOLOGY, HERMENEUTIC PHENOMENOLOGY, AND RHETORIC (book pp. 72–78)

What is remarkable: Heidegger explicitly rejects the title of philosophy in favor of philology even for this region that exceeds the horizon of classical philology. His distinguishing his project from Jaeger's would naturally have played a role; but Heidegger himself provides a few more indications. At one point, somewhat addressing his students, he remarks that "it would perhaps be a good idea if philosophers would resolve to consider what it actually means to speak to others" (GA 18 169f) — a critique of philosophical teaching's lack of self-conscious reflection on the *Sachcharakter* (substantive character) of speaking, listeners, and discourse itself. Along similar lines: "philosophy today is in better shape [than philology], provided that it maintains the fundamental presupposition that things could not be better" [*es sei alles in bester Ordnung*] (note 14 = GA 18 6, citing the manuscript at 334). That is, philosophy maintains the illusory fundamental presupposition that it does not itself stand under any presuppositions of which it must become conscious; it divests itself of any claim such presuppositions might make; it regards itself as presuppositionless and *letztbegründend* (having an ultimate foundation). Philology is therefore the attempt to conduct one's research and teaching while confronting the claim made by the presuppositions involved in such practices, while seeking to understand the fundamental conditions of one's own discourse.

This is confirmed by what is explicitly stated concerning philology in the introduction to the course. According to Heidegger, the philological intent of the lecture "by nature" entails certain presuppositions (note 15 = GA 18 5, 333f). The first of the six is that, in view of the philological intent, it is Aristotle alone whose discourse is relevant — philology is obviously not just a particular way of access to Aristotle (a mere method to be applied to an object selected in advance) but is instead a fundamental matter of concern in its own right. The second: the philologist must not regard himself as so far advanced that he has nothing more to learn. The sixth: the philologist must attribute to the historical past in general, to which Aristotle belongs, a saying-power that reaches the philologist himself. That is, on his own the researcher and teacher can never achieve adequate reflection on what is said, but remains dependent for that on an impulse from tradition; the essential impulse can come only from Aristotle. The third, fourth, and fifth concern the particular status of the discourse called for. Conceptual discourse is not simply the appropriate medium for definition and communication; it is the substance of all scientific research. And this scientific research is not simply an external and more or less incidental circumstance of life, but rather a possibility of existence for which human beings must choose and decide. In a certain contrast to religious existence, philology is the possibility in which human existence takes up a position with regard to its own interpretation that is centered solely upon itself. Hence to Heidegger's concept of philology belongs further: as the reflection of research and teaching upon its own discourse, a reflection that relies on an impulse originating with Aristotle, philology deals with itself as a science, and thereby with the distinctive possibility of human existence that seeks to come to an understanding of itself without recourse to divine revelation — that is, philology is the existentially-ontologically grounded directive of scientific research. Heidegger's lecture is accordingly directed to students in every department, challenging them not so much to philosophize or to take up the study of Aristotle, but rather to turn to their own, freely chosen disciplines, to "really *grasp* it and to pursue it in such a way that the *activity* of conceptual *research* comes to *life*" (note 16 = GA 18 14, 339). Not in the sense of a scientific propaedeutic that must be completed before science proper can begin, but rather in the sense of a task that, in connection with the objects of study, is always to be undertaken anew (note 17 = GA 18 339: "the precondition of being-able-to-be-attentive is that one *stands in the matter*").

With this we have gained a clue to a more precise understanding of the definition of philology as "passion for the knowledge of that which has been expressed and of its self-expression" (note 18 = GA 18 333; the manuscript wording, with the trailing "and of self-expression" missing in the student-notes text at GA 18 4). This definition takes its orientation from the two elements, both rooted in Greek, that comprise "philo-logy," according to which philology is a *philia* of *logos*, a "love of speaking." When Heidegger explicates this "love" as a "passion for knowledge," he gives precedence to a theoretical conception as opposed to a practical one (which would have been equally plausible): philology is not a matter of practical exercise of speech, but rather of its theoretical cognition (*Erkenntnis*). However, such theoretical cognition does not stand in contrast to the practical exercise of speech by virtue of its indifference toward its object. Instead, like the practical exercise of speech, it too remains carried along by "passion." Here we should think of Aristotle's notion of *pathos* — which Heidegger translates in his lecture course as "attunement" (*Befindlichkeit*), and which is thereby designated as the historical impulse for Heidegger's own existential-ontological thematization of the attunement of *Dasein* (GA 18 4, 120, 168). Just as, with Aristotle, theory represents a state of being composed (*Gefasstsein*) against the *pathos* of fear (specifically: that what is constantly there [*das Immerseiende*] could perhaps one day cease to be — GA 18 289f), so likewise existential ontology takes science as a possibility of existence grasped by *Dasein*, attuned and disposed by moods. For the philologist, the question of the cognition of speech (*Erkennen des Sprechens*) is a matter of the philologist's attuned and comprehending being-in-the-world (*befindlich-verstehendes In-der-Welt-sein*).

In his definition of philology, Heidegger unfolds speech as the duality "of that which has been expressed and of its self-expression" [*des Ausgesprochenen und des Sichaussprechens*]. He thereby indicates the structural multiplicity displayed by the Aristotelian concept of *logos*. To wit: (1) *Legein*, "speaking" in the sense of accessing — that is, in the sense of an approach to something, addressing it and showing it forth — this speaking about something is essentially both speaking with and to others, or again, the ability to listen to such speaking and expressing of oneself (*Sich-selbst-Aussprechen*), that is, self-expression (*Sichaussprechen*). (2) *Legomenon*, "the spoken," "that which has been expressed" (*Ausgesprochenes*) in the sense of an answer, that is, what is shown forth by the being that was addressed (*von dem angesprochenen Seienden*). (3) *Legein* and at the same time *legomenon*, "speaking" and "that which has been expressed" in the sense of something that just pops into one's head and comes out, something that I don't really do and that therefore no longer really belongs to me, but rather has become generally available public property (GA 18 17, 19f, 104f, 123f, 212, 216f, 276f, 304, 341, 358).

The task of philological knowledge is to separate the *logos* into its multiple structural moments. The fact that philology aims in particular at conceptual speaking in scientific research and teaching means that this conceptual speaking should be situated in the structural whole of human speaking and made transparent in its genesis from that speaking. Philology tries to show what makes up the conceptuality of concepts as distinct from nonconceptual speaking, as well as how this conceptuality is rooted in speaking as the fundamental attunement of the being of human beings, and thereby in *Dasein* itself as the being of human beings. For this purpose it investigates the everyday self-expression of human beings with others concerning whatever contributes to the end of its concerns — which is where the average understanding, in which *Dasein* comports itself as just anyone (*als Man*), can find expression (GA 18 55f, 63f). But because *logos* in the third sense, the sense of something that just pops into one's head and comes out — in short, idle chatter — comes to prominence, so that the genuine meaning is ever more obscured, this means that the possibility of the concept first appears in the possibility one has of disregarding everyday concerns and of appropriating the genuine meaning in an original way, as a countermove against idle chatter (GA 18 217, 276f). Let it also be said, however, that the concept can also and immediately sink back into idle chatter and thereby betray what was originally appropriated from it (note 19), so that, on the conceptual level, philology must delimit authentically conceptual speaking from that which is inauthentic.

How then does the project, sketched above, of an existential-ontological philology that exceeds the horizon of classical philology, stand in relation to the still more comprehensive project of a hermeneutic phenomenology, which Heidegger had pursued since the War Emergency Semester of 1919, and which finally wound up being systematically drafted in *Sein und Zeit*, published in 1927? Heidegger mentions phenomenology only sparingly in the lectures on Aristotle, and then, characteristically, only in the form of an allusion to what is Aristotelian in phenomenology and what is phenomenological in Aristotle. In one place there is even a kind of criticism of phenomenology, when Heidegger asserts the superiority of an Aristotelian insight over a claim of phenomenology (GA 18 199, 237, 254). Talk of hermeneutics, by contrast, seems to be on the rise: in preference to logic and ontology, it is considered to be the more appropriate title for the thematization of *Dasein*, and, in the sense of the interpretation of concrete *Dasein*, it is designated as the topic of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (note 20 = GA 18 110 and 337; cf. 270, 351, 380f, 403). Above all, however, hermeneutics is present as a matter of fact in that Heidegger distinguishes as the three moments of conceptuality in *Dasein* the *concrete fundamental experience*, the *guiding claim*, and the *prevailing intelligibility*, and he accounts for their possibility in *Dasein* by means of distinguishing *fore-having*, *fore-sight*, and *fore-grasping* as the three moments of the interpretedness of *Dasein* (GA 18 13f, 270f, 338f, 354f).

As hermeneutical, phenomenology seeks access to the things themselves from the starting point of the interpretedness of *Dasein*. Indeed, it does so in such a manner that, by means of the explicit appropriation of its fore-having, fore-seeing, and fore-grasping, it throws into relief — along with the interpretedness that is always governing in a historical *Dasein* — the distortions through which *Dasein* conceals the things. However, to the extent that the current prevalence of a form of interpretedness, along with the distortions inherent in it, is actually carried by the *logos* that, as everyday idle talk, or even as inauthentic conceptual talk, governs all interpretations of *Dasein* in an ever greater distance from things, to such an extent **philology — in the sense of the thematization of the authentic conceptual speech that takes shape as a countermovement against such idle talk — is disclosed as a necessary element of hermeneutic phenomenology**: it is the element with which hermeneutic phenomenology takes into account the leading function of speech in the dominance of the interpretedness of *Dasein* (note 21 = GA 18 358, 276).

Some of what Heidegger says about Aristotle's *Rhetoric* makes clear that in certain respects he sees this conception of a hermeneutic-phenomenological philology prefigured there: the *Rhetoric* attests to a primordial viewing of speech, the phenomena of which are surveyed and distinguished in terms of the basic possibilities *Dasein* has of speaking-with-one-another (*Miteinandersprechen*) — the result being an interpretation of concrete *Dasein* as being-with-one-another (*Miteinandersein*) (note 22 = GA 18 110, 113, 117, 139, 358, 276; note 22 also collects the famous identifications: "*The Rhetoric is nothing else than the interpretation of concrete Dasein, the hermeneutic of Dasein itself*" (GA 18 110); "The concrete instance of the primordiality of seeing is the entire Rhetoric" (GA 18 61); and so on).

There are, however, two respects in which the concerns of philology cannot be equated with those of rhetoric. (1) In the first place, philology — even if passionate — remains a theoretical cognition of speech, whereas rhetoric, as treated by Aristotle, represents the *practical* possibility of seeing what speaks for the subject matter for whoever is resolved to convince others of something (GA 18 114f). Only insofar as Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, in the sense of the treatment of rhetoric as a practical possibility, maintains a theoretical character throughout its general reflection on the fundamental conditions and possibilities of discourse — only to this extent can Aristotle's *Rhetoric* be regarded as a predecessor to Heideggerian philology. (2) In the second place, philology has to do primarily with the conceptual speech of scientific research and teaching, whereas rhetoric, by contrast, deals primarily with the everyday speaking-with-one-another that *Dasein* directs toward the regulation of its everyday being-with-one-another. To be sure, this everyday speaking-with-one-another is made thematic in philology as well, to the extent that it needs to disclose idle talk as the negative impulse with respect to the development of conceptual speech; but it exceeds the scope of rhetoric at least in the direction of that which Aristotle himself delimits from rhetoric as **dialectic** (note 23 = GA 18 127f on *paradeigma*/*enthymema* vs. *epagoge*/*syllogismos*). It is precisely in connection with dialectic that Heidegger, at the beginning of the course, explains how he proposes to deal philologically with philosophy: "*Dialektikē* has, to be sure, its seriousness (the same that has been lost by every philosophy that has become sophistry). But this is only the seriousness of an attempt at seeing what could ultimately be meant (by the concepts). In this sense we are dealing with philosophy" (GA 18 7).

## 3. Contributor's Key Concepts

- **Philology (Heidegger's existential-ontological sense)** — the existentially-ontologically grounded directive of scientific research; *philia* of *logos* understood as theoretical cognition that nonetheless remains "carried along by passion."
- **Passion for the knowledge of the expressed and of self-expression** (*Leidenschaft der Erkenntnis des Ausgesprochenen und des Sichaussprechens*) — Heidegger's hypothetical formula at GA 18 4, 333 (manuscript wording at 333; student-notes at 4 truncated). - **Rhetoric as hermeneutic of Dasein** — per GA 18 110: "The Rhetoric is nothing else than the interpretation of concrete Dasein, the hermeneutic of Dasein itself."
- **Hermeneutic phenomenology** — Heidegger's recasting of Husserl's reflexive phenomenology, present in SS 1924 by allusion and "as a matter of fact" in the fore-having/fore-seeing/fore-grasping fore-structure.
- **Three moments of conceptuality / fore-structure of interpretedness** — concrete fundamental experience / guiding claim / prevailing intelligibility, accounted for by fore-having / fore-seeing / fore-grasping.
- **Attunement (Heidegger's translation of *pathos*)** — Aristotle's *pathos* as Heidegger's *Befindlichkeit*; the historical impulse for the BT thematization. Theoretical cognition of speech is attuned: *befindlich-verstehendes In-der-Welt-sein*.
- **Idle chatter / Gerede** — the third sense of logos; philology delimits authentic conceptual speaking from inauthentic.
- ***sui ipsius interpres*** — Aristotle as his own interpreter; Luther's *sola scriptura* applied; no philosophical authorities admitted besides Aristotle.
- **Topology of conceptuality** — philology / rhetoric / dialectic: rhetoric is everyday speaking-with-one-another; philology is conceptual scientific speech (countermovement against idle chatter); philology exceeds rhetoric in the dialectical direction.

## 4. Contributor's Main Positions

(See JSON `main_positions[]` for full premises/targets/evidence. Eight positions:)

- **P1**: Heidegger engages in philology in a rigorously academic sense — measured by enlistment of multiple philologists, application of genuinely philological methods, and centering of original Greek text and concepts.
- **P2**: Heidegger is NOT entirely a classical philologist: misses extensive engagement with research literature; positively, his autochthony-guided concept-discussions exceed classical philology's horizon.
- **P3**: Heidegger's hypothetical formula (philology = passion for knowledge of the expressed and of self-expression) is a determinate redefinition of philology as the existentially-ontologically grounded directive of scientific research, against philosophy's presuppositionless self-conception.
- **P4**: Philology as Heidegger develops it preserves the Aristotelian structure of *pathos*/*Befindlichkeit* in the philologist's mode of attuned and comprehending being-in-the-world.
- **P5**: Philology is a NECESSARY ELEMENT of hermeneutic phenomenology — the element with which hermeneutic phenomenology takes account of the leading function of speech (logos) in the dominance of the interpretedness of Dasein.
- **P6**: Aristotle's *Rhetoric* prefigures Heidegger's hermeneutic-phenomenological philology because the Rhetoric attests to a primordial viewing of speech and yields interpretation of concrete *Dasein* as *Miteinandersein*.
- **P7**: Heideggerian philology cannot be equated with rhetoric — (1) theoretical vs. practical; (2) conceptual-scientific vs. everyday. Philology exceeds rhetoric in the dialectical direction.
- **P8** (methodological): Heidegger's SS 1924 procedure constitutes a methodologically authoritative model — any reading must register both the rigorously philological-textual operations and their existential-ontological exceeding of classical philology.

## 8. Tensions & Open Questions

(See JSON `tensions_and_open_questions[]` for full descriptions. 8 tensions: 3 internal, 4 cross-essay, 1 open question.)

**Internal**:
- **T1**: The "rigorous philology AND not entirely classical philology" verdict — calibrated against two opposing absolute readings, but the precise relation of Heidegger's procedure to classical philology remains partly open.
- **T2**: Theoretical-vs-practical conception of philology — theoretical cognition is "carried along by passion," but the boundary between theoretical-as-attuned philology and Aristotelian-rhetorical practice is a matter of degree.
- **T3**: Aristotle/historical-past asymmetry — Aristotle alone is admitted as philosophical authority, but the "saying-power" of the historical past in general is invoked (sixth presupposition); Michalski preserves the textual claim but does not address the apparent restriction.

**Cross-essay**:
- **T4** (= Phase 0 VT7): Phenomenology vs. philology — Michalski answers synthetically: phenomenology is hermeneuticized, hermeneutic phenomenology requires the philological moment, and philology in this enlarged sense IS the existentially-ontologically grounded directive of scientific research.
- **T5**: Michalski's philological framing of GA 18 contrasts with Kisiel's protopolitical reading (HR-06-KISIEL). Both cite GA 18 110 but draw different categorial consequences. This is the most direct intra-volume methodological contrast on how GA 18 is to be read.
- **T6**: Michalski's reading of philology as theoretical-cognition-of-speech is in tension with Hyde's reading (HR-04-HYDE) of the call of conscience as "an ontological and emotional form of epideictic rhetoric." The point of contact is *Befindlichkeit*/pathos. [INTERP-low.]
- **T7** (= Phase 0 VT3): Michalski supports the SS 1924 → BT 1927 continuity reading (book p. 76: hermeneutic phenomenology "finally wound up being systematically drafted in *Sein und Zeit*"). This places him with Gross/Hyde/Struever against Pöggeler's rupture reading.

**Open question**:
- **T8**: Manuscript-vs-student-notes distinction at the philology-formula (note 18) — Michalski as editor commits to manuscript wording (GA 18 333). Other contributors who cite GA 18 4 freely may be working from the truncated student-notes layer.
