## Section 1: Unit Metadata

**Author**. Nancy S. Struever (professor emerita, Johns Hopkins University); discipline: intellectual history / rhetoric. Original English (no translator). Author of *The Language of History in the Renaissance* (1970), *Theory as Practice: Ethical Inquiry in the Renaissance* (1992), and numerous essays on Vico, Hobbes, and the rhetorical tradition. Her contribution to this volume comes out of long engagement with the early-modern rhetorical tradition (Cicero, Hobbes, Vico) read backward into Hellenic rhetoric and forward into 20th-century philosophy.

**One-sentence thesis**. Heidegger's SS 1924 reading of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is the volume's most rigorous biological-temporal bridge between Aristotle and *Being and Time*: rhetoric is *not* a *technē* but a *dynamis* (potential) for theorizing the *Alltäglichkeit* of *Miteinandersein* — and *Alltäglichkeit*, properly read, is not "ordinariness" but "timefulness," the political-rhetorical solvent of eternity that colonizes BT's temporal program from within.

**Two German epigraphs frame the essay** (book p. 105):
> *Rhetorik ist nichts anderes als die Auslegung des konkreten Daseins, die Hermeneutik des Dasein selbst.*
> — Heidegger, SS 1924 [GA 18, 110]

> *Rhetorik [ist]… die erste systematische Hermeneutik der Alltäglichkeit des Miteinanderseins*
> — Heidegger, *Sein und Zeit* [BT 178, §29]

These are the same two passages Gross uses as the structuring quotations of his Introduction (HR-01-GROSS-A). Struever and Gross thus share the volume's defining textual frame: both read GA 18 110 / BT 178 as the keystone passage where Heidegger identifies rhetoric with the hermeneutic of Dasein. Where Gross's deployment is genealogical (the long arc of pathos from Aristotle through Christian theology to Heidegger), Struever's is *temporal-political-biological*: she reads the same identification through the lens of *time* as the irreducible category that Aristotle's rhetorical reading of *Physics III*, *De motu animalium*, *De partibus animalium*, and *NE II/VI* makes operative in SS 1924.

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

The essay has the most elaborate section structure of any contribution in the volume — Roman (I, II) + Arabic (1–4 under I; 1–5 under II) + lettered (A, B, C under II.3) subsections. This complexity reflects Struever's combination of (i) close philological reading of GA 18, (ii) intellectual-historical contextualization of SS 1924 against 20th-century time-philosophy (Husserl, Dilthey, Blattner), and (iii) constructive argument about Heidegger's politics of time.

### I. THE PLACE OF RHETORIC IN THE ARISTOTELIAN PROGRAM: LIFE AND TIMES (book pp. 105–115)

Opens by claiming SS 1924 is an "extraordinary opportunity for the historian of rhetoric" because Heidegger reads the *Rhetoric* "as a mode of inquiry, as, indeed, giving us a life science, an account of our defining life capacities." The ingenious strategy of the lectures is to use the *Rhetoric* to gloss the *Grundbegriffe* (basic concepts: being, good, virtue, happiness, end, potency/act) — and thereby to re-temporalize and re-politicize the central terms of Aristotelian philosophy. Heidegger reads the polis as the "potentiality of human life," "the authentic life" (GA 18 46); rhetoric construes political life, constitutes community (*koinōnia*), and "claims to *be* politics" (GA 18 136). The key sentence: "the Greeks lived in oratory" (*daß die Griechen in der Rede lebten*, GA 18 108).

**Subsection 1 — "Heidegger's Account of the Nature of Rhetorical Inquiry: Rhetoric as Life Science" (book pp. 106–108).** Adopts Pierre Aubenque's reading of *Rhetoric* as "fully rhetorized psychology" — topics already organized for argument. Heidegger intricates the life capacities (*pathē*, *orexis*, *prohairesis*, *hexis*, *Besorgen*) with rhetorical strategic concerns (*pistis*, *doxa*, *endoxa*, *topoi*, *enthymēma*); the enthymeme etymologically discloses its root in *thymos* — "affective desire," "heart" (GA 18 128). Heidegger's lectures on *doxa* (GA 18 136–158) are "crucial for the account of politics to be derived from discourse." The rhetor's purpose is to push Dasein into doxa = continuous pragmatic engagement with time. *De anima* 428a 20–21 supplies the doxa/pistis relation; *pistis* is not pure belief but "that which speaks *for* a thing" (GA 18 118). Heidegger's signature sentence: "*Die doxa ist die Weise, in der wir das Leben in seiner Alltäglichkeit da haben*" (GA 18 138). The section closes with the negative-definition thesis: rhetoric is "not a *technē*, the inauthentic definition (GA 18 114), but a potentiality for theorizing, a *dynamis tou theoresai* (Rhet. 1355b26, GA 18 122). It is a potential, with all its peculiar time-fraughtness, not an *energeia*, an actuality." Aristotle's stipulation of *legein* as *pithanon* (GA 18 116) marks rhetoric as "irrevocably timebound."

**Subsection 2 — "Rhetoric and Politics: Time and Motion Studies" (book pp. 108–110).** Opens with the syllogism that anchors the essay: "If life, then motion, *kinēsis* (GA 18 286). If motion, then time. If the basic concern of life studies is movement, *Sein-als-Bewegtsein* (286), then the primary strategies of rhetoric must deal with time." Reads SS 1924's *pathos* discussions as a long meditation on *Rhet*. 1378a19–21 ("the emotions are all those feelings that so change men as to affect their judgements [*kriseis*]"). *Kinēsis* gives "the authentic Da-Charakter to Dasein" (GA 18 287); ontological significance of passions = our capacity for change (*Veränderlichkeit*). Passions are not *Wissen* but *Befindlichkeiten* (GA 18 232); "*Das eidos der pathē ist ein Sichverhalten zu anderen Menschen, ein In-der-Welt-Sein*" (GA 18 207). The rhetor must be oriented to the hearer's dispositions to do his work, *ergon* — "bring Dasein into doxa, make us 'take to heart' an opinion." *Logos* functions only in a Dasein defined as a lively practice of doxa, cares, and passions. The fear-discussion (GA 18 246–263) reveals "Greek genius… great sensitivity to temporal dimensions of affect": *phobos* has peculiar tense (future), voice (subjunctive), and mode (the possible); *phobos* is "already there" (GA 18 276) as *Boden*, ground; *phobos bouleutikous poiei* (Rhet. 1382b21–22, GA 18 259–260) = fear makes us deliberators. The *phoberoi* (fearsome ones), gentle (*praoi*), and ironic (*eirōnes*) define the bearing of Miteinandersein (GA 18 256–263). "At no point does the Heideggerian discussion wander far from the considerations of demands of time."

**Subsection 3 — "Time is of the Essence: Heidegger's Formulation of *Alltäglichkeit*" (book pp. 110–113).** This is where Struever does her most rigorous lexical work. Heidegger contrasts the philosophic obsession with timeless truths (*aei on*, GA 18 140) and rhetoric's *Zeitlichkeit*. "Because of its interest in *Aussagen*, articulation, speaking-out to someone, rhetoric develops an account of life in its dailiness, its timefulness, its radical specificity of time, its care for tense: 'Being itself as care and care-full speech is *timely*; it cares for the *still not-at-hand*, speaks about the *already-appeared*, investigates that which is *now with us*.' [*Das Sein in sich selbst als Besorgen und besorgendes Sprechen ist zeitlich, besorgt das noch-nicht-Vorhandene, spricht über das schon-Geschehene, betrachtet das Jetzt-Daseiende* (GA 18 131).]" The three rhetorical genres — deliberative, judicial, epideictic — are temporal-modal explications. Aristotle Rhet. 1356a4–6 (deliberation about contingent) names the three tenses ("having been," "being in the future," "being now"); deliberation is timeful, tenses imply modalities. *Kairos*, the Hellenic primary canon: measure of time, the moment, response to it, the appropriate strategy — *to prepon* (Greek), *quod decet* (Latin). Heidegger's account of *Nicomachean Ethics* (GA 18 183–199) recasts "time and timedness, timefulness of action and decision," with the *Rhetoric* as source.

Subsection 3's most consequential micro-argument is the *meson*/*kairos*/*hexis* synthesis. Aristotle's *meson* shifts from medicine to ethics: "*meson für die praxis ist der kairos*" (GA 18 144). Heidegger focuses on the distinction between *meson* as object (geometric/arithmetic, the medical tradition) and *meson pros hēmas* (mean-relative-to-us, agent's capacity). *Meson* integrated with *kairos* "is not to be arrived at in a geometrical mode. Rather *meson* is *Jeweiligkeit* (temporal particularity — 'particular while') in respect to the agent, for Dasein is ever *jeweiliges* (respective) Dasein" (GA 18 201). *Hexis* is the *Grundbestimmung* of *aretē* (GA 18 185); Heidegger deploys *dynamis*, *entelechia*, *energeia* to represent "the movements of potency and actualization in life phenomena." Hexis is *dynamis*, *Gefasstsein* (185) — "a potential for having; not pure duration but timebound, acquired and lost." Hexis is "*ein Wie des pathos*" (GA 18 184); "the movement, or lack of movement, from potency to act describes a basic dimension of moral agency." Closes with the epistemic claim: topoi and endoxa have *Revisionsfähigkeit* (GA 18 138), epistēmē does not. Aristotle's grasp of foreshortened rhetorical argument (audience "takes more to heart" short chains, GA 18 133). *Apeiron* (GA 18 292) undermines philosophical arguments devoted to stability — "foundationalist accounts of Greek ethics are late, extraneous applications of the *Grundbegriffe*."

**Subsection 4 — "*Alltäglichkeit* as Qualification of *Miteinandersein*" (book pp. 113–115).** Coins the essay's titular term: "*Alltäglichkeit* defines everydayness not as 'ordinary' but as 'timefulness,' the timely character of practical life: it designates the continuous, if intermittent, repetitious demands of daily life, its iterability. '*Miteinandersein*' stipulates as primitive, ineluctable, living-with-one-another. Man is not self-sufficient (GA 18 96); life, any life, is there (*da*) for another being (GA 18 241)." Aristotle's account of pathē supplies the basic interpersonal structure; Hobbes (per Heidegger) defines passions as "intrinsically social, interpersonal, rather than simply physiological" (GA 18 207); per Dumouchel, emotions are "qualifications of life-in-the-world" (GA 18 46–47).

The subsection also articulates the antifoundationalist claim. Aristotle attributes passions to the *whole person*, not just the soul (GA 18 122, 192, 197). The formula *Seele als ousia* specifies *Sein eines Lebenden* (GA 18 30), "both notes and relegates Aristotle's entification" — a rejection of mind-as-cabinet-of-consciousness. "'Mind is not at all pure': '*Das noein des Menschen ist kein reines* (GA 18 202). It is dependent upon imagination, among other faculties, and imagination is, of course, dependent upon bodily sensation; imagination is the *Boden* of *noein* (GA 18 199).' Thus impurity supports *Alltäglichkeit*, timefulness, as hermeneutical focus; impurity prejudices pure duration."

Closes Section I with the politics-of-hearing reading. "*Das Sprechen sein telos hat beim 'Hörer,' beim akroatēs*" (GA 18 123); "*Das akouein, 'Hören,' ist die eigentliche aisthesis*" (GA 18 104). The hearer defines the speaker's task. Heidegger explores temporality as qualifying response, focusing on living-with-one-another. Rhetoric 1358b1 anchors the primacy of hearer in determining rhetorical *telos*. Cassin/Narcy on Met. 1006a24–26: responsibility for argument's success rests with listener (*hypomenon*), not the demonstrator (*apodeiknys*). Section closes: "Heidegger perhaps responds to more of the *Rhetoric* text than he cites in his characterization of rhetoric as hermeneutic."

### II. MEDITATIONS ON TIME (book pp. 115–127)

The pivot from "place of rhetoric in Aristotelian program" (Section I) to "meditations on time" (Section II) is precisely the move from textual exegesis to constructive argument: Section II's task is to show what *kind* of time-philosophical innovation Heidegger's rhetorical reading produces and how it relates to Husserl, Dilthey, Blattner, and BT.

**Subsection 1 — "Making a Place for Rhetoric in the History of Inquiry" (book pp. 115–117).** Anchored by SS 1924 epigraph: "*Es gilt nicht Neues zu sagen, sondern das zu sagen, was die Alten schon meinten*." Rhetoric for Heidegger has three counterintuitive qualities: (i) "better than a *Sprachphilosophie*" (GA 18 117); (ii) "not simply a purely formal verbal *technē*"; (iii) "exists inside politics." Aristotle "corrects Plato": *Rhetoric* has "great revisionary force." Plato "misses" Dasein (GA 18 37); Aristotle "gets Socrates right" (GA 18 184); Aristotle gives a better account of Greek doxa (GA 18 140). Hellenistic/medieval rhetoric became *school* discipline (GA 18 110). Aristotle's careful analysis of pathē in *Rhet*. 2 connects to heavy influence of rhetorical *Affektenlehre* on Stoa (GA 18 122) and Christian theology from patristic period through Thomas and Luther (GA 18 177). Cites Wilhelm Dilthey as the only modern thinker who recognized "this vital historical initiative" (GA 18 177–178).

**Subsection 2 — "Time in and as Discipline" (book pp. 117–119).** Contains the volume-key claim: "While Heidegger may have thought he had effaced rhetoric in his new philosophical program of *Being and Time*, rhetoric effectively colonized his philosophy from within." Rhetorical accounts are "qualities of incarnate, interactive timefulness" (n. 23). "Rhetorical inquiry is corrective because of its temporal values, that is, time is corrective. Again, dailiness destroys theoretical complacency (GA 18 130–131), and theory grows out of *Alltäglichkeit* (GA 18 66)." Rhetoric is *paraphyes* (offshoot) of dialectic and ethics; *antistrophē* of dialectic (GA 18 125–130). Rhetorical time intervenes between Aristotelian *epistēmē* (timeless, *aei on*, GA 18 140) and Heidegger's qualification: "*Ich verstehe gar nicht den Seinscharakter der epistēmē, wenn ich nicht das Wovon [pros ti] in Betracht ziehe*" (GA 18 324). "Rhetorical discipline depends on dailiness; rhetorical practice makes public, *offenbar*, or *sichtbar* (GA 18 136), the true, *Wahr*, in the verisimilar, *wahrscheinlich* (GA 18 122)." *Logos* as *horismos* is not *alltäglich* (GA 18 36). Rhetoric does not address individuals but invents problematic; unlike medicine, rhetoric has no specific domain of expertise, is not a *technē* (GA 18 114). Aristotle's *De anima* 433a3–6: distance between knowledge of healing and healing itself; "life as praxis has nothing to do with *technē* (GA 18 183); rhetorical pragmatism is investigation, not instrument." Closing: "when we lost Greek rhetoric, we lost Greek politics."

**Subsection 3 — "Modern Times: Specific Twentieth-Century Revisions of Time" (book pp. 119–123).** Anchored by Peirce epigraph ("There is no mechanical truth in saying the past determines the future, rather than the future the past"). The most intellectual-historically dense subsection of the essay.

*A. Manifolds (book pp. 119–121).* Per Blattner's *Heidegger's Temporal Idealism* (1999): Dasein's temporality is "non-sequential manifold of future, present, and past, a complex simultaneity." BT Division I innovations represent "fundamental commitment in Heidegger's theory to a temporal manifold, rather than to a linear sequence of past, present, and future 'nows.'" Struever's claim: Heidegger in SS 1924 "found a very different account of temporality within the political parameters of the *Rhetoric*" — the rhetorical-political reading of Aristotle seeds the BT manifold. "In SS 1924 Aristotle 'improves on' Plato in his rhetorical account of the experience of time; the definitions produced are a Greek ontology *in nuce*, of being in motion (GA 18 321)." BT's tense-definitions: future is not "out there" but "coming to be" as process. Aristotle's *dynamis* reformulated in BT as "pressing ahead into," "for the sake of which" (n. 29). *Purposiveness, readiness, ambition* are key concepts (n. 30): potentialities, not complete actualities (contrast Aristotelian *energeia*). For past: affectivity is central — "the facticity of the past is a lingering, affect is *schon da*" (n. 31). Habit described in SS 1924 as process, "present-ing," a *Gegenwärtigen des Habenden* (GA 18 175); Greek focus on present as "making present" (GA 18 192). "In the *Rhetoric* and in *Being and Time*, passions, potentialities, and habits are ineluctably temporal, yet at the same time blurring, undermining the neat divisions of a flow of past, present, and future linearity." Politics requires simultaneous consideration of all three; "in politics there is no pure present, no unmotivated future, no isolated past. It is a political manifold."

*B. Realism in Aristotle, Dilthey, and Husserl? (book pp. 121–122).* SS 1924 lectures gloss the descriptive psychology of Dilthey and phenomenological *epochē* of Husserl. Heidegger appreciates "Dilthey's historical tact"; the 1924 lectures express "Diltheyan" concern for time as intrusive, pervasive, qualifying. Per Makkreel, Dilthey's temporality is realist; Dilthey's time derived from *Innewerden* (inner becoming), not Kantian *Vorstellen* (representative consciousness). "Dilthey rejects… the claim that time is the ideal form of inner experience." Heidegger "appreciates Dilthey's historical tact." The Aristotelian-rhetorical reading "gives a classical justification for the investigative habits of the psychological and phenomenological research of the early twentieth century."

*C. SS 1924 and SS 1925: "The History of the Concept of Time" (book pp. 122–123).* Per Kisiel on SS 1925 GA 20: time is again the temporality of Dasein; rhetoric's *Alltäglichkeit* "offers a way into the problematic." SS 1925 contains "brief, but significant, intrusion of rhetoric, where Heidegger defines rhetoric as 'the first part of logic, rightly understood'" (n. 41 = GA 20 264) and "diffuse echoes of the rhetorical formulae of SS 1924: there is the connection of language, life, and truth, since life is language capacity (the Aristotelian *zōon logon echon*); thus life capacity is political capacity, and thus discourse as social practice, *Miteinanderreden*, has priority over language as concept." Closes: "in SS 1925 a recollection of a rhetorical politics of language, an investigational initiative that underwrites the politics of time."

**Subsection 4 — "Special Effects of the Rhetorical Connection: Politics First" (book pp. 123–125).** Heidegger contrasts two Aristotelian statements: GA 18 127 "*Die Ethik gehört in die Politik*" (ethics belongs to politics) vs. GA 18 134 "*Rhetorik … steht innerhalb der politikē*" (rhetoric is inside politics). The first conveys "dependency of ethics on politics"; the second conveys rhetoric as *intrinsic*, "not dependent." *Rhetoric* 2's operations (*doxa, endoxa, pathē, prohairesis, pisteis, krisis*) "exhaust the limited repertoire of political acts." "Rhetorical material does not stand under, or derive from, theoretical axioms; insofar as rhetoric locates elemental functions specifically, it epitomizes political action, rather than supplements a general preexistent theory."

Two effects of integrated political/rhetorical interests: (1) *meson* defined as *Jeweiligkeit* is "functional, not immoral" — Dasein experiences only appropriate or inappropriate nows (per Blattner n. 43); (2) constancy of change — passion's basic nature as disposition is changeability (Meyer). The volume-key axial sentence: "Rhetoric gives us the means for and the analysis of authenticity. And there is no part of the political that does not manifest the constraints of time. In sum, a Heideggerian politics of time appears, even if briefly, in SS 1924 — a politics perhaps hypothesized by Arendt in her early fascination with Heidegger." Engagement with Stanley Fish on "inspired adhoccery" (multiculturality demands case-by-case improvisation); Fish fails because he doesn't take temporality seriously. Cicero (*De legibus*) on Roman law's "continuous identity"; Vico on figurative legal fictions. The *mesotēs* problem: geometric/mechanical definitions reflect "philosophical desire to empty life experience" but in SS 1924 "rhetorical kairos as meson in practice counters meson as theory, timeless and naive." "The study of language as it lives in *Alltäglichkeit* (GA 18 62) is a consideration of the experience of time, a thick texture of articulated habits, dispositions, and opinions."

**Subsection 5 — "Conclusion: The Uses of the Quarrel of Rhetoric and Philosophy" (book pp. 125–127).** *Rhetoric* does three things for Heidegger: (i) "recaptures the original Greek argument in all its incarnate, patho-logical complexity"; (ii) "expresses the Hellenic *endoxa*, and thus serves as an intellectual history"; (iii) "furnishes a way into, an analysis for, the account of life as authentic, the political life." Rhetoric's strength is its "self-limitation to acts of articulation"; rhetoric "rejects the odd premise that there are 'unarticulated thoughts' of a 'cabinet of consciousness' — that apolitical piece of furniture."

The pivotal interpretive move on Blattner: "if Blattner is correct that *Alltäglichkeit* is later eroded by the philosophical program of *Being and Time*, and slides into everydayness, ordinariness, and inauthenticity, in the 1924 lectures the extraordinary emphasis on Hellenic political rhetoric as originary matrix is at the same time an emphasis on *Alltäglichkeit* as timeful, or the solvent of eternity, the mode of eroding the timeless. Timeful politics, whose concepts are in nature, substantiate Heidegger's use of the *Physics* to express an ontology of oscillation between and transformation of potency and act, *dunamis* and *energeia*… This, I would argue, is of crucial interest for *Being and Time*. And Blattner asserts that, even later, Heidegger had absolutely no interest in eternity" (n. 46). Heidegger uses Aristotle's rhetorical revisions "as a model or prototype for his own revisions. It is a little alarming to see how intricated the Aristotelian and Heideggerian programs are in SS 1924 — it is very difficult to separate his admiring account from his own revisionary ambitions." Politically calibrated demurral: "the later relation between Heidegger's philosophy and his politics is a troubling one. My claim is simply that rhetorical hermeneutics enabled him to give lucid, deft accounts of basic political elements in SS 1924." Closes on Kisiel: SS 1924 is "embarrassment of riches" — "arguably, the best twentieth-century reading of Aristotle's *Rhetoric*." The closing question: "Is it the case that our political philosophy is difficult because it is insufficiently rhetorical?"

## Section 4: Contributor's Main Positions / Claims

Twelve positions captured in the JSON. Five are *core_essay_thesis* (P1, P2, P3, P4, P8); the rest are *supporting_argument* or *methodological*. The five core theses are:

- **P1 — Aristotle's *Rhetoric* IS a life science, internal to political life** — anchored at *innerhalb der politikē* (GA 18 134); supported by zoological-treatise intrications (GA 18 207–219) and *zoē praktikē meta logon* (GA 18 105). Rejects rhetoric-as-manipulation reading.
- **P2 — *Alltäglichkeit* means *timefulness*, not "ordinariness"** — the essay's titular claim. Catalytic agent of temporality in SS 1924; primary qualification of Miteinandersein in BT 178. Distinct from BT's later slide toward "everydayness" (contests Blattner).
- **P3 — Rhetoric is *dynamis tou theoresai*, not *technē*** — anchored at Rhet. 1355b26 / GA 18 122. Rhetoric's incompleteness ("not a complete *Wissen*") is its strength — describes "radical incompleteness, the unending, time-ridden task of politics."
- **P4 — Passions are not *Wissen* but *Befindlichkeiten*, dispositions oriented to others' dispositions** — anchored at GA 18 207, 232. Heidegger attributes pathē to whole person, not just soul (GA 18 122, 192, 197). Genetic source of BT §29 *Befindlichkeit*.
- **P8 — Rhetoric *colonizes* Being and Time from within** — anchored at p. 117 ("rhetoric effectively colonized his philosophy"). Continuity thesis: SS 1924 → BT genetic link. Direct opposition to Pöggeler's restriction thesis (Ch 7).

Seven supporting positions (P5–P7, P9–P12) cover: kairos/meson/Jeweiligkeit; antifoundationalist impurity of mind; hearing > speaking; Heideggerian politics of time anticipated by Arendt; redescription of rhetorical weakness as strength; the alarming intrication of Heideggerian and Aristotelian programs; SS 1924 as best 20th-century reading of *Rhetoric*. Together they form the supporting scaffolding for the core continuity-thesis.
