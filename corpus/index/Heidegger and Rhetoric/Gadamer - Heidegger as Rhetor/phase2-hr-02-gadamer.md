## 1. Unit Metadata

This is the volume's only first-person witness essay. Conducted across 1999-2001 with Gadamer (then 99-101 years old), the interview was reviewed in revision by Gadamer and Kemmann together and "documents the point our conversation had reached by December 4, 2001" — Gadamer's last interview of any substance before his death March 2002. The interview format imposes a distinctive analytic discipline: italic interviewer questions and roman responses are typeset distinctly, but the *italics are not merely solicitations* — Kemmann's questions stake analytical positions that organize the volume's reading of Gadamer's testimony. The essay's argumentative structure is the four Roman-section division: I. BIOGRAPHICAL INTRODUCTION (book pp. 47-48); II. ON THE STATUS OF RHETORIC (book pp. 48-49); III. THE RHETORIC OF PHILOSOPHY: USING THE EXAMPLE OF MARTIN HEIDEGGER (book pp. 49-55); IV. THE PHILOSOPHY OF RHETORIC IN THE UNIVERSE OF LINGUISTICALITY [SPRACHLICHKEIT] (book pp. 55-62).

**Essay thesis (one sentence)**: Aristotle's *Rhetoric* I.2 defines rhetoric correctly as *dynamis* (a power belonging to being-human), not as a *technē* — and Heidegger's 1924 lecture course made this *dynamis*-of-*logos*-grounded-in-*pathē* into the hermeneutics of facticity that survives in *Truth and Method*; what Heidegger lost was not the rhetorical insight but the conversational form proper to it.

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### I. BIOGRAPHICAL INTRODUCTION (book pp. 47-48 / PDF 53-54)

The opening question — "How did you, Professor Gadamer, come to rhetoric — through Heidegger?" — sets up Gadamer's longest sustained autobiographical sequence. Gadamer's response *displaces* the expected answer (rhetoric came through Heidegger's lecture) by reporting a more complex pre-history: he had already, before encountering Heidegger, "realized that I couldn't do anything, that I didn't have the tools, and for that reason I studied classical philology — and under the direction of Prof. Lommatzsch I studied especially rhetoric, both Attic and Roman rhetoric, Cicero and Quintilian." Lommatzsch (n. 3) "had given me Cicero to read so that I could practice my Latin"; under Friedländer (n. 4) Gadamer began to ask "what was slowly developing between Latin and Greek." The biographical line that Kemmann's question would have placed *as* Heidegger's seminar-effect is here repositioned: rhetoric came to Gadamer through *philology*, then *was confirmed* by Heidegger's Aristotle teaching. The order matters: Gadamer's late-rhetoric (Cicero/Quintilian) competence is the predisposition that enabled his receptivity to Heidegger's *dynamis*-reading.

The decisive 1923 Freiburg moment is recounted carefully. Gadamer arrived as "messenger from Marburg" (sent by Natorp and Hartmann, n. 2), having been "completely enthralled" by Heidegger's 1922 *Phänomenologische Interpretationen zu Aristoteles* — the so-called "Natorp Report" (n. 1, *Dilthey-Jahrbuch* 6, 1989). Heidegger invited him "privately and confidentially, to read Aristotle with him alone once a week in the evening. And there I grasped, to my great surprise, that *logos* has something to do with speaking. In Marburg's Neo-Kantianism logos was just 'reason.' Yes, I was astonished." This is Gadamer's foundational moment: the discovery that *logos = legein* (speaking), not *logos = ratio* (reason). The discovery's affective weight ("astonished") tracks its philosophical magnitude — the entire Marburg-Cohen-Natorp-Hartmann doctrinal frame yields to a single pedagogical scene of Aristotle-read-aloud.

Two further biographical anchors are set in this section. (i) The SS 1923 Freiburg seminar on Aristotelian *phronēsis* "occurred, and that ignited my thinking to a much greater degree." This 1923 phronēsis seminar is the seedbed for the Section IV closing claim that "*phronēsis* is a whole virtue; it is not an ability, but a being, as Heidegger correctly stated" (book p. 61). (ii) Plato's *Symposium*, "particularly the last phase where Socrates is told, 'You will not understand what I will now say.' Isn't it the truth? *[laughs]* — Well, that's the origin of my hermeneutics." The Symposium passage establishes hermeneutic finitude — the insufficiency of any single utterance to convey what is meant — as Gadamer's hermeneutic origin point, *prior to and independent of* Heidegger.

The section closes with a critical confession: "the topic that chiefly interested Heidegger at that time, the *pathē* doctrine, this aspect of Aristotle's rhetoric — that you correctly remark Heidegger especially emphasized then — that topic I did not understand at the time. I was still too immature for that. After the lecture we, the students, seldom discussed it. The development that Heidegger himself achieved is something other than what I slowly accumulated, coming from my completely different starting point." The retrospective acknowledgment that the young Gadamer *missed* the pathē-doctrine frames Section IV as a delayed reckoning with what was always there — and reframes the whole interview as Gadamer's late-life recovery of the pathē-thesis he was once "too immature for."

### II. ON THE STATUS OF RHETORIC (book pp. 48-49 / PDF 54-55)

This is the volume's shortest section but its most doctrinally compressed. Kemmann opens by referencing two prior Gadamer conversations — with Kühne-Bertram & Rodi (*Dilthey-Jahrbuch* 11, 1997-98, n. 5) and with Grondin (*Gadamer Lesebuch* 1997, n. 6) — in which Gadamer "pleaded for the broad concept of rhetoric" that "contained all the humanities until modern times." The prior position is the Vico-Dilthey-Hellenistic broad-rhetoric tradition. Kemmann's question pivots: "Your teacher, Martin Heidegger, to the contrary, referred directly to Aristotle and already established rhetoric as the *dynamis* of humans." Kemmann then quotes the volume's locus classicus directly — Heidegger's GA 18, 114 (n. 7): "Aristotle defined rhetoric (1.2) as *dynamis*. This definition is to be retained even though Aristotle often designates it as *technē*. This designation is incorrect, whereas *dynamis* is the correct definition." The quotation is the volume's primary input on tension VT2; it is also the *single passage* Phase 0 identified as the volume's locus classicus (Phase 0 §1).

Kemmann presses: "What is at stake in this alternative (technē/dynamis)?" The italicized question is itself an analytical claim — that the alternative *has* a stake worth identifying; that the dynamis-side is the live option. Gadamer's response is layered. First, *technē* is "a very general expression, *if* one did not mean mathematics specifically. So the special character of Heidegger's statement is that it is a probing attempt." Second, the diachronic reading: "Heidegger was still using *technē*, until he began emphatically using the concept of *energeia*. And so he associated the concept of *dynamis* with the concept of *energeia*, and missed the other one. That is the reason." The dynamis-energeia coupling (Met. IX/Theta) is what eventually displaced the technē-rhetoric register in Heidegger's thinking; but Gadamer's "missed the other one" registers a critical residue — the productive/poietic dimension that *technē* would have grafted onto rhetoric is *not* preserved in the dynamis-energeia coupling. This is Gadamer's subtle correction of Heidegger's framework.

The section's affirmative close: "Even from today's perspective, I would want, like Heidegger, to characterize rhetoric as more a *dynamis*. That should indicate that rhetoric belongs to being human and is not just an occasional ability. Actually, everyone is a speaker." Three commitments are made simultaneously: (i) the *dynamis*-reading is correct (alongside Heidegger); (ii) rhetoric is *constitutive* of being-human (not occasional, not occupational); (iii) universality — "everyone is a speaker." This last clause is the section's tonally definitive statement, stripped of philological apparatus.

### III. THE RHETORIC OF PHILOSOPHY: USING THE EXAMPLE OF MARTIN HEIDEGGER (book pp. 49-55 / PDF 55-61)

Section III is the longest section and structurally the most complex. Kemmann opens with a Heidegger quotation from SS 1924 (n. 8 = GA 18, 169f): "I only wish to point out that it might be more appropriate if philosophers could decide to consider what speaking to others really means." Kemmann's framing question — "Who are the 'philosophers' and what led to their losing sight of speaking to someone and conversation?" — invites Gadamer to identify Heidegger's targets. Gadamer names them: "Richard Kroner in Freiburg and Nicolai Hartmann in Marburg," and, with qualification, Husserl ("he actually never tried to hide his admiration for Husserl, at least not at that time"). The Hartmann-Heidegger contrast is then developed: Hartmann was "a knight — so aristocratic and reserved" who "had a wonderful relationship with his students" but lacked successful teaching; Heidegger was the rhetorical teacher who "fascinated me right away."

A substantive philosophical claim follows. Hartmann "carefully read Aristotle, [but] it did not actually transfer to his own thinking; whereas Heidegger accomplished this and fascinated me right away." The contrast is *philological-pedagogical*: not "did Hartmann read Aristotle" (yes) but "did Aristotle come alive for Hartmann's students" (no). Heidegger's rhetorical mastery converted philological reading into living philosophy: "That Aristotle suddenly became a phenomenologist was exactly what Heidegger accomplished and was quite the exception" (book p. 50).

The second sub-question intensifies: "Did Heidegger take to heart in his own thinking his remark about philosophers?" Gadamer's response is unguarded: "Heidegger's manner of lecturing was already enormously fascinating — oh yes, rhetorically masterful! I wouldn't say that his written work was equally convincing, but as a speaker he had an enormous radiance. Enormous, you cannot even imagine. We were all awestruck!" The published *Heidegger* and the speaking *Heidegger* are sharply distinguished. The next sentence — "He worked so long on Husserl, until Husserl believed him about Aristotle" — captures the rhetoric of phenomenological recognition: Heidegger had to *persuade* Husserl that Aristotle was a phenomenologist, and Husserl's eventual belief is itself a rhetorical-philosophical achievement.

The third sub-question — "What does speaking to others concretely mean for Heidegger?" — yields the section's most candid biographical disclosure: "Ah, this question is very interesting. It was very difficult to speak with Heidegger because he wanted to understand step by step. So he did not terribly much like to discuss with me. I understood too quickly. He didn't like that; he needed the steps. In this he was naturally far superior to me and thereby was a much greater thinker in that he continually saw intermediate steps. I mean, I always learned an incredible amount when I listened to him — but it was not a conversation. I must say that until the end of my studies, I still didn't know whether he thought I was any good at all" (book p. 50). Gadamer's hermeneutics emerges from this contrast: Heidegger refused *Gespräch* (conversation), preferring *Vortrag* (lecture) and step-by-step exposition that Gadamer's quick comprehension *interrupted*. The Heideggerian-conversational refusal is structural, not personal. The Hannah Arendt anecdote that follows — "How he educated Hannah Arendt, wouldn't you agree? She was an interesting, talented Jewess" — registers Heidegger's pedagogical possessiveness in a different register, while the Jakob Klein-misjudgment story ("But that's totally crazy ... in such a small town everyone would know about it!") establishes Heidegger's success at audience-management across registers.

Gadamer's own retrospective: "I had been able to sanction Löwith's own path as a bold path, although at first when Löwith returned to Heidelberg, he sharply and publicly attacked Heidegger" (book p. 51). The Löwith case shows Heidegger's recoverable humanity — he could "be very humane at the appropriate moment." But the Bultmann-Heidegger pairing "worked completely," whereas Hartmann-Heidegger "didn't succeed, and successful teaching was clearly on Heidegger's side."

The section's pivot to *language* (book p. 52): "behind all of Heidegger, there was the wish to overcome the Latin elements in the language of philosophy, and justly so. There are only Latin elements in all the foreign words in our discussion. *'Diskussion'* — I can say what I want, but it is, of course, just Latin. Kant had still lectured in Latin, then he translated and finally began to speak German. However, his students had were always still in Latin. Even for Heidegger this problem with Latin remained, so that he always strove to find the natural German language." The anti-Latin thesis is structural: Heidegger's whole linguistic project was to break the Latin overlay on philosophical German. Gadamer endorses this project ("justly so") but flags its ongoing incompleteness even in Heidegger.

The Scheler death anecdote (book pp. 52-53) supplies the section's most extended exemplum. Max Scheler died May 19, 1928; Heidegger's obituary lecture (the following Monday, printed in *Metaphysische Anfangsgründe der Logik*, GA 26, n. 9) was the venue. "I will never forget the moment when he dealt with the death of Scheler." But Gadamer immediately shifts to a more revealing story: in "this famous lecture, 'The Hermeneutics of Facticity'" (n. 10 = GA 63, the SS 1923 lecture; the chronology is slightly elided), Heidegger "came to speak about Scheler. 'On this point Scheler makes this reference: "Aristotle wrote in *de partibus animae*..."' And then Heidegger roared from the lectern, 'Now gentlemen, ladies and gentlemen, what has Scheler just quoted? This text doesn't exist.'" Gadamer privately corrected Heidegger: "Excuse me, professor, you have misunderstood something here. You didn't recognize a typographical error. Instead of *anima-e*, there must be an *l*, therefore *animal* period, and then it would be *de partibus animal(ium)*. This text does exist." Heidegger: "Hum, aha, he's correct." In the next lecture Heidegger publicly retracted: "I wish to make a correction; naturally it was thus and so from *de partibus animalium*. I only return to this so that no one would come to the insane idea that I wanted to refute Scheler with the help of a typo." Gadamer's reading: "He would become so possessed by something but was also immensely accomplished in extracting himself."

The de partibus animae/animal anecdote is rhetorically functional. It shows (i) Heidegger's polemical intensity; (ii) his philological accountability; (iii) Gadamer's quiet correction-from-below; (iv) Heidegger's capacity to retract publicly without losing rhetorical command. The anecdote is *exemplum* of Heidegger-as-rhetor — the masterful orator who is also philologically vulnerable and correctable.

The section's final pivot — to the SS 1924 lecture's stated aim — sets up the section's affective climax. Kemmann quotes (n. 10 = GA 18, 3): "The lecture does not aim at something philosophical. It concerns the understanding of basic concepts in their conceptuality. The aim is philological and is to practice somewhat more the reading of philosophers." And (n. 12 = GA 63, 329, the *Hermeneutics of Facticity* close): "What is at stake is not to say something new, but to actually say what the Ancients meant." Kemmann's question — "Does this remark indicate a genuine philosophical point — or does it concern 'mere rhetoric'?" — is deliberately provocative. Gadamer's response is exact: "The final sentence is very emphatic. What is at stake is not to say something new, but to actually say only what the Ancients meant. That was his colossal demand. However, the first sentences, I would think, are a type of self-defense. He said that in order not to fall into the warranted suspicion that he just wanted to push his own views. That's the meaning. I'm quite sure that's what he wished to say here" (book p. 53). The split — colossal demand on top, self-defense underneath — preserves the philosophical seriousness of the philological-aim claim while diagnosing the rhetorical strategy that frames it.

The section closes with Heidegger's *teaching*-thesis (n. 13 = GA 63, 327): "Teaching implies, according to just its meaning: to speak to another, approach the other in the manner of communicating. The actual being of a teacher is: to stand before an other and to speak to him, and in such a way that the other, listening, follows him." Gadamer's reaction: "Listening follows him!" The exclamation is Section III's affective climax — a moment in which the structure of teaching (speaking-such-that-the-other-listening-follows) is named with the urgency of recognition. The closing sub-section (book pp. 54-55) extends the thesis to writing: "the conversation with the audience is sometimes present even in written texts. Actually all of my works were spoken. I then changed them with difficulty and effort into a written form that should preserve as much of the spoken character as possible. For that reason they read better, whereas most other peoples' texts do not allow one to notice that one is being addressed." Gadamer's whole corpus is hereby positioned as *Gespräch*-derivative, oral-in-origin.

### IV. THE PHILOSOPHY OF RHETORIC IN THE UNIVERSE OF LINGUISTICALITY [SPRACHLICHKEIT] (book pp. 55-62 / PDF 61-68)

Section IV is the section the volume's tension VT2 (rhetoric as dynamis vs. technē) is *resolved* in — not by abandoning the dispute but by identifying rhetorical *dynamis* with the human capacity-for-understanding. The section's argumentative arc moves through (a) a political reckoning (book pp. 55-56), (b) a poetics-versus-rhetoric reckoning (book p. 56), (c) the pathē-as-ground-of-logos thesis (book pp. 57-58), (d) the Sprachlichkeit-as-hermeneutics definition (book pp. 59-60), (e) the rhetoric-as-overarching-concept claim (book p. 60), (f) the phronēsis-as-virtue and rhetoric-politics balance (book pp. 61-62), and (g) the closing communal-listening definition of rhetoric/hermeneutics.

Kemmann opens (book p. 55) with two SS 1924 citations. First (n. 16 = GA 18, 117): rhetoric concerns "speaking as a basic mode of human being, as being with one another. Therefore, an understanding of this *legein* also presents new aspects concerning the comprehension of the being of being with one another." This is the SS 1924 thesis that Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* yields a *hermeneutic of Mitsein*. Second (n. 17 = "Die Sprache" 1950 in *Unterwegs zur Sprache*, 32): the later Heidegger "distanced himself from a nominalistic philosophy of language. But, at the same time, in his consideration of 'the living of mortals in the speaking of language,' he avoided returning again explicitly to rhetoric." The opening question crystallizes the chapter's titular problem: "What did Heidegger win, what did he lose, in doing this?" — that is, in moving from the SS 1924 explicit-rhetoric register to the late "speaking of language" register without naming rhetoric.

Gadamer's response begins (book p. 55): "Difficult to say. Naturally also in my case, since my encounter with him was at first so dramatic." Then the political turn: "Later, a certain distance appeared (between us) due to his political mistake and the way he nevertheless played along, understandably so, for a little bit. However, everybody who was not blind must have known. He erred when he did this. Still, a deficiency in the handling of his case is that no one said clearly, what I, however, say at every opportunity: He was the sole university president who occupied a German professorial chair, and who did not retain his presidency for even one year." The political reckoning is concise but principled: Gadamer affirms the error ("he erred"), affirms Heidegger's collusion ("the way he nevertheless played along"), affirms the publicness of what should have been seen ("everybody who was not blind must have known") — and yet protests the case-handling-deficiency by asserting the factual proportion that Heidegger's rectorate lasted less than a year. Footnote 31 cites Gadamer's own essay "Über die politische Inkompetenz der Philosophie" (*Hermeneutische Entwürfe*, Tübingen: Mohr Siebeck, 2000, 35-42) for the developed treatment.

The political failure is then linked structurally to Heidegger's later poetics-only turn. Kemmann: "Did his retreat out of politics contribute to his neglect of nonpoetic forms of speaking?" Gadamer: "Yes, I believe so. Concerning poetic forms of speaking, he just had such a trust in his own philosophical fantasy that he did not consider the other forms worthwhile. ... Heidegger was not to be deterred. Naturally, he could always bring his own good thoughts to any reasonable idea. But one must nevertheless say that poetic interpretation was his weakest area" (book p. 56). The Bröcker poetry anecdote follows: Heidegger arrived at Walter Bröcker's with "his poetic attempts"; Bröcker said "Oh yes, that. The fire is over there." "And Heidegger was not offended by this!" Gadamer extends: "Also the part he played with Celan — he is himself to be blamed. Although it grieved him and he was prepared for complete admiration. But — oh well." The political withdrawal and the poetics-only turn are *correlated*: both forms of withdrawal from rhetorical-political-conversational engagement.

The section's central pivot (book pp. 57-58) is the *pathē*-as-ground-of-logos thesis. Kemmann's frame quotes Heidegger SS 1924 layered triple-citation: (n. 18 = GA 18, 117): "how *logos* had its ground in *pathē*. The *pathē* are 'the fundamental possibilities, by which Dasein primarily orients itself, is attuned.'" (n. 19 = GA 18, 262): "The possibility to speak about things is first presented within the so-characterized attunement [*Sich-Befindens*] and being-in-the-world, to the extent that the appearance, which the thing has in its immediate environment, has been removed." (n. 20 = GA 18, 289): "Greek ontology also took its beginning from a *pathos*. ('The discussion of the being of beings out of a fear that it will at some point no longer be.')" The triple citation establishes (i) pathē as fundamental possibilities of attunement; (ii) Sich-Befinden as the pre-articulate ground from which speaking emerges; (iii) Greek ontology *itself* as pathos-originating — Greek philosophy is born of fear-of-non-being. Kemmann's question — "In this context what is the meaning of characterizing *pathē* as 'ground' and 'fundamental possibilities'?" — invites Gadamer to interpret a text Gadamer once admitted he had not understood at the time (book p. 48).

Gadamer's response is the interview's most carefully calibrated philosophical statement. "That is the distrust of speaking. So speaking *is* not the first! There is still something, which ... therefore, one makes a decision about something without at the same time being able to say it to others. In this way one can, so to speak, justify what Heidegger said. It makes good sense to say that" (book p. 57). Speaking is *not* primary; pre-articulate decision precedes articulation. Kemmann presses: do *pathē* have *argumentative* power? "It almost sounds like the *pathē* are a final judge — if one reversed the path and from *logos* returned to *pathos*." Gadamer: "Yes, but with the caveat that it is also — warranted [*bewährt*]. It is not simply a game" (book p. 57). The German *bewährt* is the section's most important single word: it protects pathos from collapsing into pure-affective relativism while preserving its ground-status. Pathē are not arbitrary; they are *witnessed*, *tested*, warranted by life. "This circumstance then leads him to *logos*. I remember how he said, '*logos*,' and I was completely puzzled that it should mean 'language' and not 'reason.' I was completely puzzled, but then over and again it became very clear." The puzzle of legein-vs-ratio (Section I anchor) returns here; in light of pathē-as-ground, the legein-translation makes sense.

Gadamer's signature thesis follows: "*Logos* is able to express something in such a way that what is really intended is transferred to the other. And yet every logos is in some manner incomplete. That was decisive, I believe, also for Heidegger" (book p. 57). *Every logos is incomplete* — this is the source-formulation for the closing communal-listening claim (book p. 62) and for the *Wahrheit und Methode* hermeneutic finitude. The completeness/incompleteness of logos is structural, not contingent failure.

Kemmann then introduces the term that names Gadamer's hermeneutic advance: "Nevertheless must not a being-affected [*Betroffensein*] already exist, before one can speak about it?" Gadamer: "There is no doubt that Heidegger saw that. I mean, my own accomplishment beyond him, if one may call it that at all, goes exactly in this direction!" (book pp. 57-58). The self-location is exact: Gadamer extends Heidegger by foregrounding *Betroffensein* as the pre-articulate moment that conversation must answer to. Heidegger had the pathē-thesis; Gadamer carries the pre-articulate further. "Heidegger himself is motivated by an almost childish compulsion in his professional development. One did not see this because he didn't make it very public. In public he would express this in words. In this sense he also saw that words are not the first" (book p. 58).

The fear-uncanniness sequence (book p. 58) is more complex. Kemmann (n. 22 = GA 18, 261): "When we feel uncanny we begin to speak." Gadamer's reading: "I would say this is said in slight mockery." But the genuine residue: "the avoidance of the uncanny itself ... what is meant by the uncanny is the lifelong question concerning the afterlife. Fear is fear of death!" Gadamer hesitates to take fear as ultimate: "fear, I believe, is not his last word." The Heideggerian death-fear is a *partial* truth, not the whole. Heidegger's last visit to Gadamer: "On the day I saw him for the last time, I still remember that he came toward me in the yard, taking his wife's arm. Five days later he was dead, or eight days, I cannot remember. We were actually very cheerful. ... how he came, guided by his wife's arm, moved me very deeply. That was anything but a hiding from one's own fear. No, it was more, but fear always played a part in Heidegger" (book p. 58). The image — Heidegger guided by his wife's arm — supplies a final witness: Heidegger acknowledged fear-of-death without being reduced to it.

Gadamer's own conclusion to the section's first half (book p. 59): "I conclude: *ignoramus* is our function. ... we cannot understand them any differently than as we, as all humans, similarly deal with all cases of destruction." The Latinity is approved here — *ignoramus* is the Latin tag for human-finitude, an exception within Gadamer's general anti-Latin agreement with Heidegger.

The Sprachlichkeit-as-hermeneutics definition follows (book p. 59). Kemmann asks: "How do you yourself see the relationship between linguisticality and attunement [*Befindlichkeit*]?" Gadamer: "*Pathei mathos* (n. 24 = Aeschylus, *Agamemnon*, 177) is the decisive statement. One learns through experience. That is not a particularly exceptional idea. I have only recalled this statement because one always views it from epistemological points of view. But in life we do not relate to ourselves according to epistemological perspectives; rather we must live our own experiences." The Aeschylean adage couples pathos with mathēsis without epistemological mediation. Gadamer's defense of Heidegger's pathē-doctrine *against epistemological objections* runs through pathei mathos: experience is not converted into propositional knowledge; it is *lived*.

The body-and-language extension follows. Kemmann (n. 25 = GA 18, 203): "in fact the body participates in the genesis of *pathē*." Gadamer to Grondin (n. 26 = "Die Logik des verbum interius", 30): "pointing... is also already language and the whole body speaks too, the language of gesture, the language of intonation, and so on. Linguisticality is only an approximating concept. One must go much, much further." Sprachlichkeit so understood includes the body-as-speaking. "How far must one then go?" "As far as it is at all possible! One can even speak by being silent. That was also one of Heidegger's favorite problems. He said being silent is speaking ... that even being silent is speaking. He liked that a lot, already in Marburg" (book pp. 59-60). Heidegger's *Schweigen-als-Sprechen* thesis is endorsed.

The Sprachlichkeit-definition arrives (book p. 60): "Then what does linguisticality [*Sprachlichkeit*] mean? *Everything that can allow something to be understood. Therefore, hermeneutics; thereby its indefinability.* That humans can express themselves in gestures, noises, spoken and written words is, however, something external. It is much more important how one moves within linguisticality and that one is always prepared to seek new possibilities that allow the other to speak and that allow you to comprehend what he wished to say." Sprachlichkeit *is* hermeneutics; its scope is whatever allows understanding. The "indefinability" clause is striking — hermeneutics is a *practice*, not a closed concept.

The next paragraph stakes the volume's most extensive single claim: "No one who expresses something says what he really means. Because no one can ever have the last word, everything that one says only tends in a particular direction. And thereby remains vulnerable and understandable. *In truth, rhetoric is the overarching concept for everything that has to do with the other*" (book p. 60). Rhetoric is the *master concept*. Hermeneutics, in this register, is rhetoric properly understood. The Section II broad-rhetoric position (rhetoric containing all the humanities until modern times) is here philosophically grounded.

Kemmann (n. 27 = GA 18, 165): "The interaction with the *pathē*, Heidegger says, is a question of *hexis*: 'Ethos and pathē are constitutive of *legein* itself.'" And to Grondin (n. 28 = "Dialogischer Rückblick", 292): "for the 'old, honorable concept of rhetoric' you referred to that form of knowledge that is discussed in Plato's *The Statesman*, that is to know how 'to find the right word at the right moment.' Clearly such knowledge requires *dynamis* as Aristotle defined it. 'To be able to see what speaks for a thing,' says Heidegger" (n. 29 = GA 18, 118). Kemmann's question: "What relationship does the rhetorical *dynamis* have to the human ability to understand?" Gadamer: "*It is the same dynamis!*" (book p. 60). The exclamation is the section's most concise philosophical statement. Rhetorical *dynamis* (Aristotle Rhet. I.2) is identical with the hermeneutic capacity to understand. The volume's doctrinal core sits on this five-word answer.

The implications are drawn out: "We consider what speaks for a thing. But if one does not know that one only says the half of it, then the other can also not understand you. The other needs exactly what one has not said. ... We have understood each other, but that cannot be put into words again. Also, our figures of speech are like that: 'I get along well with him.' (Literally, 'I understand myself well with him.') ... One only speaks about understanding when it is not obvious. And when one has understood something, one has not yet agreed to it; rather one now knows what the other meant. Therein lies the winnings. I emphasize this so much, because this is the way we come to understand one another! It consists first in just listening to the other and considering whether he couldn't perhaps also be correct" (book p. 60). Understanding is consitituted by (i) listening; (ii) considering the other's potential rightness — even before agreement. *Considering whether he couldn't perhaps also be correct* is the constitutive moment of hermeneutic understanding, distinct from agreement.

Kemmann pivots (book p. 61) to phronēsis: "What is the status of knowing in rhetoric? Is it *technē* or *phronēsis*?" Gadamer: "In experience one does not know how to hold these apart. They are intertwined. Basically, I believe that is how one must respond. *Phronēsis* is a whole virtue; it is not an ability, but a being, as Heidegger correctly stated." Kemmann: "And so we notice more quickly when a discourse lacks appropriateness, than when it is present?" Gadamer: "Yes, but also we cannot express everything, even when it is very moderate. The speaker who is not self-critical is on the wrong path." Phronēsis-as-virtue secures the structural reason rhetoric and ethics are bound: rhetoric (dynamis), like phronēsis (virtue/hexis), is *not* an instrumental technē.

The closing-section pivot: rhetoric-and-politics. Kemmann (n. 30 = "Dialogischer Rückblick", 295): "Aristotle ... appears to have understood rhetoric more narrowly when he placed it as a *technē* within politics and thereby established it as the practical coming to a decision. Freeing rhetoric from this determination has not necessarily agreed with that. Again and again it is even claimed that the broad sense of this concept leads right away to demanding too much rhetoric or to surrendering it to complete relativity. How do you evaluate this risk? Does rhetoric need to be tied to politics?" Gadamer: "Well, you cannot say that in general. It depends. You once read my essay (n. 31 = 'Über die politische Inkompetenz der Philosophie') about why the philosopher does not really live for politics. ... already both are there. I am tempted to go into that, yet I must [see the limitations]. And if one does not achieve this perspective that includes both, then politics will just not become politics, but something that is the opposite of politics: that one knows everything ahead of time. The politics that one wanted as politics turns out not to be one. Rather, politics is exactly what one instinctively does and that then takes effect by itself" (book p. 61). Politics that knows everything in advance ceases to be politics — a Heideggerian-existential point about the openness-of-political-action.

Kemmann's summary statement — "A philosophical rhetoric would take the political into account, but not be absorbed into the political" — receives Gadamer's affirmation: "That is it. But it is certainly a very arduous task to correctly use and choose between the broad concept of rhetoric and the one common today" (book pp. 61-62). The formula *take into account but not be absorbed* is the section's concise rhetoric-politics binding.

The closing remarks (book p. 62) gather the section's themes. On rhetoric pedagogy: "It is surprising that you never really held a class on rhetoric, although rhetoric often plays a part in your writings." Gadamer: "To be a good defender of my option, it was just not possible. ... I no longer felt that I was at the apex of my philological knowledge. There were rather heavy demands placed on me." This is a *concession* — Gadamer admits he did not develop a rhetoric pedagogy and links this to philological-competence concerns. The first-five-years-of-life thesis follows: "It always depends on the first five years of life. ... In these first five years one learns to speak. ... it is the colossal power of learning to speak — it is the building of the world!" The English-as-commerce + mother-tongues thesis: "But there will be mother tongues everywhere. And that is what we must fight for. Every language has a new point of view." The closing line: "But surely it is true that rhetoric is, by far, not yet a possible word for what it itself is. In the end it is a communal listening. That is what my hermeneutics always had in view: there is no last, definitive word. That is given to no one. If the other misunderstands me, then I must speak differently until he understands me. We are all always only underway." Then Kemmann's ceremonial close: "Professor Gadamer, thank you very much for this conversation."

The closing sentence — *We are all always only underway* — is the interview's distillation of Gadamer's hermeneutic finitude: rhetoric/hermeneutics is the form of life of speakers always-on-the-way to understanding, never arrived. The titular Heidegger-as-rhetor is Gadamer-as-rhetor by extension; the conversation is itself the form rhetoric takes when it has been hermeneutically completed.
