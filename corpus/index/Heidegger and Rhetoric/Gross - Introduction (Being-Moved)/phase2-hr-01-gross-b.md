## 1. Unit Metadata & One-Sentence Thesis

Gross's Section IV argues that pathos is the very ground out of which logos springs forth and on which logos remains continuously suspended — a rhetorical-ontological priority Heidegger discovers in Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II during SS 1924, articulates in *Being and Time* (1927) under the partially equivalent name *Befindlichkeit*, but progressively loses through the hermeneutic turn that converts the rhetorical-political dimension of human community into the formal individuality of "the resolute Freeman" (BT 434), with measurable political consequences traceable into the 1933 Nazi turn.

In one sentence: **the priority of pathos is the volume's most-cited single thesis, and it is irreducibly bound to a political-philosophical critique of Heidegger's own post-1924 trajectory.**

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

The argumentative arc of Section IV unfolds in four movements, then closes with a programmatic political-philosophical denouement and the 49 numbered notes.

**Movement 1 — Bridge from Section III to Section IV (book p. 27, top)**. The transitional paragraph at the top of book p. 27 — still typeset within Section III but argumentatively initiating Section IV — quotes Heidegger's GA 18 122 verbatim in German: *"[Diese pathē, 'Affekte' sind nicht Zustände des Seelischen, es handelt sich um eine Befindlichkeit des Lebenden in seiner Welt … Die Affekte spielen eine fundamentale Rolle bei der Bestimmung des Seins-in-der-Welt, des Seins mit und zu anderen (122)]."* This is the structurally central GA 18 quote: affects are *not* psychic states; they *are* the Befindlichkeit of the living being in its world. Gross then specifies the canonical BT 178 endorsement — Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II "must be taken as the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being-with-one-another" — and announces that "SS 1924 is where this discovery was made public for the first time." The Cicero-tradition (pathē as conditions-of-soul) and the Roman-rhetorical-tradition (pathē as ancillary to logos) are explicitly rejected as readings of Aristotle: "Aristotelian pathos, [Heidegger] argues in his unique phenomenological language, serves as the very foundation for logos, or social discourse" (book p. 27).

**Movement 2 — The Roman compromise and its discontents (book pp. 27–30)**. Section IV proper opens (book p. 27, "IV. THE PRIORITY OF PATHOS") with the Platonic caution against emotion (*Gorgias* 459c) framed as a political symptom — "the symptom of a profoundly divisive political philosophy separating experts from the masses." The Roman tradition's compromise is traced through Cicero's *De oratore* (Antonius's passionate-style speech, 2.44.186–187; the diction-rich-and-copious aphorism, 2.52.214) and Quintilian's *Institutio oratoria* 6.1.7 ("Appeals to emotion are necessary if there are no other means…"). Cicero's *De inventione* opening provides the compromise's signature formula: *ratio atque oratio*, "powerful reasoning allied with powerful speech." But the Roman compromise — "by sheer fiat" (book p. 29) — leaves logos with the monopoly on truth's prestige and treats pathos as "regrettably necessary." Gross's exemplar of the contemporary continuation of this prejudice is Troels Engberg-Pedersen (in Rorty's anthology, note 45): pathos is *Wahrheitsfindung*'s reluctant supplement when addressing imperfect mass audiences. Gross's polemic is sharp: "When not, one may presume, the account of human emotion often added to rational discourse as a pragmatic afterthought may be unceremoniously dropped."

**Movement 3 — Heidegger's two-Aristotles (book pp. 30–32)**. The hinge moment of Section IV: Aristotle's *Rhetoric* I.1 (1354a) is essentially Platonic — the famous straightedge-rule analogy ("as if someone made a straightedge rule crooked before using it") condemns pathos. "But Heidegger finds a very different Aristotle in book 2, where pathos provides the very condition for the possibility of judgment, or *krisis*." Heidegger's GA 18 169 quote — given both in English and verbatim German — programmatically opens the pathos investigation: "With the demonstration of the fundamental role that the pathē play in *krinein*, we realize at the same time the possibility of seeing more concretely the ground of logos itself. *[Mit dem Aufweis dieser fundamentalen Rolle der pathē im krinein selbst bekommen wir zugleich die Möglichkeit, den Boden des logos selbst konkreter zu sehen (169)]*." Pathē are *not* afterthoughts — "they are, one could say, before-thought."

The doxa-pathos cell follows. Doxa is *not* propositional opinion but pretheoretical *Dafürsein* — "authentic being-with-one-another in the world," *eigentliche Entdecktheit des Miteinanderseins-in-der-Welt* (GA 18 149). Doxa is structured by *Ja-sagen* without examination ("kein Untersuchen, Reflektieren," GA 18 137) and by openness (*Offenheit*) that always could-be-otherwise (*Es könnte auch anders sein*). The Thales example — saying yes to water as the *archē* of Being "in some preconscious way" — illustrates doxa as protolinguistic disposition. The block-quote climax at GA 18 151 (book pp. 31–32) gives Heidegger's most concentrated formulation: *"So ist die doxa Boden, Quelle und Antrieb für das Miteinanderreden"* — doxa is ground, source, and motive for speaking-with-one-another. Pathos is what makes doxa dynamic rather than fixed: "the pathos — a negotiation over doxa — is always latent."

**Movement 4 — The limits-of-the-human and the Christian-theological genealogy (book pp. 33–34)**. Gross's pivotal limits-claim: "It is precisely our limits that make us human (not God or simple animal) and motivate us to eloquence. *Moving and persuading people is thus an essential human activity*" (book p. 33). This bridges to the Christian-theological genealogy. Heidegger's pathos doctrine is "secular" but draws on a tradition: Augustine (*De civitate Dei*, *Pelagianische Schriften*), Damascenus (*De fide orthodoxa* II), Gregory of Nyssa, Nemesius, pseudo-Dionysius Areopagita, and Dilthey's *Weltanschauung und Analyse des Menschen seit Renaissance und Reformation* — a constellation of patristic-medieval-modern figures Gross declines to reconstruct in detail (delegating that to "an intellectual historian"). His narrower thesis: "pathos alone has the power to provide logos with a critical moment, *kairos*."

The Lutheran climax (book p. 34) is structural. Klaus Dockhorn — the romanticist whose 1966 review of Gadamer's *Wahrheit und Methode* in the *Göttingische Gelehrte Anzeigen* is cited in note 8 and at length in note 48 — argued that Luther's *Affektenlehre* "grounds Enlightenment philosophy, opening the way for what would later become the 'hermeneutics of facticity' practiced by both Heidegger and Gadamer." Luther recognized that without affect, the past Passion of Christ would remain intangible and the future fear of God's wrath abstract; Dockhorn's Luther-thesis is: *"Belief realizes itself in affect — must complete itself in affect — because reason is incapable of making past and future present"* (note 48 long German block-quote). Heidegger's pathos doctrine "secularizes Luther's rhetorical *Affektenlehre*" — and his analysis of fear specifically "draws on this Lutheran tradition while rewriting its residual metaphysics." This is the volume's signature genealogical move: Aristotle → Christian theology → Luther's *Affektenlehre* → Heidegger's hermeneutics-of-facticity (and Gadamer's), via Dockhorn's reading.

**Movement 5 — The fear analysis and the daseinsmäßige genesis of speech (book pp. 34–38)**. The structural center of Section IV. SS 1924 "shows that logos and doxa can be bound by way of fear. When touched by fear, our belief is mobilized: community and its Other are defined, we ask 'What is to be done?' and we can be moved to action." But — crucially — "for Heidegger it is not fantasy of future misfortune represented by the rhetor that moves the audience in its belief, as suggested in the Roman tradition and reiterated by Luther. We can appeal to the intentional mind only after the fact. Instead belief and its articulation are composed in a fearsome situation made immediate (*kairos*). We are moved ultimately not by fantasy, but by concrete possibilities contoured by experience" (book p. 34).

This is the explicit anti-Quintilian moment. Quintilian *Institutio oratoria* 6.2.29 — "things absent are presented to our imagination *(phantasia)* with such extreme vividness that they seem actually to be before our very eyes" — and the murder-scenario at 6.2.31 give the Roman fantasy-model: passions are products of individual mentation, mediated by *phantasia*. Heidegger reverses this: "in fact individual mentation is the product of passion" (book p. 35). The reversal is total: *phantasia* does not generate pathos; pathos generates the conditions under which *phantasia* could function at all.

The "three states of nonhuman being" passage (book p. 35) follows: dog (no belief), dogmatist (belief but no being-subject-to-change), Descartes (perfectly rational but disembodied). Each lacks something pathos provides — dynamism, traffic between inside and outside, subject-to-movement. Pathos is then characterized in fear's modal-temporal phenomenology: "unsettling" *(Unruhe)*, "confusing" *(Verwirrung)*, "mixed-up" *(Durcheinandergeraten)* (GA 18 183). The volume's most-cited single Heidegger pathos definition follows in long English-and-German parallel block-quotes (book pp. 35–36, GA 18 171): *"Pathos is a sudden change [Umschlagen] and hence a transformation to … [Werden zu] out of a previous situation, but not a sudden change that would take its own course. Instead, it is a way of disposing oneself toward the world that stands at the same time in a possible relation to hexis."* Pathos is structurally bivalent — being-suddenly-overcome contains within itself the possibility of being-composed-for *(Gefaßtsein dafür)*; "I can compose myself again."

The phenomenology of fear continues with the relational-temporal account (book pp. 36–37, GA 18 256–257). Aristotle's nine-types-in-three-categories typology of fearsome people (the ill-disposed, advantage-seekers, the cowardly: *von schlechter Gesinnung / auf eigenen Vorteil / feige*) is given in Heidegger's German. But the structural move is Heidegger's: "I fear these people not because I can imagine the damage they could do (I can equally imagine the threatening qualities of Atilla the Hun), but rather because my concrete relationship to this person — past and present — positions me unfavorably with respect to his or her capacity to harm me in the near future." Possibility *(Möglichkeit / Möglichsein)* is fear's structural feature (GA 18 253); the indeterminate *(Unbestimmten)* maximally intensifies fear, hence "the reserved" *(Zurückhaltenden)* and "the ironic" *(Ironischen)* are most feared (GA 18 257).

The daseinsmäßige genesis of speech (book p. 37, GA 18 261) is the climactic identification: "Heidegger describes fear as 'die Befindlichkeit, die zum Sprechen bringt'" — the attunement that *brings into speech*. The Heidegger quote in German is structurally definitive: *"Wenn uns unheimlich ist, fangen wir an zu reden. Das ist ein Hinweis für die daseinsmäßige genesis des Sprechens: das Sprechen zusammenhängt mit der Grundbestimmung des Daseins, die durch die Unheimlichkeit charakterisiert ist (261)."* Pathos is not just one structural moment among others — pathos is the genetic origin of speaking as such. "Heidegger concludes, [pathos] is the ground of logos, providing the mood and motivation necessary for the speaking animal to emerge and find its place in the world." The Heidegger summary that follows — the GA 18 262 long block-quote (book p. 38, in both English and German) — gives the volume's most-cited single Heidegger ontological-rhetorical formulation: the pathē "compose … the ground upon which language grows and to which expressions return"; they "provide the fundamental possibilities in which Dasein finds itself and orients itself." Then: *"we are presented with the possibility of coming to a particular matter-of-fact (Sachlichkeit) that, in a certain sense, returns to the way the world is seen as prefigured by the pathē."* Sachlichkeit *itself* is rhetorical.

**Movement 6 — The political turn and the 1933 thesis (book pp. 39–41)**. The ornament-marked transition (book p. 39, the floating tilde) opens the political-philosophical denouement: "Whatever happened to this more agreeable Heidegger who seemed at one time to be working on the very sort of pluralism Arendt would later characterize as the antidote to Heideggerian politics?" Forward-reference to Kisiel Ch 6 (psychohistorical Weimar-and-Ruhr explanation) is followed by a more philosophical claim: the 1930s Nietzsche-reading is the bridge. Heidegger's long passage on Affect/Passion/great-will (Heidegger *Nietzsche* I, 48–49, note 49) is quoted at length: passion "displays the self-composed superiority characteristic of great will [italics added by Gross]." "The body politic, in other words, is subordinated to the so-called great will. Ominous words indeed."

Heidegger's 1933 speeches and writing — the Rectoral Address, withdrawal-from-the-League-of-Nations rhetoric, the resolute-Freeman-to-resolute-nation progression, "adherence to the German Führer" — are characterized as "a far cry from the rhetorical pluralism that characterized Heidegger's nascent political philosophy in 1924." The 1924 Heidegger's GA 18 127 thesis is then quoted (book p. 40): *"The determination of Being-with-one-another in politikē founds that which rhetoric brings to language. [Die Bestimmung des Miteinanderseins in der politikē berührt das, was in der Rhetorik zur Sprache gebracht wird (127)]."* The reading is generously pluralist: humans have political existence (*bios politikos*) only as "actions are coordinated in a common world"; ethics shows "how Being-in-the-world is differentiated, and rhetoric how Dasein's articulations are situated in time and place."

But by *Being and Time*, "suspicion of *das Man* and his sociality had grown, while the domain of authentic Being-with had withered." The hermeneutic method "was to penetrate to yet a more formal and universal level of Being, and the Aristotelian triad of politics, ethics, and rhetoric were to be revealed as derivative social phenomena." Five BT pages anchor this thesis: BT 238 (ethics reconstructed via care, ordinary phenomena drop out), BT 344 ("only by authentically Being-their-Selves in resoluteness can people authentically be with one another"), BT 159 (the half-hearted "same affair in common" suggestion), BT 434 ("rarefied domain of the resolute Freeman"). "This was Heidegger's other critical turn, and one that has significant consequences for how we understand Heidegger's politics — whether present-at-hand in the most obtrusive way (the Rector's Speech of 1933) — or conspicuously absent (the late Heidegger's silence about Auschwitz)."

The closing thesis (book p. 41): "SS 1924 thus marks at the same time the rhetorical turn in twentieth-century philosophy and a fundamental revision of the rhetorical tradition itself." Heidegger "artfully coaxed elements of the rhetorical tradition from their ethical and political home to be incorporated in an account of 'fallen' discourse and poetic speech acts." This depoliticization continues in twentieth-century philosophy of language: speech-act theory (Grice, Searle, Habermas, Sperber) takes over the illocutionary/perlocutionary analysis but with no political-ethical context — only an "unevaluated background of unarticulated predispositions." "Then into this amorphous background is swept the power manifest in discursive histories, and rhetoric is depoliticized. Ironically Martin Heidegger's early work on Aristotle's rhetoric allows us to reconsider how political, ethical, and emotional context is in fact constitutive of everyday language."

**Movement 7 — The 49 Notes (book pp. 41–45)**. The notes are a research artifact. Notes 1–7 anchor the GA 18 reading: note 1 (the Bröcker-vs.-Schalk philological correction against Michalski's editorial choice), note 2 (BT 178 + the standard Macquarrie/Robinson hereafter-cited convention), note 4 (Karl Löwith's transcript at DLA Marbach 75.7452 — a primary archival pointer), notes 5–7 (Kisiel's *Genesis of Heidegger's Being and Time*, three pages — 16, 283, 292 — used for the genetic continuity claim). Notes 8–23 build the secondary Lutheran-and-hermeneutic genealogy: Shuger, Dockhorn, Van Buren, Gadamer (multiple page-citations to *Truth and Method* and *Philosophical Hermeneutics*), Schleiermacher. Notes 24–46 accumulate the Christian-rhetorical and Roman-rhetorical bibliography (Fynsk, Villa, Arendt's *Human Condition*, Kennedy on classical rhetoric's Christian reception, Rorty's anthology, Garver's *Aristotle's Rhetoric*, Derrida's *Margins*, Pecora's middle-voice essay, Ferry & Renaut, Heidegger's *Letter on Humanism* and *Poetry, Language, Thought*, Skinner's *Reason and Rhetoric in the Philosophy of Hobbes*). Notes 47–48 provide the long German block-quotes that anchor the *Affektenlehre* genealogy: note 47 quotes Heidegger's own German on the medieval *Affektenlehre* and the role of fear; note 48 quotes Dockhorn at length on Luther's Tischreden formula *"Dialectica docet, Rhetorica movet"* and the past-and-future-made-present principle. Note 49 closes with the Heidegger *Nietzsche* I 48–49 reference.

**Rhetorical structure**. Hybrid genealogical-philological argument terminating in a political-philosophical critique. The argumentative arc moves from negative critique (Roman compromise + Engberg-Pedersen) through positive textual reading (the GA 18 §§ on doxa, pathos, fear) to political-philosophical denouement (the 1933 thesis). The German-and-English bilingual block-quotes are a structural feature: every load-bearing GA 18 quote is given in both English (Gross's translation) and German (verbatim), establishing philological control. Section IV is also Section-IV-of-the-Introduction — i.e., it is *the* place where the volume's titular thesis ("rhetorical ontology") gets its ontological-substrate development.

The 14 key concepts of HR-01-GROSS-B cluster into four families:

1. **Pathos-priority cluster** (the volume's signature thesis): "the priority of pathos," "Roman compromise" (negative foil), "Affektenlehre" (genealogical bridge), "phantasia as Roman vs. Aristotelian-Heideggerian critique" (the Quintilian foil).

Each concept is given in Gross's own register — e.g., pathos is *not* glossed as "the Aristotelian/Heideggerian doctrine of emotion-and-reason" but specifically as "the very substance in which propositional thought finds its objects and motivation" (book p. 4 forward-reference). The Gross-specific definitions resist generic summary in ways the JSON Section 3 attempts to preserve.

## 4. Main Positions (See JSON Section 4 for full table)

Ten positions are extracted from Section IV. **P1** (the priority of pathos), **P5** (without pathos no traffic between inside and outside), **P7** (rhetorical pluralism of 1924), **P8** (BT's hermeneutic narrowing), **P9** (1933 turn as philosophical consequence), and **P10** (depoliticization-by-hermeneutics) are *core_essay_thesis*. **P2** (doxa as *Dafürsein*), **P3** (fear as relational-temporal-possibility), **P4** (pathos as *Umschlagen*-with-hexis), and **P6** (Lutheran *Affektenlehre* as genealogical bridge) are *supporting_argument*.

The structural relations among these ten are:
- **P1 ⇐ P2 + P3 + P4**: the priority-of-pathos thesis rests on the doxa-as-*Dafürsein* analysis (P2), the fear-as-relational-temporal analysis (P3), and the pathos-as-*Umschlagen* analysis (P4).
- **P1 ⇒ P5**: from the priority of pathos follows the no-pathos-no-community claim.
- **P6 historicizes P1**: the Aristotelian recovery has a Lutheran-theological mediation (the Dockhorn thesis).
- **P7 ⇒ P8 ⇒ P9 ⇒ P10**: the rhetorical pluralism of 1924 (P7) is what BT narrows (P8); that narrowing is what enables the 1933 turn (P9); the broader pattern is the depoliticization of rhetoric (P10).

The claim P9 — that Heidegger's 1933 turn is the political consequence of the BT-era depoliticization — is the volume's most contested thesis. Gross does not give a tight syllogism; the 1930s Nietzsche-reading is the implicit middle term ([INTERP-low]: but Section IV does not actually argue that the Nietzsche reading is *necessary* for the 1933 turn, only that it is *symptomatic*). T6 in JSON Section 8 flags this gap.

## 5. Interlocutors

The 24 interlocutors fall into five families.

**Aristotle** is the primary text — *two* Aristotles in tension (Rhet I.1 anti-pathos vs. Rhet II.5 phobos-as-condition-of-judgment). Heidegger reads Aristotle II against Aristotle I via GA 18; Gross extends.

**The Romans** — Cicero, Quintilian — supply the negative foil (the "by sheer fiat" compromise). **Plato** is the original anti-rhetorical philosopher; *Gorgias* 459c is the diagnostic locus.

**The Christian-theological tradition** — Augustine, Damascenus, Gregory of Nyssa, Nemesius, pseudo-Dionysius — is the genealogical context Gross declines to elaborate. **Luther** is the focal point: his *Sermo de poenitentia*, *Tischreden*, and *Affektenlehre* are central. **Dockhorn** (the romanticist philologist) is the mediating reader — his 1966 Gadamer-review supplies the Luther-to-Heidegger interpretive bridge.

**The contemporary critics**: **Engberg-Pedersen** (apologetic-condescending) is critiqued sharply; **Rorty**'s anthology, **Garver**'s book on character-and-rhetoric, **Kennedy**'s classical-rhetoric history, and **Shuger**'s Renaissance-grand-style study are bibliographic anchors. **Kisiel** is the genetic-historical authority Gross relies on without quite endorsing every move (Kisiel's psychohistorical-Weimar reading is forward-referenced to Ch 6). **Michalski** — paradoxically the GA 18 editor and Ch 3 contributor — is critiqued philologically over the Bröcker-vs.-Schalk variant at GA 18 110.

**The post-Heideggerian political-philosophical tradition**: **Arendt** (the counter-Heideggerian Heideggerian), **Pecora** (middle-voice ethics-and-politics), **Ferry & Renaut** (the polemical anti-anti-humanism critique), **Derrida** (briefly), **Skinner** (briefly).

The interlocutor structure is itself an argument: Aristotle as primary; Cicero/Quintilian as the Roman compromise to be overturned; Augustine-through-Luther as the suppressed theological tradition; Dockhorn as the recovery-mediator; Engberg-Pedersen as exemplar of the prejudice to be dismantled; Arendt as the counter-image of what Heidegger could-have-been-but-wasn't; Heidegger himself as the fallen genius who saw the truth in 1924 and lost it by 1933.

## 8. Tensions & Open Questions

Eight tensions are recorded; three are internal to the essay, three are cross-essay (volume-level), one is cross-pipeline, one is philological.

**T1 (internal)**: Aristotelian recovery vs. Lutheran inheritance. Gross holds both but does not fully reconcile them. *[INTERP-low]*: the lateral framing "draws on this Lutheran tradition while rewriting its residual metaphysics" is suggestive rather than systematic.

**T2 (internal)**: SS 1924 → BT continuity vs. rupture (intra-Heidegger). Gross navigates this by distinguishing genetic continuity from substantive continuity. *[INTERP-low]*: whether this distinction is sustainable is a live question — either Heidegger's BT *Befindlichkeit* preserves the SS 1924 pathos doctrine or it doesn't; "genetic-yes-but-substantive-no" risks a polite equivocation that conceals what is actually at stake.

**T3 (cross-essay, VT5 of phase0)**: pathos as ontological substrate (Gross) vs. pathos as cognitive-rhetorical resource (Hyde HR-04). Both cite GA 18 §§ on pathē. Gross dissolves the orator into pre-cognitive Befindlichkeit; Hyde retains the orator's intentional structure for epideictic.

**T4 (cross-essay, VT1 of phase0)**: rhetoric central (Gross) vs. rhetoric restricted (Pöggeler HR-07). Section IV's title "The Priority of Pathos" implicitly states the centralization claim; Pöggeler Ch 7 takes the opposite. Gross's depoliticization-by-hermeneutics thesis (P10) actually predicts Pöggeler's view as a *symptom* of the post-1924 trajectory — Gross provides the genetic story for what Pöggeler observes as synchronic "restriction." The cross-essay relation is thus not bare opposition but explanatory complementarity.

**T5 (cross-pipeline, VT11 of phase0)**: BT pipeline records *Befindlichkeit* (BT D1 U5, §§29-30) as central; HR pipeline records the pathos genealogy. Gross argues pathos does conceptual work BT *conceals* with the term *Befindlichkeit*. Phase 3D cross-link will need to flag this productive tension.

**T6 (internal)**: The political ascription move. Gross treats the 1933 turn as politically-philosophically *consequent* on the BT-era depoliticization. *[INTERP-low]*: But Section IV does not provide a tight syllogism — the leap from "BT depoliticizes rhetoric" to "1933 follows" is mediated by the 1930s Nietzsche reading and by Heidegger's 1933 speeches. Whether the Nietzsche-reading is *necessary* for the 1933 turn or one *symptom* among others is left underdetermined; the essay assumes (without arguing) that the same theoretical disposition that abandons rhetorical-pluralism also predisposes toward great-will politics.

**T7 (internal)**: The *Ja-sagen*-without-examination paradox. Doxa is yes-saying-without-examination AND structural openness ("Es könnte auch anders sein"). *[INTERP-low]*: How yes-saying-without-examination can simultaneously be openness-to-revision is a structural question Gross sidesteps. The implicit answer (doxa is provisionally fixed AND structurally revisable, like a hexis with built-in temporality) is not unpacked.

**T8 (philological)**: The Bröcker vs. Schalk transcript variant at GA 18 110 (Note 1). Michalski (volume Ch 3 contributor and GA 18 editor) follows Schalk's "aristotelischen Logic" reading; Gross follows Bröcker's "aristotelischen Rhetorik" reading. Conceptually important — "forgetting to ask about the concrete function of Aristotelian Rhetoric/Logic" is a different claim — and Gross flags it against the volume's editorial machinery (his own co-contributor's editorial choice).

## Summary

HR-01-GROSS-B reconstructs Heidegger's SS 1924 reading of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II as the locus where pathos is given ontological priority over logos — a reading Gross extends through a Christian-theological-Lutheran genealogy (via Dockhorn) and turns into a political-philosophical critique of Heidegger's BT-era hermeneutic narrowing and the 1933 Nazi turn. The sub-unit is unusually citation-dense: 16 GA 18 page-anchors, 6 BT page-anchors, 4 Aristotelian text-anchors, plus the Lutheran-Roman-Christian apparatus across 49 notes. The volume's signature thesis — "the priority of pathos" — receives here its most concentrated single articulation, and the depoliticization-by-hermeneutics critique (book p. 41) frames the entire volume's argumentative project. Most cross-essay edges anticipate Phase 3 productively: Gross supports Struever and Kisiel, contrasts with Hyde on the cognitive/ontological pathos register, and *explains* (rather than opposes) Pöggeler's restricted-conception thesis as a downstream symptom of the very depoliticization Gross diagnoses.
