## 2. Hierarchical Outline — the argumentative arc

Pöggeler's essay closes the volume in direct titular opposition to the opening contribution. Where Gross's introduction had situated rhetoric "at the heart of Heidegger's fundamental ontology," Pöggeler's title announces a *restriction*. The whole essay is the working-out of that restriction in two registers: temporal (rhetoric is bounded to 1923-28) and substantive (the rhetorical material that Heidegger absorbed was curtailed in transit into *Being and Time*'s existential framework). The architectonic moves backward in time from 1947 toward Marburg, then forward again to the post-1947 mature project, in a deliberate inversion of the volume's prevailing temporal frame.

### Topology and the 1947 Indictment (preamble, book pp. 161-164)

The preamble opens by placing Heidegger in what Pöggeler calls "the troubled year 1947." Indicted for his National Socialist entanglement, Heidegger privately publishes *Aus der Erfahrung des Denkens* (GA 13, 75ff). Pöggeler reads this small text as the inauguration of a new philosophical concept: the *Topologie des Seins*. Thoughtful poetics is identified with the dwelling-place (*Ortschaft*) of essence, and Heidegger separates himself from "half-poetic reason." For Pöggeler this is not stylistic but categorical: the systematic, essence-articulating register of Heidegger's earlier work has been replaced by a place-figure.

The preamble then jumps a decade forward to 1957's *Der Satz vom Grund* (GA 10), Pöggeler's second temporal pivot. Where the 1929 Husserl Festschrift contribution *Vom Wesen des Grundes* still operated within essence-articulation, the 1957 lecture has abandoned that systematic register. Heidegger is now "listening for language," reading the principle of reason through Dilthey, Leibniz, Hegel, and Nietzsche (*Birth of Tragedy* §15, where the principle of reason is named a "supreme metaphysical delusion"). The structure is that of *Erörterung* — a "discussion" whose German etymology Pöggeler unpacks: the verb *erörtern* contains *Ort*, place; to discuss is to bring into place. Curtius's *Toposforschung* and Auerbach's *Mimesis* (which calls itself a topology of Western literature and identifies *Leitworte* lexically extractable from texts) form the immediate rhetorical-historical background.

Yet Heidegger himself, in a December 12, 1958 letter to Pöggeler that the essay paraphrases from private correspondence, distances his usage from these historical connotations, insisting on a literal sense — topology as the speaking of place — and identifying the practice with the disclosure of the self-concealed (*das Entbergen des Sichverbergens*) of being. The crystallization of this place-figure comes in the *Geviert* (fourfold) with its four corners (earth, sky, mortal, gods) inscribed crosswise on *Seyn*. Pöggeler positions Vico's *De nostris temporis studiorum ratione* (1708 lecture, published 1947 by Küpper), Viehweg's *Topik und Jurisprudenz* (1953), Hartmann's dialectic-as-technique, and Apel's "linguistic-philosophical bridge building" as parallel attempts to revive an Aristotelian topics-tradition — and notes pointedly that Heidegger was "not involved in this alchemy." The preamble ends on what is structurally the essay's emotional center: a regret that Heidegger did not further develop the early exploratory path.

### The Path to Being and Time (Section I, book pp. 164-168)

Section I reconstructs Heidegger's intellectual formation as a sequence of dissolutions: the Catholic theologian arrives at Husserlian phenomenology (Freiburg, 1916); the catastrophe of World War I provokes a reversal of Husserl's transcendental turn; and Franz Overbeck dissolves the synthesis of antiquity and Christianity. Original Christianity is eschatological, and Heidegger's 1920-21 lectures (GA 60) take up the Pauline letters in this light. By 1919, in the first postwar lecture course (GA 56/57), Heidegger had combined Husserl's intuition with Bergson's intuition-as-life-flow into hermeneutic intuition; the move toward *Faktizität* is in place before Marburg.

The 1922 *Natorp-Bericht* — Heidegger's sketch for an Aristotle book — provides the decisive translation. Heidegger relates *NE* VI to the *Metaphysics* and *Physics*, characterizing *phronēsis* as conscience (*Gewissen*) and thereby discrediting metaphysical reason; Luther is enlisted against Aristotelian-Thomist theology. The affective and pathic register of Aristotle is being channeled into the ethical-existential register BT will inhabit. (Pöggeler's text contains a slip — "book Theta" of the *NE* — that should read book VI, since *Theta* is the Greek letter for the sixth book.)

Marburg 1923-28 is the rhetorical episode proper. Pöggeler highlights (n. 28) that Heidegger's WS 1925-26 lecture course was originally announced as "Aristotle: Rhetoric" and renamed before delivery to "Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy" (the now-standard GA 18, attached to SS 1924). The rename is itself a restriction. The *Sophist* course (GA 19, WS 1924/25) sharpens the Plato-rhetoric opposition: Plato refuses rhetoric, Aristotle classifies it under topics within practical philosophy, and Heidegger uses the contrast without endorsing rhetoric constructively.

BT (1927) is dedicated to Husserl but indebted to Scheler for the realm of the emotional. Heidegger develops fear via Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II.5 and links fear to anxiety along Christian lines via the Augustinian *timor castus* / *timor servilis* distinction (tracked through Dilthey, n. 36). The Stoic-Renaissance *Nec spe nec metu* is displaced by Pauline fear-and-trembling.

The section's critical center is the BT §29 passage on Aristotle's *Rhetoric* as the first systematic hermeneutic of everydayness (*Alltäglichkeit*) of being-amongst-one-another. Pöggeler reads this passage — which several other contributors treat as load-bearing positive endorsement — as restrictive. The *Rhetoric* is not preserved as a constructive resource but *confined* to everydayness, which BT characterizes as the mode of being of *das Man* and as *Öffentlichkeit*. Pöggeler asks: does Heidegger assume the political to be confined to everydayness and to the businesslike dealings of the "they"? The negative answer is built into Heidegger's text. Heidegger leaves behind not only Aristotle but Scheler as well when he ceases to set passions over against virtues — foreclosing the Nietzschean ressentiment-analysis Scheler had practiced. Section I closes by noting that Aristotle and Aquinas both raised the question of the image of justice that any "new rhetoric" (Perelman and Olbrechts-Tyteca, 1958) would have to address; Heidegger failed to ask this question. The unasked question is the restriction.

### From Aristotle to Nietzsche (Section II, book pp. 168-172)

Section II tracks Heidegger's post-1924 trajectory away from rhetoric toward poetics. Pöggeler opens with a methodological observation: Heidegger's GA 18 questions are *philological*; he proceeds from Aristotle's definitions. Humans are simultaneously linguistic and political, distinguished through speech from other animals (Aristotle *Politics* I.2 on *zōon politikon* and *zōon logon echon*). Aristotle's *Rhetoric* addresses the way the polis moves toward decisions; the speaker sets out from a shared opinion (*endoxon*) and returns to it. This is not a matter of scientific proof (*apodeixis*) — the apodeictic of the *Posterior Analytics* is the foil. Rhetoric proceeds from an ability to share the opinions (*doxa*) of others. The speaker is characterized by *ethos*, which Heidegger glosses as *Haltung*, and *pathos*, which he glosses as *Befindlichkeit*.

Here Pöggeler isolates the decisive conceptual substitution. *Befindlichkeit* is an existential-ontological category, not a rhetorical resource; by glossing *pathos* with *Befindlichkeit*, Heidegger dissolves the rhetorical *pathē* of book II into the existential structure of attunement. The orator's deliberate use of pathos is surrendered.

At the close of GA 18 Heidegger asserts that *kinēsis* (drawing on *De anima* III) possesses both *poiētikon* (creativity-capacity) and *pathētikon* (feeling-capacity). From this Pöggeler reads BT's *Destruktion* as proceeding from manual activity: *praxis* becomes *poiesis*, being must cling to the *eidos*, and the pathic is relegated to *Befindlichkeit* and *Stimmung*. Pöggeler asks how someone who had spent a year on Aristotle could fail to find *praxis* in the communication of common issues. Plato becomes, for Heidegger, "the aristocrat"; Aristotle is "son of a medical doctor"; both are wedded to a two-thousand-year "ontology of shoemaking." Scheler felt this charge absurd; Oskar Becker (Heidegger's first mentor) lexically refuted such readings; the polemic is symptomatic.

The fall 1929 turn to Nietzsche unfolds as a "completely new fashion" of the *Destruktion* that BT had been meant to lead to a systematic reconstruction. Reason is unfair to life and history; Hölderlin is placed beside Nietzsche; in the 1938 conversion-year essays Heidegger makes Nietzsche jointly responsible for National Socialism — Nietzsche becomes the most excessive metaphysician of metaphysics' demise. Heidegger fails to respect Nietzsche's own listening to the Romantics and to their theories of irony — the path that bumps, via Behler and Derrida, into Nietzsche the rhetorician.

The Letter on Humanism (composed fall 1946, published 1947, collected at *Wegmarken* / GA 9, 145ff) is the post-rhetorical document par excellence. Heidegger differentiates himself from Sartre; the *Kehre* is announced; in the very first sentence, action (*Handeln*) is glossed as bringing-to-fulfillment (*Vollbringen*), said as *poiēsis*. Renaissance humanism is taken to derive from Rome (Herder rejected); Hölderlin is enlisted against Goethe's citizen-of-the-world and the Romantics; and the polemic against the "dictatorship of publicness" (*Öffentlichkeit*) — the same term BT had used to characterize *das Man* — is simultaneously a polemic against rhetoric's polis-orientation. The Letter does not, Pöggeler notes, confront the published reports of Heidegger's 1933 actions.

The 1962 Greece trip (documented in *Aufenthalte* / *Sojourns*) supplies a biographical allegory: Heidegger imagined himself reading Heraclitus in the cabin of his ship and never once alighted on Patmos. The isolated cabin-dweller and the Naples-inhabit — the city Vico had thought from — represent contradictory worlds; Heidegger's *Eigentlichkeit* is structurally opposed to the public-spirited rhetorical tradition. And yet, Pöggeler adds, the reader cannot escape the impression that even when hidden, Heidegger touches the rhetorical tradition again and again. Ijsseling's *Rhetoric and Philosophy in Conflict* (n. 44) supplies the diagnosis: late Heidegger's truth-of-language adheres to the ancient Greek understanding of language as in Isocrates' panegyric on the power of speech. The rhetorical tradition is the philosophical tradition's constitutive other.

The closing pages turn to the Heidegger-Ebbinghaus correspondence (Manfred Baum transcript; originals unpublished). The January 4, 1924 letter announces the "Hermeneutics of Historical Knowledge" lectures; the November 22, 1950 letter recalls Freiburg-Immental walks and confesses Heidegger's possible lifelong preoccupation with religious questioning; Ebbinghaus's reply discusses tolerance. The 1953 Trakl essay (*Merkur* 61, reprinted in *Unterwegs zur Sprache* 1959) supplies Heidegger's own self-definition of *Erörterung*: discussion points to place, and place gathers and permeates everything.

The essay closes with Ebbinghaus's published draft-letter, which etymologically links *erörtern* back to Aristotle's *Topics*: to discuss is to carry contemplation through prescribed places (*Örter* / *loci*). And then the closing image: *Holzwege* (woodpaths) are not wood-paths-in-a-forest but paths driven through a wood to carry out timber; they end blind where timber has been made; they deceive the traveler. The function of the image is clear: the path of the topology of being is one such *Holzweg* — appearing to discover but in fact ending where Heidegger's pre-decisions (against publicness, against Vico, against the rhetorical-public dimension) have already foreclosed the destination.

The final paragraph delivers Pöggeler's verdict interrogatively. Is it sad if philosophers follow diverse paths, eventually able only to wave to one another across the intervening distance? Perhaps it is the nature of philosophy that all proceed from different places on the periphery, aiming at a common center, still bound by the prejudices of the age to that starting-point in thinking and politics. Rhetoric, in Pöggeler's closing sentence, seeks to follow up such binding. Heidegger made remarkable contributions to that search but did not exhaust its tradition and its future possibilities.

## 3. Key Concepts

Thirteen interlocking concepts organize the essay; six are central, seven supporting. **Topology of being** (*Topologie des Seins*, also *Seyn* with crosswise strikethrough) — Heidegger's mature post-1947 concept, introduced in *Aus der Erfahrung des Denkens* and glossed in the December 12, 1958 letter to Pöggeler — is the literal speaking of place, the thinking of truth as the disclosure of the self-concealed of being. The *Geviert* (fourfold of earth, sky, mortal, gods), introduced in essays on Thought and Poetry from 1941-47 (translator's note 12), is its geometric crystallization. **Restricted conception of rhetoric** is the titular thesis, carrying two compounded senses: temporally bounded (rhetoric central to neither pre-Marburg formation nor post-1928 mature thought) and substantively curtailed (Heidegger failed to ask after justice, relegated *pathos* to *Befindlichkeit*, surrendered rhetoric to the "they").

**Discussion** (*Erörterung*; verb *erörtern*) is what the topology of being practices. The German, from *Ort* (place), cannot be rendered in English without losing the place-figure (translator's note 46); in Heidegger's gloss from the 1953 Trakl essay, discussion points to place, and place gathers and permeates everything. The hidden genealogy of the term, as Ebbinghaus's draft-letter records, is the Aristotelian topics-tradition. **Topoi as guiding-words** (*Leitworte*) is the rhetorical-literary concept Pöggeler holds out as the more adequate frame Heidegger's *Topologie* partly inhabits without acknowledging — Auerbach's *Mimesis* presents Western literature as a topology, and Curtius's *Toposforschung* in the medieval-Latin tradition supplies the parallel. Heidegger does indeed in *Der Satz vom Grund* let speak the guiding words of Western thought, but these are genealogical guiding-words (the principle of reason), not rhetorical *Leitworte* in Auerbach's sense.

**Phronēsis as conscience** (*Gewissen*) is Heidegger's 1922 *Natorp-Bericht* translation: the conceptually decisive move that channels the rhetorical-pathos register into the ethical-existential register of BT. **Pathos as Befindlichkeit (attunement)** is the corresponding gloss in GA 18; the existential-ontological category replaces the rhetorical category, losing what the orator deliberately deploys, and *pathos* is "relegated" to attunement and to mood (*Stimmung*). **Movement as *poiētikon* and *pathētikon*** captures the close of GA 18: *kinēsis* possesses both creativity-capacity and feeling-capacity, and Pöggeler reads this as the conceptual track for BT's poiesis-emphasis — *praxis* becomes *poiesis* and Aristotle's *praxis*-as-rhetorical-communication is restricted to manufactural metaphor.

**Holy fear vs. slavish fear** (*timor castus* vs. *timor servilis*) reaches Heidegger through Dilthey's Renaissance-and-Reformation analyses (n. 36); the Augustinian distinction is imported to read Aristotelian *phobos* (*Rhetoric* II.5) and supplies the Christian substrate beneath the seemingly Aristotelian fear-analysis. **Authenticity (*Eigentlichkeit*) vs. the public-spirited rhetorical tradition** is Pöggeler's most concise diagnosis: Heidegger does not consider the polis as the setting for decisions and treats publicness only as the mode of being for the "they"; the 1962 Greece trip is the biographical allegory. **Unconcealment** (*Unverborgenheit*) and self-concealment (*Sichverbergen* / *Entbergen des Sichverbergens*) — Heidegger's mature truth-of-language concept — adheres strictly, on Ijsseling's reading, to the ancient Greek understanding of language as in Isocrates' panegyric. **Holzwege as deceptive paths** is the closing image, drawn from Ebbinghaus's draft-letter: paths driven through a wood to carry out timber, ending blind, deceiving the traveler. **Topology vs. Aristotelian topics** is the structural disambiguation: Heidegger's *Topologie* suggests properties of being unchanged by historical or other interpretation — not Aristotle's *topics*, which are commonplaces for argument; Vico and Viehweg's *Topik und Jurisprudenz* develop the Aristotelian topics-tradition Heidegger keeps his *Topologie* distinct from.

## 4. Main Positions / Claims

The essay deploys eight argumentatively load-bearing positions, four core and four supporting.

**P1** (core). Heidegger's serious engagement with rhetoric is restricted to the Marburg lecture courses of 1923-28; rhetoric is not a constitutive concept of his mature thought. Rhetoric is absent from *Aus der Erfahrung des Denkens* and the Letter on Humanism; *Der Satz vom Grund* (1957) abandons systematic essence-articulation; the 1958 letter to Pöggeler distances "topology" from historical connotations; and the *Geviert*, the Trakl essay (1953), and *On the Way to Language* (1959) develop a place-figure that is poetic, not rhetorical. The position targets Gross's titular thesis that Heidegger "relocates rhetoric at the heart of his fundamental ontology" and the volume-wide assumption (in Hyde, Struever, Kisiel) that BT §29 / GA 18 110 is a load-bearing positive endorsement.

**P2** (core). On the path to *Being and Time*, Heidegger restricted crucial dimensions of Aristotle's *Rhetoric*; what looks like an embrace is in fact a curtailment. BT §29 confines the political to everydayness (*das Man* / *Öffentlichkeit*) rather than extending it to deliberative public assembly; *Befindlichkeit* replaces the rhetorical *pathē*; Heidegger's cessation of setting passions over against virtues leaves Aristotle *and* Scheler behind, foreclosing ressentiment-analysis; and the new-rhetoric question about justice (Perelman) goes unasked. This targets Hyde's reading of the BT call of conscience as ontologically epideictic — for Pöggeler, BT §§54-60 is where rhetoric is *lost*, not preserved — and Gross's reading of *pathos* as substance of propositional thought.

**P3** (supporting). The 1923-28 engagement was rhetorical only because it was a philological Aristotle interpretation; what Heidegger thematizes is the Plato-Aristotle contrast on rhetoric, not rhetoric as a constructive concept. The WS 1925-26 rename from "Aristotle: Rhetoric" to "Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy" is itself the restriction; the *Sophist* course (GA 19) sharpens the Plato-vs-rhetoric opposition. This targets Kisiel's reading of GA 18 as originating a rhetorical protopolitics with continuous afterlife and Michalski's reading of the philological orientation as methodologically sustaining.

**P4** (supporting). The Letter on Humanism (1947) is the post-rhetorical document par excellence. *Animal rationale* relegates the human to the level of the animal; Hölderlin replaces both Goethe's citizen-of-the-world and the Romantics; the polemic against the "dictatorship of publicness" simultaneously polemicizes against rhetoric's polis-orientation. Heidegger's inability to confront the published reports of his 1933 actions is displaced into this polemic; Renaissance humanism is rejected as Roman; the 1962 Greece-trip biography confirms. This targets the volume's positive-amplification cluster that reads late Heidegger as continuous with the Marburg engagement.

**P5** (supporting). Even when hidden, Heidegger "touches the rhetorical tradition again and again"; the relationship is one of buried debt, not theoretical inheritance. *Erörterung* has the Aristotelian topics-tradition as unacknowledged genealogy; Ijsseling shows the late truth-of-language adheres to Isocrates' understanding of language; Helmut Kuhn argued topics could approximate Heidegger's discussion, even becoming fundamental ontology — yet Heidegger himself was not involved in this alchemy. The verdict is mournful, not celebratory.

**P6** (supporting). The topology of being is structurally distinct from the rhetorical topics tradition. The 1958 letter brackets historical connotations; the *Geviert* is poetic-cosmic; the guiding-words of *Der Satz vom Grund* are genealogical, not Auerbachian *Leitworte*; and Vico's *New Science* and *De nostris temporis* are rejected by Heidegger as "Latin and Italian, therefore following the Roman transformation of the legacy of the ancients." This targets any reading that conflates *Topologie* with Aristotelian topics.

**P7** (core, implicit). Pöggeler's silence on BT 178 / GA 18 110 is itself a position: he treats the passage not as positive valorization but as the moment Heidegger confines the political to everydayness, surrendering the deliberative-public dimension Aristotle had named. The omission of the rhetorical-deliberative-public dimension *is* the restriction. This targets Hyde, Struever, Gross, and Kisiel, all four of whom cite BT 178 / GA 18 110 as load-bearing positive evidence (Phase 0 VT9).

**P8** (methodological). There is a fundamental temporal asymmetry in how the volume's contributors read Heidegger: Pöggeler tracks *backward* from 1947 (post-Marburg, post-rhetoric); the other contributors track *forward* from 1924 (Marburg, rhetorical). The essay's structural movement (1947 → 1957 → 1958 letter → Marburg → final position) frontloads the topology-of-being concept that displaces rhetoric. The temporal asymmetry produces opposite verdicts on the same loci — and the framing disagreement is baked into the volume's architecture: it opens with Gross's centrality-thesis and closes with Pöggeler's restriction-thesis.

## 5. Interlocutors and Traditions

**Aristotle** is the primary object of the Marburg engagement: the *Rhetoric* supplies the BT §29 hermeneutic-of-everydayness material; the *Topics* is the proper home of rhetoric in Aristotle's classification; *NE* VI's *phronēsis* becomes Heidegger's *Gewissen*. Heidegger borrows Aristotle but curtails the rhetorical-deliberative-public dimension. **Plato** functions as foil: Plato refuses rhetoric, Aristotle classifies it under topics within practical philosophy. Heidegger's *Sophist* course (GA 19, WS 1924/25) sharpens this opposition without endorsing rhetoric, and both Plato and Aristotle end up, in the late polemic, taken to be wedded to a two-thousand-year "ontology of shoemaking" — a charge Scheler treated as absurd.

**Husserl** is the phenomenological starting-point Heidegger reverses; *Being and Time* is dedicated to him; the 1929 Husserl Festschrift contribution *Vom Wesen des Grundes* is the still-systematic register Heidegger later abandons. **Wilhelm Dilthey** is the hermeneutic-of-life proximate source. The *Introduction to the Human Sciences* (n. 4) sees Leibniz and Hegel as final formulators of metaphysical thinking in the principle of reason; Renaissance-and-Reformation analyses (n. 36) inform Heidegger's pathos-via-Christian-tradition reading; the Dilthey-Hegel work, transmitted via Julius Ebbinghaus, connects to Heidegger's announced "Hermeneutics of Historical Knowledge" lectures (January 4, 1924).

**Hans-Georg Gadamer** — Heidegger's student and Pöggeler's habilitation supervisor — is an ally. *Truth and Method* (n. 19) and "Rhetorik, Hermeneutik und Ideologiekritik" (n. 18) are cited for the late acknowledgment that hermeneutics had older roots in rhetoric, not first universalized by Schleiermacher. **Friedrich Nietzsche** plays a double role: *Birth of Tragedy* §15 (n. 5) names the principle of reason a "supreme metaphysical delusion" (the Nietzsche Heidegger reads through), but the Nietzsche-the-rhetorician — listening to the Romantics and their theories of irony, recovered via Behler and Derrida — is the side Heidegger fails to honor. In 1938 Heidegger makes Nietzsche jointly responsible for National Socialism and "most excessive metaphysician" of metaphysics' demise. **Max Scheler** is the acknowledged BT debt for the realm of the emotional; Scheler's ressentiment-virtue analysis is what Heidegger leaves behind when he ceases to set passions over against virtues (Pöggeler's "Ressentiment und Tugend bei Max Scheler," n. 34).

**Erich Auerbach** and **Ernst Robert Curtius** together represent the rhetorical-literary topology tradition Pöggeler holds out as the more adequate concept. Auerbach's *Mimesis* (n. 8) and *Vier Untersuchungen zur Geschichte der französischen Bildung* (n. 9) identify *Leitworte* lexically extractable from texts (example: "la cour et la ville" for the French classics). Curtius's *European Literature and the Latin Middle Ages* (n. 7) develops *Toposforschung* in the medieval-Latin tradition, with an anti-Cartesian humanism inspired by Bergson that Heidegger did not pursue.

**Giambattista Vico** is the Neapolitan anti-Cartesian whose *De nostris temporis studiorum ratione* (1708 lecture, edited by Schalk and translated by Otto for Küpper, 1947) reverses Cartesian apodicticity via topical *inventio argumentorum*. In conversation, Heidegger would not reconsider Vico: the judgment was fixed — Vico, having written in Latin and Italian, followed the Roman transformation of the legacy of the ancients. Pöggeler treats this biographical refusal as conclusive evidence of restriction (*New Science*, *Liber metaphysicus*, and Pöggeler's "Vico und die humanistische Tradition" are cited, n. 20). **Theodor Viehweg** (*Topik und Jurisprudenz* 1953, n. 13) and **Karl-Otto Apel** (with his Vico-inflected "linguistic-philosophical bridge building," nn. 16-18) figure as a neighborhood of post-Aristotelian topics-traditions Heidegger did not join. **Jürgen Habermas** is cited (n. 16) for *Theorie und Praxis* (1963), in agreement with Wilhelm Hennis that practical philosophy could establish ties to rhetoric — though Heidegger's anti-publicness polemic implicitly targets Habermas-style communicative rationality.

**Samuel Ijsseling** (*Rhetoric and Philosophy in Conflict*, n. 44) is a direct ally whose reading that late Heidegger's truth-of-language adheres to Isocrates' understanding of language grounds the hidden-but-persistent thesis. **Julius Ebbinghaus** is Heidegger's correspondent (son of Hermann Ebbinghaus, Dilthey's adversary). The Heidegger-Ebbinghaus correspondence is unpublished; Pöggeler cites a Manfred Baum transcript (nn. 26, 47). The January 4, 1924 letter announces the "Hermeneutics of Historical Knowledge" lectures; the November 22, 1950 letter recalls Freiburg-Immental walks and confesses Heidegger's possible lifelong preoccupation with religious questioning; the published draft-letter elaborates the etymology of *erörtern* and supplies the *Holzwege* image that closes the essay. **Helmut Kuhn**'s 1970 *Gadamer-Festschrift* essay (n. 15) — with Pöggeler's reply, in Riedel's *Rehabilitierung der praktischen Philosophie* vol. 2 — supplies the strongest formulation of the topics-to-fundamental-ontology genealogy Heidegger himself avoided. **Chaim Perelman** (with Olbrechts-Tyteca, *La nouvelle rhétorique* 1958, n. 35) raises the question of justice that Heidegger failed to ask.

**Pöggeler himself** is the volume's most prolific self-citing contributor: of eleven prior works in the volume bibliography, nine appear in this essay's footnotes (including *Der Denkweg Martin Heideggers*, *Heidegger in seiner Zeit*, "Dichtungstheorie und Toposforschung," *Friedrich Nietzsche und Martin Heidegger*, and *Neue Wege mit Heidegger*). The density of self-reference encodes a generational positioning. **Walter Bröcker** and **Karl Löwith** supply SS 1924 transcripts; **Manfred Baum** supplies the Heidegger-Ebbinghaus correspondence transcript.

## 8. Tensions and Open Questions

Seven tensions are flagged in the JSON. **T1** (Volume-framing disagreement, Phase 0 VT1): Pöggeler's restriction-thesis stands in direct opposition to Gross's centrality-thesis; the volume opens with Gross and closes with Pöggeler, and the disagreement is built into the architectural decision. **T2** (Silence on BT 178 / GA 18 110): Hyde, Struever, Gross, and Kisiel all treat this passage as load-bearing positive evidence; Pöggeler reads the substantively similar BT §29 as the moment of restriction. The contrastive omission is itself an argumentative move ([INTERP-low]). **T3** (Internal tension on *Erörterung*): Heidegger's late discussion terminology has a rhetorical-topical genealogy yet Heidegger explicitly refuses this genealogy in the 1958 letter; the restriction is never complete. **T4** (Pöggeler vs. Kisiel on the fate of rhetoric): Kisiel Ch 6 §VI tracks a three-phase trajectory in which rhetoric persists in transformed form; Pöggeler sees an abandonment. There is partial convergence at Kisiel's Phase III ("Archaic-Poietic") and Pöggeler's Hölderlin/Heraclitus pivot, though neither thematizes the overlap ([INTERP-low]). **T5** (Omission of the 1933 Rectoral Address): Pöggeler's opening names 1947 as the indictment-year but treats the indictment as occasion rather than substantive theme. The Letter on Humanism is read for *Kehre*, not for political evasiveness; this contrasts sharply with Kisiel's §VII and Gross's part III. Pöggeler's relative silence on Heidegger's politics is itself a position: topology of being supplants both rhetoric *and* politics. **T6** (Bibliographic asymmetry): Pöggeler's eleven self-citations are the volume's highest — the dean-speaks framing of the closing essay by the author of *Der Denkweg Martin Heideggers* (1963) encodes a generational handoff ([INTERP-low]). **T7** (Bibliography Section A weight): Section A lists eight Heidegger loci where rhetoric is "defined"; loci 1-7 are the Marburg cluster, and only locus 8 (1951/52 *Was heißt Denken?*) is post-Marburg. Pöggeler does not cite locus 8 directly; the list documents "rhetoric mentioned" rather than "rhetoric defined" ([INTERP-low]).
