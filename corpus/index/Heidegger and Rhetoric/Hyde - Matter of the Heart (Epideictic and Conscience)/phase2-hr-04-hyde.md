## 1. Unit Metadata & Thesis

Hyde's chapter advances the volume's most direct **BT–Aristotle bridge**. Its thesis: the Heideggerian *Ruf des Gewissens* (BT §§54–60), heard as a silent ontological-temporal interruption that summons Dasein to authentic being-toward-itself, **operates structurally as primordial epideictic rhetoric** — a showing-forth (*epideixis*) of the heart of human existence. This bridges Aristotle's epideictic genus (*Rhetoric* I.3, I.9) to Heidegger's existentialia of conscience and discloses what Heidegger himself, despite his violent appropriation of the *Rhetoric* in 1924 and *Being and Time*, never quite admitted: that **rhetoric** (and not poetry alone) **has ontological-disclosive status**.

Hyde performs a deliberately calibrated rhetorical strategy: the chapter is itself epideictic — it sounds a call of conscience to readers (especially philosophers and rhetoricians) about Heidegger's own missed opportunity. As Hyde signals on p. 83: "I also intend to sound such a call, and with a bit of violence, too." The argument is constructed almost entirely through Aristotelian categories interpreted through Heidegger's GA 18 lecture-course glosses, with BT §§54–60 as the principal text and BT 178 as the volume-wide-cited Heidegger-on-rhetoric formula.

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

The essay has five Roman-numeral sections plus the prefatory opening and the 46-note endnote block. The structural movement is from a Gadamer-anchored Heidegger-as-rhetorician opening, through three theoretical sections (emotion → enthymeme → conscience) that progressively layer Aristotle on Heidegger and Heidegger on Aristotle, to the climactic bridge thesis (Section IV: epideictic as ontological showing-forth), and finally to the political-philosophical conclusion that interrogates Heidegger's later abandonment of rhetoric.

### Opening (book pp. 81–84 / PDF pp. 87–90)

Hyde's opening is itself rhetorical: he stages Gadamer's witness (Gadamer's *Heidegger's Ways*, Stanley trans. SUNY 1994, 31–32) on what it was like to listen to Heidegger lecture on Aristotle in early-1920s Freiburg/Marburg. Gadamer reports Heidegger's "critical and de(con)structive" powers, Heidegger's freeing of the original Aristotelian text from "the overlay of the scholastic tradition and from the miserable, distorted picture that critical philosophy of the period had of Aristotle." The 1923 NE seminar treated *phronesis*: Aristotle's practical wisdom which "admits of no reference to a final objectivity in the sense of science." Gadamer continues that this "violent appropriation of the Aristotelian text [by Heidegger]" reminds him of how *the call of the conscience* in *Being and Time* "is what first makes the 'Dasein in human beings' visible in its ontological and temporal event-structure" — an analogy that **frames the entire chapter**.

Hyde immediately glosses the violence: it is "philosophical" rather than physical, the "violence of hermeneutic phenomenology" that exposes "complacency" and "tranquillized obviousness" of everyday interpretations; the "interruption" is meant to "incite specific feelings of anxiety and joy." Heidegger's textual violence is itself an act of conscience — calling students to attention to the lost matter of phronesis.

The opening then quotes the volume's most-cited single Heidegger passage, BT 178: "Contrary to the traditional [scholastic] orientation, according to which rhetoric is conceived as the kind of thing we 'learn in school,' this work of Aristotle [the *Rhetoric*] must be taken as the **first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being with one another**." Hyde stakes his twofold operation: (i) extending Heidegger's affirmation of rhetoric's ontological status, and (ii) himself "sounding such a call" — performative epideictic. The bridge thesis is set: "the call of conscience operates as a primordial form of rhetorical (that is, *epideictic*) discourse."

### I. EMOTION AND RHETORIC (book pp. 84–88 / PDF pp. 90–94)

Hyde frames Plato's anti-rhetoric position (*Gorgias*, *Phaedrus*, *Sophist*: rhetoric inhibits *noēsis*-grounded *epistēmē*) and acknowledges Plato's partial concession in the *Phaedrus* (260d-e): rhetoric may serve truth. He pivots to Aristotle's two clarifications: (1) emotional character constitutes a person's spirited potential for judging the true/just/virtuous; (2) rhetoric is *dynamis* (faculty/power, not *technē* in the manipulative sense) — "the counterpart of Dialectic" (*Rhet.* 1354a1).

The section's central exhibit is Heidegger's German GA 18 [163] gloss of Aristotle's *Rhet.* II thesis (1377b24, "put hearers into the right frame of mind"): "*Es muß bei der Vorgabe anderes in Hinsicht gestellt werden, es muß Rechnung getragen werden der Stimmung derjenigen, zu denen gesprochen wird, die jeweilige Lage der Dinge und die Art and Weise, wie man selbst zur Sache steht.*" And: "*Er bedarf des Verständnisses der Möglichkeiten der Stimmung, um sie in der rechten Weise zu wecken und zu lenken.*" Here the orator must understand the possibilities of *Stimmung* (mood, paralleling *pathē*) in order to rouse and guide them "in der rechten Weise." Hyde explicitly contests the Macquarrie/Robinson rendering "aright" (note 10) and proposes "in a right and just manner" to capture the moral sense of *recht* — a rare instance in this chapter where Hyde flags translation as contested.

Hyde then negotiates Heidegger's BT §27 publicness analysis dialectically. The negative pole — "in this averageness with which [publicness] prescribes what can and may be ventured, it keeps watch over everything exceptional" (BT 165) — reads Heidegger as suspicious of crowd-conformism. But Hyde insists Heidegger has a positive pole too: "publicness belongs to Dasein's positive constitution" (BT 167). Citing Dreyfus 1991 (p. 155) — "the source of the intelligibility of the world is the average public practices through which alone there can be any understanding at all" — Hyde rescues a positive Heideggerian account of doxic publicness. He clinches this with the GA 18 [149] formula: "*Die doxa ist die eigentliche Entdecktheit des Miteinanderseins-in-der-Welt*" (doxa is the authentic discoveredness of being-with-one-another in the world). Doxa is not merely the corrupt *Gerede*; it is the ontological space in which rhetorical work becomes possible.

The section's most extensive technical material is Aristotle's analysis of anger (*orgē*), worked at length: *Rhet.* 1378a32–34 (anger as "conspicuous revenge for a conspicuous slight"), 1378b3–5 ("the angry man is aiming at what he can attain, and the belief that you will attain your aim is pleasant"), 1380b5 ("time puts an end to anger"). Hyde reads anger as a temporally-modifying emotion: anger collapses imagined future revenge into present immediacy. He then explicitly translates Aristotle into Heideggerian existentialia: this is "a perceptual restructuring of a person's existential temporality and spatiality." Anger's interrelation with fear (*phobos*) at *Rhet.* 1382a30, 1382b20, 1383a15–20 — fear "forces the realization that what was heretofore perceived to be remote is actually close at hand" — and with pity (*eleos*) at *Rhet.* 1382b26, 1385b15–34, 1386a1–3, 1386b5–10 — pity arises through identification (lack of remoteness, cf. Burke 1969) — completes the existential-spatial restructuring picture.

The section concludes by reading Heidegger's BT §30 (fear) and §40 (anxiety) as continuations of Aristotle's pathē-analysis: "the temporal and spatial workings of fear are to be distinguished from the more 'primordial' temporal and spatial workings of anxiety" (BT 179–182, 228–235). Heidegger's "in-depth analysis of the ways in which [the *pathē*] are interconnected in their foundations" — what Heidegger admittedly omits — is what Hyde recovers.

### II. THE ENTHYMEME (book pp. 88–90 / PDF pp. 94–96)

Section II treats *enthymēma* (*Rhet.* I.2). Aristotle's enthymeme is a "syllogism" (1356b3) but a special one — "out of materials that call for discussion ... [and] debate" (1357a35–39) — operating "in the hearing of persons [untrained thinkers]" who cannot follow long argument-chains (1357a1–4) and proceeding with few propositions because "if any of these propositions is a familiar fact, there is no need even to mention it; the hearer adds it himself" (1357a15–20). The enthymeme is thus audience-cooperative: the hearer completes the argument.

Hyde then resolves the apparent *Rhet.* III contradiction (1418a11–14: "avoid the enthymeme form when you are trying to rouse feeling") by reading *enthymeisthai* (the verbal cognate) dually: it means both "giving consideration" (cognitive) AND "taking something to heart" (emotional). This dual reading is grounded in Heidegger's GA 18 work: theoretical assessment is "derivative" of practical and emotional involvement (GA 18 [95–107, 122f, 146f]); the enthymeme's primary function is "*sich etwas zu Herzen nehmen*" (GA 18 [128]) — taking-to-heart — rooted in the "ground and soil of the *logos*" itself ("*den Boden des logos selbst*", GA 18 [169]).

P. Christopher Smith's *Hermeneutics of Original Argument* (1998, pp. 27–29) is endorsed as the critical secondary source on this redirection. The section ends by linking the enthymeme back to the call of conscience: both invite concerned, embodied take-up; Heidegger's reading "extends" rhetoric's ontological status, and the call of conscience is the existential analogue of enthymematic taking-to-heart.

### III. THE CALL OF CONSCIENCE (book pp. 90–94 / PDF pp. 96–100)

Section III is the most direct exegesis of BT §§54–60 in the volume. Hyde recapitulates the orator's task — to rouse the *pathē* "in der rechten Weise" — and frames it as collaborative deliberation requiring formation of common interest. He cites Aristotle's *Politics* III 1281b1–6 (the famous "Many" passage: "Each of them by himself may not be of a good quality; but when they all come together it is possible that they may surpass — collectively and as a body, although not individually — the quality of the few best") and *NE* VI 1141b8–11 on *phronesis* as concerned with what is humanly deliberable.

Hyde activates the etymology: *phronesis* is rooted in *phrēn* (the heart); "collaborative deliberation" is *syn-eidesis* (Greek, "knowing together") / *con-scientia* (Latin, "knowing together"). **Conscience IS this togetherness-of-knowing.** The textual warrant comes from Taminiaux 1992 (*Heidegger and the Project of Fundamental Ontology*, p. 141), reporting from manuscripts of Heidegger's *Sophist* / *Philebus* / *Basic Concepts of Ancient Philosophy* lectures: Heidegger said "*phronesis* is *Gewissen* [Conscience] set in motion which makes an action transparent [*durchsichtig*]." Gadamer 1976 (*Philosophical Hermeneutics*, p. 201) confirms the same point about the 1923 NE seminar. This is Hyde's strongest single-textual warrant for the BT-conscience / Aristotle-*phronesis* identity (P3).

Hyde then turns to BT proper. The *Ruf des Gewissens* "comes forth originally in the way in which our temporal and spatial existence (Dasein's Being-in-the-world) confronts us with a fundamental challenge calling for a response": the contingency of the future, the "not yet," the task of "creating a meaningful existence." The call is **not a human invention** — "we did not create the ontological (temporal/spatial) structure of existence." Conscience precedes ethics: "the call of conscience is always already operating before the specific ethical prescriptions and prohibitions of [moral] language."

The call is silent. Hyde quotes BT 318 (§56): "The call dispenses with any kind of utterance. It does not put itself into words at all. ... *Conscience discourses solely and constantly in the mode of keeping silent*." He immediately glosses Heidegger's account of language as showing: "To speak means to say, which means to **show** and to let [something] be seen. It means to communicate and, correspondingly, to listen, to submit oneself to a claim addressed to oneself and to comply and respond to it" (per *Zollikon Seminars* 215). Conscience calls and Heidegger's discourse responds.

The call is disruptive: an "abrupt arousal" (BT 316) that interrupts complacent everyday involvements. Conformity belongs to the inauthentic state of being-with-others because it "operates to relieve us from the burden of explicitly facing the challenge" of resoluteness. The disruption brings anxiety: BT 342 — "the anxiety of conscience" — anxiety and conscience "go hand in hand." Anxiety arises through illness, crisis, or other disruption that "exposes us to the contingency and uncertainty inherent in our temporal existence." Hyde quotes BT 232 (citing 317–335 in conjunction): "Anxiety makes manifest in Dasein its Being towards its ownmost potentiality-for-Being, a potentiality which it always is and that is spoken of directly with the saying of the call of conscience."

The section closes by linking the call back to Section II: the enthymeme's "ground and soil of the *logos*" is precisely the existential-temporal openness disclosed by anxiety and articulated by the call. The BT analyses of fear, anxiety, and conscience are the existential-ontological completion of Aristotle's pathē-analysis and enthymeme-analysis.

### IV. EPIDEICTIC DISCOURSE (book pp. 94–100 / PDF pp. 100–106) — section opening on rotated PDF p. 100

Section IV opens with Hyde's appeal to his prior monograph (Hyde 2001, *The Call of Conscience: Heidegger and Levinas, Rhetoric and the Euthanasia Debate*, U of S. Carolina Press), where the moral discourse of the euthanasia debate is treated at length. The euthanasia debate "is quite capable of reaching down to the heart of human being" — rhetoric here is both deconstructive (calling existence into question) and reconstructive (helping people come to terms with sanctity-of-life vs. right-to-die-a-dignified-death issues). The opening then cites E. M. Cope (*An Introduction to Aristotle's Rhetoric*, Macmillan 1867, p. 121): epideictic is the "demonstrative," "showy," "ostentatious" rhetoric, contrasted with deliberative and forensic; and Cope's etymological note that epideictic embraces both encomium and invective ("praise of a man's good qualities").

Aristotle's *Rhet.* I.3 framework — three genres, *epideiktikon* / *sumbouleutikon* / *dikanikon* — and *Rhet.* I.9 epideictic loci of *aretē* (excellence/virtue) ground the formal analysis. Hyde's reading of epideictic at p. 95: "a beholding and a showing-forth (*epideixis*) of what is and as a competence for acknowledging others whom the orator desires to instruct as he or she gains and maintains their interests."

The argumentatively decisive paragraph follows on p. 95 (legible, post-rotation):

> "Of course, one cannot make this claim by simply appealing to Aristotle, for the essential connection here between the call of conscience and epideictic rhetoric, and it is Heidegger, not Aristotle, who directs us toward an ontological understanding of the first of these two phenomena. Aristotle, however, speaks of the second phenomenon in a more robust way than what Heidegger admits when he associates the category of epideictic speech with a eulogy, '*eine Lobrede*' (125)." [GA 18 [125] is Heidegger's only direct gloss of epideictic discourse.]

Hyde stakes the bridge thesis explicitly:

> "Heidegger certainly helps us to see the ontological potential of his ancestor's thinking on rhetoric and related matters, especially as he emphasizes throughout his lecture course on Aristotle (e.g., 104, 123, 169) how rhetoric (like the call of conscience) demands a hearing from those whom the orator would move with his discourse. Still, [his] violent appropriation of the Aristotelian text is not violent enough when it comes to revealing how the saying of the call of conscience has something fundamentally rhetorical about it: conscience calls; **it appeals to us most forcefully by way of an emotion (anxiety) that transforms time and space, opens us to the openness of our own existence, and thereby challenges us to think and act for the purpose of creating a meaningful existence** that perhaps marks an improvement and in our communal being-with-others. **The call of conscience, which lies at the heart of human being, is a showing-forth (*epideixis*) of this heart, a rhetorical revelation par excellence**, whereby a call for concerned thought and decisive action is announced."

Hyde then candidly acknowledges Heidegger's omission (p. 96): "Neither in his 1924 lecture course nor in *Being and Time* does Heidegger ever attempt to relate the call of conscience and epideictic discourse." He frames the late-Heidegger trajectory as the productive contrast: in the later works "Heidegger continues to think of this primordial temporal process as a way of attending to the 'call of Being itself'" — and the late-Heidegger "call of Being" is associated not with *epideixis* (rhetorical showing-forth) but with *poiēsis* (poetic bringing-forth, *Letter on Humanism*; *QCT* 10–11; *PLT* 62–74). "What is the difference between *epideixis* and *poiēsis*, between the rhetorical act of showing-forth the truth and the poetical act of bringing-forth the truth?" — a question Heidegger never explicitly posed. The chapter's bridge thesis is meant precisely to question Heidegger's omission.

### V. CONCLUSION: WHERE ART THOU, RHETORIC? (book pp. 97–99 / PDF pp. 103–105)

Section V is the chapter's political-philosophical pivot. Hyde opens with a counterfactual: had Heidegger appreciated rhetoric's connection to "the heart of human being," he might have responded more "heartfeltly" to the Nazi turn and the Holocaust. He then quotes Heidegger's notorious 1949 statement (cited via Wolin 1990 *Politics of Being*, p. 168): "Agriculture is today a motorized food industry, in essence the same as the manufacture of corpses in gas chambers and extermination camps, the same as the blockade and starvation of countries, the same as the manufacture of atomic bombs." Hyde calls this "morally and rhetorically inappropriate." For the particular showing-forth of death "calls for witnesses who can speak the truth of the horror that faces them by using words in such a way that the resulting rhetoric becomes itself a showing-forth of what is — an epideictic event that is moving enough to have its witnesses realize how dreadful the consequences can be when people know not how to take to heart the presence and cries of others who would have us never forget what was done to them."

Jeremiah 24:7 is cited as the Old-Testament conscience-rhetoric parallel ("I will give them a heart to know Me"). Then Gadamer's *Truth and Method* 2nd rev. ed. (xxxviii) is cited for the philosophical caution: "What man needs is not just the persistent posing of ultimate questions [for example, the meaning and truth of Being], but the sense of what is feasible, what is possible, what is correct, here and now. The philosopher, of all people, must, I think, be aware of the tension between what he claims to achieve and the reality in which he finds himself."

The section pivots to Cicero as the Roman recovery of conscience-rhetoric. Six Cicero loci are cited: *De oratore* 3.16.61 (the "absurd and unprofitable severance between the tongue and the brain"); *De oratore* 3.14.55 (orators sans virtue would be "putting weapons into the hands of madmen"); *De officiis* 1.7.22 (philosophy as essential to the well-equipped orator); *Orator* 33.118; *De officiis* 1.6.19 (active life vs. study); *De oratore* 1.8.32 (the orator's moral function — "to bring help to the suppliant, to raise up those who are cast down, to bestow security, to set free from peril, to maintain men in their civil rights"). Cicero, practitioner of the *vita activa*, equips knowledge for civic action through the "art of eloquence" (*oratio*).

The chapter's final paragraphs extend the political reading: BT's "considerateness" (*Rücksicht*) and "forbearance" (*Nachsicht*) at BT 159 are the rhetorical-ethical dispositions Nazism violated. BT 436's claim — "Only in communicating and struggling [with others] does the power of destiny become free" — is the closest Heidegger comes to an authentic-community rhetoric. Aristotle and especially Cicero ("who emphasize the essential relationship that exists between rhetoric and democracy") provide the support Heidegger could have but did not draw on. The closing line: "as Heidegger developed his philosophy, a sincere concern for the truth and goodness of rhetoric disappeared from view... such a disappearance has never been a healthy sign for rhetoric." Cicero's *De oratore* 3.16.61 critique "is well worth remembering when journeying on Heidegger's path of thought that leads away from rhetoric and the call of conscience and toward poetry and the call of Being."

## 4. Main Positions / Claims (8 positions)

**P1** (core thesis): The Heideggerian *Ruf des Gewissens* (BT §§54–60) operates structurally as primordial epideictic rhetoric — silent, ontologically-prior, summoning Dasein to authentic self-responsibility.

**P2** (core thesis): Aristotle's pathē-analysis (*Rhet.* II.1–11) supplies Heidegger with the rhetorical-existential vocabulary that BT operationalizes as fear (§30), anxiety (§40), conscience (§§54–60); GA 18 (SS 1924) is the conceptual hinge.

**P3** (supporting): *Phronesis* IS *Gewissen* "set in motion" that makes an action *durchsichtig* (transparent) — Heidegger said this in his SS 1924–26 lectures (Taminiaux 1992 from manuscripts; Gadamer 1976 confirms from the 1923 NE seminar).

**P4** (supporting): Anger, fear, pity are not psychological epiphenomena but operations that restructure existential temporality and spatiality — precisely what BT's mood-analysis enacts.

**P5** (supporting): The enthymeme's audience-completion function bridges to Heidegger's *sich etwas zu Herzen nehmen* (GA 18 [128]) — *enthymeisthai* is dual (cognitive + emotional). This dissolves the *Rhet.* III contradiction.

**P6** (core thesis): Heidegger's later "call of Being" is associated with *poiēsis* rather than *epideixis*; his never-explicit treatment of the difference is a productive omission Hyde's chapter contests.

**P7** (core thesis): Heidegger's failure to acknowledge rhetoric's ontological status correlates with his moral-rhetorical failure (1933, 1949 agriculture-extermination equation, 1930s–40s silence) — the absence of conscience-rhetoric leaves a vacuum Cicero's tradition would have filled.

**P8** (methodological): The chapter is itself epideictic — it performs the rhetoric it theorizes, sounding a call of conscience to readers about Heidegger's missed opportunity, "with a bit of violence" following Heidegger's own pedagogical model.

## 8. Tensions & Open Questions (9 tensions; T1–T9)

**T1** (internal): Hyde acknowledges Heidegger "never attempts to relate the call of conscience and epideictic discourse" (p. 96). Hyde's bridge thesis (P1) is therefore a calibrated extrapolation — not a contradiction but a declared productive ambiguity.

**T2** (internal): Tension between conscience-as-silent (BT 318) and conscience-as-rhetorical (P1). Hyde resolves through the *Sage* / *Aufzeigen* reading: language at the ontological level is showing-and-letting-be-seen, not utterance.

**T3** (cross-essay, VT5): Pathos as cognitive resource (Hyde — orator-deployable) vs. ontological substrate (Gross, *Befindlichkeit*-substrate). Compatible at register-distinction but operationally distinct. Hyde's chapter does both registers — pathē are "rooted in the emotional fabric of a person's existence" AND deployed by the orator. **Hyde's epideictic-conscience claim SUPPORTS Gross's pathos-as-substrate claim (E11).**

**T4** (cross-pipeline, VT10): Hyde's epideictic emphasis vs. BCAP unit U-FP3a's deliberative emphasis. Heidegger's GA 18 §§13–14 centers on deliberative rhetoric; Hyde imports the I.3 typology to isolate the third (epideictic) genre as the conscience-bearer. Heidegger himself only briefly acknowledges epideictic at GA 18 [125] as "*eine Lobrede*." This is productive cross-pipeline tension.

**T5** (cross-essay): Hyde aligns broadly with HR-01-GROSS, HR-02-GADAMER, HR-05-STRUEVER, HR-06-KISIEL on continuity (SS 1924 → BT) but distinguishes himself by register: Gross's pathos genealogy is more Stoic-Lutheran; Struever's *Alltäglichkeit* reading is more biological-temporal; Kisiel's protopolitical reading is more political; Hyde's is most distinctively rhetorical-epideictic. **Hyde is the only contributor whose chapter is structured around BT §§54–60 specifically.**

**T6** (cross-essay): Hyde's continuity thesis directly opposes HR-07-POGGELER's restriction thesis. Hyde acknowledges Heidegger's late turn to poetry as missed opportunity; Pöggeler reads it as decisive abandonment.

**T7** (internal): Hyde acknowledges (p. 96, citing Kisiel 1993, 296–97) that Heidegger's gravitation toward HEARING risks losing the speaker's active involvement. Resolution: the chapter ITSELF is epideictic (P8) — Hyde IS the orator; the call-of-conscience phenomenon proper is hearer-side.

**T8** (cross-pipeline, [INTERP-low]): BT pipeline unit BT-D2-U2 (§§54–60) treats conscience within the existentialia framework; Hyde retroactively assigns rhetorical structure. Phase 4 question: supplementation or revision of BT-D2-U2? Likely supplementation.

**T9** (internal): Section V's political move sits structurally apart from Sections I–IV's textual analysis. The connection (P7) is rhetorical rather than logical — appropriate to an epideictic chapter that performs what it argues for.
