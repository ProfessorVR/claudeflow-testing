## 1. Thesis/Aim

Holmqvist et al. (*Behavior Research Methods* 55, 2023, pp.364-416) identify a gap between what is empirically known to affect eye-tracking data quality and what researchers actually report in published studies. Their argument proceeds in three moves: first, a scoping review of the empirical literature on how instrument, methodology, environment, and participant factors affect the accuracy, precision, data loss, and higher-order measures obtained from an eye tracker; second, a comparison of that empirical foundation against five existing reporting guidelines and against a database of 207 published eye-tracking studies of judgement and decision-making (from Fiedler et al., 2019), to show that guidelines vary substantially and do not match actual reporting practice; and third, the derivation of a flexible, minimal reporting guideline — distinct from a mandatory reporting *standard* — built from what the empirical review shows actually matters to a study's conclusions (FE, p.365). The paper's motivating stakes are explicitly the "replication crisis" (citing Open Science Collaboration, 2015, that only about a third of psychology findings replicate) and the claim that eye-tracking research is particularly vulnerable to this because of "an over-reliance on the performance of the eye trackers and their default algorithms and settings" (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.365).

## 4. Measures & methods

This is the paper's core content and the reason for its inclusion: a field-wide catalogue of eye-tracking apparatus, signal properties, and analysis pipelines, offered as the empirical basis for what any eye-tracking study — boredom-related or otherwise — should report.

**Measurement techniques.** Table 1 and the *Eye-tracking methods* section catalogue six families: video-based Pupil-Corneal Reflection (P-CR, the dominant commercial method, computing gaze from the relative pupil-centre/corneal-reflection position in the eye-camera image); Dual-Purkinje Imaging (DPI, an analogue system using the P1/P4 corneal-lens reflection pair); Electrooculography (EOG, estimating orientation from periorbital electrical potential); scleral search coils (a wire coil on the sclera read via an oscillating magnetic field); limbus tracking (iris-sclera border detection); and retinal image-based tracking (registering fine eye movements via retinal vasculature) (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., pp.367-368). Table 2 summarizes 42 cross-comparative benchmarking studies of these systems, concluding that between-tracker differences in precision can span roughly two orders of magnitude while accuracy distributions overlap more but vary widely within a single tracker depending on participant eye physiology and gaze eccentricity (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., ~p.370).

**Data quality quartet.** Four core data-quality measures are formally operationalized: *accuracy* (mean deviation of reported vs. true gaze position during a post-calibration validation procedure, in degrees of visual angle); *precision* (reproducibility of gaze position under a stable true gaze, via RMS sample-to-sample deviation — Eq. 1, RMS-S2S = √[(1/(n-1))Σ(xi-xi+1)²+(yi-yi+1)²] — or standard deviation/BCEA of a fixation segment); *data loss* (proportion of samples lacking gaze coordinates, e.g. blinks accounting for roughly 2% loss); and *system latency* (time from an actual eye movement to the corresponding output sample, measurable via five distinct methods including artificial-eye diode switching and high-speed camera comparison) (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., pp.368-370). All four have "0" as their ideal value; worse quality is higher values throughout.

**Calibration.** The paper details the calibration procedure at length: participants fixate a sequence of pre-defined targets (commonly 2-9 points) while the tracker fits a mapping (polynomial or geometric-model-based) between sensor values (P/CR positions) and screen coordinates; the specific number and arrangement of points is itself "a choice of a specific set of equations" affecting resulting accuracy (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., ~p.384). Calibration target choice affects fixation dispersion; calibration luminance should match subsequent stimulus luminance because of the pupil-size artefact; and participant-controlled calibration confirmation (vs. leaving target-fixation timing to the software) improves accuracy, though it is not standard practice (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., pp.384-385).

**Sampling frequency and signal processing.** Sampling frequencies for modern video-based trackers range roughly 30-2000+ Hz, with some analogue systems (DPI, scleral coils) digitized up to 10,000 Hz; the paper recommends determining sampling frequency *empirically* from the recorded signal rather than trusting the nominal spec, since several trackers (EyeTribe, Pupil Labs, Tobii 1750, SMI REDm/RED250) show unstable inter-sample intervals (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., pp.388-389). Spatial precision measures (RMS-S2S, STD, BCEA, and power-spectrum-slope "noise colour") each capture a different signal property and should be reported together per Niehorster et al.'s (2020c) four-measure framework; filtering can markedly change these values and should always be reported by name and settings (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.389).

**Fixation and saccade detection.** The paper surveys the major event-detection algorithm families: I-DT (dispersion-threshold, typically 0.75-1.5° spatial / 50-150ms temporal), I-VT (velocity-threshold, typically 20-100°/s peak velocity), the noise-adaptive NH2010 algorithm (Nyström & Holmqvist, 2010), the noise/data-loss-robust I2MC (Hessels et al., 2017, designed for infant research), and machine-learning end-to-end detectors — GazeNet (Zemblys et al., 2019) and the Deep eye movement classifier (Startsev et al., 2019) — that learn from human-coded examples and additionally detect smooth pursuit (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., pp.391-392). Algorithm choice and threshold settings have large, well-documented effects: Holmqvist et al. (2012) found that degrading precision from 0.03° to 0.37° increased mean fixation duration from 430ms to 630ms and cut the number of detected fixations by roughly a third on the same underlying data (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.392).

**AOI and higher-order measures.** Area-of-Interest analysis (dwell time, hit rate, transitions between AOIs) is sensitive, non-linearly, to AOI size and margin, with effect sizes described as large (Hessels et al., 2016; Orquin et al., 2016) (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., pp.393-394). Higher-order measures built atop AOI/event output include scan-path analysis, hidden Markov models, recurrence quantification analysis, gaze-transition entropy, and heatmap-based analysis, all of which inherit and propagate any upstream data-quality problems (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., pp.394-395).

**Pupil dilation / pupillometry.** Pupil-size signals are singled out for two systematic artefacts — the pupil-size artefact (light-driven pupil constriction/dilation altering the P-CR gaze estimate itself) and the pupil-foreshortening artefact (off-axis gaze deforming the imaged pupil shape, artificially shortening measured diameter) — both of which the paper flags as "of particular importance for experiments using the pupil size as a measurement for estimates of the participant's psychological state (e.g. cognitive load or arousal)" (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.379). A dedicated subsection (*Arousal, mental fatigue and cognitive workload*, ~p.383) reviews a large controlled-study literature in which mental workload and arousal are positively associated with pupil dilation (Kahneman & Beatty, 1966; Ahern & Beatty, 1979; Hess & Polt, 1964), with blink rate and microsaccade findings that are more mixed and task-dependent (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.383).

**VR-specific data quality (directly relevant to this cluster's dataset bridge).** The *Special recording environments* subsection reports that head-mounted VR eye trackers are comparatively understudied but that Pastel et al. (2021) found significantly poorer precision in SMI Vive VR goggles than in SMI glasses (with accuracy differing only in some conditions), and Stein et al. (2021) measured end-to-end latency of common VR headsets at 45-81ms (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.377).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Reporting practices diverge sharply from what the empirical review shows matters: only 12.5% of the 207 surveyed studies report the recording environment, while eye-tracker model (90.8%) and sampling frequency (75.8%) are commonly reported despite sampling frequency mattering less than data quality does in most cases (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.395-396).
- The five existing reporting guidelines compared "differ substantially from one another," were built on small-group consensus rather than the present empirical review, and are "not widely used" (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.365).
- The paper concludes that existing guidelines are "incomplete and inconsistent, and often biased towards specific research fields" (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., ~p.400).
- The derived minimal core (Table 7) comprises nine strongly-recommended items: eye-tracker details/technique; sampling frequency (empirically determined); setup/geometry including head stabilization; recording environment; participant instructions; empirically-determined data quality (accuracy/precision/data loss for gaze; at least data loss, ideally precision, for pupil size); processing/analysis-step description with parameters; firmware/software versions; and exclusion criteria (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., pp.399-400).
- The paper explicitly rejects a single one-size-fits-all checklist in favor of a flexible structure: Table 6's full item catalogue, Table 7's minimal core, and Tables 8-18's context-specific supplements (clinical/case studies, wearable/unconstrained recording, pupil-size estimation, AOI research, multi-screen setups, gaze-contingent research, saccade-reaction-time studies, etc.), on the ground that "not all of the many aspects in Table 6 are relevant for each and every study" (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.400).
- A conceptual distinction is drawn and maintained throughout: *reporting guidelines* (informed-choice recommendations) versus *reporting standards* (mandatory, authority-approved requirements); this paper deliberately delivers only the former, while noting its findings could seed the latter (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., p.365).
- Several gaps are conceded in the empirical review itself, e.g. "controlled studies of the effect of vibrations on eye-tracking data quality appear to be lacking," and "very few studies have been made of the effect on higher-order analyses of changing settings and varying data quality" (FAITHFUL-Holmqvist et al., pp.377, 395).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This is the strongest methodological-authority candidate anywhere in the Boredom Secondary cluster for a `grounds-measure` bridge to the not-yet-built Boredom Experiment (VR Attention Study) dataset entry's eye-tracking channel — not because it studies boredom (it does not), but because it *is* the field's own empirically-derived reporting standard, distilled from a scoping review plus a 207-study reporting-practices audit plus five competing guidelines. Table 7's nine-item minimal core (eye-tracker technique; empirically-verified sampling frequency; setup/geometry; environment; participant instructions; empirically-determined accuracy/precision/data loss for gaze and pupil signals; processing/analysis parameters; firmware/software versions; exclusion criteria) is a directly usable checklist against which Part III's own eye-tracking methods section — whenever the VR Attention Study dataset entry is built — should be audited. The `grounds-measure` edges below are tagged `****** UNVERIFIED:` since the dataset entry does not exist on disk *(P/anticipatory-application)*.

*(anticipatory-application)* The paper's VR-specific findings are unusually concrete and load-bearing for this cluster: Pastel et al.'s (2021) finding that VR-integrated (SMI Vive) eye-tracker precision is measurably worse than glasses-based tracking, and Stein et al.'s (2021) 45-81ms VR headset end-to-end latency figures, are direct cautions against assuming that desktop- or glasses-based eye-tracking benchmarks transfer unmodified to Part III's VR pole. Any VR-vs-2D-PC eye-tracking comparison Part III designs should report device-specific precision and latency separately for each delivery condition rather than a single pooled figure *(P/anticipatory-application)*.

*(anticipatory-application)* The Ayres et al. (2021) meta-finding cited at ~p.383 — that eye-movement measures of cognitive load are more sensitive than heart-rate, skin, and brain (EEG) measures across 33 experiments — is a cross-strand methodological consideration for how Part III should weight its EEG (Strand D) versus eye-tracking (Strand E) channels if both are instrumented in the same dataset: this is a reason to treat eye-tracking as a primary rather than merely confirmatory measure of engagement/disengagement, not a claim this source makes about boredom specifically, since the meta-study concerns cognitive load broadly *(P/anticipatory-application)*.
