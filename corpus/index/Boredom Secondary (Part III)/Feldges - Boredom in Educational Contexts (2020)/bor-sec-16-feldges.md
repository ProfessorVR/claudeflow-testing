# bor-sec-16 — Feldges & Pieczenko, "Boredom in Educational Contexts: A Critical Review" (2020)

**Type:** critical literature review (psychology + neuroscience + phenomenology) · **Discipline:** philosophy of education / educational phenomenology, reviewing educational-psychological and neuroscientific literatures · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand B — humanities-pedagogy application unit; the single most directly relevant Strand-B source to Part III's own survey/EEG/eye-tracking student-boredom frame, since it explicitly stages a psychology-vs-phenomenology confrontation over how to classify and measure academic boredom.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Feldges and Pieczenko (Encyclopaideia 24.57, 2020) stage a three-way conversation — psychological, neuroscientific, and educational-phenomenological — about boredom in schooling, and use a recently developed four-type taxonomy of academic boredom (Götz & Frenzel, 2006) as the test case. Their central claim is that this taxonomy, however practically useful for classroom recognition, conceptually conflates antecedent states, "actual" boredom, and resultant/directed-emotion states into a single undifferentiated category of "boredom" (FE, p.9-10). Building their phenomenological apparatus mainly from Husserl (via the German educational phenomenologist Günther Buck) rather than from Heidegger, they argue that approaches driven chiefly by a wish to control and avoid boredom foreclose two further questions: whether boredom might have a legitimate, even necessary, place in institutionalized education, and whether much of the boredom teachers observe is in fact an artifact of the pedagogical practice — specifically "frontal instruction" — rather than an intrinsic feature of learning as such (FE, p.11-12).

## 2. Structure

The article runs seven numbered sections across fifteen pages, plus a bilingual (English/Italian) abstract and author notes on p.1.

1. **Introduction** (p.2) — motivates the topic via Götz et al.'s (2007) estimate that students experience boredom in roughly half of lessons, frames a third-person/naturalistic versus first-person/phenomenological dichotomy (citing Nagel and Wittgenstein on boredom's private, subjective character), and previews the plan to bring educational psychology (via Götz & Frenzel, 2006) into conversation with Husserl and Buck.
2. **Defining and categorising boredom** (p.3-4) — surveys competing definitions (Fenichel, Lipps, Pekrun), stages the mood-versus-emotion debate via Heidegger's Being and Time and a single citation of Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics (FCM), Schopenhauer, Bollnow, and Prinz, and closes by bracketing trait-boredom in favor of state-boredom.
3. **Psychological and neuroscientific accounts of boredom** (p.4-6) — lays out psychology's "epistemological and ontological dilemma," the functional/regulatory account of boredom (Bench & Lench, 2013), autonomic-arousal measures (London et al., 1972), the Executive Control Network (ECN) / Default Mode Network (DMN) neuroimaging literature (Danckert & Merrifield, 2018; Andrews-Hanna, 2012), and introduces Götz & Frenzel's (2006) four-type taxonomy of academic boredom.
4. **Phenomenology and the bored Self** (p.6-9) — introduces Buck's *Lernen und Erfahrung* (learning as a process of experiencing, not a teleological endpoint), Jean-Luc Marion's account of self-abandonment in boredom, and three Husserlian building blocks: the stream of consciousness (§4.1), the pure ego (§4.2), and Husserl's phenomenology of internal time-consciousness — retention/protention and the subjective-versus-cosmic-time split (§4.3).
5. **What is it like to be bored?** (p.8-10) — applies the Husserlian framework type-by-type to Götz & Frenzel's taxonomy: indifferent boredom (Type 1) as an antecedent, relaxation-adjacent "in-between stage"; calibrating boredom (Type 2) as the closest fit to "genuine" boredom; goal-seeking (Type 3) and reactant (Type 4) boredom as increasingly shading into directed, escape-oriented emotion rather than the mood of boredom itself.
6. **Discussion** (p.10-12) — argues the mood/emotion distinction is neglected by the mainstream educational-psychological literature (naming Pekrun & Linnenbrink-Garcia's *International Handbook of Emotions in Education*), reframes the normative question as whether boredom deserves "a better reputation," and closes by asking how much classroom boredom is actually produced by frontal, compliance-demanding instruction rather than being intrinsic to schooling.
7. **Conclusion** (p.12-13) — restates the definitional impasse, the taxonomy critique, and the two open normative questions, closing on a bucket-versus-roof analogy: recognizing and managing boredom's symptoms (buckets under a leak) versus asking the bigger structural question about educational practice (fixing the roof).

## 3. Constructs

Mapped onto the controlled construct axis, the source treats:

- **profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)** — cited once, via Heidegger's claim that profound boredom "removes all things and men and oneself" into "a remarkable indifference" and "reveals being as a whole" (Heidegger, 1993, cited at p.105 of the German edition; quoted at Feldges & Pieczenko p.3). Used only to establish that boredom is a non-intentional mood, not to develop FCM's own structure (see §5 below).
- **situational vs. trait/state boredom** — explicitly named and deliberately narrowed: the paper "will not focus upon the trait or personal pre-disposition to be bored but exclusively consider state-boredom as a token of experienced boredom" (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.4).
- **boredom proneness** — touched on only as the bracketed "trait" pole of the state/trait distinction above; not independently theorized (FE, p.4).
- **mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought** — central to the Type-2 "calibrating boredom" discussion: "free roaming of thoughts, the thinking about hobbies, spare time and one's interests" is explicitly tied to the switch from the Executive Control Network to the Default Mode Network (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.9).
- **attentional disengagement / meta-awareness** — mapped onto Husserl's pre-reflective versus reflective self-given-ness distinction: a non-explicit, non-intentional awareness ("lingering in indifferent boredom") is contrasted with explicit reflective self-awareness ("I am bored") (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.8); ECN deactivation is glossed as the neural correlate of the outward-attention withdrawal.
- **boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)** — introduced via Bench & Lench (2013): boredom "has a functional purpose," informing the individual about being bored and motivating a search for a more interesting environment or goal (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.5). Elpidorou's (2017) "regulatory theory of boredom" is cited in the same vein.
- **flow / engagement (contrast pole)** — appears indirectly through Kuh et al.'s (2008) student-engagement literature, invoked to warn that over-stimulating "edutrainer" pedagogy is no better than under-stimulating boredom (FE, p.11); the paper does not engage flow/Csikszentmihalyi by name.
- **monotony / meaninglessness** — grounded in Lipps's (1903) definition of boredom as a conflict between "the need for intense mental activity and a lack of incitement to it, or an inability to be incited" (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.3).
- **temporality of boredom (time-drag)** — worked out in detail via Husserl's retention/protention account of internal time-consciousness: subjective and "cosmic" (objective) time can "come apart" during a bored episode, so that "time may be perceived as moving slower than it objectively does" (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.9).

Not treated at all: **being-bored-by / being-bored-with** (FCM's first and second forms are never named or distinguished — the paper moves straight from a single third-form citation to Husserl); no engagement with predictive-processing models or ML classification of boredom.

## 4. Reading of FCM

This source's engagement with FCM is a citation-pointer, not a reading. A single verbatim passage — profound boredom "removes all things and men and oneself... into a remarkable indifference. The boredom reveals being as a whole" (Heidegger, 1993, p.105; quoted at Feldges & Pieczenko p.3) — is invoked in §2 alongside Bollnow and Schopenhauer purely to establish that boredom is a non-intentional mood (*Stimmung*) rather than an intentionally-directed emotion, a distinction the paper borrows in the same breath from Being and Time's discussion of anxiety (*Angst*, as opposed to *Furcht*) as an inner-worldly, non-object-directed mode of being (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.3).

FCM's actual argumentative apparatus — the tripartite structure of the three forms (being-bored-by, being-bored-with, and profound boredom proper), the impersonal "it is boring for one" (*es ist einem langweilig*) formula, or the Augenblick-oriented individuation that the third form performs — is never engaged, walked through, or extended anywhere in the paper. Heidegger's role is essentially exhausted by this one quotation; the paper's substantive phenomenological machinery for the rest of the article (stream of consciousness, pure ego, retention/protention) is built from Husserl instead, mediated by Buck's educational-phenomenological appropriation of Husserl. The `bridges-to-fcm` edge recorded below should accordingly be read as a citation-pointer only, not as a sustained reading of FCM — consistent with the brief's note that a bridges-to-fcm edge is not mandatory for Strand B units and should not be forced.

## 5. Findings/Claims

- No commonly agreed definition of boredom exists across the psychological, neuroscientific, and phenomenological literatures surveyed; the concept remains "contested, complex, multidimensional and constructed" (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.4, citing Vodanovich, 2003; Elpidorou, 2017).
- Intentionality (object-directedness) is the clearest available marker distinguishing moods (boredom, anxiety — non-object-directed) from emotions (object-directed); Heidegger, Schopenhauer, Bollnow, and Prinz converge on treating boredom as a mood, but the mainstream educational-psychological literature (Pekrun's five-component account; Götz & Hall's "academic boredom") does not observe the distinction, instead addressing moods as "low-intensity emotions" (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.4, p.11, quoting Pekrun, 2015, p.2).
- Neuroscientific evidence — ECN deactivation coupled with DMN activation — unexpectedly redescribes well onto the Husserlian account of a switch from outward to inward directedness of attention; the authors call this fit "remarkable" precisely because neuroscience is usually read as supporting cognitive psychology rather than phenomenology (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.11). However, DMN activation is necessary but not sufficient for boredom, since it also occurs during meditation-induced depersonalization without boredom being present (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.5, citing Lutz, 2007; Sierra, 2009).
- Götz & Frenzel's (2006) four-type taxonomy of academic boredom (indifferent, calibrating, goal-seeking, reactant), plotted on excitation-by-negative-valence axes, is argued to conflate: an antecedent/precursor state (Type 1, indifferent boredom — closer to relaxation and depersonalization than to boredom's essential unpleasantness, "not «there» yet"); a form the authors treat as closest to genuine boredom (Type 2, calibrating boredom — unpleasant, inactive, mind-wandering, DMN-consistent); and resultant, increasingly directed-emotion states (Types 3-4, goal-seeking and reactant boredom — which shade into the desire to escape rather than the mood of boredom itself) (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.9-10).
- This conflation matters practically: a classification that folds antecedents, "pure" boredom, and consequences together under one label licenses treating the whole spectrum as something to be recognized, regulated, and controlled, which forecloses asking whether boredom might have a legitimate or even necessary role in education (FE, p.11-12).
- Drawing on Buck's account of learning as a "process of experiencing" rather than a teleological, achievement-endpoint process, the authors reframe boredom (and its failure-to-learn counterpart, frustration) as potentially revealing something about the structure of the educational encounter itself, not merely as an obstacle to eliminate (FAITHFUL-Feldges, p.6-7).
- The paper poses, without resolving, two normative questions: (a) whether boredom deserves "a better reputation" as an intrinsic, perhaps even socially necessary feature of institutionalized schooling (e.g., training in deferred gratification), and (b) how much educationally evoked boredom is actually an artifact of poor pedagogical practice — specifically "frontal instruction" requiring a compliant, receptively-but-passively engaged student — rather than an unavoidable feature of learning as such (FE, p.11-12).
- Closing analogy: a purely recognition/control-oriented psychological approach to boredom is like putting buckets under a leak; a phenomenological approach that asks bigger structural questions about education is like fixing the roof (FE, p.12-13).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit's chief methodological caution for Part III concerns instrument construction: because Feldges and Pieczenko argue that Götz & Frenzel's widely used four-type taxonomy conflates antecedent, core, and resultant states under one "boredom" label, any Part III survey item that resembles or borrows from that taxonomy's category language should be read as potentially capturing a mixture of precursor restlessness, core unpleasant disengagement, and escape-motivated agitation rather than a single unitary construct — a caution worth applying directly to the F7 "WE unmet want" survey reading already flagged in the corrected VLE-proximity analysis *(anticipatory-application)*.

*(anticipatory-application)* The mood-versus-emotion (intentionality) distinction this source foregrounds supports Part III's own phenomenological register for "boredom" as an attunement (mood) rather than a transient low-arousal emotion on a Pekrun-style achievement-emotion scale; where Part III's instruments must use emotion-scale language for practical survey purposes, this source offers a citable warrant for treating the resulting self-report data as an imperfect proxy for a mood-level phenomenon rather than as the phenomenon itself *(anticipatory-application)*.

*(anticipatory-application)* The paper's observation that DMN/ECN neuroimaging findings unexpectedly redescribe well onto a phenomenological (rather than a purely cognitive-psychological) account is a usable warrant for Part III's own attempt to interpret EEG/physiological signal through a phenomenologically informed construct of boredom, rather than assuming that physiological data must be read exclusively through control-value / achievement-emotion theory *(anticipatory-application)*. That said, this source's own measurement discussion — self-report interviews, autonomic arousal (heart rate, skin conductance), and general DMN/ECN neuroimaging — does not include EEG-specific, pupillometric, or gaze/eye-tracking measures. *(anticipatory-application)* Finally, the paper's closing distinction between boredom as possibly intrinsic to institutionalized (frontal-instruction) education and boredom as a symptom of specifically poor pedagogical practice offers Part III a two-pronged interpretive frame for its own desktop-3D versus video-only strata and lab-VR versus deployment-2D-PC poles: elevated self-reported boredom in the more passive, frontal-instruction-adjacent delivery condition (deployment-2D-PC) could be read either as a structural feature of that format or as a design/pedagogical shortfall specific to that condition, and Part III's discussion should keep both readings live rather than collapsing to either by default *(anticipatory-application)*.
