## 1. Thesis/Aim

Cheval, Miller, Orsholits, Berry, Sander, and Boisgontier open from a gap in exercise psychology: prior work on attentional bias toward physical-activity (vs. inactivity) stimuli relied almost entirely on indirect, reaction-time-based paradigms (Stroop, dot-probe), and, to the authors' knowledge, only one prior study (Giel et al., 2013) had used eye-tracking for a more direct measure — and that study targeted anorexia nervosa patients and athletes, not a healthy general population (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.5-6). The stated objective is "to extend current insights in the associations between the usual level of physical activity and attentional bias toward physical activity versus inactivity stimuli" by combining indirect and direct indicators in one design (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.6-7). The authors pair a visual dot-probe task with an eye tracker in 77 healthy young adults spanning low to high usual physical-activity levels, using reaction time (indirect), first-gaze location and gaze duration (direct visual-attention indices), and pupil dilation (arousal/value index) as four dependent measures (FE, ~p.3, 6-7). Four hypotheses: more physically active participants, relative to less active ones, will (H1) detect a dot faster where a physical-activity image had been; (H2) look first toward physical-activity images; (H3) gaze longer at them; and (H4) show greater pupil dilation viewing them (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.7). An exploratory fifth aim asks whether the eye-tracking indices and pupil dilation predict RT performance (FE, ~p.7).

## 3. Constructs

Mapped onto the controlled construct axis, this source's own vocabulary treats none of the eleven boredom constructs directly — there is no first-, second-, or third-form FCM boredom, no boredom proneness, no mind-wandering, no monotony/meaninglessness, no temporality-of-boredom, and no functional/regulatory account of boredom anywhere in the text. Two constructs can nonetheless be populated legitimately, via analogical readings this corpus entry supplies rather than anything the source itself asserts:

- **attentional disengagement / meta-awareness** *(P/anticipatory-application)* — the apparatus is built entirely around attentional orienting and engagement/disengagement, though never framed as boredom. The Introduction uses "disengage" analogously: a person "spontaneously allocate[s] attention toward physical activity opportunities... and... disengage[s] from opportunities to minimize effort" (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.4). Read against this cluster's attention-failure boredom constructs (Eastwood et al.; Fisher/Hamilton), the gaze apparatus operationalizes the *same mechanism* — sustained vs. withdrawn visual engagement — applied to a different eliciting content (relevance/reward, not tedium). A structural, not textual, parallel.
- **flow / engagement (contrast pole)** *(P/anticipatory-application)* — the paper's own term "attentional engagement" (its label for gaze duration, FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.7) supplies a genuine textual anchor, though the source never invokes Csikszentmihalyi or flow. Sustained gaze toward physical-activity stimuli, read as indexing content perceived as *relevant to current concerns* (Pool et al., 2016, FE ~p.5), is analogically continuous with this cluster's flow/engagement pole — attention willingly and sustainedly held, opposite boredom's withdrawal.

All other constructs are genuinely absent, not merely under-emphasized. **Profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)**, **being-bored-by (1st form)**, and **being-bored-with (2nd form)** find no purchase in a paper with no Heideggerian or phenomenological register whatsoever. **Situational vs. trait/state boredom** and **boredom proneness** are absent as boredom constructs, though the study's own design — a trait-like "usual physical activity level" (IPAQ) moderating a within-session attentional response — has a loose statistical-design resemblance to trait-proneness-moderating-state designs elsewhere in this cluster, noted here only in passing, not scored. **Mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought**, **monotony / meaninglessness**, and **temporality of boredom (time-drag)** are never mentioned. **Boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)** comes closest to a secondary parallel — the Discussion frames attentional bias as functioning "in the regulation of physical activity behaviors" (FE, ~p.17) — but since the source's regulatory story concerns reward-learning rather than boredom's disengage-and-seek-novelty function, this is left as an observation (see §7) rather than a scored construct edge, to avoid diluting the two load-bearing analogical links above.

## 4. Measures & methods

The experiment centers on a **visual dot-probe task** adapted from MacLeod, Mathews, & Tata (1986) and reusing the exact stimulus set from Kullmann et al. (2014): five image pairs, each showing the same woman either physically active or physically inactive, matched for color, brightness, visual complexity, valence, and arousal so that energy expenditure is the only systematically varying feature (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.9-10). Each trial opened with a central fixation cross (white background) for a randomized 800-1100 ms, contingent on the participant's own gaze landing on it; two images (one active, one inactive) then appeared simultaneously on the left and right of the screen for 4000-4500 ms; a black dot then replaced one image, and participants pressed "S" (dot left) or "L" (dot right) as fast as possible (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.9-10). The task comprised 6 practice and 80 experimental trials, dot location and stimulus side fully counterbalanced, and is openly archived on Zenodo (doi:10.5281/zenodo.3371190) — a transparency level notably higher than most sources in this cluster (FE, ~p.10).

**Apparatus note (extraction limitation):** the extracted text specifies the eye-tracking outcome variables in operational detail (first gaze, gaze duration, pupil dilation, all logged per trial) but at no point names the eye-tracker's manufacturer/model, sampling rate, screen-to-eye distance, or calibration procedure — details that would ordinarily appear in an eye-tracking methods section. This information may reside in a supplementary methods section, or in Figure 1 (an image, not machine-readable text) not captured by this `pdftotext` extraction; it should not be inferred or fabricated, and is flagged here as a genuine gap in this corpus entry's apparatus description rather than papered over.

Eighty-four students were recruited via posters, the university library, and the Psychology Department Research Subject Pool; exclusion criteria included psychiatric/neurological history, severe mental disorder, and psychotropic-medication or illicit-drug use (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.8). Sessions ran individually in a light-standardized basement cubicle; before the dot-probe task, participants completed an unrelated 8-10-minute RT task (data not used here) and a confound questionnaire (hunger, thirst, PA the current/previous day, sleep, caffeine, cigarettes) (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.8). Seven of 84 recruits did not complete the physical-activity questionnaire, yielding a final **N=77** (52 women, 25 men; mean age 21.7 ± 3.8; mean BMI 21.5 ± 3.1 kg/m²), split descriptively into 34 "active" and 43 "inactive" via the 150 min/week moderate-to-vigorous guideline (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.13). Usual physical activity was measured continuously via an adapted **International Physical Activity Questionnaire** (IPAQ; Craig et al., 2003); a 2-item scale also captured intention to be active (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.8, 10).

Four dependent measures were derived from this single paradigm: (1) **reaction time** to detect the dot (log-transformed; incorrect responses [0.9%] and RTs <200/>1500 ms [0.4%] excluded), the indirect behavioral index; (2) **first gaze location** (logistic mixed model) — the attentional-orienting index; (3) **gaze duration**, relative % viewing time (linear mixed model) — the attentional-engagement index; and (4) **pupil dilation**, max pupil-size difference between active/inactive stimuli (values <2mm/>4.5mm and diffs >|1|mm excluded) — the arousal/value index, grounded in value-representation literature (Pauli et al., 2015; Prévost et al., 2013; Seymour et al., 2007; Pool, Pauli, Kress, & O'Doherty, 2019) (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.10-12). All four outcomes were modeled with linear/logistic mixed-effects models in R (lme4, lmerTest), participants and stimuli as crossed random effects (Boisgontier & Cheval, 2016; Judd, Westfall, & Kenny, 2012), convergence pursued through sequential optimizers (BOBYQA, Nelder-Mead, nlminb/optimx, L-BFGS-B), effect sizes as conditional pseudo-R² (MuMIn) (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.11-12). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Geneva Canton (CCER2019-00065).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- **H1 (RT) confirmed, conditionally:** a dot-location x usual-PA interaction (b=-.02, p=.002) showed only high-PA participants (+1SD) detected the dot faster at the active-stimulus location (b=-.025, p=.003); low/moderate-PA participants showed no advantage. 40% of RT variance explained; no gender effect (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.13-14).
- **H2 (first gaze) confirmed, conditionally:** odds of first-gazing at the active stimulus rose with usual PA (OR=1.14, p=.012), significant at moderate (OR=1.42) and high (OR=1.61) PA but only a trend at low PA (OR=1.24, p=.065); 28.1% variance; no gender effect (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.14-15).
- **H3 (gaze duration) confirmed, conditionally:** relative viewing time toward active stimuli rose with usual PA (b=7.8, p<.001) — 20.2% longer at high, 12.4% at moderate PA, no difference at low PA; 28.8% variance; no gender effect (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.15-16).
- **H4 (pupil dilation) disconfirmed in direction:** rather than greater dilation, participants showed a *greater decrease* in pupil size viewing active stimuli as usual PA increased (b=-.01, p=.008; -.024mm high PA, -.016mm moderate PA, ns at low PA); only 13.9% variance. The authors themselves caution the effect (~0.02mm) is "very small... and could hardly be distinguished from simple measurement error" (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.16-17, 19).
- **Predicting RT from eye-tracking indices:** greater gaze duration predicted faster RT at the active-stimulus location (p<.001; 36.3% variance), but neither first gaze nor pupil dilation did. For RT at the inactive-stimulus location, pupil dilation showed only a marginal, gender-moderated association (women only) (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.17).
- Six named limitations: self-reported (not accelerometer) PA; correlational design; no neutral/irrelevant stimulus category; images depicting only a woman; luminance not measured as a pupil covariate; and the dot-probe task's own documented reliability/validity concerns (Rodebaugh et al., 2016; Schmukle, 2005; Pennington et al., 2019) (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.20-22).
- A further flagged confound: ~20 minutes of enforced sitting may have suppressed attraction toward inactivity stimuli via "outcome devaluation" (Pool, Pauli, Kress, & O'Doherty, 2019) (FAITHFUL-Cheval et al., ~p.21-22).

## 7. Analytical role

*(P/anticipatory-application)* This source is a plausible `grounds-measure` candidate for the not-yet-built Boredom Experiment (VR Attention Study)'s eye-tracking/attentional-bias channel — **flagged `****** UNVERIFIED`** since that dataset entry does not yet exist and its actual instrumentation is unconfirmed. Cheval et al.'s three-tier design (first gaze for orienting, gaze duration for engagement, pupil dilation for arousal/value, derived from one dot-probe paradigm and cross-validated against behavioral RT) offers a template a VR attention study could adapt: replacing "physical activity vs. inactivity" image pairs with "engaging vs. boring/monotonous" VLE-content pairs, and asking whether the same three gaze/pupil indices track a trait-like predictor (here usual PA level; there boredom proneness or prior VLE engagement). The mapping is speculative and content-independent — the source never mentions boredom, VR, or education — but the measurement architecture itself is genuinely reusable *(P/anticipatory-application)*.

*(P/anticipatory-application)* The paper's own H4 finding — a small, direction-reversed, self-acknowledged near-noise pupil-dilation effect — is a substantive caution for any Part III or dataset design treating pupil dilation as a simple, linearly-scaled arousal proxy. Cheval et al. found *smaller* pupils associated with *greater* stimulus relevance, opposite their hypothesis, and explicitly warn against over-interpreting an effect this size. A VR attention study wagering on pupillometry as a boredom/engagement index should budget for exactly this kind of small, possibly bidirectional, easily confounded signal rather than assuming pupil dilation tracks engagement monotonically *(P/anticipatory-application)*.

*(P/anticipatory-application)* Finally, the paper's theoretical frame — attentional bias reveals what is currently *relevant to a person's concerns* (Pool et al., 2016) — offers Part III a reusable interpretive vocabulary distinct from this cluster's phenomenological and educational literatures. Where Strands A-C ask what boredom *means*, this source's paradigm asks a narrower empirical question: does sustained visual attention toward a stimulus index its perceived relevance, independent of self-report? That question, and its dot-probe/eye-tracking answer format, is the operational core this unit contributes to the cluster's measure axis *(P/anticipatory-application)*.
