# bor-sec-23 — Murphy, Hill, McDonagh & Faherty, "Mundane Emotions: Losing Yourself in Boredom, Time and Technology" (2023)

**Type:** journal article (qualitative marketing/consumer-research interview study, read through an existential-phenomenological, Heidegger-informed lens), *Marketing Theory* 23(2): 275–293 · **Discipline:** marketing/consumer research (Thompson et al.'s existential-phenomenological interview method) applied to first-person COVID-19 lockdown accounts · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand C's first-built unit, and its clearest example of a non-philosophy discipline nonetheless organizing an entire empirical study around Heidegger's FCM typology; the cluster's primary source for boredom's tribal-marketing/consumer-culture register and for the "being bored together" theme; a close but methodologically and disciplinarily independent parallel to bor-sec-08 (Hughes) — same publication year, same general project of reading pandemic-lockdown boredom through FCM, but built on semi-structured interviews (N=15, 20 interviews) rather than a large-N open-ended survey, and on a twofold rather than threefold reduction of Heidegger's typology.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Murphy, Hill, McDonagh and Faherty pursue a doubled aim (FE, p.275–277). Descriptively, they ask "how do different temporal dynamics structure consumers' experience of boredom?" (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.277), using interviews conducted during the 2020–2021 UK/Ireland COVID-19 lockdowns to identify two kinds of boredom — superficial and profound — each tied to a distinct temporal structure (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.275). Conceptually, the paper's larger ambition is to correct an imbalance in marketing and consumer research, which has extensively theorized the shared, dramatic, positive emotions of "consumer tribes" (football crowds, ravers, festival-goers) while having "little to say" about boredom and other undramatic, everyday affects (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.275–277). To this end the authors coin the term "mundane emotions" to name the affective textures that flow from routine, and position boredom as the paper's test case (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.276).

The paper's substantive argument, stated most fully in the Discussion, is that boredom is not simply the negative, individually-experienced state the marketing literature assumes it to be (FE, p.288–289). Superficial boredom — a "situational restlessness" from which people desire distraction — is explained as the predictable output of a "dominant temporal regime" combining digital connectivity (which fragments and de-sequences time) and acceleration (which compresses and speeds up activity); consumers caught in this regime oscillate in a "recursive loop" between boredom and digitally-mediated distraction that never actually resolves the boredom (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.282–284, 289). Profound boredom, by contrast, is explained as arising from an abundance of uninterrupted, solitary time — exactly the temporal condition the pandemic's furlough schemes and lockdowns exceptionally produced — and, following Heidegger, is read as harboring a "utopian," self-transformative potential: several participants used their profound boredom to discover new "tribal passions" (cooking, cycling, baking) that reoriented their sense of self and, in some cases, their careers (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.286–289). The paper closes by asking what it means to be bored *together*, arguing — against the assumption that boredom is purely individual — that boredom can spread within a shared activity (a couple watching a film) and that shared rejection of boredom has historically been a tribe-forming force (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.289–290).

## 2. Structure

The article has an unnumbered introduction plus four main parts, followed by Discussion subsections.

1. **Introduction** (p.275–277) — situates the paper within tribal-marketing research on shared emotion (Cova and Cova's consumer tribes); notes the pandemic's disruption of tribal gathering (festivals, sport, live music); coins "mundane emotions"; states the research question.
2. **"Boredom in consumer culture"** (p.277–279) — reviews how marketing/consumer research treats boredom: as a "modern" mental state tied to individualization, standardization, and digital-age "sameness"; and, when studied directly, as a negative state opposed to "flow" that firms must design away. Pivots to philosophy via Gardiner's point that boredom can harbor "subversive insight."
3. **"Heidegger on boredom"** (p.278–280) — expounds Heidegger's (1995) FCM account of superficial and profound boredom, boredom's relation to time, and situates the study within consumer-research temporality literature (Torres's "temporal regimes"); states the pandemic's dual temporal situation (furlough vs. enforced home-working) as the paper's empirical opportunity.
4. **Methods** (p.280–281) — 15 participants (Ireland/England, ages 20–60), 20 interviews (5 follow-ups) across 2020–2021, semi-structured and phenomenological (after Thompson et al., 1989); iterative first-order coding (35 codes) then second-order abstraction against Heidegger's typology and temporal-regimes literature, yielding three temporal regimes.
5. **Findings** (p.281–289), in two matched halves:
   - *Superficial boredom* — "Experiencing superficial boredom" (Jenny), "Mundane social media consumption" (Paul, Laura), "Connectivity" (Charlotte), "Acceleration" (Jamie, Mark).
   - *Profound boredom* — "Experience of profound boredom" (Catherine), "Consuming solitude" (Richard), "Abundance of time and the discovery of passions" (Charlie, Darren, Stephanie).
6. **Discussion** (p.287–290) — "Beyond tribal emotions and towards investigations of mundane emotions"; "Boredom in an age of distractions" (the paper's central re-theorization of boredom as potentially generative); "Being bored together" (boredom's shareability; punk-era tribal rejection of monotony); closes noting the unaddressed role of class/material conditions.

## 3. Constructs

- **profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)** — the paper's central positive finding: "an existential discomfort in which people struggle with their sense of self," yet one that, "ultimately," catalyzes the "discovery of tribal passions" (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.275). Explicitly cited to Heidegger's "undifferentiated no one" (Heidegger 1995: 135, quoted at Murphy et al., p.286) and to the opening onto "authentic being-in-the-world" in a "moment of vision" (Heidegger 1995: 295, quoted at Murphy et al., p.287).
- **being-bored-by (1st form)** — the only situative form Murphy et al. cite by name and example, via the train-station/airport-lounge case: being "held in limbo, unable to progress our own interests," and "left empty" by one's own disinterest and the unavailability of meaningful alternatives (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.278). This structural pair (limbo/emptiness) is then used, unaltered, as the diagnostic template for every superficial-boredom interview extract.
- **being-bored-with (2nd form)** — not treated. Murphy et al. state that "Heidegger describes two kinds of boredom" (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.278), collapsing FCM's tripartite structure into a binary; the dinner-party example and the second form's distinctive "more diffuse and pervasive" quality are never mentioned. See §4 below.
- **situational vs. trait/state boredom** — not treated in its psychometric sense (no individual-differences literature, no scale). Murphy et al.'s own "superficial" vs. "profound" distinction is phenomenological/existential, not a state/trait personality construct, though the terminological proximity ("situational restlessness") risks conflation and is worth flagging for cross-cluster disambiguation.
- **boredom proneness** — not treated; no psychometric instrument of any kind is used in this qualitative interview study.
- **mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought** — thinly treated, at one remove: cited via Heath and Nixon's (2021) work on "daydreaming" as (a) a symptom the dominant marketing literature associates with negative boredom, and (b) a parallel "mundane emotion" the authors gesture toward for future research (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.278, 287–288). Not analyzed in the interview data itself.
- **attentional disengagement / meta-awareness** — not treated; outside this paper's phenomenological register.
- **boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)** — not explicitly cited (Elpidorou's regulatory-theory literature does not appear in the reference list), but structurally paralleled: the paper's central move — profound boredom as "utopian" and generative of new, self-relevant pursuits rather than simply pathological — runs in the same direction as a functional/adaptive account of boredom's signal value, even though Murphy et al. reach it through Heidegger, Benjamin, and Gardiner rather than through cognitive-psychological regulatory theory (P/anticipatory-application observation, not Murphy et al.'s own framing).
- **flow / engagement (contrast pole)** — treated and contested: the authors identify "boredom is the opposite of a 'flow state'" (Mathwick and Rigdon, 2004) as the dominant consumer-psychology assumption underwriting servicescape-design efforts to eliminate boredom, and position their own account of profound boredom's generative potential as a corrective to this purely deficit-based framing (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.278, 288–289).
- **monotony / meaninglessness** — a central, load-bearing construct throughout: lockdown imposed a "'Groundhog Day'-like monotony" in which "[l]ife appeared on pause" (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.276); Catherine's account of lockdown "at its worst" as "a dull Groundhog Day" with "[n]othing exciting going on, day after day" is offered as the clearest first-person instance (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.285).
- **temporality of boredom (time-drag)** — treated via the paper's own "temporal regimes" apparatus rather than Heidegger's threefold escalation: superficial boredom is explained by connectivity (time fragmented and de-sequenced) and acceleration (time compressed, activity sped up); profound boredom is explained by an inverse condition, an "abundance of uninterrupted time" (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.279, 283–286). Notably, the authors explicitly reject a naive time-drag reading of Heidegger's own claim that boredom is "a feeling of time" (Heidegger 1995: 80): "Heidegger's point isn't that time feels like it is slowing down when bored" (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.279, citing Anderson, 2004) — a qualification of the construct as controlled-vocabulary-labeled.

## 4. Reading of FCM

Murphy et al.'s FCM exposition (p.278–280) is real but reduced. They cite Heidegger's (1995) *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* — the same McNeill/Walker translation Strand A works from — by page number three times (pp.80, 135, 295), and correctly reproduce the first form's own internal structure (being held in limbo; being left empty) via the train-station/airport-lounge example (fcm-04-first-form territory, FCM §§19–23, book pp.78–105). But the paper states flatly that "Heidegger describes two kinds of boredom" (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.278), never mentioning the dinner-party example or the second form's distinctive "more diffuse" quality at all (fcm-05-second-form, FCM §§24–28, book pp.106–131). This is a genuine simplification, not merely a terminological choice: everything the paper calls "superficial boredom" — including Charlotte's account of a couple's diffuse, drifting inattention while watching a film together, which reads far closer to Heidegger's *second* form (bored *with* an activity one nominally chose) than to the first form's determinate, externally-imposed situation — is folded into the single first-form template of limbo-plus-emptiness (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.283). Whether this collapse is defensible interpretively or simply elides a distinction the data itself strains against is a live question this unit does not resolve on Murphy et al.'s behalf; it is flagged rather than corrected.

Profound boredom (fcm-06-third-form, FCM §§29–38, book pp.132–159) is treated more substantively and is the paper's real payload. Murphy et al. quote Heidegger's characterization of profound boredom as leaving one "an undifferentiated no one" (Heidegger 1995: 135, at Murphy et al., p.286) and, crucially, adopt his claim that "this boredom wishes to tell us something" — glossed via Benjamin's "dream bird that hatches the egg of experience" and Gardiner's characterization of profound boredom's "utopian" qualities (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.278–279) — as the interpretive key that licenses reading their participants' passion-discoveries as instances of FCM's revelatory structure rather than as incidental lockdown hobbies. The later citation to Heidegger (1995: 295) for "authentic being-in-the-world" opening up in a "moment of vision" (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.287) is textually harder to place: this page number falls well beyond the boredom chapters' own range as established by this cluster's other FCM citations (book pp.78–168, cf. bor-sec-08's mapping), suggesting either a citation drawn from FCM's later material on selfhood and world-forming applied retrospectively to the boredom discussion, or an imprecision in the source's own page reference; this unit notes the discrepancy honestly rather than silently reconciling it.

Finally, the paper's own "Boredom and Time" subsection touches fcm-07-particular-profound's territory (the historical/epochal question of whether profound boredom remains possible under contemporary conditions) without citing that material directly: following Svendsen, Murphy et al. note that profound boredom was historically the privilege of those with free time, and ask — citing Buchanan, Gardiner, and Hand — "whether profound boredom is even possible" in an era of ubiquitous digital distraction (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.279). The paper's abstract explicitly frames this as its extension of Heidegger: "extending Heidegger's theory of boredom to analyse contemporary boredom in an era where digital technology is ubiquitous" (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.275). This is a genuine extension, not restatement — Murphy et al.'s empirical answer is that the dominant contemporary temporal regime (connectivity plus acceleration) makes superficial boredom the modal experience and squeezes out the solitude profound boredom requires, so that profound boredom appears only under the exceptional, imposed conditions of pandemic lockdown (FE, p.289).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Superficial boredom is a common, situational restlessness from which consumers desire distraction; it is produced by a dominant temporal regime combining connectivity (fragmentation/de-sequencing of time) and acceleration (compression of time, speeding of activity) (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.282–284).
- Consumers caught in superficial boredom enter a self-perpetuating "recursive loop": social media (doom-scrolling, mindless feed-checking) is sought as escape from boredom but itself produces the emptiness constitutive of further boredom, so the distraction never actually resolves the state it responds to (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.282–283, 289).
- Digital connectivity can make boredom *shared*: one partner's phone-distraction while watching a film together can de-absorb both partners from the joint activity, spreading superficial boredom within a couple (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.283–284).
- Profound boredom arises from an abundance of uninterrupted, solitary time — a temporal condition the pandemic's furlough schemes and lockdowns exceptionally produced — and is experienced as an intense indifference toward, and struggle with, one's own past/present/future self (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.285–286).
- Despite its unsettling character, profound boredom proved generative: participants used lockdown's forced solitude to discover new "tribal passions" — cooking, baking, cycling, plant propagation, musical instruments — several of which prompted "fresh starts," including one participant's decision to leave a call-centre job and retrain toward a new career (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.286–287).
- These discovered passions were valued, in participants' own accounts, partly for their entrepreneurial/economic potential (a baking hobby becoming a side business, a cycling passion becoming a training-and-gym-ownership plan), which the authors read as evidence of a broader socio-economic "subjectivation" of non-work time as untapped productive opportunity (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.287–288).
- Boredom is not simply the negative, purely individual state the marketing/consumer-research literature assumes; profound boredom in particular has "utopian," self-transformative potential, and boredom generally can be shared/collective rather than strictly individual (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.288–290).
- Historically, tribes have formed out of a *shared rejection* of boredom/monotony — the authors' example is UK punk culture's rejoinder to Fordist-era monotony — suggesting boredom can function as a rallying point against, not merely a target for escape by, tribal formation (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.289–290).
- The study leaves open how material conditions and class shape differential access to profound boredom's generative potential (only those with the financial security of furlough, rather than economic precarity, could convert enforced idleness into passion-discovery), flagged as a limitation for future research (FAITHFUL-Murphy et al., p.290).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* Murphy et al. is a valuable methodological counterweight for Part III precisely because it is not a philosophy paper: it shows a large, non-philosophical discipline (marketing/consumer research) successfully operationalizing Heidegger's boredom typology onto ordinary first-person interview data using a standard qualitative coding procedure (first-order codes to second-order themes), which is a closer methodological analogue to a dissertation combining survey analysis with Heideggerian interpretation than bor-sec-08's own large-N open-ended-survey design *(anticipatory-application)*. Three specific points transfer directly.

First, the paper's temporal-regimes mechanism — connectivity fragments/de-sequences time, acceleration compresses it, and together these two forces produce superficial (not profound) boredom as the modal contemporary experience — offers Part III a ready-made, non-Heideggerian-jargon vocabulary for explaining *why* boredom in a VLE or deployment environment might skew toward the situational/superficial pole rather than the profound: a densely-scheduled, notification-saturated, always-connected learning environment structurally produces superficial restlessness-and-distraction rather than the solitude profound boredom requires, independent of any claim about student disposition *(anticipatory-application)*.

Second, the paper's central re-theorization — that boredom, and specifically profound boredom, can be generative rather than simply a deficit to be designed away — is a caution against treating any measured "student boredom" in Part III's survey data as straightforwardly a design failure to be minimized; Murphy et al.'s finding that several participants' most significant passion-discoveries required going *through* rather than around an uncomfortable, unstructured period is a genuine complication for any framework that treats boredom-minimization as an unambiguous good *(anticipatory-application)*.

Third, "being bored together" is a direct, ready-made precedent for any Part III discussion of boredom's group/co-presence dimension in the VLE: Charlotte's account of one partner's phone-distraction de-absorbing both partners from a shared film is a clean, concrete case of superficial boredom spreading within a dyad via a shared but unevenly-attended-to activity, which bears directly on how a VLE's proximity-voice and group-assignment structure might transmit (rather than merely individually experience) disengagement *(anticipatory-application)*. This point also converges, independently and in the same publication year, with bor-sec-08 (Hughes)'s separate critique that Heidegger's individualist conception of boredom has no room for a collective turning-away from the world — two disciplinarily unrelated 2023 papers pushing on the same limitation in Heidegger's account from pandemic data is a notable cross-cluster resonance worth citing as convergent evidence, not merely a coincidence of publication date *(anticipatory-application)*.
