# Raffaelli, Mills & Christoff (2018) — *The Knowns and Unknowns of Boredom: A Review of the Literature*

**Type:** review article (*Experimental Brain Research* 236:2451–2462, DOI 10.1007/s00221-017-4922-7) · **Discipline:** cognitive psychology / cognitive neuroscience (synthesizes behavioral, fMRI, and EEG literatures on boredom) · **Depth:** deep (12pp) · **Corpus-role:** Strand-C keystone; PRIMARY in-cluster grounding for the debate axis *boredom as unified construct ↔ heterogeneous family*.

## Thesis / Aim

Raffaelli, Mills, and Christoff (R,M&C) set out to synthesize, across psychology, neuroscience, and education, "the neural, cognitive, and behavioral correlates of boredom" (p.2451) into a two-column ledger: correlates that are "known" — consistently observed across studies — and correlates that remain "unknown" — inconsistently observed (p.2451, abstract). The paper is explicitly a roadmap rather than a new empirical study: its stated goal is "to provide a roadmap for researchers who wish to better understand boredom and further investigate boredom and its various correlates" (p.2451). Two framing choices matter for how this source should be used downstream. First, R,M&C restrict their scope throughout to boredom as a transient *state* rather than as a *trait* disposition (p.2452) — trait boredom (boredom proneness) appears only as an occasional secondary correlate, never as the review's target phenomenon. Second, the review's own diagnostic payoff, withheld until the Conclusion, is that the field's persistent inconsistencies (especially around arousal and the brain regions recruited during boredom) may trace to two distinct and separable causes: sloppy, non-standardized operationalization of what is treated as a single construct, and the live possibility — evidenced but not yet validated — that "boredom" may not name one construct at all but "an empirically validated taxonomy of different types of boredom" waiting to be characterized (p.2451, abstract; p.2460). The review does not adjudicate between these two explanations; it argues both need addressing before the field can make further progress.

## Structure

1. **Introduction: What is boredom?** (p.2451) — motivates the review via open questions about boredom's relation to arousal, mind-wandering, time perception, mood, and fatigue; states the roadmap aim.
2. **Table 1 + Definitions and functions of boredom** (p.2452) — surveys competing theoretical schools (psychodynamic, existentialist, arousal, cognitivist), then narrows to two working definitions: Mikulas and Vodanovich's (1993) first common definition and Eastwood et al.'s (2012) now-standard definition, plus Bench and Lench's (2013) functional/adaptive-emotion account.
3. **Boredom as an attentional failure** (pp.2452–2454) — the Mismatch hypothesis (arousal/task-difficulty mismatch, the Yerkes–Dodson law, flow as boredom's contrast pole, Freeman et al.'s 2004 EEG-engagement-index vigilance study, educational/Vygotskian zone-of-proximal-development examples); Task-unrelated thoughts; Attributions of attention (Critcher and Gilovich 2010; Damrad-Frye and Laird 1989).
4. **Time perception** (pp.2454–2455) — the consistent finding that boredom is associated with overestimation of elapsed time.
5. **Agency** (p.2455) — reduced perceived freedom to choose one's own activity as a consistent negative correlate.
6. **Dimensions of affect** (pp.2455–2457) — Valence (consistently negative, and mild rather than intensely aversive) versus Arousal (inconsistent: subjective reports skew low-arousal, autonomic measures skew high-arousal — the directional fractionation hypothesis); Different boredom types (Goetz et al.'s 2014 five-category experience-sampling taxonomy).
7. **Neural correlates** (pp.2457–2459) — Brain regions (the three published fMRI studies to date: Ulrich et al. 2014; Mathiak et al. 2013; Danckert and Merrifield 2016 — convergent default mode network [DMN] activation, divergent hippocampus/insula involvement); Neural oscillations (EEG alpha and beta power, engagement indices).
8. **Mood and fatigue** (pp.2459–2460) — flagged as merely "probable" correlates given how little direct evidence exists.
9. **Conclusion** (p.2460) — two forward recommendations: standardize the operationalization of boredom across studies, and determine empirically whether distinct types of boredom genuinely exist.

## Constructs

R,M&C's own vocabulary is drawn almost entirely from cognitive/affective psychology and neuroscience, not from phenomenology, and maps onto this cluster's construct axis unevenly — some rows receive sustained treatment, several receive none at all.

- **Situational vs. trait/state boredom** is an explicit scoping decision, not a topic under debate: the review states outright that it "encompasses the correlates of boredom as a transient state, rather than boredom as a trait" (p.2452). Trait boredom re-enters only as an occasional secondary correlate — e.g., trait-level boredom proneness predicting overestimated time duration (Danckert and Allman 2005) and elevated heart rate (Merrifield and Danckert 2014) — never as an object of sustained analysis.
- **Boredom proneness** appears narrowly, as whatever the Boredom Proneness Scale (BPS; Farmer and Sundberg 1986) operationalizes, cited twice as a trait-level covariate rather than developed as its own construct (p.2452, p.2456 approx.).
- **Mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought** receives a full dedicated subsection (pp.2453–2454) and the review's only explicit terminological intervention: R,M&C deliberately avoid the term "mind-wandering" in favor of "task-unrelated thoughts," citing Christoff et al. (2016) for a more precise framework (p.2453, n.1) — a choice worth flagging since much of the surrounding literature (and much of this cluster) uses the terms loosely as synonyms.
- **Attentional disengagement / meta-awareness** organizes the entire middle third of the paper under the heading "Boredom as an attentional failure" (p.2452) and resurfaces in the "Attributions of attention" subsection, where Critcher and Gilovich (2010) are read as arguing that negative task appraisals arise only once task-unrelated thoughts have reached metacognitive awareness (p.2454).
- **Boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)** is adopted, not merely reported: Bench and Lench's (2013) claim that boredom functions as "an emotional cue that one needs to pursue a goal different from what one is currently pursuing" (p.2452) is folded directly into the review's working definition of boredom, alongside Eastwood et al.'s (2012) aversive-state definition.
- **Flow / engagement (contrast pole)** is treated as boredom's structural opposite throughout: Csikszentmihalyi's flow (1975, 1990) supplies the "just-challenging-enough" pole against which both under-stimulation and over-stimulation boredom are defined (pp.2452–2453), and Ulrich et al.'s (2014) fMRI study directly contrasts flow, boredom, and anxiety as three points on the same difficulty-mismatch continuum (p.2458).
- **Temporality of boredom (time-drag)** gets its own subsection: overestimation of elapsed time is named one of the review's most consistent correlates (pp.2454–2455), linked to increased physiological arousal and to trait-level boredom-proneness scores.
- **Monotony / meaninglessness** appears only glancingly, as one line among several competing definitional schools — existentialist theories (citing Frankl 1992) are noted as holding that "boredom results from the emptiness following feelings of meaninglessness" (p.2452) — and is then dropped; it is never adopted as an analytic category or returned to elsewhere in the paper.
- **Profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)**, **being-bored-by (1st form)**, and **being-bored-with (2nd form)** are entirely absent — see Reading of FCM below.

## Reading of FCM

This source does not engage Heidegger's *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* at all, directly or indirectly, and no `bridges-to-fcm` edges are asserted for this unit. Nothing in the reference list (108 entries, entirely psychology/neuroscience/education journals and monographs) cites Heidegger, any Heidegger commentator, or any phenomenological source. The single point of near-contact is the review's brief nod to "existentialist theories" of boredom in the Definitions section, where Frankl's (1992) logotherapeutic account of boredom-as-emptiness-after-meaninglessness is listed as one of four competing schools (alongside psychodynamic, arousal, and cognitivist accounts) before the review moves on to adopt the Eastwood/Bench-and-Lench cognitive-functional definition instead (p.2452). Frankl is cited once and never returned to; existentialism is treated as a historical school to be surveyed and set aside, not as a live theoretical option the review adopts or argues against. Combined with the review's explicit state-boredom scoping (which brackets out precisely the register — a *Grundstimmung*, not a transient state — that Heidegger's third form occupies), this confirms that forcing an FCM bridge here would misrepresent the source. Per the cluster's own instructions, FCM engagement is not mandatory for Strand C, and this unit is a clean case of a source that genuinely has none.

## Findings / Claims

1. **Consistent ("known") correlates** (Table 1, p.2452; distributed through pp.2452–2459): over- and under-stimulation relative to task demands; increased frequency of task-unrelated thought; overestimation of elapsed time; reduced agency/perceived choice; negative valence; and, among the small fMRI literature, DMN activation.
2. **Inconsistent ("unknown") correlates**: arousal is the review's central open problem — subjective reports point to boredom as low-arousal (Geiwitz 1966; Pekrun et al. 2002), while autonomic measures (heart rate, electrodermal activity) point to boredom as high-arousal (London et al. 1972; Merrifield and Danckert 2014), a discrepancy formalized as the *directional fractionation hypothesis* (Lacey 1959; Lacey and Lacey 1970) (pp.2455–2456). Recruitment of the hippocampus and anterior insula is similarly inconsistent across the three available fMRI studies — active in Ulrich et al. (2014), deactivated in Mathiak et al. (2013), unreported in Danckert and Merrifield (2016) (pp.2457–2458) — a divergence R,M&C attribute partly to the fact that all three studies operationalized boredom differently (arithmetic under-stimulation; video-game inactivity plus negative affect; a boring video versus sustained attention versus rest) (p.2458).
3. **Probable correlates**: mood and fatigue are flagged as plausibly related to boredom via shared attentional-allocation mechanisms, but the direct evidence is judged too sparse to call either a consistent correlate (pp.2459–2460).
4. **Evidence for boredom subtypes**: Goetz et al.'s (2014) experience-sampling study is presented as the strongest available evidence that boredom may not be one thing — five clusters (indifferent, calibrating, searching, reactant, apathetic) emerged from valence-by-arousal ratings, with individuals tending to report the same category repeatedly over time, suggesting stable individual differences rather than random noise (pp.2456–2457). R,M&C immediately qualify this evidence, however: the five-type classification should "be applied cautiously" since some categories (e.g., positively valenced "indifferent" boredom) sit awkwardly against the near-universal finding that boredom is negatively valenced, and self-reported arousal levels are known to diverge from autonomic arousal measures (p.2457).
5. **The Conclusion's two-pronged diagnosis** (p.2460): R,M&C argue that the field's inconsistencies trace to (a) non-standardized operationalization of boredom across studies — different labs measuring different things under the same name — and, separately, (b) the open, unresolved possibility that distinct types of boredom exist with different antecedents and signatures. They recommend both a more precise, shared operational definition and dedicated empirical work to test — and, if warranted, formally validate — a taxonomy of boredom types, explicitly hedging the latter with "if different types of boredom do indeed exist" (p.2460).

## Constructs-treated & measures-operationalized (page-anchored)

As a literature review, this source *discusses* a wide range of measurement approaches drawn from the studies it synthesizes but *runs* none of them itself — there is no original data collection here, only synthesis and a compact re-creation of one figure (Goetz et al.'s Fig. 1, p.2457).

- Situational vs. trait/state boredom — p.2452 (explicit state-only scoping); pp.2456, 2456 approx. (trait-boredom correlates noted secondarily)
- Boredom proneness — p.2452, p.2456 approx. (BPS discussed as the standard trait-level covariate, not administered by this paper)
- Mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought — pp.2453–2454 (dedicated subsection; terminological correction, n.1)
- Attentional disengagement / meta-awareness — p.2452 (organizing frame), p.2454 (Critcher and Gilovich's appraisal-after-metacognition argument)
- Boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account) — p.2452 (Bench and Lench 2013, adopted into the working definition)
- Flow / engagement (contrast pole) — pp.2452–2453 (Csikszentmihalyi, Yerkes–Dodson), p.2458 (Ulrich et al. 2014 fMRI three-condition design)
- Temporality of boredom (time-drag) — pp.2454–2455 (dedicated subsection)
- Monotony / meaninglessness — p.2452 (single citation to Frankl 1992, not adopted)
- Self-report / survey scale — p.2452 (BPS, Farmer and Sundberg 1986); pp.2456–2457 (Goetz et al. 2014 experience-sampling ratings) — surveyed from the literature, not administered
- Heart rate / HRV — pp.2455–2456 approx. (London et al. 1972; Merrifield and Danckert 2014; directional fractionation hypothesis) — surveyed
- Behavioral (RT / errors / performance-monitoring) — pp.2452–2453 (vigilance-task decrement, Freeman et al. 2004), p.2459 (attentional blink, SART, Stroop task) — surveyed
- EEG alpha/theta power — pp.2458–2459 (Oswald 1962 alpha hypothesis; Pope, Bogart, and Bartolome's 1995 engagement-index formula using beta/[alpha+theta]; Tabatabaie et al. 2014 beta findings) — surveyed, and explicitly flagged as scarce, high-value future-work territory (p.2459)
- *No pupillometry, gaze/fixation, blink/eye-closure, predictive-processing, ML-classification, EEG functional-connectivity, or EEG eyes-open-vs-closed measures appear anywhere in this source.* This is a striking absence given the breadth of the review's other coverage, and it is consistent with the paper's 2017-acceptance date, predating most of the eye-tracking and machine-learning boredom literature this cluster's Strands D/E cover.

## Analytical role (anticipatory-application)

*(P/anticipatory-application) — the following are our interpretive links to Part III, not claims R,M&C make themselves.*

First, and most directly: this review is the primary in-cluster combatant on the axis *boredom as unified construct ↔ heterogeneous family*, and — like the Elpidorou and Freeman chapter's handling of the profound-boredom axis elsewhere in this cluster — it does not resolve cleanly to either pole. R,M&C's argued position is diagnostic rather than declarative: they show that the field's persistent inconsistencies (above all in arousal and in the recruited brain regions) are compatible with two distinct explanations, and they decline to pick between them. The first explanation keeps boredom unified in principle but indicts sloppy, non-standardized measurement across labs (p.2460). The second — supported by Goetz et al.'s (2014) five-category experience-sampling evidence and treated with genuine (if hedged) seriousness — holds that "boredom" may denote a family of related but distinct states with different valence-arousal signatures, which would make cross-study inconsistency a real empirical signal rather than mere noise (pp.2451, 2456–2457, 2460). Their own forward-looking recommendation — develop "an empirically validated taxonomy of different types of boredom" (p.2451, abstract) — commits them to taking the heterogeneous-family hypothesis seriously as an open, worth-testing possibility, while their repeated hedging ("preliminary evidence," "if different types of boredom do indeed exist," p.2451, p.2460) keeps them from asserting it as established. Part III should cite this review, not a generic "boredom research" gloss, whenever it needs a source that names the unified-vs-heterogeneous question as the field's central unresolved problem, rather than a source that argues for one side.

Second, the review's arousal discussion offers Part III a direct caution for any future physiological measurement: R,M&C document that subjective boredom reports and autonomic arousal measures diverge in direction (the directional fractionation hypothesis, pp.2455–2456), meaning that if Part III's own instruments (or the not-yet-built VR Attention Study dataset) pair a self-report boredom item with heart rate, EDA, or other autonomic channels, convergence between the two should not be assumed as a validity check — divergence is the more empirically expected outcome, per this review's own synthesis.

Third, the operationalization critique generalizes directly to survey design. Since R,M&C's own diagnosis is that much of the field's inconsistency stems from different studies measuring different things under the single label "boredom" (p.2460), Part III's own survey should be explicit about which operational definition its own items instantiate — e.g., an Eastwood-style "wanting, but being unable, to engage" item (p.2452) targets something conceptually different from a low-arousal-and-dissatisfaction item in the Mikulas-and-Vodanovich mold (p.2452), and the two need not covary.

Fourth, the Goetz et al. (2014) five-type taxonomy offers Part III a candidate — but explicitly unvalidated — lens for interpreting heterogeneous student responses: if some students' boredom reports pattern as restless/high-arousal ("reactant" or "searching") while others pattern as flat/low-arousal ("apathetic" or "indifferent"), this review's own citation of that evidence gives warrant to treat the difference as a possible genuine subtype distinction rather than pure measurement noise — while respecting R,M&C's own caution (p.2457) that the five-type classification itself remains provisional and should not be imported wholesale.
