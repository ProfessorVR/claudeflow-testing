# bor-sec-24 — Clark & Hassert, "Undecidability and Opacity of Metacognition in Animals and Humans" (2013)

**Type:** Opinion Article (Frontiers in Psychology, Cognition section) · **Discipline:** cognitive science / philosophy of mind — comparative and developmental metacognition research methodology, argued via an analogy to Gödel's incompleteness theorems and illustrated with an eye-tracking/pupillometry case study · **Depth:** map · **Corpus role:** Strand C (psychology/cognitive science, empirical-behavioral, non-EEG). **Honesty flag:** this three-page piece never once uses the word "boredom" or any cognate (mind-wandering, monotony, tedium, ennui, task-unrelated thought, attentional lapse). A full-text search confirms zero hits. Its presence in this cluster is not warranted by direct boredom content — there is none — but by a general epistemological argument, transferable to boredom research by analogy only, about why self-report and implicit behavioral/physiological measures of an opaque internal state can and do diverge.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Clark and Hassert (Frontiers in Psychology 4:171, 2013) argue that metacognition — defined either epistemologically as "knowledge about knowledge" or operationally as "behavior about behavior" (Koriat, 2007) — inherits the undecidability problems Kurt Gödel (1931) proved for self-referential axiomatic systems such as Russell and Whitehead's *Principia Mathematica* (FE, p.1). Because metacognitive monitoring is a system (an agent, or per Bahrami et al. 2010/2012, a group) making statements about its own states, and because strong logicomathematical consistency excludes strong completeness (Kreisel, 1967), the "knotted statements" of metacognition may be true, false, or true-but-unverifiable "in theory as well as in subjective and objective practice" (FE, p.1, citing Raatikainen, 2005). The authors use Paulus, Proust, and Sodian's (2013) eye-tracking and pupillometric study of implicit metacognition in 3.5-year-old preschoolers as their central empirical illustration: implicit measures (gaze, pupil dilation) can outperform explicit self-report as an index of memory-confidence accuracy, yet the same data cannot rule out a deflationary reading on which the behavior in question is nothing more than automatic, non-reflective associative responding (Penn & Povinelli, 2007; Hampton, 2009). The piece closes on a qualified empiricist optimism, reporting that Gödel himself (1951/1995) denied his theorems proved the existence of humanly unsolvable problems.

Nowhere in this argument does boredom appear as topic, example, or illustrative case. The subject matter is metacognition in general — its definition, its formal-logical limits, and the specific empirical difficulty of validating implicit versus explicit measures of confidence/memory-monitoring in language-limited (animal, preverbal, or preschool) populations.

## 2. Structure

As an Opinion Article the piece has no numbered subheadings; it runs as continuous argument across three pages plus a references list and one inset diagram (Figure 1).

1. **Definitional opening + Gödelian argument** (p.1, approx.) — defines metacognition epistemologically/operationally (Koriat, 2007); argues its nested self-referential structure imports Gödel's (1931) undecidability and Kreisel's (1967) consistency/completeness tradeoff.
2. **Fallibility of cognition/metacognition + three sources of operational opacity** (p.1, approx.) — notes recognized flaws in feature detection, inference, memory, and introspection (Nisbett & Ross, 1980; Kahneman et al., 1982); most researchers sidestep Gödelian self-reference for basic/clinical questions about this "stubbornly opaque hypothetical construct" (brain injury, schizophrenia, HIV, gambling contexts cited); identifies three sources of opacity — poor accessibility in language-limited agents, unreliable explicit/implicit synergism, and occasional independence of metacognition from cognitive skill — then introduces Paulus et al.'s (2013) preschooler study as illustrating all three.
3. **Test paradigm and results** (p.1-2, approx.) — forced-choice declarative recognition scored for accuracy/efficiency, cross-correlated against explicit self-report ("scalar feelings") and implicit reactions (gaze, saccades, pupil dilation, pressor effects, skin conductance). Implicit gaze/pupil measures are reported as sometimes superior to explicit self-report, suggesting monitoring at a "preattentive or non-conscious level." Figure 1 (inset) diagrams the undecidability/opacity relationship between epistemological and operational definitions.
4. **Historical lineage and the case against verbal self-report** (p.2, approx.) — traces the behavioral paradigm to Flavell (1979), Thorndike (1911), Köhler (1927), and comparative/developmental primatology; reviews reasons self-report is unreliable (confabulation, illusory perception, memory cueing, affect bias, knowledge lean; Nisbett & Wilson, 1977; Berry & Broadbent, 1984); notes some authorities hold that progress requires improved behavioral models (Terrace & Son, 2009; Kepecs & Mainen, 2012).
5. **Ecological stakes and the behavioral-test-validity debate** (p.2, approx.) — the ecological relevance of developmental metacognition research (parenting, education, crime, public health) demands care interpreting test paradigms; whether standard behavioral tests actually reduce opacity is "spirited at best" a debate, given susceptibility to incidental/(pre)attentive disturbance.
6. **The automaticity indictment and Gödel's own optimism** (p.2-3, approx.) — the "most alarming" skeptical position (Penn & Povinelli, 2007; Hampton, 2009) holds that behaviorally measured metacognition could be nothing more than automatic, non-reflective associative response — a reading Paulus et al. (2013) could not negate for some findings. Correlative neurometric evidence (EEG, PET, fMRI, SQUID, via Fleming & Dolan, 2012) does not resolve this. Closes reporting Gödel's (1951/1995) own view that his theorems do not establish humanly unsolvable problems.
7. **References** (p.3) — approximately 45 sources spanning philosophy of mathematics/logic (Gödel, Kreisel, Lucas, Penrose, Raatikainen), judgment-and-decision-making psychology (Kahneman, Nisbett & Ross), and comparative/developmental metacognition research (Flavell, Hampton, Smith, Terrace & Son, Fleming & Dolan, Paulus et al.).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Metacognition's nested, self-referential structure (an agent or group monitoring itself) imports the undecidability Gödel (1931) proved for axiomatic systems that describe themselves; strong consistency excludes strong completeness (Kreisel, 1967), so metacognitive claims may be true, false, or true-but-unverifiable "in theory as well as in subjective and objective practice" (FAITHFUL-Clark & Hassert, p.1).
- Operational opacity has three sources: poor subjective accessibility in language-limited agents; unreliable synergistic/antagonistic explicit-implicit components masking true capacity from agent and observer; and occasional independence between metacognition and cognitive skill (FE, p.1).
- In Paulus et al.'s (2013) paired-associates paradigm with 3.5-year-olds, implicit confidence judgments (gaze, pupil area) were sometimes superior to explicit self-report for indexing memory accuracy; increased eye responses coincided with increased (pre)attention demand, suggesting monitoring can occur at a preattentive or non-conscious level (FAITHFUL-Clark & Hassert, p.2).
- Verbal self-report is unreliable for reasons including confabulation, illusory perception, cued or irretrievable memory, affect bias, and knowledge lean (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977; Berry & Broadbent, 1984), which is cited as the motivation for the field's paradigm shift toward behavioral/physiological measures (FE, p.2).
- A skeptical, "most alarming" position (Penn & Povinelli, 2007; Hampton, 2009) holds that behaviorally measured "metacognition" in animals and language-challenged humans could be nothing more than automatic, non-reflective, low-level associative response contingencies — and Paulus et al. (2013) themselves admit this reading could not be ruled out for some of their pupil-size/memory-retrieval findings (FAITHFUL-Clark & Hassert, p.2-3).
- Correlative neurometric evidence from non-invasive EEG, PET, fMRI, and SQUID recordings (cited via Fleming & Dolan, 2012) does not resolve the automaticity challenge; the authors state this "sadly almost reaffirms" Gödel's Incompleteness result — metacognition's nested referential structure may prevent it from ever being fully transparent to empiricism (FE, p.3).
- Despite this, the authors side with empiricists (including Paulus and colleagues) in holding that perfecting metacognition paradigms "gives fair cause for optimism"; they close by noting Gödel (1951/1995) himself denied his theorems proved the existence of humanly unsolvable problems, believing such matters knowable "with empirical certainty" through rational empiricism, for external or internal (self) observers alike (FAITHFUL-Clark & Hassert, p.3).
