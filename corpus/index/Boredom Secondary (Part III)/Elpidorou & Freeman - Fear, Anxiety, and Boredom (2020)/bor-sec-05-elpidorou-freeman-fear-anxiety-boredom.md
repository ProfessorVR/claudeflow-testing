# Elpidorou & Freeman (2020) — *Fear, Anxiety, and Boredom*

**Type:** handbook entry (comparative exposition) · **Discipline:** philosophy — phenomenology of affectivity (Heidegger and Sartre) · **Depth:** deep (~16pp manuscript) · **Corpus-role:** Strand-A companion piece; the cluster's only explicit Sartrean (non-Heideggerian) reading of boredom, and the entry that names fear and anxiety as boredom's nearest phenomenological kin.

## Thesis / Aim

The entry's governing claim is that affectivity is not a contingent add-on to human existence but constitutive of it: "we exist the way we do because of and through our affective experiences" (p.1). Elpidorou and Freeman (E&F) develop this claim through two philosophers in sequence rather than through argument of their own. Part 1 reconstructs Heidegger's account of mood (*Befindlichkeit*/*Stimmung*) and argues that fear, anxiety, and profound boredom are not merely three items on a list of affects but a graded series: fear is an inauthentic, world-directed affective state that Heidegger himself inconsistently calls a mood; anxiety and profound boredom are true *Grundstimmungen* ("fundamental attunements") that individuate Dasein and open the possibility of authentic existence. Part 2 turns to Sartre's *Sketch for a Theory of the Emotions* and reconstructs emotions as embodied, unreflective "magical" transformations of one's relation to the world, undertaken when practical, instrumental means for resolving a difficulty have failed. E&F then perform the entry's one genuinely novel move: since Sartre never discusses boredom, they construct a Sartrean account of it themselves, treating perceived meaninglessness as the "difficulty" and the desire to escape one's situation as boredom's "magical" resolution. The conclusion raises, without resolving, a conciliatory possibility (Sartrean emotion may already presuppose something like Heideggerian *Befindlichkeit*) and a shared limitation (neither philosopher seriously treats positive affects).

Because this is a *Handbook* entry rather than a research article, its ambitions are expository and synoptic rather than argumentative; the authors say so explicitly of the Sartre section ("Our aim is not completeness but rather accessibility and clarity," p.10) and, by footnote, of several Heidegger-side omissions (temporality of the fundamental moods is flagged and deferred, not argued, p.16 n.3).

## Structure

1. **§1 Heidegger and Affectivity** (pp.1–7)
   - §1.1 *Befindlichkeit* and *Stimmung* (pp.2–3) — the ontological/ontic pairing; mood as disclosive of thrownness, being-in-the-world, and what matters.
   - §1.2 Fear, Anxiety, and Boredom (pp.3–7) — fear as inauthentic quasi-mood (pp.3–4); anxiety as *Grundstimmung* (pp.4–5); the three forms of boredom named, with discussion confined to the third, profound boredom (pp.5–7).
2. **§2 Sartre and Affectivity** (pp.7–12)
   - §2.1 Emotions as Lived Transformations (pp.7–9) — the *Sketch*'s "magical" theory of emotion.
   - §2.2 Fear and Boredom (pp.9–12) — Sartre's own passive/active fear (pp.10–11); the authors' original Sartrean extension to boredom (pp.11–12).
3. **§3 Conclusion** (p.13) — a conciliatory suggestion and a shared critique of both accounts' neglect of positive affectivity.

## Constructs

- **Profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)** is the entry's one sustained boredom topic (pp.5–7): defined via the impersonal "*es ist einem langweilig*," the collapse of Dasein into an "undifferentiated no one," the merging of the three temporal ekstases, and a culminating "call to action" in the *Augenblick* (moment of vision).
- **Being-bored-by (1st form)** and **being-bored-with (2nd form)** are each given a single defining clause (p.6, citing FCM §§19–23 and §§24–28 respectively) and then explicitly bracketed: "we limit our discussion to the third type of boredom" (p.6). Neither is elaborated, exemplified, or returned to.
- **Temporality of boredom (time-drag)** is named as a gap rather than developed: footnote 3 states plainly that the entry is "unable to discuss" the temporality of fear, anxiety, or profound boredom, and directs readers to the authors' own 2015 companion piece for the "expanded discussion" (p.16 n.3).
- **Monotony/meaninglessness** becomes central only in the Sartrean half: the "difficulty" that gives rise to boredom is glossed as "a perceived meaninglessness, lack of interest, or challenge" (p.11) — a construct E&F introduce themselves rather than find in Sartre.
- **Mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought** and **attentional disengagement/meta-awareness** receive a one-sentence, citation-only mention: "we cannot maintain our attention to the situation at hand; we mind-wander and alternative goals and situations... become salient" (p.11, citing Eastwood et al. 2013 and Elpidorou 2018a).
- **Boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)** surfaces in the entry's most consequential single claim about boredom: "boredom could serve as the means out of a difficult situation and into a better (i.e., more fulfilling) life" (p.11–12, citing Elpidorou 2018b directly).
- **Situational vs. trait/state boredom** and **boredom proneness** are absent altogether — the entry never adopts the state/trait psychological vocabulary that structures bor-sec-04's treatment of the same authors' fuller chapter.
- **Flow/engagement (contrast pole)** is not named, but a structural analogue exists on the Heidegger side: profound boredom's "call to action" and the *Augenblick* function as the positive telos toward which the third form opens (p.7), without the vocabulary of flow ever appearing.

## Reading of FCM

E&F's FCM reading here is deliberately compressed and non-revisionist — a scaffold for the entry's comparative interests (anxiety/boredom, then Heidegger/Sartre) rather than an argument about FCM in its own right. The one-clause definitions of the first and second forms track fcm-04's and fcm-05's own section ranges exactly (FCM §§19–23 for *Gelangweiltwerden von etwas*; FCM §§24–28 for *Sichlangweilen bei etwas*, p.6). The third-form exposition draws its textual anchors squarely from fcm-06's territory: the "undifferentiated no one" formula (FCM 135/203, p.6, matching fcm-06 §2's own citation of the same passage), the claim that profound boredom "no longer permits" any passing of time (FCM 136/205, p.6), and — the entry's most substantive FCM-specific claim — the reading of entrancement's "telling refusal" as itself "a call for action" issuing in the *Augenblick*, where Dasein is called "to become the author of its own existential meaning" (FCM 148–152/222–28 and FCM 149/224, p.7), which corresponds to fcm-06's own §32b ("impelled toward the *Augenblick*"). E&F do not contest or extend any of this exegetically; their one genuine interpretive move is external to FCM's own text — placing profound boredom in explicit structural parallel with anxiety (both from *Being and Time*, not FCM), arguing the two Grundstimmungen "reflect" one another (p.6) and share two features: a distinctive revelatory function and the power to compel Dasein toward authenticity (p.7). This is a synthetic bridge the entry builds between two different Heidegger texts, not a claim internal to FCM itself, and it is worth flagging as the entry's one small but genuine extension of the primary source. A second, smaller interpretive liberty is footnoted rather than argued in the main text: the authors regularize Heidegger's own inconsistent terminology, treating anxiety (and, by the parallel they draw, profound boredom) as *Grundstimmung* rather than *Grundbefindlichkeit*, and fear as more emotion-like than mood-like despite *Being and Time* §30's own wording (p.14 n.7, p.15 n.8) — an editorial regularization the entry performs on Heidegger's text rather than a reading Heidegger authorizes explicitly.

## Findings / Claims

1. Fear is Heidegger's own named mood (*Stimmung*, BT §30) but E&F argue, following Freeman (2014), that it more closely resembles an emotion — an occurrent, intentional, agent-experienced state — and is in any case *inauthentic*: fear is a "fleeing and a falling" that evades one's thrownness rather than disclosing it (pp.3–4).
2. Anxiety, unlike fear, has no determinate object or cause; it individuates Dasein for its "ownmost potentiality-for-Being" and, though it appears to be a crisis of meaninglessness, is in fact the condition for authentic self-disclosure (pp.4–5).
3. Profound boredom and anxiety are structural siblings: both are unbidden, overpowering, all-encompassing; both possess a "distinctive revelatory function"; and both compel Dasein "to face its own existence and to become authentic" (p.7) — a parallel E&F draw explicitly, not one Heidegger himself states in these terms in FCM.
4. Sartre's emotions are pre-reflective, embodied "magical" transformations of one's relation to a world that has ceased to yield to instrumental, means-ends action; the transformation "changes the world magically" by changing one's *consciousness* of the world, not the world materially (pp.7–9).
5. Passive fear (fainting) and active fear (fleeing) are, on Sartre's account, the same underlying phenomenon — negation of a threat via negation of one's consciousness of it, or via a "magical" reversal of the space one inhabits (pp.10–11).
6. E&F's own Sartrean construction of boredom: the "difficulty" is perceived meaninglessness, lack of interest, or challenge; the "solution" boredom offers is dissatisfaction plus a strong desire to engage elsewhere, manifest as mind-wandering, felt time-dilation, and physiological change (p.11) — importantly, boredom "repels" rather than merely leaves one unengaged, distinguishing it from apathy (p.11).
7. This Sartrean reading is explicitly non-pathologizing: "a Sartrean take on boredom safeguards one from assuming an overly negative take on boredom" (p.11), and can motivate "the means out of a difficult situation and into a better... life" (p.11–12, citing Elpidorou 2018b).
8. E&F immediately qualify their own construction: because we are often reflectively *aware* that we are bored and act deliberately against it (checking phones, taking walks), a strictly non-reflective "magical transformation" model sits uneasily with lived boredom, and "if boredom involves a magical transformation, it is not one that strips our world of its means-ends relationships" (p.12) — a self-directed criticism, not an endorsement.
9. The same worry generalizes to Sartre's theory as a whole: it may be too inflexible (an all-or-nothing switch between concernful and magical worlds) and, per Fell (1965) and Weberman (1996), unable to accommodate positive emotions (p.12).
10. Conclusion: a conciliatory possibility — Sartrean emotions, grounded in "experienced difficulties that we cannot overcome with pragmatic means," may already presuppose something like Heidegger's *Befindlichkeit*, since such difficulties are difficulties only because the world already matters to us (p.13). Both accounts, however, share a deeper limitation: neither investigates positive affective states (joy, love, awe, hope) with comparable seriousness (p.13).

## Analytical role (anticipatory-application)

*(P/anticipatory-application) — the following are our interpretive links to Part III; none are claims E&F make about students, VR, or the dissertation itself.*

This entry's explicit deferral of boredom's temporality (p.16 n.3) is itself useful signal for Part III: it confirms that the *temporal* phenomenology needed for framing survey items about felt time-dilation belongs to bor-sec-03 (Elpidorou & Freeman's *Affectivity in Heidegger II*), not to this shorter companion piece, and Part III should cite accordingly rather than pulling temporal material from wherever "Elpidorou & Freeman" happens to appear.

Second, the entry's own Sartrean extension of boredom — meaninglessness as difficulty, escape-seeking as magical resolution — supplies Part III with a second, non-Heideggerian philosophical vocabulary for exactly the disengagement-motivated behaviors (mind-wandering, phone-checking, walking away) that a student-boredom survey is likely to probe, and one that dovetails with, rather than duplicates, Elpidorou's own regulatory-theory papers (bor-sec-25, bor-sec-26) already anchoring this cluster's deficit-vs-functional debate axis.

Third, and more cautionary: E&F's own hesitation about their Sartrean account — that reflective awareness of being bored (and voluntary counteraction) sits awkwardly with a purely pre-reflective "magical transformation" model (p.12) — previews, from a philosophical rather than methodological angle, this cluster's self-report-vs-physiological-marker debate axis. A survey item that asks a student to *report* boredom is already, on this entry's own logic, capturing something more reflective than the pre-reflective disengagement a gaze or EEG channel would register; Part III should not assume the two are transparent proxies for a single underlying state.

Fourth, the entry's closing admission — that neither Heidegger nor Sartre seriously investigates positive affectivity (p.13) — is a caution against treating "flow" or "engagement" as boredom's simple photographic negative in Part III's own framing; on this entry's own terms, resolving boredom does not guarantee flow, only the possibility of authentic (Heidegger) or freely re-instrumentalized (Sartre) engagement with a situation.
