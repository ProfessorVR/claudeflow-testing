# bor-sec-19 — Thomson, "Heidegger on Ontological Education, or: How We Become What We Are" (2001)

**Type:** journal article (Heideggerian philosophy of education / ontohistorical critique of the modern university), *Inquiry* 44 · **Discipline:** philosophy (Heidegger studies, philosophy of education); no empirical, affective-science, or attention-science content · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand B's source for the broader Heideggerian argument that the university's crisis is *ontohistorical* — an effect of "enframing" (the technological understanding of being) — rather than merely institutional or economic. The word "boredom" (and "Langeweile") does not occur anywhere in this text; its contribution to the cluster is the pedagogical/institutional argument-frame (paideia, Bildung, enframing, "letting learn") into which Part III's student-boredom material can be set, plus one legitimate construct link via monotony/meaninglessness.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Thomson argues that Heidegger's philosophy of education should be read as a double deconstruction (*Destruktion*, not *Zerstörung*): a negative moment that traces our contemporary educational crisis — the "instrumentalization, professionalization, vocationalization, corporatization, and technologization" of the university — back to an underlying ontohistorical logic he calls "enframing" (*das Gestell*), and a positive moment that recovers, from Plato's allegory of the cave, a forgotten "essence of paideia" capable of reorienting education against this technologized drift (FE, p.243–244). Heidegger's own diagnosis of the university thus both precedes and, on Thomson's reading, exceeds the institutional critiques of contemporaries like Bill Readings, because it locates the fragmentation of departments and the hollowing-out of "excellence" in a single underlying historical logic rather than treating them as separate symptoms (FE, p.243). The essay's stated aim is accordingly twofold: to reconstruct Heidegger's ontohistorical critique of the technologization of education (§II) and to reconstruct his positive, cave-derived vision of "ontological education" (§III), before sketching (§IV) how a re-essentialized notion of educational "excellence" might reunify teaching and research and heal the university's disciplinary fragmentation (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.244–245).

Nowhere in this argument does Thomson engage boredom, mood (*Stimmung*), or affectivity as such; the paper's closest neighbors in Heidegger's corpus are "On the Essence of Truth" (1929), the 1929 Inaugural Lecture "What is Metaphysics?", "Plato's Teaching on Truth" (1931/1940), and "The Question Concerning Technology" — not the 1929–30 *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (FCM) lecture course in which Heidegger analyzes profound boredom. This is a piece about ontology, history, and pedagogy, not about mood-attunement in FCM's technical sense.

## 2. Structure

1. **I. Introduction** (p.243–245) — Frames Heidegger's "deconstruction" of education as inseparably negative (clearing away distorting interpretations) and positive (recovering "primordial experiences"); previews the essay's double "future need" (a critical diagnostic perspective, and a positive alternative) that structures §§II–III (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.243–245).
2. **II. Heidegger's Ontohistorical Critique of the Technologization of Education** (p.245–252)
   - **II.1 From the Essence of Truth to the History of Being** (p.245–248) — Explains Heidegger's technical, verbal sense of "essence" (*Wesen*, as how beings "essence" or "remain in play," not a fixed Kripkean rigid designator); traces how "the essence of truth" (aletheia, unconcealment) becomes, for Heidegger, "the history of being" (*Seinsgeschichte*), a series of ontotheologically grounded historical "epochs" (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.245–248).
   - **II.2 The History of Being as the Ground of Education** (p.248–249) — Argues that because metaphysics grounds our basic sense of "what is," changes in the reigning ontotheology transform our understanding of "education" too; identifies our own epoch as "enframing" (*das Gestell*), grounded in a Nietzschean ontotheology of beings as "eternally recurring will-to-power," which reduces all entities — including students and teachers — to optimizable "resources" (*Bestand*) (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.248–249).
   - **II.3 Education as Enframing** (p.249–252) — Reads Heidegger's 1929 Inaugural Lecture ("the rootedness of the sciences … has dried up and died," FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.250–251) as an early diagnosis of the hyper-specialized, fragmented "Multi-versity" (Clark Kerr's later term); credits and then contests Bill Readings' and Timothy Clark's materialist accounts of the "university of excellence" for lacking Heidegger's underlying ontohistorical explanation and for collapsing into cynicism about the university's future (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.251–252).
3. **III. Heidegger's Return to Plato's Cave: Ontological Education as the Essence of Paideia** (p.252–260)
   - **III.1 Ontological Education Against Enframing** (p.252–256) — Reconstructs "the essence of paideia" from "Plato's Teaching on Truth": genuine education "lays hold of the soul itself" rather than filling an "empty container" with information (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.252–253); recovers a twofold sense of *Bildung* — forming (impressing an unfolding character) and pre-conception (*Vor-bild*, the measure-giving vision that guides the forming) — against its contrastive term *apaideusia*, "lack of education" (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.255–256).
   - **III.2 The Pedagogy of Ontological Freedom** (p.256–258) — Maps Plato's cave onto four pedagogical stages: (1) captivity to shadows/resources, (2) negative freedom (recognizing "the fire" as enframing), (3) positive/"real" freedom (ascent into "the open," attained as "dwelling"), and (4) return to the cave to teach — the stage in which, Thomson stresses, "learning culminates in teaching" (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.256–258).
   - **III.3 What Is Called Teaching?** (p.258–260) — Develops Heidegger's account of teaching (*lehren*) as fundamentally a "letting learn" (*lernen lassen*), harder than mere "instructing" (*belehren*) because the teacher must be "more teachable than his apprentices" (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.259–260); defines learning as bringing what we do into "co-respondence" with whatever addresses us as essential (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.260).
4. **IV. Conclusion: Envisioning a University of Teachers** (p.260–262) — Argues that re-essentializing "excellence" (*arete*) as the cultivation of Dasein's world-disclosive essence could reunite teaching and research (since all "positive sciences" rest on unexamined ontological posits ontological education would teach students to interrogate) and restore a substantive, shared sense of purpose to the fragmented university community (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.260–262).

- **being-bored-by (1st form)** / **being-bored-with (2nd form)** — not treated; Thomson never engages FCM's tripartite typology.
- **situational vs. trait/state boredom** — not treated.
- **boredom proneness** — not treated.
- **mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought** — not treated.
- **attentional disengagement / meta-awareness** — not treated. The cave allegory's first stage, in which students are "engrossed" in shadows taken for reality (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.256–257), superficially resembles an attentional-capture vocabulary, but Thomson's own point is ontological (a mode of revealing that occludes beings' being), not attentional-cognitive; treating it as an instance of this construct would misrepresent the source's register, so it is noted here only as a false friend.
- **boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)** — not treated.
- **flow / engagement (contrast pole)** — not genuinely treated. "Dwelling" (*wohnen*) and the "positive freedom" of stage three of ontological education (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.257–258) gesture toward an achieved, absorbed comportment toward beings, but this is presented as ontological disclosedness, not as flow-theoretic engagement/absorption; no construct-level claim is made here, and no edge is drawn for it.
- **monotony / meaninglessness** — **the one construct genuinely treated**, and substantially so. Enframing's "limitless quantification" reduces all qualitative relations to calculable, optimizable "resources" (*Bestand*) "lacking intrinsic meaning" (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.249), producing what Thomson calls, following Heidegger, an "ontological drift toward meaninglessness" — one whose "impact... can barely be noticed by contemporary humanity because they are continually covered over with the latest information" (FAITHFUL-Thomson, p.253–254, quoting Heidegger's "Traditional Language and Technological Language"). This is a genuine, if boredom-silent, engagement with meaninglessness as an ontohistorical condition of the technologized university and its members (including students), and it is the basis for this unit's sole treats-construct edge.
- **temporality of boredom (time-drag)** — not treated. Thomson discusses Heideggerian historicity extensively (*Seinsgeschichte*, ek-sistence as "standing-out" into history), but this is the temporality of historical epochs, not the lived phenomenology of boredom's dragging present; the two discourses do not overlap here.

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Heidegger's "history of being" grounds our historically changing understanding of "education," because metaphysics supplies the basic presuppositions of "what is" that any regional understanding (including the educational) presupposes (FE, p.248–249).
- Our own epoch, "enframing," is grounded in an "unthought" Nietzschean ontotheology of beings as eternally recurring will-to-power; this ontotheology drives the transformation of all entities, including students, teachers, and knowledge itself, into optimizable "resources" (FE, p.248–249).
- Heidegger's 1929 diagnosis of the university's hyper-specialization and loss of a "rootedness" in its own essential ground anticipates, by decades, the fragmented "Multi-versity" later diagnosed by critics such as Bill Readings and Timothy Clark — but those later critics, lacking Heidegger's ontohistorical framework, offer diagnosis without a positive alternative and slide into cynicism about the university's future (FE, p.250–252).
- The recovered "essence of paideia" — genuine education as a soul-transforming turning-around, not information transmission into an "empty container" — is Heidegger's positive alternative, developed through a close, idiosyncratic reading of Plato's cave allegory as a four-stage pedagogy of ontological freedom culminating in the return to teach (FE, p.252–258).
- Teaching (*lehren*) is "letting learn" (*lernen lassen*), harder than learning because the teacher must be an exemplary, ever-more-teachable learner; "instructing" (*belehren*), by contrast, models education as mere information transfer and thus perpetuates enframing (FE, p.259–260).
- Re-essentializing the currently "empty" ideal of educational "excellence" as Aristotelian *arete* — cultivating Dasein's world-disclosive essence — could, Heidegger hopes, reunify teaching and research (since every "positive science" rests on unexamined ontological posits ontological education would teach students to interrogate) and restore a substantive, shared purpose to the university community (FE, p.260–262).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* Thomson's paper contributes no boredom-construct data to Part III's student-boredom frame directly, but it supplies two structural resources this cluster can draw on when situating that frame institutionally. First, Thomson's account of enframing's "ontological drift toward meaninglessness" — an effect that becomes harder to notice the more thoroughly it proceeds, because it is "covered over with the latest information" — offers a institution-level analogue to the affective flattening (monotony/meaninglessness) Part III's survey-analysis chapter locates at the level of individual student experience in VLE/deployment contexts; the two are not the same claim (Thomson never frames enframing's effects as boredom, mood, or any first-personal affective state), but the analogy of an intensifying-yet-self-concealing meaninglessness is worth flagging as a possible institutional-scale complement to a student-scale account *(anticipatory-application)*.

Second, Heidegger's "letting learn" pedagogy — on Thomson's reading, the teacher's task is to attune students' receptive comportment (*hören*, hearkening) rather than transmit information, and teaching succeeds only insofar as it cultivates the student's own capacity to respond to what "addresses" them — is a resource Part III could draw on if it wants to argue that mood/attunement, rather than information delivery, is the pedagogically relevant unit of analysis for VLE or classroom design; again, this is our own extension of Thomson's non-affective argument, not a claim Thomson makes about boredom *(anticipatory-application)*.

Third, because Mertel's "Heidegger, Technology and Education" (2020, bor-sec-17) postdates this 2001 paper by nineteen years, no literal citation relationship is possible between them; nevertheless, Thomson's early (1929-rooted) account of enframing's technologization of the university plausibly anticipates themes a 2020 treatment of Heidegger-and-educational-technology would independently need to engage. This is flagged here strictly as a forward-looking thematic parallel for future cross-linking within the cluster, not as a citation *(anticipatory-application)*.
