# Elpidorou (2018) — *The Bored Mind is a Guiding Mind: Toward a Regulatory Theory of Boredom*

**Type:** journal article (*Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 17:455–484, DOI 10.1007/s11097-017-9515-1; published online 10 July 2017) · **Discipline:** philosophy of mind / affective science, analytic and empirically engaged (not Heideggerian) · **Depth:** deep (30pp) · **Corpus-role:** Strand-C keystone; PRIMARY in-cluster grounding, together with its companion piece bor-sec-26 ("The Good of Boredom"), for the debate axis *boredom-as-deficit ↔ boredom-as-functional/adaptive* and for the construct row *boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)*.

## Thesis / Aim

Elpidorou's governing claim, stated in the abstract and repeated at the close of the introduction, is that boredom should be understood as a functional emotion that is "both informative and regulatory of one's behavior" (p.456). On this reading boredom does two things at once: it informs the subject of the presence of an unsatisfactory situation, and it motivates the subject to pursue a new goal once the current one ceases to be satisfactory, attractive, or meaningful (p.456). The paper does not propose a new definition of boredom, nor does it try to adjudicate whether features like the perception of meaninglessness are proper parts of the state or merely its antecedents (p.460); Elpidorou brackets that project deliberately, arguing that an accurate experiential characterization is sufficient to support the functional argument he wants to make (p.460).

The article positions itself as advancing the boredom literature in two ways. First, it offers what it calls a comprehensive, up-to-date review of the empirical literature on boredom's character and function, building on and updating earlier attempts (his own included) to specify boredom's function (p.457). Second, it argues that adopting a regulatory perspective is theoretically advantageous on three counts: it synthesizes an otherwise disparate literature by showing how boredom's various components work together to execute one function; it is continuous with the general view that emotions are evolutionarily shaped functional processes; and it opens genuinely new, empirically testable research questions (p.457, p.474). The paper is explicitly a synthesis-and-theory piece, not a report of new data — every empirical claim it makes is a citation to someone else's finding, marshaled in service of the regulatory argument.

## Structure

1. **Introduction** (pp.455–457) — motivates the topic via boredom's prevalence and cost, and states the twofold informative/regulatory thesis.
2. **State boredom** (§2, pp.457–468) — 2.1 *Formal features* (pp.458–460): four properties (duration, continuity, concreteness, susceptibility to situation) that distinguish state from trait boredom as a matter of psychological kind, independent of content. 2.2 *The character of state boredom* (pp.460–468): nine substantive characteristics (a)–(i), from dissatisfaction through neural correlates. 2.3 *Summary* (p.468): the nine characteristics compressed into four components — affective, cognitive, volitional, physiological.
3. **Boredom as a self-regulatory emotion** (§3, pp.468–473) — 3.1 argues boredom qualifies as a genuine emotion (pp.468–469); 3.2 states and defends the twofold function (pp.469–471); 3.3 explains what it is for boredom to succeed or fail at that function, including its self-effacing character and its limits (pp.471–473).
4. **The value of a regulatory approach** (§4, pp.474–476) — three second-order arguments for the regulatory model's theoretical payoff: comprehensiveness/coherence, consistency with evolutionary accounts of emotion (including the adaptive-value case), and fruitfulness/testability (new predictions linking state-boredom malfunction to trait boredom).
5. **Conclusion** (pp.475–476).

## Constructs

Mapped onto the cluster's construct axis:

- **Boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)** is not one construct among others here — it is the paper's entire argumentative object, developed across §3 and §4 (pp.468–476). Every other construct the paper treats is treated *in service of* this one.
- **Situational vs. trait/state boredom** receives the paper's most systematic conceptual work: four formal features (duration, continuity, concreteness, susceptibility to situation) are used to distinguish state from trait boredom as different psychological *kinds*, not merely different degrees (pp.458–460). Elpidorou also notes, as a standing criticism of the field, that most existing research has focused on trait boredom despite trait boredom being conceptually and empirically *dependent on* prior state-boredom episodes — "surprising," on his account, given that a measure of trait boredom logically presupposes a settled account of the state it is a proneness toward (p.457).
- **Boredom proneness** is treated as the trait construct operationalized by the Boredom Proneness Scale (BPS; Farmer and Sundberg 1986) and the Boredom Susceptibility Scale (ZBS; Zuckerman 1979), both surveyed from the literature rather than administered (p.457). Boredom proneness resurfaces at p.475 in the paper's most concrete testable prediction: a strong negative correlation between locomotion (a general disposition to initiate and follow through on goal-directed action) and boredom proneness (citing Struk et al. 2016), offered as initial confirmation that failures of the regulatory function are what constitute the trait.
- **Mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought** appears within the "failure to sustain attention" characteristic (p.461), where Elpidorou treats the causal direction between mind-wandering and boredom as an open empirical question, but suggests that even if mind-wandering is merely a consequence of boredom rather than its cause, it may function adaptively — as a means of surfacing more fulfilling alternatives to a bored subject (p.461).
- **Attentional disengagement / meta-awareness** is treated at length under two of the nine characteristics — "disengagement from the environment" and "failure to sustain attention" (pp.460–461) — where Elpidorou draws heavily on Eastwood, Frischen, Fenske, and Smilek's (2012) "unengaged mind" account, which holds that attentional failure is boredom's underlying mechanism. Elpidorou does not simply adopt this deficit-flavored characterization wholesale; he later folds it into the regulatory account by arguing that disengagement is functionally apt — it is precisely what frees the subject to notice alternative, more satisfying situations (p.469–470).
- **Flow / engagement (contrast pole)** is addressed directly (pp.463–464) via Csikszentmihalyi's account of flow as a pleasant, effortless, autotelic experience. Elpidorou is careful to reject a simple negation — "it would be a mistake to identify boredom with the opposite of flow" (p.463) — on the grounds that many non-boring states are also non-flow states, and that Csikszentmihalyi's own model (skills exceeding challenge) does not fit findings that academic boredom can arise when challenge exceeds skill (p.464).
- **Monotony / meaninglessness** is treated as one of two ways a situation can be "non-optimal" for a subject — insufficiently meaningful or insufficiently/excessively challenging (pp.463–464) — and again in §3.3's account of meaning-restoration as one of boredom's characteristic self-regulatory outcomes (pp.471–472, citing Van Tilburg and Igou's program of studies).
- **Temporality of boredom (time-drag)** receives the single longest treatment of any construct in the paper (pp.461–463): the phenomenon of time appearing to slow or drag during boredom is explained via the internal-clock model (pacemaker/switch/accumulator), with separate treatment of prospective versus retrospective time estimation and of the complicating role of physiological arousal.
- **Profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)**, **being-bored-by (1st form)**, and **being-bored-with (2nd form)** are not discussed. This paper's entire analytic vocabulary is state/trait boredom; Heidegger's tripartite phenomenology is never invoked as a framework (see *Reading of FCM* below).

## Reading of FCM

This source does not substantively engage Heidegger's *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics*. "Heidegger 1983/2001" is cited exactly three times (pp.461, 463, 471), and on every occasion it appears bundled inside a string-citation alongside five or six other authors (Gabriel 1988, Greenson 1953, Hartocollis 1972, Tze et al. 2013, Wangh 1975, Watt 1991) in support of a single, narrow observation: that boredom involves an altered, typically slowed, perception of the passage of time. There is no discussion anywhere in the paper of Dasein, *Befindlichkeit*, moods as ontological structure, the tripartite forms (becoming-bored-by / being-bored-with / profound boredom), the two structural moments (*Leergelassenheit*/being-left-empty, *Hingehaltenheit*/being-held-in-limbo), or any other FCM-specific textual claim. Given Elpidorou's demonstrated fluency with Heidegger elsewhere in this cluster (bor-sec-02 through bor-sec-06), the absence here is best read as a deliberate disciplinary choice rather than an oversight: this paper stays inside analytic philosophy of mind and empirical affective science, and reserves the Heideggerian material for the companion literature it produces separately (and, later, for bor-sec-04 and bor-sec-06). No `bridges-to-fcm` edge is warranted for this unit.

## Findings / Claims

1. **Four formal features individuate state from trait boredom as a matter of kind** (§2.1, pp.458–460): duration (state is transitory; trait endures), continuity (state is gap-free within an episode; trait need not be continuously manifest), concreteness (state is directly observable/introspectable at a time; trait must be inferred), and susceptibility to situation (state can be induced and alleviated by proximal situational factors; trait cannot be created or removed by a single exposure).
2. **Nine characteristics constitute state boredom's experiential signature** (§2.2, pp.460–468): dissatisfaction with one's present state; disengagement from the environment; failure to sustain attention; altered (slowed) perception of the passage of time; perception of insufficient meaning or non-optimal challenge; an unresolved but genuinely mixed relationship to physiological arousal (low, high, or both depending on circumstance); characteristic motor and vocal expressions (backward head tilt, restrained-then-sudden movement, low-pitch/narrow-range speech); inconclusive but active neural-correlates research (lower left-DLPFC beta activity, resting-state-like default-mode-network activation with an anterior-insula anticorrelation not seen in ordinary rest, delayed ERP components in high sensation-seekers); and feelings of constraint or lack of agency (though not a necessary feature, since boredom also arises in unconstrained leisure). These nine are compressed at p.468 into four components — affective, cognitive, volitional, physiological — each of which, per §3.2, contributes to the regulatory function.
3. **Boredom qualifies as a genuine emotion, not merely a mood or a trait** (§3.1, pp.468–469): it is a transitory, multicomponent response to an appraised situation, and recent empirical work shows it is dissociable from superficially adjacent states — from anger, sadness, and frustration on the criterion of perceived meaninglessness and unchallenge (Van Tilburg and Igou 2012), and from apathy, anhedonia, and depression on the criterion of its distinctive, strong volitional pull toward an alternative situation, where those other states involve motivational loss instead (Goldberg et al. 2011).
4. **Boredom's function is twofold**: it informs the subject of the presence of a non-interesting or unsatisfactory situation, and it promotes escape from that situation, so that when the function succeeds boredom is self-effacing — it "gets us unstuck when we find ourselves stuck" (p.469, quoting Fahlman et al. 2013, p.68).
5. **Each of the four experiential components does functional work, including the components a deficit reading would treat as mere impairment.** Boredom's unpleasantness is not incidental: negative affective states have disproportionate motivational impact relative to positive ones (Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer, and Vohs's 2001 "bad is stronger than good" finding), so an aversive signal is well suited to actually produce behavioral change (pp.469–470). Disengagement and attentional failure are not simply cognitive malfunction; they are what frees attention to notice alternative, potentially more satisfying situations (p.470). Physiological arousal changes, whatever their direction, either contribute to disengagement (low arousal) or facilitate the pursuit of alternative goals (high arousal) (pp.470–471).
6. **Behavioral evidence corroborates the escape-motivation claim with unusual force**: participants left alone with only their thoughts chose to self-administer electric shocks rather than remain unstimulated (Wilson et al. 2014); a monotonous condition increased both chocolate consumption and self-shocking relative to a neutral condition (Havermans et al. 2015); and a follow-up isolated boredom specifically (rather than negative affect generally) as the driver of increased self-shocking, since sadness induction did not produce the same effect (Nederkoorn et al. 2016) (pp.471–472). Risk-taking's correlation with boredom proneness is read the same way (p.472).
7. **The regulatory account is elaborated via self-determination theory** (Deci and Ryan 1985): boredom is not a situation one is intrinsically motivated to engage with (it is definitionally flow-absent), yet it functions as extrinsic motivation to change one's behavior in order to escape the aversive state (pp.472–473).
8. **The function can fail, and failure is (on this account) partly constitutive of trait boredom.** When state boredom systematically fails to move a subject out of an uninteresting situation, or moves the subject into a new situation that itself fails to satisfy, the result is prolonged or frequent state-boredom episodes — which, given how trait boredom is actually measured, would register as elevated trait boredom (pp.474–475). This yields two testable predictions (locomotion should correlate negatively with boredom proneness; ability to initiate and commit to behavioral change should correlate negatively with boredom frequency) and two candidate mechanisms for the trait (motivational failure to initiate change, versus cognitive/affective failure to choose satisfying alternatives, or both) (pp.475–476).
9. **Three considerations make boredom's adaptive value plausible** (§4, pp.474–475): self-regulatory flexibility is generally adaptive for organismal survival; narrowing the thought-action repertoire toward escaping non-meaningful situations conserves scarce self-regulatory resources (Baumeister et al. 1998; Muraven and Baumeister 2000); and boredom can indirectly promote the positive emotion of interest, which on Fredrickson's broaden-and-build model builds durable psychological resources (p.475).
10. **An explicit normative caveat limits the account's reach**: exercising boredom's function does not guarantee well-being — the "more interesting" situation one moves toward on account of boredom may itself be risky or costly — and, more importantly, not every boring situation ought to be escaped. Boredom "signifies a lack of interest and not a lack of importance" (p.473), so its call for change must sometimes be resisted rather than obeyed, as when academic material is boring but still worth persisting with.

## Analytical role (anticipatory-application)

*(P/anticipatory-application) — the following are our interpretive links to Part III, not claims Elpidorou makes himself, except where he is directly quoted or paraphrased with a page anchor.*

This is the unit that gives the cluster's deficit-vs-functional debate axis its textual anchor, and the case for its centrality is worth making explicitly rather than asserting by fiat. Elpidorou does not caricature or dismiss a "deficit" reading of boredom; he largely *concedes* its descriptive premises. Six of his nine characteristics of state boredom — dissatisfaction, disengagement, attentional failure, altered time-perception, ambiguous-to-negative arousal, and (sometimes) felt constraint — are exactly the features a pure-deficit account would cite as evidence that boredom is nothing but a psychological failure mode: an attention deficit, an arousal deficit, a meaning deficit. Elpidorou's argumentative move (§3.2, pp.469–471) is not to deny these features but to reinterpret their functional significance: disengagement is what frees attention for something better; the aversiveness is what gives the signal motivational teeth (via the independently-established negativity bias, Baumeister et al. 2001); and the ambiguity of arousal reflects two different functional postures (low arousal disengaging, high arousal mobilizing) rather than a confound to be explained away. This is the paper's actual argued position on the debate axis: boredom-as-functional/adaptive, not by ignoring the phenomenology a deficit account relies on, but by giving that same phenomenology a different explanatory role (pp.468–476, esp. p.474's three-part adaptive-value case). Part III should cite this mechanism — reinterpretation of conceded deficit-features as functional components — rather than a looser gloss like "Elpidorou thinks boredom is good," when it needs to explain *how* the regulatory account answers a deficit account on the latter's own descriptive ground.

This has a direct payoff for Part III's own survey data on student and VLE boredom. Where a naive deficit framing would treat self-reported classroom boredom as straightforward evidence of attentional or motivational failure on the student's part, Elpidorou's account licenses treating the same report as a signal that the current activity is misaligned with the student's operative goals or interests — informative and (potentially) adaptive rather than simply diagnostic of deficiency. But the paper's own normative caveat (p.473) cuts against over-applying this move: boredom signals a lack of *interest*, not a lack of *importance*, and its call for change is one Elpidorou explicitly says should sometimes be resisted rather than obeyed. Part III should therefore use this source to complicate, not simply invert, a deficit reading of student boredom — the regulatory account is not a license to treat every instance of boring-but-important material as a design failure.

Two further points bear on how this unit relates to the rest of the cluster. First, the citation direction runs forward, not backward: this paper (published online 2017, print 2018) cites its own companion piece as "Elpidorou (in press). The good of boredom. Philosophical Psychology" (p.471, in the discussion of goal-directed movement) — i.e., bor-sec-26. The two papers were evidently written and submitted close together, with bor-sec-26 developing the normative/adaptive-value case this paper only sketches in §4. Conversely, this paper cites none of Elpidorou's own Heideggerian work in this cluster (bor-sec-02, bor-sec-03) despite temporal availability (both 2015), which suggests the regulatory-theory project was, at this stage, being kept analytically separate from the phenomenological project; it is only in the later papers — bor-sec-04 (2019, citing "Elpidorou 2014, 2018a, 2018b" for the regulatory account underlying profound boredom's motivational character, pp.21–22) and bor-sec-06 (2023, citing this paper at p.3 and p.8 as the source's "own fuller regulatory-theory account underlying the Sartrean functional reading") — that Elpidorou himself imports this paper's regulatory thesis into the Heideggerian side of his corpus. Part III should track that stretch as an authorial move made in the *later* papers, not as something this paper itself claims about profound boredom; this paper's regulatory account is argued exclusively at the level of ordinary state and trait boredom, with FCM's third form never in view.

No `grounds-measure` bridge is asserted here; this is flagged strictly as a future candidate, contingent on a dataset entry that does not yet exist.
