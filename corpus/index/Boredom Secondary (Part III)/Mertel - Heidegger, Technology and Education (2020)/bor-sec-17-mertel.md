# Mertel, K. (2020). Heidegger, Technology and Education

**Type:** journal article (Continental philosophy of education) · **Discipline:** philosophy of technology / philosophy of education · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand B secondary reading on the Heidegger–education axis; this entry carries **no direct boredom-construct engagement** — the string "bored/boredom/tedium/ennui/monoton-" does not occur anywhere in the source text — and is included for its argument about Gestell (enframing), appropriation, and the "culture of achievement" (*Leistungskultur*) in education, which bears on Part III's student-disengagement frame only by analogy, never in the source's own vocabulary.

## Thesis / Aim

Mertel defends the continuing viability and "emancipatory potential" of Heidegger's philosophy of technology for education (FAITHFUL-Mertel, p.467). The argument has two moves. First, he situates Heidegger's account of technology as *Gestell* ("enframing") within the emancipatory project of the early fundamental ontology of *Being and Time*, arguing that the "appropriative" reading — centered on language and self-appropriation — lets the later *Seinsgeschichte* (history of being) historicize and concretize fundamental ontology rather than replace it, dissolving the charge that Heidegger's late work is fatalistic, dystopian, or merely diagnostic (FAITHFUL-Mertel, pp.467–468, 473). Second, given the importance Heidegger placed on education as a site of resistance to technology's reifying and alienating effects, Mertel argues against the currently dominant "skilled bodily coping" reading of Heideggerian education (associated with Hubert Dreyfus and Mark Wrathall) and for an alternative that centers language and "moral articulacy" as education's proper aim (FAITHFUL-Mertel, pp.467, 476–477).

## Structure

1. **Introduction** (p.467) — technology as *Gestell*/enframing; reality disclosed as homogeneous "standing reserve" (*Bestand*); the corresponding mode of access is "calculative thinking."
2. **Fundamental Ontology as an Emancipatory Project** (pp.468–474) — Dasein's self-alienation as reification; the hermeneutics of facticity as a project of "de-alienation"; formal indicators as a non-reifying mode of language; appropriation (*Aneignung*) as Dasein's fundamental hermeneutical relation to self/other/world; "ownedness"/"unownedness" as better translations of *Eigentlichkeit*/*Uneigentlichkeit* than "authenticity"/"inauthenticity."
   - *Language and Appropriation in Fundamental Ontology* (pp.469–472) — language as "the taming of the overwhelming"; appropriation tied to "mineness" (*Jemeinigkeit*); education redefined as "collaborative co-appropriation" that helps students "come into their own" rather than meet fixed "learning outcomes" (FAITHFUL-Mertel, p.472).
   - *Situating Enframing Within Fundamental Ontology* (pp.472–474) — against Marcuse's and Feenberg's reading of enframing as a static, all-encompassing transcendental structure; enframing is instead a historically inherited, revisable horizon; education's task is to make the question of Being "matter" to students rather than transmit fixed doctrine; enframing's "de-worlding" effect reduces students to their function "within the system" (Crowell, cited p.474) — echoing Thomson's point about the "impossible quantification of the qualitative" (cited p.474).
3. **Education as Response to Enframing** (pp.475–482) — critique of vague "Technologically Enhanced Learning" (TEL) initiatives (via Bayne, 2014, p.475); the corrective to calculative thinking is *Gelassenheit* ("releasement") and *Besinnung* ("sensibility"/reflection), glossed as "sober thinking" (via Wrathall, p.476).
   - *Thinking as Gelassenheit and Besinnung* (pp.476–478) — a personal "workspace" example distinguishing a purely functional vocabulary from a "eudaimonic" one; schools reframed as "worlds of significance," not homogeneous administered spaces; language's world-disclosing function (via Gadamer); Heidegger's dialogue with a Japanese interlocutor as a model of multilingual, non-instrumental language education (pp.478–479).
   - *The Limits of the Skilled Coping Model* (pp.479–482) — using Dreyfus's and Wrathall's own example of sport as "non-optionalised engagement," Mertel argues professional/academy sport has been colonized by economic imperatives (football academies, US college sports), undermining its claimed emancipatory function; amateur "streetball" offered as a marginally better case, though still commercially inflected (pp.479–480). This motivates the broader claim that skilled coping alone cannot supply the "spread-out substantial picture" (via Iris Murdoch) needed for a standing site of resistance; the *Leistungskultur* ("culture of achievement") is introduced as enframing's by-product, linked to burnout and student anxiety (via Neckel & Wagner 2013; Haubl 2011), the "race to the top" beginning in pre-kindergarten, and the "educational consultancy" industry (pp.480–481). Workers' and students' "loss of words" (inability to find a *Gegen-wort*, "counter-word") is framed as a moral, not merely cognitive, loss (p.481). A critique of Feenberg's optimistic reading of social media as a site of Heideggerian "saying" follows, including the dating-apps example, arguing that the issue is the *quality*, not the mere fact, of communication (pp.482–484).
4. **Conclusion** (p.484) — the "appropriative" approach, emphasizing moral articulacy and multilingualism, is better equipped than the pragmatist/skilled-coping approach to found an emancipatory philosophy of education.

## Findings / Claims

- Heidegger's philosophy of technology is best read as a continuation, not an abandonment, of the emancipatory aims of fundamental ontology; the "early/late" discontinuity thesis "insulates" his technology critique from its ethical roots (FAITHFUL-Mertel, pp.467–468).
- Formal indicators and appropriation give fundamental ontology an ineliminably "ontic," revisable dimension; enframing is therefore an "inherited horizon of intelligibility," not a "closed" or fully deterministic one (FAITHFUL-Mertel, pp.469–470, 473).
- Education, properly conceived, is a "collaborative process of co-appropriation" oriented toward students "coming into their own" and cultivating a "voice," not toward standardized "learning outcomes" (FAITHFUL-Mertel, p.472).
- Enframing's "de-worlding" tendency in educational contexts converts individually meaningful learning environments into a "homogenous field of technical intervention," with the effect of individualizing what are actually social/systemic pathologies — treating symptoms as located in the student rather than the environment (FAITHFUL-Mertel, pp.473–474, 481).
- The pragmatist "skilled bodily coping" model of Heideggerian education (Dreyfus, Wrathall), while capturing something real about embodied, non-optionalized engagement, cannot by itself resist enframing because its paradigmatic examples (professional/academy sport) are themselves already colonized by economic imperatives, and because the model neglects language altogether (FAITHFUL-Mertel, pp.479–480).
- The "culture of achievement" (*Leistungskultur*) is a significant, under-discussed by-product of enframing in education, linked empirically (via cited sources, not Mertel's own data) to burnout and rising student anxiety, beginning as early as pre-kindergarten (FAITHFUL-Mertel, pp.480–481).
- The proper response to enframing is the cultivation of "moral articulacy" — an evaluative, non-instrumental vocabulary — supported by multilingual education, since inhabiting more than one language is what allows the "fusion of horizons" needed to see one's own ontological commitments from outside (FAITHFUL-Mertel, pp.478–479, 484).
- Andrew Feenberg's optimistic view that online platforms already constitute a "tidal wave" of authentic Heideggerian "saying" mistakes quantity of communication for quality; Mertel contests this reading directly (FAITHFUL-Mertel, pp.482–484).
