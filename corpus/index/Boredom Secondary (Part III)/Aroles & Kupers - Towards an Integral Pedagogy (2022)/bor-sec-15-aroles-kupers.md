# Aroles & Küpers (2022) — *Towards an Integral Pedagogy in the Age of 'Digital Gestell'*

**Type:** journal article (*Management Learning*, Vol. 53(5), pp.757–775, DOI 10.1177/13505076211053871) · **Discipline:** organization/management-education studies — Heideggerian philosophy of technology (*Gestell*/*Gelassenheit*) applied to pedagogy, not phenomenology-of-mood or psychology of boredom · **Depth:** deep (~19pp) · **Corpus-role:** Strand-B outlier. Boredom-adjacent by way of *Gestell*'s homogenizing effect on pedagogy and the engagement/disengagement stakes of telepresence, but the source never uses the word "boredom" and does not engage FCM's tripartite mood-analysis at all — it draws instead on Heidegger's later technology essays.

## Thesis / Aim

Aroles and Küpers (A&K) address three interconnected challenges facing universities and business schools as COVID-accelerated digitalisation reshapes teaching and learning: (1) the shift from direct embodied co-presence to technologically mediated telepresence, (2) the need to re-embody digital teaching and learning activities, and (3) the need to rethink the purpose and relevance of business-school teaching itself (p.757, Abstract). Their governing method is a "phenomenological lens," specifically Heidegger's concepts of enframing (*Gestell*) and releasement (*Gelassenheit*), extended "to the digital context" (p.758) and focused on (re-)embodiment. The article's own resolution is prescriptive rather than merely diagnostic: teachers and learners need the capacity to "reflectively move between embodied and digital(ised) forms of learning and teaching" (p.757), an ethos the authors name an "integral pedagogy" cultivated through "engaged releasement" (pp.757, 770). Keywords given by the authors — body, digitalisation, education, Heidegger, pedagogy, telepresence (p.757) — confirm that boredom, mood, and affect are not the article's own organizing vocabulary; embodiment and technology are.

## Structure

1. **Introduction** (pp.757–759) — COVID-19 context, phenomenological framing, three-challenge preview, and article roadmap.
2. **Technologies, embodiment and learning: 'Digital Gestell'** (pp.759–761) — 2.1 the Heideggerian concept of enframing (*Gestell*) (p.759): definition, danger, and critique (Verbeek 2005; Ruin 2010) (p.760); 2.2 embodiment and the multifaceted nature of technologies (pp.760–761): Heidegger's neglect of the body (Aho 2005, 2010; Schalow 2006) and the case for retaining *Gestell* despite its dating.
3. **Online teaching, learning and pedagogy** (pp.761–762) — 3.1 contextualising online learning (literature survey since the late 1980s); 3.2 embodiment and learning: a phenomenological perspective (dataism, datafication, learnification, "machine behaviourism").
4. **Challenge 1 — Shifting from co-presence to telepresence** (pp.762–764) — "Other and othering" (peer-learning loss, p.762–763); "Feeling, sensing and experiencing" (lecture-as-theatrical-event, non-verbal cues, p.763); "Here and there" (bi-localized simultaneity, "far-but-close" paradox, pp.763–764).
5. **Challenge 2 — Re-embodying teaching and learning activities** (pp.764–766) — re-embodiment via post-phenomenology (Ihde, Verbeek) and *homo faber* (p.765); "re-embodying online education" via affect-shaping learning materials (Ward 2018) (pp.765–766).
6. **Challenge 3 — Re-purposing teaching in business schools** (pp.766–768) — "Beyond reductionism" (critique of silo-type, apparatus-governed curricula, via Agamben 2009) (p.767); "Digitality and repurposing" (integral pluralism, Wals's emancipatory pedagogy, Biesta's qualification/subjectification/socialisation) (pp.767–768).
7. **Discussion** (pp.768–770) — moving between co- and telepresence (Han's *homo digitalis*); political implications of enframing/mediation (Beverungen et al. 2019); an ethos of releasement (*Gelassenheit*) for the age of digital *Gestell* (Ingold's education of attention, Verbeek's politicized post-phenomenology).
8. **Conclusion** (pp.770–771) — recap of the three challenges, releasement as ethical-political practice, closing call for "integral pedagogical practices in the spirit of engaged releasement."

## Constructs

A&K's own vocabulary sits almost entirely outside the cluster's boredom-construct axis; the mapping below is therefore necessarily thin, and honesty about that thinness is itself the analytically useful finding.

- **Monotony / meaninglessness** is treated only by functional analogy. *Gestell*'s effect on education is described as making learning and teaching activities "increasingly regimented and homogeneous," tending toward "strict conformity and homogenisation" (p.760); Challenge 3 diagnoses business-school curricula as reproducing "'more of the same'" through a "functionalistic 'silo type' disciplinary and instrumentalising mentality" delivered via "prefabricated templates, pre-programmed arrangements, and linear lists in PowerPoints" (p.767). This is a structural/institutional account of homogenization and flattening that shares monotony's *content* (sameness, meaning-emptying, rote repetition) without ever being named "monotony" or linked to boredom research.
- **Flow / engagement (contrast pole)** is the construct A&K come closest to treating directly, under the heading of engagement/disengagement risk. Peer-learning and the "responsive engagement with that which is different" are described as diminished, not eliminated, by telepresence (pp.762–763); re-embodied online materials are proposed specifically to make "engagement with and commitment to the learning process" possible again (pp.765–766, citing Ward 2018); and "engaged releasement" is offered, in the Discussion, as the ethos that recovers engagement against enframing's pull toward disengaged, instrumentalised relations (pp.769–770).
- **Attentional disengagement / meta-awareness** appears only glancingly, via the loss of eye-contact and non-verbal cues in telepresence and the observation that those in "disembodied" relations are "susceptible to external interruptions and distractions" (p.763). This is not attention research; it is a phenomenological description of reduced multisensory contact.
- **Temporality of boredom (time-drag)** is addressed, but in a different register than the cluster's usual sense. The "Here and there" subsection analyzes telepresence's *bi-localized simultaneity* — being in two places "at once," a "community of time without... community of physical space" (quoting Zhao, p.764), and the "far-but-close" paradox of virtual co-presence (Wilson et al. 2008, p.764). This is a temporality of *dislocation and simultaneity*, not boredom's stretched, dragging *Zeitvertreib*; the two should not be conflated even though both concern time-experience under mediated conditions.
- **Profound/deep boredom, being-bored-by, being-bored-with, situational vs. trait boredom, boredom proneness, mind-wandering/task-unrelated thought, and boredom-as-regulatory-signal** are **not discussed anywhere in this source** — not by name, not by functional description, not in the reference list. This is expected: A&K's disciplinary home is organization/management-education studies, and their Heideggerian resource is the philosophy of technology (*Gestell*/*Gelassenheit*), not *Being and Time*'s mood-analysis or FCM's tripartite boredom structure.

## Reading of FCM

This source does not engage FCM's boredom analysis, and no bridge to fcm-04/05/06/07 should be forced. A&K's Heideggerian apparatus is drawn from two different, later texts — "The Question Concerning Technology" (1977), source of *Gestell* (p.759), and *Discourse on Thinking* (1966), source of *Gelassenheit* (p.769) — neither of which is cited in relation to boredom, mood, *Befindlichkeit*, or *Stimmung* anywhere in the article. The word "boredom," "mood," and "attunement" do not appear in the text at all (confirmed by direct search of the full extraction). What A&K do share with FCM is only a family resemblance at a much higher level of generality: both diagnose modernity's technologized mode of being as narrowing or flattening Dasein's engagement with beings-as-a-whole — A&K via *Gestell*'s "instrumentalises and technicises the world as a resource" (p.759–760), FCM via profound boredom's disclosure of *Seinsverlassenheit* (the abandonment of beings). *(P/anticipatory-application)* — this parallel is our own observation, not a connection A&K draw themselves, and it should not be cited as though the source offers an FCM reading. ## Findings / Claims

1. **The digital *Gestell* diagnosis** (pp.759–760). Enframing names the "expression of the 'essence' of the world-forming nature of technology" (p.759) that constitutes an epoch; in its digital form, everything appears within immediate reach and "callable," and both technology-users and the technologized are at risk of being rendered exploitable, calculable "objects" (pp.759–760). A&K concede the standard critiques of *Gestell* — that it is "too abstract and/or too nostalgic" (Verbeek 2005) and prone to "monolithic characterisations" of technology (Ruin 2010, p.760) — but retain the concept anyway, reframing the task as asking what Heideggerian thinking still "has to offer for the 21st century" (Georgakis and Ennis 2014, p.760) rather than defending its empirical fidelity.
2. **Challenge 1 — co-presence to telepresence** (pp.761–764). The shift to telepresence constrains "the character, richness and depth of (embodied) encounters" (p.762) even as it preserves a form of presence; peer-learning among students is disproportionately affected, since informal, embodied encounter (not lecture content) is what telepresence most degrades (pp.762–763); non-verbal cues, eye-contact, and multisensory experience are sharply reduced (p.763); and telepresence produces a distinctive bi-localized, "far-but-close" temporal-spatial structure rather than simple absence (pp.763–764).
3. **Challenge 2 — re-embodying digital teaching and learning** (pp.764–766). Drawing on Ihde's post-phenomenology, A&K argue cyberspace "is not a disembodied reality" (p.765): the sensorial body remains partly anchored in the physical world even while extended into virtual space, and human–technology relations are reciprocal ("human-prostheses inter-relations," Ihde 2012, p.765) rather than merely instrumental. Practically, this licenses designing online materials that deliberately "reshape" learners' existing bodily and affective habits (Ward 2018, pp.765–766) rather than treating digital delivery as a neutral container for content.
4. **Challenge 3 — re-purposing business-school teaching** (pp.766–768). Digitalising a reductionist curriculum "would only reproduce 'more of the same'" (p.767); A&K read business-school curricula through Agamben's concept of "apparatus" — a "set of practices, bodies of knowledge, measures, and institutions" that "orient" conduct (Agamben 2009, p.767) — and argue digitalisation is an opportunity to re-assess not just delivery but the "how," "what," and "why" of teaching, prioritising social welfare and sustainability over "individual business success" (pp.766–768), citing Wals's (2020) emancipatory pedagogy as a model.
5. **The releasement response** (pp.769–771). Against enframing, A&K propose *Gelassenheit* — an "engaged letting-be" that is not indifference but active, receptive practice (p.769) — as the ethos proper to an "integral pedagogy" able to move between embodied and digital modes. Releasement is explicitly characterised as ethical and political, "neither a freedom of an elitist mastery, nor a tranquillised withdrawal" (p.770), and the conclusion frames post-digital education as irreducibly ambivalent: simultaneously restrictive and newly enabling (pp.770–771).

## Analytical role (anticipatory-application)

*(P/anticipatory-application) — the following are our interpretive links to Part III, not claims Aroles & Küpers make themselves.*

First, the digital-*Gestell* diagnosis supplies Part III with institutional-level vocabulary for a mechanism the dissertation otherwise frames at the level of the individual learner: A&K's account of enframed pedagogy becoming "regimented and homogeneous" (p.760) via "prefabricated templates" and "linear lists in PowerPoints" (p.767) describes a *structural* source of monotony that could sit alongside, rather than compete with, Part III's own construct of boredom proneness — the claim being that some deployment contexts (asynchronous 2D-PC delivery, template-driven VLE content) may be more heavily enframed than others (live discussion, lab-based VR), independent of any individual student's trait disposition to boredom. This should be framed as a candidate institutional co-factor, not as evidence about boredom itself, since A&K never measure or even name boredom.

Second, the "Other and othering" analysis of peer-learning loss under telepresence (pp.762–763) resonates with, without directly addressing, the memory-noted correction to Part III's own survey reading: that the VLE's spatial-proximity-voice co-watching feature and universal group-assignment mean any "WE unmet want" gap in item F7 is better read as a student-side activation failure than a design/grouping absence. A&K's point — that informal, embodied peer encounter is what telepresence degrades most, even when formal peer-facing features exist (p.763) — offers a plausible mechanism (design provides the affordance; embodied habituation to using it is what's missing) for *why* activation, not availability, is the locus of the gap. This is a resonance we are drawing, not a claim A&K make about VLEs, surveys, or students specifically.

Third, "engaged releasement" (*Gelassenheit*) is offered here as a candidate name for the receptive, attentive pedagogical stance that Part III's discussion of engagement (as the positive pole opposite student boredom) might already be gesturing toward without a name. A&K are explicit that this is not passive withdrawal but an active practice of letting phenomena show themselves (p.769) — a useful conceptual resource if Part III wants a term for the teacher-side or design-side disposition that counters monotony-driven disengagement, distinct from any construct that names the *student's* affective state.

Fourth, and as a caution rather than a contribution: this source carries no measures, no operationalized constructs, and no empirical data of any kind. `****** UNVERIFIED:` any attempt to ground a `grounds-measure` bridge to the (not-yet-built) Boredom Experiment (VR Attention Study) corpus entry would be unfounded here — there is nothing in this source to ground. Part III should cite Aroles & Küpers strictly for conceptual vocabulary (*Gestell*, *Gelassenheit*, telepresence, re-embodiment, integral pedagogy) and never as empirical support for, or against, any boredom-construct claim.
