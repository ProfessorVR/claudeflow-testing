# bor-sec-48 — Hadjioannou, "Angst as Evidence: Shifting Phenomenology's Measure" (2019)

**Chapter 4 of 12 in Hadjioannou (ed.), *Heidegger on Affect* (Palgrave, "Philosophers in Depth" series, 2019), book pp.69–104 — the volume's longest chapter, written by Christos Hadjioannou himself in his separate capacity as a contributing author, distinct from his role as the volume's editor.** **Type:** monograph-style argumentative chapter in an edited philosophy collection · **Discipline:** philosophy (Heideggerian/Husserlian phenomenology; epistemology of evidence) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand G — builds a self-contained methodological argument about how moods (specifically angst) can count as phenomenological "evidence"; contains no boredom-specific material whatsoever, but bears a genuine, textually-anchored structural relationship to FCM's own methodological unit on awakening (not ascertaining) a fundamental attunement.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Hadjioannou argues that *Being and Time* (BT) is not only an ontological revision of traditional metaphysics but, more fundamentally, a revision of phenomenology's own measure of epistemic justification — of what is allowed to count as evidence (FE, pp.69–71). Both Husserl and Heidegger run transcendental, foundationalist projects that aim to ground philosophical knowledge in evidence (FE, pp.72–75), but where Husserl anchors evidence in originary intuition disclosed after the epoché — clear, distinct, apodictically certain, and internal to a reflecting consciousness — Heidegger relocates evidence in a pre-reflective, indeterminate, non-apodictic existential feeling: angst (FE, pp.79–82). Angst functions, on this reading, as the hermeneutic equivalent of what analytic epistemologists call a "justifier of knowledge" (FAITHFUL-Hadjioannou, p.71): it is the evidence that phenomenologically grounds Heidegger's interpretation of Dasein's thrown, finite, temporal structure, as disclosed in authentic existence (FE, p.71). The chapter's governing claim is that this substitution is a genuine epistemological shift (FE, p.71), not a merely thematic swap of one mood for another as an object of study: Heidegger radicalizes Husserl's own concept of evidence, remaining committed to some notion of evidential grounding — Hadjioannou coins the label "quasi-evidentialist" (FE, p.94) — while rejecting the mentalism and internalism built into Husserl's, and much of contemporary analytic epistemology's, construal of evidence as justified, internal, mental-state-based certainty (FE, pp.93–97).

## 2. Structure

The chapter has six named divisions plus fourteen substantial endnotes, several of which are extended discursive excursuses rather than mere citation apparatus.

1. Unnumbered Introduction (pp.69–71) — states the thesis, previews the four-section-plus-conclusion roadmap, and nominates angst as evidence for Dasein's finite, temporal structure.
2. §2, "Phenomenology as a Foundationalist Project: Grounding Knowledge/Interpretation in Evidence" (pp.72–75) — establishes both Husserl and Heidegger as Kantian-style transcendental projects, via Carman's analogy between Kant's "epistemic conditions" and Heidegger's "hermeneutic conditions" (p.73); argues that resoluteness (*Entschlossenheit*) functions methodologically like Husserl's epoché (p.75).
3. §3, "Husserl's Conception of Evidence" (pp.75–79), in two subsections: 3.1 "Originary Intuition and Apodictic Certainty" (pp.75–77) reconstructs Husserl's Principle of All Principles and the shift, across his career, from an ideal of adequation to one of apodicticity (via Hans Bernhard Schmid's five-stage periodization); 3.2 "Husserl's Mentalist Evidentialism" (pp.77–79) uses Philipp Berghofer's contemporary-epistemology reading to classify Husserl as a mentalist evidentialist in Earl Conee and Richard Feldman's technical sense.
4. §4, "Heidegger's Critique of Husserl and the Notion of Evidence in *Being and Time*" (pp.79–93) — the chapter's center of gravity, in four subsections: 4.1 "Heidegger's Alternative Beginning: Affective Evidence vs. Intuitive Evidence" (pp.79–82); 4.2 "Heidegger's Critique of Husserlian Evidence" (pp.82–89), itself in three parts — 4.2.1 "Care for Certainty: Science, Knowledge and Purification" (pp.83–85), 4.2.2 "Criteria for Truth: Clarity and Distinctness" (pp.85–86), 4.2.3 "The Mangling of Evidence" (pp.86–89) — reconstructing Heidegger's 1923/24 lecture course *Introduction to Phenomenological Research* (IPR); 4.3 "Evidence in *Being and Time*" (pp.89–91); 4.4 "Angst: The Authentic Certainty of Resoluteness" (pp.91–93).
5. §5, "Angst and Mentalist Evidentialism" (pp.93–97) — the closing tripartite argument that BT's quasi-evidentialism is incompatible with mentalist evidentialism because (a) Heidegger denies epistemology's/certainty's priority over ontology, (b) angst is not a mental state, and (c) angst is not internal to a bounded subject.
6. §6, "Concluding Remark" (pp.96–97) — restates the radicalization thesis.
7. Notes 1–14 (pp.97–104) — several are substantial: n.7 engages Katherine Withy's 2012 article on angst's methodological role at length, agreeing that angst is analyzed for what it shows about doing philosophy rather than about how one leads a life generally (p.98); n.8 is an extended excursus (pp.98–102) on translating *Befindlichkeit*, engaging closely with Elpidorou and Freeman's 2015 survey of translations (Macquarrie & Robinson's "state-of-mind," Haugeland's "findingness," Guignon's "situatedness," Dreyfus/Blattner/Crowell's "affectedness," Stambaugh's "attunement") and defending "disposition" via an argued homology with Aristotle's διάθεσις, traced to Hadjioannou's own 2013 essay. A brief acknowledgements note situates the chapter as output of Hadjioannou's Irish Research Council postdoctoral fellowship at University College Dublin under Dermot Moran's mentorship (p.97).

## 3. Constructs

This chapter treats none of the cluster's boredom-specific constructs directly — "boredom" and "*Langeweile*" occur nowhere in its roughly thirty-six pages. That absence deserves honest registration precisely because this is a full-chapter-coverage entry in a boredom-organized cluster: Hadjioannou's own argument is about the general epistemic status of moods as evidence, worked out entirely through angst, Husserl, and the 1923/24 IPR lectures, with no boredom-specific material anywhere in view.

What the chapter does supply is a fully worked example of the argument-form any other mood — including profound boredom — would need in order to be established as phenomenological evidence in Heidegger's sense: first, show the mood is pre-reflective and disclosive rather than a reflectively-intuited object; second, show that reflective objectification of it is distorting rather than clarifying; third, show it discloses Dasein's structural whole (thrownness, temporality, being-towards-death, or, mutatis mutandis, totalizing indifference) rather than a determinate object. Because FCM's own methodological claim — that a fundamental attunement must be *awakened*, not *ascertained* (fcm-03, book pp.59–65) — runs precisely this argument-form for attunement in general, two clearly-flagged analogical links are recorded below and in the accompanying edges file, against `profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)` and `temporality of boredom (time-drag)`. Both are our construction (P/anticipatory-application), not a claim this chapter itself makes about boredom.

## 4. Reading of FCM

The chapter never mentions the 1929/30 lecture course (*Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics*, FCM) or profound boredom. Its central argument, however, bears a striking — and, on inspection, genuinely substantive — structural resemblance to FCM's own "awakening, not ascertaining" methodology (fcm-03, §16), and the parallel is worth drawing out carefully rather than either forcing it or dismissing it as merely thematic.

FCM's claim (fcm-03, book p.62) is that treating a fundamental attunement as an object of "ascertaining" — of empirical or introspective demonstration — is not merely difficult but self-defeating: objectifying an attunement into an object of knowledge achieves the contrary of an awakening, since the attunement is thereby destroyed, or at least weakened and altered. Hadjioannou's chapter reconstructs, independently and from different textual material (BT and the 1923/24 IPR lectures rather than the 1929/30 FCM lectures), what is recognizably the same claim applied to angst specifically. Heidegger's most sustained argument for this, per Hadjioannou's reconstruction of §4.2.3, is that Husserlian "reflection" — the very act supposed to secure originary-intuitive evidence — is what distorts affective phenomena such as anxiety, joy, and terror (FAITHFUL-Hadjioannou, pp.87–88, citing IPR 220); Heidegger's own words, as quoted by Hadjioannou, insist that anxiety "is instead a phenomenon of existence itself" and cannot be grasped as a "manner of being-related-to-something" (direct quote ≤12 words, IPR 220, p.88). The same logic recurs at SZ 136, cited at p.81: dispositions disclose "prior to all cognition and volition" (direct quote, SZ 136, p.81), which is precisely why measuring their evidentiary status against the apodictic certainty of theoretical cognition is illegitimate (paraphrase, pp.81–82).

This is the same reflection-destroys-the-phenomenon structure FCM asserts for "ascertaining" an attunement — not a loose family resemblance but the identical inferential move (objectifying or reflectively grasping an affective/attunement phenomenon converts or destroys it, so evidence for it must come by a different, non-objectifying route) applied to a cognate but distinct target (angst rather than attunement-in-general or profound boredom). Because Heidegger works out recognizably the same argument at two different points in his career — 1923/24–1927 for angst-as-evidence, 1929/30 for attunement-in-general, culminating in profound boredom as the paradigm case — Hadjioannou's chapter can fairly be read as supplying the earlier, angst-specific instance of the very methodological move FCM later generalizes onto boredom (P/anticipatory-application: this synthesis across the two texts is ours, not either author's own claim).

A second, equally load-bearing parallel: both texts rely on the identical BT passage (SZ 134–138) for the claim that Dasein is never without attunement. Hadjioannou quotes SZ 134 directly on the "pallid, evenly balanced lack of mood" (direct quote ≤12 words, p.90) to argue that even apparent moodlessness is itself a disclosive way of being burdened by facticity (FE, p.90). FCM's fcm-03 (§17, book p.68) builds its own "concealment of the most powerful" thesis — that the least-noticed attunements, including a possible profound boredom, are the ones that pervasively determine Dasein — on precisely this same textual basis. The two chapters draw structurally identical conclusions (concealment does not equal absence; the most pervasive attunement is the hardest to ascertain) from the same primary-text material, independently of one another, which is stronger evidence of genuine methodological convergence than a merely thematic echo would be.

A third, more speculative bridge, flagged as weaker: Hadjioannou's account of anxiety's "authentic certainty" (§4.4, pp.91–93) — that Dasein's Being-certain about death cannot be computed by ascertaining how many cases of death one encounters (paraphrase, SZ 264–265, p.92) and does not belong to the graded evidentiary order of the present-at-hand at all (paraphrase, p.92) — runs the identical evidentiary argument-form that FCM's third form of boredom would require to establish profound boredom's own disclosure of Dasein's totality (fcm-06). Hadjioannou does not extend his argument to boredom, and this bridge should be read as exploratory rather than textually forced by the chapter itself.

A fourth, more incidental point of contact concerns the conscious/unconscious framing FCM explicitly refuses for attunement (fcm-03, §16b, book pp.60–62: waking/sleeping "are not equivalent to consciousness and unconsciousness"). Hadjioannou's own text, summarizing Berghofer's account of Conee and Feldman's mentalist evidentialism, notes in passing that "some unconscious, indeterminate state of anxiety may be a mental state" without thereby qualifying as a justifier (paraphrase ≤12 words, Berghofer 2018 as quoted at p.78–79). The two texts reach this point from opposite directions and for different reasons — FCM denies that conscious/unconscious is the relevant axis for attunement at all, while Hadjioannou's Husserl-derived framework merely denies that an unconscious mental state can do evidentiary work — but both converge on refusing to let the conscious/unconscious distinction settle the evidentiary or ontological status of an affective phenomenon. This is a weaker, more incidental parallel than the first two, and is recorded as such.

Net assessment: the FCM bridge here is genuine and substantive at the methodological level — the reflection-distorts-the-phenomenon argument and the concealment-is-not-absence argument are both textually anchored in material Hadjioannou and fcm-03 independently draw on — even though the chapter itself never applies its argument to boredom. This is a stronger case than most cross-strand thematic bridges in this cluster, because it rests on shared primary-text evidence (SZ 134–138 in particular) rather than on analogy alone.

## 5. Findings/Claims

1. BT's ontological revision of traditional metaphysics is inseparable from, and arguably secondary to, a methodological revision of what phenomenology accepts as evidence; Heidegger's dedication of BT to Husserl is read as a mark of intellectual honesty precisely because the book quietly overturns Husserl's own method (FE, pp.69–71, n.1).
2. Both Husserl and Heidegger run transcendental/foundationalist projects seeking a priori conditions of meaning grounded in evidence; per Carman, Kant's "epistemic conditions" have a hermeneutic analogue in Heidegger's "conditions of interpretation" (FAITHFUL-Hadjioannou, p.73, citing Carman 2003).
3. Resoluteness (*Entschlossenheit*) is methodologically analogous to Husserl's epoché and Principle of Evidence: both are the normative "switch" licensing reinterpretation and grounding transcendental knowledge, even though what counts as evidence differs radically between them (FE, pp.74–75).
4. Husserl's Principle of All Principles holds that every originary presentive intuition is a legitimizing source of cognition; across his career (per Schmid's periodization) Husserl's operative ideal shifts from adequation to apodicticity — indubitability that can survive even inadequate evidence (FE, pp.76–77, citing Husserl 1982/1983, Schmid 2001).
5. Following Berghofer, Husserl qualifies as a mentalist evidentialist in Conee and Feldman's technical sense: evidence determines justification, evidence consists in mental states (originary presentive intuitions), and one's ultimate evidence is internal to one's own experience (FE, pp.77–79, citing Berghofer 2018).
6. Heidegger's rehabilitation of moods as ontological evidence is a deliberate, sometimes tacit and sometimes explicit, juxtaposition against the Cartesian/Husserlian criteria of clarity, distinctness, and apodictic certainty (FE, pp.79–80).
7. Anxiety specifically satisfies BT's own explicit methodological requirement (SZ 182) for a disclosive phenomenon capable of exhibiting Dasein's primordial totality of Being "in an elemental way" (direct quote ≤12 words, SZ 182); Heidegger nominates anxiety by name for this role (FAITHFUL-Hadjioannou, p.81).
8. Heidegger's most sustained critique of Husserlian evidence is found not in BT itself but in the 1923/24 lecture course *Introduction to Phenomenological Research*, organized around a diagnosed "care for certainty" (*Sorge um die Gewissheit*) inherited from Descartes via Husserl (FE, pp.82–84, citing IPR).
9. Descartes' own example of a clear-but-not-distinct perception — unlocalized pain — shows, on Heidegger's reading, that moods structurally fail the Cartesian/Husserlian distinctness criterion and are therefore excluded a priori from evidential status on that model (FE, pp.85–86, citing IPR 154–156).
10. Husserl's "care for certainty" distorts both intentionality (reduced to theoretical "meaning-something," *Vorstellen*) and affective/emotional life (analogized to acts of knowing); the reflective act that grounds Husserlian evidence is, for Heidegger, what mangles affective phenomena (FE, pp.86–88, citing IPR 209, 220).
11. In BT, disposition (*Befindlichkeit*)/mood (*Stimmung*) is "methodologically significant in principle" for the existential analytic (direct quote ≤12 words, SZ 139, p.90); moods disclose Being-in-the-world as a meaningful whole, and "lack of mood" is never simply nothing (FE, pp.90–91, citing SZ 134–139).
12. Resoluteness "exact[s] anxiety of oneself" (direct quote ≤12 words, SZ 305, p.92) and thereby achieves authentic certainty (Being-certain) — a certainty Heidegger explicitly distinguishes from apodictic, present-at-hand evidence: it does not belong to that evidentiary order at all (paraphrase, SZ 264–265, p.92).
13. The chapter's closing tripartite argument: (a) Heidegger rejects epistemology's/certainty's priority over ontology outright, so BT is only a "quasi-evidentialist" project, not a straightforward evidentialist one; (b) angst is not a "mental state" — *Befindlichkeit* is better translated "disposition" than Macquarrie and Robinson's "state-of-mind," since it is neither a state nor mental; (c) angst is not "internal" to a bounded subject — it arises neither from "inside" nor "outside," contra Husserl's residual individualism, rationalism, and internalism, per Crowell (FE, pp.93–97, citing Crowell 2013).
14. Overall verdict: Heidegger's BT is incompatible with mentalist evidentialism, and with internalism generally, while remaining committed to some notion of evidential grounding, without which, Hadjioannou argues, the phenomenological project would collapse into unjustified assertion (FE, pp.94, 97).
15. The everyday, inauthentic response to angst is to reinterpret it as fear of a determinate oncoming event, and then to dismiss that fear as a weakness — a double displacement that, per SZ 254, banishes angst from what Hadjioannou calls the "epistemological frame" altogether; "the 'they'" actively polices this displacement, withholding from Dasein the courage angst's evidence requires (FE, pp.92–93, citing SZ 254).
16. Crowell's threefold characterization of what angst contests in Husserl — "residual individualism, rationalism (theoretism), and internalism" (direct quote ≤12 words, Crowell 2013, 67, cited p.96) — supplies Hadjioannou's chapter with its final vocabulary for why affective evidence is non-representationalist: openness to the world is not a *forum internum*, and Dasein's passivity and finitude in being-in-the-world positively "contest such internalism" (paraphrase, Crowell 2013, 71, cited p.96–97).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This chapter's chief service to Part III is a methodological caution rather than a substantive finding about boredom, since it makes no claim about boredom at all. First, its core argument — that reflectively or objectively "ascertaining" a pre-reflective affective phenomenon can distort or destroy the very thing being measured — bears directly on Part III's own self-report boredom instrument: asking a participant to introspect on and report their own boredom is a determinate, propositional act of reflection, and this chapter's Heideggerian tradition treats the target phenomenon as pre-reflective and pre-propositional *(anticipatory-application)*. This does not invalidate self-report, but it is a genuine caution worth carrying into Part III's discussion of self-report limitations, in the same spirit as the caution already flagged in bor-sec-02 §7 that self-report captures only an "ontic trace" of a pre-reflective attunement.

Second, the chapter's account of moods as neither "internal" nor "external" but constitutively relational — disclosing self-and-world togetherness rather than a bounded subject's private state (pp.95–97) — cautions against treating boredom purely as an internal, subject-side variable to be correlated with external, environment-side variables such as VR versus 2D-PC deployment or task design; on this account the mood does not simply arise from one side or the other but discloses their prior, non-dualistic togetherness *(anticipatory-application)*. This is a caution about how to interpret correlational findings, not a design prescription.

Third, the chapter's citation of Withy's point that angst is analyzed not for what it tells us about how we live generally but specifically "how we do philosophy" (paraphrase ≤12 words, Withy 2012, p.195, quoted at p.98) is a reminder that this chapter's own angst-as-evidence argument is about phenomenology's internal methodology, not about lived anxious experience as such. The corresponding caution for Part III is to keep the dissertation's own methodological argument — how should boredom research justify its evidentiary claims — analytically separate from its empirical claims about participants' boredom *(anticipatory-application)*.
