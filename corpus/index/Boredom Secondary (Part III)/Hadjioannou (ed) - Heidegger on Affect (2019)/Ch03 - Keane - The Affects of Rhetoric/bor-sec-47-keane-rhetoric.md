# bor-sec-47 — Keane, "The Affects of Rhetoric and Reconceiving the Nature of Possibility" (2019)

**Chapter 3 of 12 in Hadjioannou (ed.), *Heidegger on Affect* (Palgrave, "Philosophers in Depth" series).** **Type:** book chapter, single-authored, in a 12-chapter edited collection by 13 distinct contributors · **Author:** Niall Keane (Mary Immaculate College, Limerick) — NOT editor Christos Hadjioannou, who authored a separate chapter (Ch.4, "Angst as Evidence: Shifting Phenomenology's Measure," beginning immediately where this chapter ends) and otherwise appears here only as volume editor · **Discipline:** philosophy (Heideggerian phenomenology; history/theory of rhetoric) · **Depth:** deep · **Pages:** book pp.47–68 (~22pp., plus five endnotes and four works-cited entries) · **Corpus role:** Strand G, full-chapter affect coverage under the cluster's expanded 12-chapter scope — this chapter has essentially no boredom content but is unusually rich for the dissertation's separate Aristotelian/Burkean rhetoric project, since it reconstructs Heidegger's own 1924 rehabilitation of Aristotle's *Rhetoric*.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Keane argues that Heidegger's 1924 summer-semester lecture course, *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* (GA 18; English *BCAP*), is the textual "soil" from which both *Being and Time* (BT, 1927) and the later *Contributions to Philosophy* (GA 65; *CP*) grow, and that this soil has been under-read (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.47). Opening with Thomas Sheehan's charge that the later Heidegger's rhetoric became "over-wrought" and "over-heated" (Sheehan 2015, 263, quoted p.47), Keane proposes that the 1924 lectures — grounded in "the concrete spirit of Aristotle's philosophy" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.47) — offer a corrective, more disciplined point of entry. The chapter's stated aim is twofold (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.47): first, to trace the genesis of Heidegger's reflections on affect (*pathos*), embodied speaking-together, the nature of possibility (*dynamis*), and the critique of actuality (*energeia*); second, to show how these 1924 reflections shape the later existential analytic of BT and, further out, the critique of the metaphysics of actuality in both BT and CP. Rhetoric is thus not a peripheral topic for Keane's Heidegger but the original site at which affect, sociality, and modality are first thought together.

## 2. Structure

The chapter carries no chapter-level abstract heading beyond its title; it unfolds as an unnumbered introduction followed by four internally numbered sections (headers reproduced from the source) plus five endnotes and a four-item reference list.

1. **Introduction** (pp.47–48, unnumbered) — states the thesis; frames Heidegger's 1924 task as recovering, "in the name of 'science'" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.48), the ground out of which Aristotle's basic concepts emerge, disclosed "by way of speaking" (*legein*) (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.48).
2. **Rhetoric as *dynamis*, not *techne*** (pp.48–51, unnumbered continuation) — Heidegger's rehabilitation of rhetoric against the Platonic *Gorgias* reading of the rhetorician as flattering "wand-waver"; the rhetoric/sophistry distinction; rhetoric's psychagogic kinship with dialectic via the *Phaedrus*.
3. **§1 "Aristotle's Rhetoric"** (pp.51–54) — the endoxic character of rhetoric, the *pisteis* (technical/non-technical), the enthymeme as rhetorical syllogism, the narrow/broad senses of "logical," and *logos* as the "fundamental mode of our being in the world" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.52).
4. **§2 "Heidegger's Appropriation of Rhetoric"** (pp.54–56) — Aristotle read as proto-phenomenologist; disclosedness (*Erschlossenheit*) versus uncoveredness (*Entdecktheit*); the three *pisteis entechnoi* (ethos/pathos/logos); rhetoric and dialectic as *dynameis* facilitated by the intellectual virtues (*hexeis*).
5. **§3 "Being with Others and Speaking with Others"** (pp.56–61) — the hermeneutical core of rhetoric prior to Arendt and Gadamer; *hedone* read as *Befindlichkeit* ("self-having"); embodiment and *pathos* as the "ground" (*Boden*) of speaking; *koinon*/*koinonia*; *doxa* as the source, not the deficiency, of everydayness; BT's citation of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* as "the first systematic hermeneutic" of everyday being-with (SZ 138, quoted p.61).
6. **§4 "On Actuality and Possibility"** (pp.61–66) — the critique of the metaphysical priority of actuality (*energeia*) over possibility (*dynamis*), keyed to *Metaphysics* Theta (1049b13); Dasein's "who," not "what"; the ontological difference as itself a possibility; the CP-era attack on the "modalities"; blocked/submerged possibility as the through-line of Heidegger's entire corpus.
7. **Endnotes and references** (pp.66–68) — five substantive endnotes (on Plato's *Phaedrus*/*Sophist*, Charles Bambach on "appropriate ethos," and P. Christopher Smith's charge of "de-acoustification") and a four-item bibliography (Kisiel 2005, Michalski 2005, Sheehan 2015, Withy 2015).

## 3. Constructs

**(a) Boredom construct-axis cross-check (honest, sparse).** This chapter contains no reference to *Langeweile*, boredom, or any cognate term; its textual base (the 1924 BCAP lectures) predates Heidegger's boredom analysis in *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (1929/30 lecture course) by five years, and Keane never gestures forward to it. None of the controlled-vocabulary boredom constructs (profound/deep boredom, being-bored-by/-with, mind-wandering, boredom proneness, flow, monotony, etc.) are treated in any direct sense. The one legitimate analogical link is architectural rather than topical: Keane's reconstruction of *hedone* as *Befindlichkeit* — a modality of "self-having" that discloses without cognizing (GA 18: 277; BCAP 188, discussed p.58–59) — describes the same general structure of attunement/disposedness that FCM later particularizes into the three forms of boredom. This is flagged honestly below (§4, "Reading of FCM") and reflected in two heavily qualified `treats-construct` edges rather than any `bridges-to-fcm` edge, since bridging to a *specific* FCM boredom-form unit here would overstate the connection.

**(b) The chapter's own affect/mood/emotion vocabulary (full treatment, per the cluster's expanded scope).** Read on its own terms, this is a chapter almost entirely *about* affect: it reconstructs Heidegger's 1924 claim that *pathos* is not "merely an annex of psychical processes" but "the ground [*Boden*] out of which speaking arises" (≤12-word paraphrase-quotation, FAITHFUL-Keane, p.58, citing GA 18: 262; BCAP 176). Affects (*pathe*) are, on this reading, the "basic possibilities" through which being-there is "primarily oriented toward itself" and "finds itself" — a "finding-oneself" (*Sichbefinden*) that is prior to and enables speech, not a private inner state layered onto it (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.58). Keane traces this through: (i) *hedone* (pleasure) reconceived as *Befindlichkeit*, threatening to collapse Aristotle's own *hexis*/*pathos* distinction (something one has vs. something one undergoes) but in fact naming a transformative "awakening" possibility within the everyday (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.58–59, citing GA 18: 277; BCAP 188); (ii) *pathos* as one of Aristotle's three *pisteis entechnoi* — the "artistic" proof that "disposes the listener" affectively (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.54); and (iii) *pathos* revisited in §4 as "a disposition of living things in their world" (≤12-word quotation, FAITHFUL-Keane, p.65, citing GA 18: 122; BCAP 83), the structure of being struck by something that simultaneously matters to us. Embodiment is explicit throughout: Heidegger's 1924 claim of "no division between 'psychic' and 'bodily acts'" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.58, citing GA 18: 199; BCAP 134) is immediately qualified by his refusal to reduce affect to psycho-physical process. *Doxa*, *koinon*, and *logos* round out the vocabulary as the social-ontological register in which affect operates: affects are not "inside us" as private feelings but are "delimited and negotiated continually" in "speaking together of what is common" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.59).

## 5. Findings/Claims

1. Heidegger rehabilitates rhetoric against the Platonic *Gorgias* reading (463a6–465e1): the rhetorician is not a "smooth talking technician" or flatterer but possesses a *dynamis* — a potentiality — to "concretize" ways of speaking that disclose something substantive, not merely persuade toward the expedient or illusory (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.49).
2. Rhetoric is more accurately a *dynamis* than a *techne* (GA 18: 114–115; BCAP 78–9): unlike medicine, geometry, or arithmetic, rhetoric has no "underlying matter" or determinate region of beings; its "knowing-the-way-around" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.49) identifies the most fitting means of disclosing something persuasive — "what speaks for a matter" — to a listener, without coercion (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.49–50).
3. Sophistry is distinguished from rhetoric not by technique but by aim: sophistry seeks to "conquer the other without knowledge of the matter discussed" (≤12-word quotation, FAITHFUL-Keane, p.50, citing GA 18: 109; BCAP 75), absorbed in "the immediate, in fashions, in babble" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.50) — for Heidegger, following Aristotle and (Keane argues) implicitly the Socrates of the *Phaedrus*, the very existence of sophistry is symptomatic of a "basic danger" native to Greek being-there (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.50).
4. Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is endoxic: it proceeds from established belief (*doxa*) via *pisteis* (the "case being made"), the rhetorical counterpart to dialectic's syllogisms. *Pistis* is a mode of demonstration that goes "directly to the heart" (Kisiel 2005, 137, quoted p.51) and is "taken more to heart" by the listener (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.51, citing GA 18: 133; BCAP 90) — Keane distinguishes narrowly logical (apodeictic/syllogistic) demonstration from broadly logical (enthymemic/rhetorical) demonstration, the latter grounded in a shared, affective "body of proof" (*soma tes pisteos*) rather than formal necessity (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.51, citing *Rhetoric* 1354a15, 1356a37–b26).
5. *Logos* is, for Heidegger reading Aristotle, "the fundamental mode of our being in the world" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.52) — not the conceptual grasp of a pre-given world but the disclosive "space of appearances" corresponding to speaking-with-others; Keane paraphrases Heidegger's own claim that "every speaking" is "a speaking to someone or with others" (≤12-word paraphrase, FAITHFUL-Keane, p.52, citing GA 18: 17; BCAP 14), such that in speaking with others about something "I express myself" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.52).
6. Aristotle is read as a proto-phenomenologist whose *Rhetoric* equips later phenomenology with the distinction, made explicit in BT, between originary "disclosedness" (*Erschlossenheit*) and founded "uncoveredness" (*Entdecktheit*) (SZ 220–221, cited p.53). Truth-disclosure occurs by way of speaking that is concerned with what it speaks of — "right in the midst of" its matter, *meta-logou* (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.54, citing GA 18: 61; BCAP 43).
7. Aristotle's three *pisteis entechnoi* ("artistic" proofs) are individuated as: (i) *ethos* — speech manifesting the speaker's credibility, integrity, and reliability; (ii) *pathos* — speech that "disposes the listener" and elicits an affective response; (iii) *logos* — speech that disclosively demonstrates the true nature of the matter at hand (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.54). For Heidegger the third type is decisive: "λέγειν itself is πίστις as the basic function of being-there itself" (≤12-word quotation, FAITHFUL-Keane, p.54, citing GA 18: 120; BCAP 82) — pre-empting BT's claim that Dasein is both understandingly discursive and affectively disclosive.
8. Speaking-with-one-another is, for Heidegger, "the clue to the uncovering" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.55, citing GA 18: 139; BCAP 95) of Dasein's basic being-in-a-world; rhetoric is taken as a "concrete guide" to the "basic possibility of speaking-with-one-another" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.55) — the interpretive distinction between the field of uncovering (ontology/ontological content) and the disclosive access-point to that field (phenomenological hermeneutics of Dasein) is, for Keane, Heidegger's chief philosophical originality here.
9. "Prior to Arendt and Gadamer," on Keane's telling, it is Heidegger who first drew attention to rhetoric's "hermeneutical core" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.56) — humans are "genuinely human" only insofar as they exist with others amid matters made "interpretatively intelligible" in conversation (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.56). This is the point at which "pure phenomenology" becomes "phenomenological hermeneutics" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.56, citing GA 18: 351; BCAP 238).
10. *Koinonia* — understanding the possibility of the being-in-common of things — is established practically, prior to any theoretical-metaphysical reflection on actuality/necessity, by "speaking-with-one-another where others are themselves speaking" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.56, citing GA 18: 50; BCAP 36). Without the *koinon*, Keane's Heidegger warns, we would be like "fish… caught in the stream" (≤12-word quotation, FAITHFUL-Keane, p.59, citing GA 18: 266; BCAP 178) — unable to reach authentic self-understanding or to project possibilities as our own.
11. Affects "participate in the doxastic formation" grounded in "being-with-one-another," which Heidegger explicitly distinguishes from the weaker "being situated alongside one another" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.59–60, citing GA 18: 47; BCAP 33) — the same distinction is, on Keane's reading, both the ground of average/everyday living-by-*doxa* and, paradoxically, the "fundamental possibility" of authentic self-understanding through "communicating, refuting, confronting" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.60).
12. BT itself credits Aristotle's *Rhetoric* as "the first systematic hermeneutic" of everyday being-with-one-another (≤12-word quotation, FAITHFUL-Keane, p.60, citing SZ 138) — for Keane this retrospective citation is the strongest textual evidence that the 1924 lectures are BT's own acknowledged root, not merely a scholarly reconstruction imposed from outside.
13. Heidegger's critique of the metaphysical priority of actuality (*energeia*) over possibility (*dynamis*), keyed against Aristotle's *Metaphysics* Theta (1049b13), can only be properly understood, on Keane's central methodological claim, by returning to the 1924 lectures: BT's talk of possibility as "higher" than actuality is retrospectively legible as already contained in the earlier course, even though BT's first readers could not have appreciated this (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.61).
14. Dasein's ontological constitution is not that of an "actual," "extant," or "present-at-hand" entity; Heidegger substitutes the "who" question for the "what" question, characterizing Dasein's existence as possibilizing and self-possibilizing rather than an actualized, produced, or determined presence (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.61–62, citing SZ 363–364). The ontological difference (between being and beings) is itself a possibility only because Dasein's own being possibilizes and performs that difference (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.62, citing GA 24: 454; BP 319).
15. In CP, Heidegger radicalizes the 1924-era critique: the origin and dominance of the metaphysical "modalities" (possibility, necessity, actuality) are more questionable than the traditional interpretation of beings as "idea," with actuality-as-*energeia* functioning as possibility's and necessity's "two horns" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.63, citing GA 65: 281; CP 221; and GA 65: 74; CP 59) — a metaphysical tradition that, almost without exception, defines possibility and necessity solely in terms of actuality, reducing even the not-yet-actual to the order of the actual.
16. Heidegger's decades-long "attack on metaphysical modality" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.65) in the name of a deeper, historically "blocked" possibility is legible across his whole corpus once identified; his 1924 reading of the *Rhetoric* — rhetoric as a "world negotiating exercise of speaking and hearing together" (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.65) and a fundamental mode of "perceiving" together (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.65, citing GA 18: 44; BCAP 32) — is, for Keane, among the earliest and clearest instances of this same attempt to locate and (where possible) "unblock" a suppressed possibility (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.66).
17. **The rhetorical-tradition connection, made explicit (of high relevance beyond this cluster).** Keane's chapter is, in effect, a genealogy of the Aristotelian *pisteis*/*ethos*-*pathos*-*logos* architecture as read by Heidegger — the same architecture that underlies the broader Aristotelian/Burkean rhetorical tradition (identification via shared *doxa*, evidentiary/affective grounds of persuasion, rhetoric's social-ontological rather than merely instrumental function). Keane explicitly credits Heidegger with anticipating Gadamer's philosophical hermeneutics and Arendt's political thought by locating rhetoric's "hermeneutical core" in being-with (FAITHFUL-Keane, p.56), and explicitly cites BT's own claim that Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is philosophy's "first systematic hermeneutic" of social existence (SZ 138, p.60) — a textual anchor of direct relevance to any project (such as this dissertation's) that treats Aristotelian rhetoric, Burkean identification/actancy, and Gross's evidentiary rigor as continuous with, rather than opposed to, ontological-hermeneutic accounts of shared meaning-making (P/anticipatory-application: the *connection* to Burke/Gross is our own; Keane himself never mentions either).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application) — the following are our own interpretive links to the dissertation, not claims Keane makes himself.*

This chapter's primary corpus value is not as a boredom-construct data point — it has essentially none — but as a rich, philologically careful reconstruction of Heidegger's *earliest* engagement with rhetoric, affect, and possibility, valuable to the dissertation's separate Aristotelian/Burkean rhetoric project independent of the boredom cluster's own organizing concerns *(anticipatory-application)*. Three links are worth flagging explicitly. First, Keane's genealogy of the *pisteis*/*ethos*-*pathos*-*logos* architecture, and his explicit claim that Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is (per BT itself) the "first systematic hermeneutic" of social existence, offers a phenomenological deepening of the identification/actancy framework already central to this dissertation's Part I (Burke, Gross, rhetorical actancy): where Burke's dramatism describes identification descriptively, Keane's Heidegger supplies an ontological account of *why* shared *doxa* and affective *pistis* are constitutive of, rather than instrumental to, being-with-others *(anticipatory-application)*. Second, Heidegger's explicit distinction between "being-with-one-another" and the weaker "being situated alongside one another" (GA 18: 47; BCAP 33, p.59–60) bears a suggestive structural resemblance to Part II's own "world-/co-disclosedness" poles (BT 145+160) — both distinguish a merely spatial/co-present togetherness from a disclosively shared one — though this chapter's 1924 vocabulary is not identical to Part II's later terminology and any use of it there should be checked against Part II's own methodology draft rather than assumed *(anticipatory-application)*. Third, and more cautiously, the same being-with/being-alongside distinction is structurally analogous (not identical) to Part III's own correction regarding the VLE's spatial-proximity-plus-voice co-watching design and its universal group-assignment — i.e., the difference between a merely co-present arrangement and a disclosively shared one is exactly the kind of design/activation distinction Part III's survey analysis already needs, though Keane's own chapter says nothing about VR, students, or attention, and this resemblance should not be over-read as evidentiary support *(anticipatory-application)*.
