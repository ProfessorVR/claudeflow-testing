# bor-sec-45 — O'Brien, "Being, Nothingness and Anxiety" (2019) — Chapter 1 of 12 in Hadjioannou (ed.), *Heidegger on Affect*

**Chapter 1 of 12 in Hadjioannou (ed.), *Heidegger on Affect* (Palgrave, "Philosophers in Depth" series).** **Type:** book chapter, single-authored, opening chapter of a 12-chapter edited collection by 13 distinct contributors · **Author:** Mahon O'Brien (University of Sussex) — NOT editor Christos Hadjioannou, who authored a separate chapter later in the volume (per this cluster's sibling units, Ch.4) and otherwise appears here only as the volume's editor · **Discipline:** philosophy (Heideggerian phenomenology / fundamental ontology) · **Depth:** deep · **Pages:** book pp.1–28 (~28pp., plus fifteen endnotes and a five-item reference list) · **Corpus role:** Strand G, opening unit — under this cluster's expanded scope this chapter's own affect/mood argument is captured in full, independent of boredom. O'Brien's chapter stakes out the volume's opening polemic: that *Being and Time* (BT, 1927) is continuous with, not superseded by, Heidegger's later (1929, 1935, 1940s) treatments of the Nothing, using **anxiety (Angst)**, not boredom, as its exemplary fundamental mood (*Grundstimmung*). It engages BT, the 1929 "What Is Metaphysics?" (with its 1943 postscript and 1949 introduction), and the 1935 *Introduction to Metaphysics* — not the 1929/30 *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (FCM) lecture course this cluster otherwise bridges to. Fellow-volume contributor Elpidorou & Freeman's chapter 8, "Is Profound Boredom Boredom?," is already built in this cluster as bor-sec-04; this opening chapter does not cite that later chapter, nor any of Slaby, Withy, or Elpidorou's other work — its only cluster-relevant citation is to Daniel Dahlstrom's 2010 journal article (not his separate chapter elsewhere in this same volume).

**Citation:** O'Brien, Mahon. "Being, Nothingness and Anxiety." In *Heidegger on Affect*, edited by Christos Hadjioannou, 1–28. Philosophers in Depth. Cham: Palgrave Macmillan, 2019. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-24639-6_1.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

O'Brien's chapter is explicitly framed as "a bit of housekeeping on Heidegger's behalf" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.1): a defense against the "myriad ways" BT was misread on first publication — as existentialism, nihilism, philosophical anthropology, psychology, humanism, subjectivism (FE, p.1) — and, more pointedly, against a persistent scholarly habit of treating the "later Heidegger" as discontinuous with, even incompatible with, the Heidegger who wrote BT (FE, p.1–2). O'Brien's thesis is that this discontinuist reading is a hermeneutic error: read on its own terms, BT already lays the groundwork for everything Heidegger will say about Being, Nothingness, and moods in 1929, 1935, and his 1940s retrospectives (FE, p.14). The vehicle for this argument is Heidegger's recurring insistence, across four decades, that "bare moods" (*Befindlichkeit*, *Stimmung*) are not psychological add-ons to cognition but phenomenal evidence of Dasein's standing exposure to the interplay of presence and absence — and that anxiety (*Angst*), understood as a fundamental mood rather than an emotion of fearfulness, is the privileged case because it discloses "the nothing" as constitutive of Dasein's own being (FE, p.11–14). O'Brien's target is explicitly named in note 1: Richard Capobianco, who argues that BT's textual difficulties force readers to treat the later Heidegger as discontinuous with the earlier (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.25 n.1). O'Brien judges this "a less than hermeneutically honest approach to a text" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.25 n.1).

## 2. Structure

The chapter (book pp.1–28) has two numbered sections, a typographic break, fifteen endnotes, and a five-item reference list; there is no abstract.

1. **§1 "Being and Nothing"** (pp.1–11) — traces Heidegger's own retrospective account of BT across the 1929 "What Is Metaphysics?" (with its 1943 postscript and 1949 introduction, both collected in *Pathmarks*/"PM") and the 1935 *Introduction to Metaphysics* ("IM"); reconstructs Leibniz's founding question ("why are there beings at all instead of nothing?"); works through Heidegger's rejection of the principle of non-contradiction as a court of appeal against talk of "the Nothing," staged against Carnap's 1932 attack; develops the chalk example from IM to show that "being" cannot be reduced to bare presence, since possibility (the chalk's capacity to be used up) belongs to what the chalk *is*; closes with an extended discussion of Husserlian intentionality (the lamp, the picture, the passing car) and Heidegger's "Origin of the Work of Art" hearing-example (the storm, the three-motored plane, the Mercedes) to show that ordinary perception already outruns bare presence.
2. **Typographic break** (p.11, mid-page) marks the transition from the historical-textual reconstruction of §1 to the systematic argument of §2.
3. **§2 "Why Moods?"** (pp.11–24) — opens with the IM 1–2 passage on despair, joy, and boredom as three affective registers in which Leibniz's question "resounds"; argues that this passage encodes Heidegger's abiding thesis that bare moods phenomenally disclose more than what is simply present; returns to BT's *Befindlichkeit*/*Stimmung* architecture (SZ §29, "prior to all psychology of moods," SZ 134); distinguishes anxiety (*Angst*) from fear (*Furcht*) via BT §40 and the 1929 lecture; develops throwness (*Geworfenheit*), falling (*Verfallenheit*)/turning-away as themselves phenomenal evidence (not counter-evidence) of what is evaded; works through anxiety's object as "nothing and nowhere within-the-world" (SZ 186–187); closes with being-towards-death and a concluding citation to BT's own stated aim of "fundamental ontology" (SZ 200).
4. **Notes 1–15** (pp.25–28) — substantive endnotes on Capobianco (n.1), the Leibnizian question's phrasing (n.3), Dahlstrom (2010) on negation and negative facts (nn.5, 8, quoted at length), Käufer (2001) on the historical context of Heidegger's engagement with logical positivism (n.7), and two long citations from Heidegger's Black Notebooks (GA 95) on disposition/*Stimmung* (n.13) and an explicit disavowal of "philosophy of existence" (n.14).
5. **References** (p.28) — five entries: Capobianco (2010), Carnap (1932), Dahlstrom (2010), Heidegger (2017, the Rojcewicz translation of *Ponderings VII–XI*), Kaeufer (2001).

## 3. Constructs

**(a) Boredom construct-axis cross-check (honest, sparse).** This chapter is about anxiety, not boredom, and none of the cluster's controlled boredom constructs receives sustained treatment. The one direct textual point of contact is a single quoted passage: introducing Leibniz's question in the 1935 lecture course, Heidegger describes being touched by the question in great despair, in heartfelt joy, and — closest to this cluster's concern — in a spell of boredom, a mood in which "the stubborn ordinariness of beings lays open a wasteland" (≤12-word direct quotation, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.11, citing IM 1–2) and it becomes a matter of indifference to us whether beings are or are not at all (paraphrase, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.11). O'Brien reproduces this passage to illustrate the general phenomenal-evidentiary role of "bare moods" (FE, p.11); he does not develop boredom as its own topic, does not cite *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (FCM, the 1929/30 lecture course where Heidegger's tripartite boredom analysis actually occurs), and never returns to the word "boredom" again after this single citation. Read narrowly, this passage touches two constructs: **profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)** — the "wasteland" language anticipates FCM's later account of the indifference of world and self, but is not FCM's own apparatus — and **monotony / meaninglessness** — the indifference-to-whether-beings-are-or-are-not idea is a compact, pre-FCM statement of boredom-as-indifference. Neither is elaborated.

**(b) Legitimate analogical link (flagged as our own reading, not O'Brien's argument).** O'Brien's central methodological claim — that anxiety is a *Grundstimmung* (fundamental/bare mood) rather than a directed, psychological feeling, and that it discloses Dasein's being-in-the-world "as a whole" rather than any particular entity (FE, p.13–14) — describes exactly the same genus of phenomenon that FCM's profound boredom instantiates as its own case. Heidegger himself treats anxiety and profound boredom as sibling *Grundstimmungen* across his corpus (both disclose the "nothing," both are said to individualize Dasein, both are contrasted with directed, ontic feelings); O'Brien's chapter, however, never makes this comparison explicit, restricting itself entirely to anxiety. This unit therefore records the anxiety/profound-boredom parallel as a `qualifies` edge tagged **(P/anticipatory-application)** rather than a `bridges-to-fcm` edge, since bridging to a specific FCM unit here would overstate a connection O'Brien's own text never draws.

**(c) The chapter's own affect/mood/emotion vocabulary (full treatment, per the cluster's expanded scope).** Read on its own terms, this chapter is substantially *about* affect: it is the volume's most sustained single treatment of *Befindlichkeit* (state-of-mind/disposition) and *Stimmung* (mood/attunement) as *existentiale* — structural components of Dasein's being, not psychological states (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.15–16, citing SZ 134). O'Brien traces Heidegger's insistence that moods are "prior to all psychology of moods" (SZ 134) and that having a mood "is not related to the psychical in the first instance" (SZ 137); that anxiety (*Angst*) is categorically distinct from fear (*Furcht*) — fear is always fear *of* some particular threatening entity, whereas anxiety is anxious about "nothing in particular" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.13, 22–23, citing SZ 186); and that this distinction is the pivot on which his entire continuity thesis turns, since it is precisely because anxiety discloses Being-in-the-world "as such," rather than any entity within it, that it can function as phenomenal evidence for a fundamental ontology rather than a contribution to psychology, anthropology, or existentialism (FE, p.16–18).

## 4. Reading of FCM

This chapter does **not** engage Heidegger's boredom analysis in *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (GA 29/30) at all. FCM is never cited in the text, the notes, or the five-item reference list. The chapter's textual base is *Being and Time* (cited throughout as "SZ," using Macquarrie & Robinson pagination), the 1929 "What Is Metaphysics?" and its 1940s retrospectives (cited as "PM," from *Pathmarks*), the 1935 *Introduction to Metaphysics* (cited as "IM"), and "The Origin of the Work of Art" (cited as "BW," from *Basic Writings*) — a cluster of texts squarely about Being, Nothingness, and anxiety, not about boredom's three forms, *Zeitvertreib* (passing the time), the train-station or dinner-party examples, or *Gelangweiltsein*.

The single point of textual contact, already flagged in §3 above, is the IM 1–2 passage in which Heidegger names boredom, alongside despair and joy, as one of three moods in which Leibniz's question "resounds" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.11). This passage is itself worth situating precisely: it comes from *Introduction to Metaphysics* (the 1935 lecture course), which is a *different* text from *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (the 1929/30 lecture course this cluster's fcm-04/05/06/07 units are built from), even though the two titles are easily confused. Heidegger's IM passage is a brief, unelaborated gesture — three moods named in one paragraph, with no train-station example, no "held in limbo," no "telling refusal," none of FCM's own apparatus. O'Brien cites it for its general illustration of how "bare moods" register something more than presence, not for any claim about boredom specifically, still less for FCM's tripartite structure.

Accordingly, this unit claims **no fcm-0N bridge**. The honest and accurate statement is that O'Brien's chapter supplies, from within BT/PM/IM rather than FCM, a rigorous, independently-argued account of the *general* structure — *Befindlichkeit* as *existentiale*, fundamental moods as disclosive of Being-in-the-world "as a whole," the priority of ontological over psychological readings of affect — that FCM's own three-forms analysis of boredom instantiates as one specific case among others (anxiety being Heidegger's other paradigmatic case). This is a genus/species relation worth noting analytically (captured via the `qualifies` edge to the profound-boredom construct in the accompanying CSV) but it would misrepresent the chapter to claim O'Brien himself connects it to FCM's boredom material — he never does.

## 5. Findings/Claims

**§1 "Being and Nothing" (pp.1–11).**
1. BT's reception was distorted from the outset by a battery of mutually incompatible misreadings — existentialism, nihilism, anthropology, psychology, humanism, subjectivism — that Heidegger "bemoans... to the end of his life" (FE, p.1).
2. Heidegger's own 1940s retrospectives on "What Is Metaphysics?" describe BT's project as already "setting out on the way to prepare an overcoming of metaphysics" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.2, quoting PM 279) and insist that any attempt to overcome metaphysics "must continue to speak the language of that which they help overcome" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.2, quoting PM 231).
3. Leibniz's question — "why are there beings at all instead of nothing?" — is diagnosed by Heidegger as resting on an unexamined assumption that "being" simply means continuous presence, such that "the Nothing" can be dismissed as "simpler and easier than any thing" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.3–4, quoting PM 190, itself quoting Leibniz).
4. Ordinary talk of absence (a "gap in the hedge," "nothing between those two pieces of hedge") shows that "being" is used of things that are not straightforwardly, continuously present; attempts to dissolve this via first-order existential quantification already presuppose that being means presence, the very assumption in question (FE, p.4–5).
5. Heidegger's chalk example (IM 32) shows that part of what it means for the chalk to *be* the chalk it is includes its possibility of being used up — a possibility that is not "added on" by thought but belongs to the chalk itself; the logician's rendering "∃xCx" cannot capture this possibility-dimension of being (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.5–7, quoting IM 32).
6. Heidegger explicitly targets Carnap and the logical positivists' invocation of the principle of non-contradiction to rule all talk of "the Nothing" illogical and nihilistic (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.5–6, quoting IM 25–26); he counters that logic itself may rest on an unexamined, prior decision about the meaning of "being" that it is not entitled to as a "gift from heaven" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.6–7, quoting IM 27).
7. The traditional strategy of asking after the "ground" of given beings (exemplified by the vineyard-infestation analogy, IM 30) already forecloses the more radical question by assuming beings are unquestionably given in the first place; posing the Nothing forces beings to be "held out... into the possibility of not-Being" instead (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.7–8, quoting IM 30).
8. Husserlian intentionality — illustrated via the lamp, the picture on the wall, and the passing car — shows that ordinary perceptual experience already imaginatively "fills out" what is not bare-presently given (the unseen side of the lampshade, the wall behind the picture, the car's identity as not slowing to turn into the driveway); Heidegger regarded this as one of Husserl's "most important philosophical insights" (FE, p.9–10).
9. "The Origin of the Work of Art" reinforces the same point from the side of hearing: we hear the storm in the chimney, the plane overhead, and "the Mercedes in immediate distinction from the Volkswagen" (≤12-word direct quotation, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.10, quoting BW 151–152) — bare sensation is a derivative abstraction, not what is originally given (paraphrase, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.10).

**§2 "Why Moods?" (pp.11–24).**
10. The IM 1–2 passage on despair, joy, and boredom is read as encoding, in miniature, Heidegger's abiding claim that "bare moods" are constant phenomenal evidence of Dasein's exposure to the interplay of presence and absence, not incidental psychic coloring (FE, p.11–12).
11. In the 1929 lecture, the "founding mode of attunement" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.12, quoting BW 100) both reveals beings as a whole and is itself "the basic occurrence of our Da-sein" (≤12-word direct quotation, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.12, quoting BW 100); Heidegger sharply distinguishes such "bare moods" from directed, thematic feelings caught up with worldly concerns.
12. Anxiety (*Angst*) is defined against fear (*Furcht*): fear is always fear *of* some particular threatening entity, whereas in anxiety things that normally matter to us are "robbed of that same significance" (≤12-word direct quotation, FE, p.13, citing BW 100) and Dasein is anxious about, and before, nothing in particular (paraphrase, FE, p.13).
13. Our habitual absorption in things and "turned-awayness" from anxiety is not counter-evidence against the phenomenal reality of the Nothing it discloses — it is itself phenomenological testament to it (FE, p.13–14).
14. BT already "carefully laid the groundwork" for everything the later texts will say: Heidegger's claim that Dasein's basic state-of-mind is "always in some kind of bare mood" (SZ 131–134) is continuous with, not superseded by, 1929/1935/1940s developments (FE, p.14–15).
15. Moods are explicitly *not* a contribution to psychology or anthropology: Heidegger insists his aim is "one of fundamental ontology" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.15–16, quoting SZ 131), and that moods must be seen "prior to all psychology... as a fundamental existentiale" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.16, quoting SZ 134).
16. A large share of Heidegger misreadings — including the standard misreading of authenticity — stems from failing to see that BT's investigation of Dasein is never meant as a "Dasein-oriented story" but is always in service of the meaning of Being in general (FE, p.17).
17. Throwness (*Geworfenheit*) names the "that it is" of Dasein's being delivered over to its "there," veiled in its whence/whither yet "disclosed in itself all the more unveiledly" (FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.19, quoting SZ 135).
18. Fallenness/turning-away (the everyday evasion of what bare moods disclose) is not evidence against the phenomenal facts of the case but evidence *for* them: Dasein for the most part evades the Being disclosed in the mood, yet "in the evasion itself the 'there' is something disclosed" (≤12-word direct quotation, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.19, quoting SZ 135).
19. Anxiety's "falling"/turning-away is not grounded in fear of entities within the world; rather, this turning-away is "grounded... in anxiety" (≤12-word direct quotation, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.22–23, quoting SZ 186), and anxiety is in turn what first makes fear possible at all (paraphrase, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.22–23) — anxiety is ontologically prior to, and the condition of possibility for, fear.
20. That in the face of which one has anxiety is "nothing and nowhere within-the-world" (≤12-word direct quotation, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.23–24, quoting SZ 186–187): not that the world is absent, but that entities within it become so insignificant that only the world's own worldhood still obtrudes — it is, in short, "the possibility of the ready-to-hand in general" (≤12-word direct quotation, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.24, quoting SZ 187), the world itself (paraphrase, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.23–24).
21. Being-towards-death and the constant "passing away"/"moving towards" of finite existence are the temporal correlate of this analysis: Dasein is never purely present, always thrown, always oriented toward its ultimate possibility of no-longer-being (FE, p.23–24).
22. The chapter closes on BT's own explicit self-description: the analytic of Dasein is not meant to ground an anthropology, but rather "its purpose is one of fundamental ontology" (≤12-word direct quotation, FAITHFUL-O'Brien, p.24, quoting SZ 200) — confirming, in O'Brien's reading, that the whole 1927–1940s trajectory was never anything but this same single project.

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit's dissertation relevance runs chiefly to **Part II**, not Part III. For **Part II** (VR/phantasmatic metaphysics): O'Brien's reconstruction of Heidegger's argument against "the metaphysics of presence" — that being is never reducible to bare, continuous presence, that possibility and absence are constitutive of what anything *is* (the chalk, the lamp, the unseen side of things we nonetheless perceive as whole) — offers Part II a rigorously argued, well-anchored philosophical vocabulary for questioning any account of virtual/mediated presence that quietly assumes "presence" is the unproblematic baseline against which virtuality is then judged deficient or supplementary *(anticipatory-application)*. The Husserlian-intentionality material (the lamp, the picture, the passing car, "we hear the Mercedes in immediate distinction from the Volkswagen") is a ready-made, citable philosophical precedent for Part II's own claims about how perceptual/imaginative "filling-out" already structures ordinary experience prior to any VR-specific argument about immersion — useful for distinguishing genuinely novel VR-phenomenological claims from restatements of a much older phenomenological point *(anticipatory-application)*. The chapter's methodological insistence that *Befindlichkeit*/*Stimmung* are existentiale rather than psychological states is also a useful caution for Part II's own metaphysics-of-affect vocabulary, reinforcing the "genus virtual-object / phantasma as originary" terminology lock by supplying an independent argument for treating structural/ontological categories as prior to occurrent psychological description *(anticipatory-application)*.

For **Part III** (the VR/survey material), this chapter's relevance is genuinely minimal and should not be forced: it contains no boredom-specific analysis, no measures, and no empirical claims, and its single boredom-adjacent sentence is a quoted aside within an argument about anxiety. Its only indirect service to Part III is the general reminder — shared with other Strand A/G units in this cluster — that whatever self-report boredom captures at the level of psychological state, Heidegger's own vocabulary insists that *Befindlichkeit* is never merely psychological; if this cluster's synthesis wants to make a parallel claim about profound boredom specifically, it should be sourced to FCM directly (e.g., via bor-sec-04, bor-sec-13) rather than to this chapter, whose own case is anxiety *(anticipatory-application)*. Finally, O'Brien's polemical continuity thesis — that the "later Heidegger" is not discontinuous with BT — is methodologically relevant wherever this dissertation cites both early- and late-Heidegger material on affect in the same argument without flagging a supposed "turn"; this chapter supplies a citable defense of doing so, at least with respect to Angst and the Nothing *(anticipatory-application)*.
