# bor-sec-54 — Raffoul, "The Ethics of Moods" (2019) — Chapter 11 of 12 in Hadjioannou (ed.), *Heidegger on Affect*

**Type:** chapter in a 12-chapter, 13-author edited collection (Palgrave, "Philosophers in Depth" series) · **Discipline:** philosophy (Heideggerian phenomenology; ethics) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand G. This chapter is authored by **François Raffoul** (Louisiana State University); Christos Hadjioannou is the volume's *editor* only (he separately authored chapter 4 of this same collection) and is never the source of this chapter's claims. A direct check of the extracted text confirms that Raffoul's chapter never once mentions boredom (*Langeweile*) or the three-form boredom analysis of Heidegger's *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (FCM, GA 29/30): the word "boredom" appears nowhere across Raffoul's pages 245–262; it surfaces only in Chapter 12's own introduction (Slaby and Thonhauser), which begins after Raffoul's chapter and its references end. Raffoul instead offers a general phenomenological-ethical account of *Stimmung*/*Befindlichkeit* (mood/disposition) as such, illustrated through homesickness, joy, hope, and elevated/depressed moods, culminating in an argument that being-in-a-mood is itself the site of an original ethical responsibility.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Raffoul argues that Heidegger's account of moods (*Stimmungen*) — usually treated purely ontologically, as the fundamental way Dasein discloses itself and world — harbors an implicit *ethics*: to find oneself in a mood is already to be called to a response, an "original responsibility" (FE, p.247). This ethics-of-moods thesis confronts an immediate objection, which Raffoul states with full force before answering it: moods display a "radical obscurity, opacity, or withdrawal" (FE, p.247) that seems to place them beyond appropriation, beyond both cognition and will, and therefore beyond the reach of ethical responsibility altogether — one is thrown into a mood one did not choose and cannot explain. Raffoul's central move, developed across the chapter's three numbered parts, is to show that this very opacity and expropriation is what *opens* rather than forecloses an ethical relation: because Dasein cannot appropriate the ground of its own being, it is called to take over, or "carry" (p.258), that very inappropriability as a task. The chapter concludes that "the ethics of moods" ultimately names a responsibility "for finitude itself" (FAITHFUL-Raffoul, p.262) — a responsibility modeled not on the Kantian self-originating subject but on a thrown existence that must answer for a facticity it did not cause.

## 2. Structure

The chapter (book pp.245–264) comprises an unnumbered introduction and three numbered parts, followed by a conclusion, four endnotes, and a reference list.

1. **Introduction** (pp.245–248) — restates the ontological status of moods in Heidegger (never merely psychological or "inner"; disclosive of Dasein's being-in-the-world as a whole); moods as what determine thinking itself, illustrated via the 1929/30 course's naming of homesickness as the fundamental mood of philosophizing; states the chapter's aim (an "ethics of moods") and its central paradox (opacity vs. responsibility).
2. **Part I** (pp.248–252) — the ontological character of disposition (*Befindlichkeit*) per BT §29: mood as a mode of primordial self-disclosure prior to, and more originary than, cognition, volition, and reflection; critique of the traditional classification of feeling as a "third," subordinate class of experience beneath reason and will; Heidegger's rejection, via the Hölderlin lectures, of moods as subjective "coloring" of an objective world, and of the term "affect" itself as implying an external impression on a passive subject; moods as neither "inside" nor "outside," constituting rather than merely accompanying Dasein's being.
3. **Part II** (pp.252–257) — restates the paradox in sharper form: moods are "beyond the reach of both will and cognition" (SZ 136), and Dasein "cannot know why" it is in a mood (SZ 134); the motif of mood as *burden* (*Last*), tracked from the 1921–22 Aristotle-interpretation courses' "difficulty of factical life" through *Being and Time*'s account of elevated/depressed moods as alleviating but never escaping this burden; Agamben's etymological reading of facticity (*facticius* vs. *nativus*) as constitutive non-originarity; the question of birth and thrownness (BT §72) as Heidegger's ontological name for natality.
4. **Part III** (pp.257–262) — the chapter's argumentative hinge: Derrida's claim that ethical responsibility arises out of aporia, not despite it; Heidegger's marginal note to SZ 134 explicitly glossing "burden" as the charge of "the responsibility of Dasein" (p.258); thrownness (*Geworfenheit*) as what is felt in a mood; withdrawal itself as an "event" that calls and claims Dasein (via *What Is Called Thinking?*); the analysis of "being-guilty" (*Schuldigsein*) in BT §58 as the taking-over of a thrownness one can never master — the paradox of being a "thrown basis."
5. **Conclusion** (p.262) — moods disclose Dasein's finitude as *Grundlosigkeit* (groundlessness); contrasts Raffoul's model of responsibility (thrown into an unchosen existence one must answer for) with the Kantian model (responsible because one is the self-originating cause of one's acts); final formulation of the ethics of moods as assuming, not overcoming, the "mystery" and "secret" of mood.
6. **Notes and References** (pp.262–264) — four endnotes (a translation note; a terminological note on *Befindlichkeit*; a long block quotation on the traditional psychologizing of feeling, from the 1929/30 course; a Derrida citation) and a short reference list (Agamben 2002, Derrida 2008, Sheehan 2018).

## 3. Constructs

This chapter is a general phenomenological-ethical treatment of mood as such, not a study of any single named mood, and boredom is not among its worked examples — those are homesickness (p.247, the fundamental mood of philosophizing), joy (p.251, via the critique of "affect"), hope (p.256, via SZ 345 on elevated moods remaining related to a burden), and unnamed "moods of elation" contrasted with depressive/burdened moods (pp.255–256). None of the cluster's eleven controlled boredom constructs receives direct treatment. Two receive an honest, clearly-flagged analogical purchase (P/anticipatory-application, not Raffoul's own claim). First, **monotony / meaninglessness**: Raffoul's extended discussion of mood as *burden* (*Last*) and of "the difficulty of factical life" (GA 61:108, p.255) — factical life's constant tendency "to make itself easy for itself" against its own constitutive heaviness — describes a structure of existential weight that the boredom literature elsewhere in this cluster (e.g., Hughes on meaninglessness and monotony) treats as boredom's affective core, though Raffoul never uses the word "boredom" to name it. Second, **temporality of boredom (time-drag)**: Raffoul's brief remark that elevated moods are possible only in an "ecstatic-temporal relation" of Dasein "to the thrown ground of itself" (SZ 345, p.256) gestures at the temporal structure of mood in general without ever specifying boredom's characteristic time-drag. Third, and most speculatively, **profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)** receives only a chapter-level structural resonance, discussed fully in §4 below. All other eight constructs — being-bored-by, being-bored-with, situational vs. trait/state boredom, boredom proneness, mind-wandering/task-unrelated thought, attentional disengagement/meta-awareness, boredom-as-regulatory-signal, and flow/engagement — receive no treatment whatsoever, direct or oblique.

## 4. Reading of FCM

Raffoul draws repeatedly on Heidegger's 1929/30 lecture course *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (GA 29/30) — but exclusively on its opening methodological material (the course's introductory sections on philosophizing and being-gripped, corresponding to roughly FCM pp.1–90 of the English translation), which frames philosophizing as arising out of a fundamental attunement (*Grundstimmung*) and names *homesickness* as that attunement (FE, p.247, citing GA 29/30:9–10; FCM 7). He also cites FCM's general phenomenology of attunement: moods as "the 'presupposition' for, and 'medium' of thinking and acting" (GA 29/30:102; FCM 68, p.248), and as pervading being-with prior to any reflection (GA 29/30:100, 306; FCM 66–67, 209, pp.251–252, 262). None of this touches Part One's dedicated boredom analysis (FCM §§19–38, the three-form analysis of superficial, self-conscious, and profound boredom), which occupies a much later stretch of the same lecture course than every passage Raffoul actually cites. The honest and accurate claim is therefore that this chapter engages FCM only at the level of its general theory of *Grundstimmung* and the disposing of thinking — material that, within the same lecture course, sets up but does not itself reach the boredom analysis that other units in this cluster (the Elpidorou & Freeman pieces, Gibbs, Zabalo, Mihačević, Quaranta) engage directly. Accordingly, this unit claims a single, deliberately weak, explicitly flagged bridge to **fcm-06-third-form**: not because Raffoul discusses profound boredom, but because the very *Grundstimmung* theory he expounds from GA 29/30's opening is the theoretical scaffolding on which FCM's own third-form analysis is later built within that same course — a textual-contiguity bridge, not a content bridge, labeled P/anticipatory-application throughout this unit's json/csv.

## 5. Findings/Claims

**Introduction (pp.245–248).**
- Moods are not psychological add-ons but disclose "an ontological truth of Dasein" (FE, p.245); "Dasein is always already in a mood" (SZ 134, p.245).
- Heidegger seeks to remove moods from psychology's purview entirely, insisting the phenomenon must be grasped as "a fundamental existential" prior to any psychology of moods (paraphrase of SZ 134, p.245–246).
- Moods determine thinking itself: philosophical concepts must be "newly extracted each time... out of the basic disposition" (GA 65:21; CP 19, p.246); thinking that lacks a *Grundstimmung* collapses into "a forced clatter of concepts" (p.246).
- The 1929/30 course names *homesickness* (*das Heimweh*) as the fundamental mood of philosophizing; philosophizing is "comprehensive questioning arising out of Dasein's being gripped" (GA 29/30:199; FCM 132, p.247), and such being-gripped is possible only from within a *Grundstimmung*.
- Raffoul's own thesis: "to be in a mood, to be thrown in a mood" already "engages... an ethical relation" (FAITHFUL-Raffoul, p.247); this thesis must confront the objection that moods' opacity forecloses appropriation (pp.247–248).

**Part I (pp.248–252).**
- *Befindlichkeit* ("disposition") is the ontological characterization of the ontical phenomenon "mood" (SZ 134, p.248); disposition discloses Dasein to itself "prior to all cognition and volition, and beyond their range of disclosure" (SZ 136, p.249).
- Disposition is not something arrived at by reflection turning back on itself; rather, it is what first makes any subsequent reflection on inner "experiences" possible at all (SZ 136, paraphrase, p.249).
- Every comportment is always already in some attunement beforehand (GA 89:210; Zo 165, pp.249–250); attunement (*Gestimmtheit*) simply *is* relationality, "being related" (*Bezogensein*), each new attunement only a re-attunement of one already unfolding (GA 89:251; Zo 203, p.250).
- Raffoul rejects the traditional schema that ranks feeling as a subordinate "third class" of experience beneath thinking and willing (p.250) and, following Heidegger's Hölderlin lectures, rejects the picture of moods as subjective "coloring" added onto an already-constituted objective world (GA 39:83; HGR 76, p.251).
- Heidegger explicitly rejects the very term "affect": "Af-ficere means 'to do something to someone'" — yet "joy is not brought upon me from the outside" (GA 89:211; Zo 166, p.251); moods belong to Dasein's own ecstatic relationship to world, not to an external causal impression upon a subject.
- Moods are neither "inside" nor "outside": "a mood assails us... [and] arises out of Being-in-the-world" (SZ 136, pp.251–252); far from an inconstant accompaniment, mood is what "gives Dasein subsistence... in its very foundations" (GA 29/30:101; FCM 67, p.252).

**Part II (pp.252–257).**
- Restates the paradox in its sharpest form: how can Dasein be responsible for a phenomenon "of which I am not the author" (p.253)?
- Moods lead Dasein before the pure "that" of its There, which "stares at it with the inexorability of an enigma" (SZ 136, p.253); "one does not know why," and cognition "falls far short" not from any remediable weakness but because "the whence and whither remain obscure" (SZ 134, paraphrase, pp.253–254).
- Only a "counter-mood" can master a mood — an insight Raffoul credits to Spinoza (p.254) — and even such mastery never frees Dasein of mood as such (SZ 136, p.254).
- The motif of *burden* (*Last*): "Being has become manifest as a burden" (SZ 134, p.254); elevated or "alleviating" moods do not escape this burden but presuppose and remain related to it — hope, for instance, "brings relief from depressing apprehensiveness" yet "remains related to a burden in the mode of having-been" (SZ 345, p.256).
- Tracing this burden back to Heidegger's 1921–22 Aristotle-interpretation courses: factical life is constitutively "hard to bear," and its "tendency to make itself easy for itself" (GA 61:100–108; PIA 75–81, p.255) is precisely a flight from, not a refutation of, this weight.
- Agamben's "Passion of Facticity" is enlisted to unpack facticity's etymology: *facticius* (made, produced — hence non-original) as opposed to *nativus* (natural, given by birth); on this reading Dasein can never "appropriate the being it is" (Agamben 2002, 100, quoted p.256), a being to which it remains irreparably consigned (paraphrase).
- The question of birth: thrownness is Heidegger's ontological name for natality, addressed explicitly at BT §72's "Being-towards-the-beginning" (*Sein zum Anfang*, SZ 373, pp.256–257); Dasein exists as *born*, yet cannot appropriate its own birth — it can never get "behind its thrownness" to release its own "that-it-is-and-has-to-be" (SZ 284, p.257).

**Part III (pp.257–262), the chapter's hinge.**
- Derrida's claim, endorsed by Raffoul, that ethical responsibility arises constitutively *from* aporia and paradox, not despite it (Derrida 2008, 68, p.257).
- Even what is not under Dasein's own decision "must be in such or such a way retrievingly appropriated" — even if only by "putting up with or shirking" it (GA 27:337, paraphrase, pp.257–258).
- The pivotal textual discovery: Heidegger's own marginal note to SZ 134 glosses "burden" (*Last*) as "das Zu-tragende" — what has to be carried — and states that the human being is "charged with the responsibility of Dasein" (SZ 134, marginal note, quoted p.258), appropriated by it; to carry (*tragen*) is "to take over one's belonging to being itself" (p.258). This note is Raffoul's textual anchor for the entire thesis: the weight of moods just *is* an original responsibility.
- Thrownness (*Geworfenheit*) names what is felt, precisely, in a mood: as Sheehan puts it (cited by Raffoul), "thrownness bespeaks *Befindlichkeit*, the a priori fact that we are affectively attuned" (Sheehan 2018, 45, p.258).
- Withdrawal is not a mere absence but "an event" in its own right — what withdraws "may even concern and claim man more essentially" than anything present (GA 8:10; WCT 9, p.260) — and it is this event of withdrawal, not any positive presence, that calls Dasein to responsibility.
- Agamben, again, distinguishes Heidegger's *Faktizität* sharply from Husserl's *Tatsächlichkeit* (the mere contingent factuality of intraworldly objects): for Heidegger "the proper trait of facticity is not *Zufälligkeit* but *Verfallenheit*" (Agamben 2002, 92, quoted p.260), and Dasein, unlike Sartre's subject thrown into a merely contingent "there," must itself *be* its "there."
- The analysis culminates in *Schuldigsein* (being-guilty): Dasein "has been brought into its 'there,' but not of its own accord" (SZ 284, pp.260–261) and can never get its own basis "into its power" (SZ 284, p.261) — yet "as existing, it must take over Being-a-basis" (SZ 284, p.261). This is the "paradox of being a 'thrown basis'" (p.261): having to make oneself the author of a facticity of which one is not, and cannot be, the author.

**Conclusion (p.262).**
- Moods disclose Dasein's finitude as *Grundlosigkeit* (groundlessness) and *Grund-verborgenheit* (concealment of the ground) (GA 29/30:306; FCM 209, p.262).
- Raffoul explicitly contrasts his model of responsibility with Kant's: I am not responsible because I am "the absolute origin of a series of causes" to whom actions may be ascribed, but because I am thrown into an existence "I do not originate yet... have to answer" for (FAITHFUL-Raffoul, p.262).
- Final formulation: the ethics of moods is "a responsibility for finitude itself" (FAITHFUL-Raffoul, p.262), a being-responsible that consists not in overcoming moods but in "assuming their mystery, of respecting their secret" (FAITHFUL-Raffoul, p.262) — being their enigma.

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit's primary dissertation relevance runs to Part II, with a secondary, honestly-qualified relevance to Part III. For **Part II** (VR/phantasmatic metaphysics and the affect-general register the cluster now tracks independently of boredom): Raffoul's chapter supplies a rigorously worked argument that *disposition is prior to reflection* — that Dasein "comes across ... 'Experiences' [*Erlebnisse*]" (SZ 136, p.249) only on the basis of a mood already disclosing world — which offers Part II a precise philosophical vocabulary for describing how a virtual environment's affective "atmosphere" precedes and conditions any subsequent reflective or evaluative stance a player/participant takes up within it *(anticipatory-application)*. Raffoul's insistence that Heidegger rejects the very term "affect" (*Affizierung*, an external impression upon a passive subject, p.251) is also a useful conceptual caution for any Part II passage that risks casually using "affect" or "affective response" in the causal-impression sense Heidegger explicitly repudiates; the dissertation's own preferred vocabulary of disclosure/attunement tracks Raffoul's, not the impression-model, more closely. For **Part III** (the VR/survey material): this chapter contains no boredom content and no measures, and its relevance should not be forced. Its one legitimate service is methodological rather than substantive: Raffoul's demonstration that Heidegger *deliberately excludes* the "affect" model — mood as an inner state impressed upon a subject "from the outside" (p.251) and then merely reported — offers a philosophical caveat for Part III's reliance on self-report trait/state **boredom proneness** measures, which by design ask a subject to report an inner, occurrent affective state as if it were exactly such an impression. Naming this caveat does not impugn the survey's empirical utility; it flags a categorial mismatch worth one sentence of methodological reflexivity when Part III's findings are framed against the dissertation's Heideggerian register *(anticipatory-application)*. Beyond this, the chapter's account of *Schuldigsein* — taking over a thrownness one did not choose and cannot master — resonates, at the level of pure analogy only, with a participant's or a researcher's own unchosen disposedness toward the study itself (fatigue, mood-on-the-day, institutional context), a resonance this unit flags but does not develop, since developing it would exceed what Raffoul's text itself licenses *(anticipatory-application)*.
