# bor-sec-53 — Tömmel, "Love as Passion: Epistemic and Existential Aspects of Heidegger's Unknown Concept" (2019) — Chapter 10 of 12 in Hadjioannou (ed.), *Heidegger on Affect*

**Type:** chapter in a 12-chapter, 13-author edited collection (Palgrave, "Philosophers in Depth" series) · **Discipline:** philosophy (Heideggerian phenomenology; intellectual biography) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand G — this cluster covers all 12 chapters of the volume for their own account of affect/mood/emotion, independent of boredom-specific relevance. This chapter is authored by **Tatjana Noemi Tömmel** (Berlin Technical University); Christos Hadjioannou is the volume's *editor* only and is never the source of this chapter's claims. Tömmel's chapter contains exactly one explicit mention of "boredom" — a brief, negative/contrastive aside — and never cites Heidegger's *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (FCM) or its 1929/30 lecture course at all; its substantive contribution lies elsewhere, in reconstructing an almost entirely neglected concept — Heidegger's theory of love (*Liebe*) — from published texts, lecture courses, and above all private correspondence with his wife Elfride, with Hannah Arendt, and with Elisabeth Blochmann.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Tömmel opens by confronting the 2014–2015 publication of Heidegger's "Black Notebooks" and the resulting confirmation of his antisemitism, treating this directly as a reason a chapter on so "unpolitical" a topic as love needs justifying at all (FE, p.227). She offers two justifications for continuing to study Heidegger regardless — his outsized influence on twentieth- and twenty-first-century philosophy, and the possibility that his early thought is not yet compromised by the "curious inability to think" of his later politics (FE, p.227-228) — and then states the chapter's aim: to reconstruct Heidegger's almost unknown concept of love (FE, p.228).

Against the "universally accepted truth" that Heidegger never wrote about love — a consensus Tömmel attributes to George Pattison's claim that the topic is "systematically excluded" from Heidegger's fundamental ontology (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.229, quoting Pattison 2006, 171) — Tömmel argues that a careful reading across Heidegger's published work, lecture courses, notes, and correspondence shows love playing a major, cross-linked role in the development of his thought, and in his private letters becoming nothing less than "the leitmotiv of his philosophical thinking" (FE, p.229). Only Jacques Derrida and Giorgio Agamben, she notes, had previously challenged the exclusion-consensus (FE, p.229).

The chapter restricts itself to two of three possible aspects of Heidegger's concept of love: the **epistemic** function (love's role in enabling cognition and phenomenological understanding, in his earliest writings) and the **existential** function (love as an alternative route to authentic selfhood, *Eigentlichkeit*, in his Freiburg and Marburg years) (FE, p.229). A third aspect — love as the interpersonal, social "will to the other's true self," which Heidegger repeatedly defines as *volo ut sis*, "I want you to be what you are" (to Hannah Arendt, 1925), and, thirty years later, as a deeper "letting-be [*das Sein-lassen*]" that "calls forth the essence" (Heidegger 1960, 19; FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.229) — is explicitly bracketed, since the volume's brief is "Heidegger on affects," not "Heidegger on *Mitsein*," and Tömmel refers readers to her own monograph for that dimension (FE, p.229-230, citing Tömmel 2013, 118-144).

## 2. Structure

The chapter (book pp.227-244) has an unheaded introduction, four numbered sections, five endnotes, and a reference list.

1. **Unheaded introduction** (pp.227-228) — the Black Notebooks and the question of whether Heidegger scholarship is still justifiable; the chapter's stated aim.
2. **§1 "Heidegger on Love"** (pp.228-230) — the exclusion-consensus and its two challengers (Derrida, Agamben); the range of sources drawn on (published work, lectures, drafts, notes, letters, poems, diaries); statement of the two-part epistemic/existential structure; the bracketed third, interpersonal aspect.
3. **§2 "From Logic to the Logic of the Heart: Heidegger's Early Works"** (pp.230-233) — Heidegger's 1912-1921 writings; the habilitation's turn from logic to a "Pascalian logic of the heart"; Max Scheler's *Liebe und Erkenntnis* (1916) as decisive influence (per Agamben's prior identification); Plato, Augustine, and Eckhart as further sources; love/*eros*/*Leidenschaft* as the "motivational foundation" of phenomenological understanding; the transition into *Being and Time*'s theory of *Befindlichkeit* as disclosive of world; the BT footnote quoting Pascal and Augustine on loving before knowing; later echoes in "What Is Called Thinking?" and the "Letter on 'Humanism.'"
4. **§3 "Love as an Alternative Path to *Eigentlichkeit*"** (pp.233-241) — the apparent conflict between *Eigentlichkeit* (reached, in BT, via withdrawal from others) and being-with-others; the 1925 wedding speech for Heidegger's brother as evidence that BT is not simply asocial; the reconstruction, from letters to Arendt and Blochmann, of love as a second, non-self-centered path to authentic selfhood; the "true break-out" of love as a kairotic event structurally parallel to being-towards-death; the derivation of this "kairologic" structure from Heidegger's 1919-1921 engagement with Pauline messianism; the temporality of love as awaiting and holding-out rather than lived duration; love's characteristic "shyness" (*Scheu*) toward the beloved, who remains present precisely by remaining distant; the passion/affect distinction from the Nietzsche lectures; and a rebuttal of Thomas Rentsch's claim that "positive" moods cannot disclose finitude as well as "negative" ones.
5. **§4 "Conclusion"** (pp.241-242) — the unifying claim that love is essentially linked to truth across all periods of Heidegger's thought; a response to Karl Jaspers' judgment that Heidegger's philosophy was "without love"; two closing reservations about whether this discovery makes Heidegger's thought more "amiable" (it does not).
6. **Notes 1-5** (pp.242-243) — cross-references to Tömmel's own 2013 monograph; a German-original citation; a note on Heidegger's later use of the term "*Logik des Herzens*."
7. **References** (pp.243-244).

## 3. Constructs

This chapter is not about boredom, and of the cluster's eleven controlled boredom constructs, only one receives direct textual treatment, and only in a single, brief, negative register; a second construct is treated purely analogically, as our own extension rather than the chapter's claim.

- **monotony / meaninglessness** — the only direct textual contact with the construct axis. Discussing why the absent beloved's absence is not experienced as deficit in Heidegger's messianic concept of love, Tömmel observes that this concept "knows nothing about... boredom and aversion," naming "being ephemeral and being routine" as the "classical enemies of love" (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.240). Boredom (and its cognate, "aversion") and routine/monotony are named, together, as the two things Heidegger's kairological, eternity-deferring concept of love is structurally immune to — not analyzed as phenomena in their own right, but invoked as the foil against which the *exceptional* character of love's "true break-out" is defined.
- **temporality of boredom (time-drag)** — not treated by the chapter itself, but a legitimate analogical construct link is available (P/anticipatory-application): the entire second half of §3 is a sustained analysis of love's temporal structure as *kairos* (the "moment of vision," *Augenblick*) set against *chronos* (ordinary, dragging, successive time) — a structure Tömmel explicitly argues is not mere duration awaited in the future but a rupture that is, in a sense, outside time altogether (FE, p.235-240). Since boredom's phenomenology is conventionally characterized by exactly the opposite temporal structure — the subjective dilation and dragging of chronological time, the absence of any kairotic rupture — this chapter's positive account of love's temporality functions, without saying so, as boredom's structural photographic negative. This is our own bridge, not a claim Tömmel makes.
- All remaining nine constructs — profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form), being-bored-by (1st form), being-bored-with (2nd form), situational vs. trait/state boredom, boredom proneness, mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought, attentional disengagement / meta-awareness, boredom-as-regulatory-signal, and flow / engagement (contrast pole) — receive no treatment, direct or oblique. The chapter's subject is love, not attention, engagement, or any occurrent affective state along those axes.

## 4. Reading of FCM

**Not applicable, and honestly so.** This chapter does not engage Heidegger's boredom analysis in *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (GA 29/30) in any way. FCM does not appear in the chapter's reference list (which runs from Agamben 2008 to von Falkenhayn 2003), and no citation format consistent with FCM (e.g., "GA 29/30," "FCM," or a citation to the three-form boredom analysis) occurs anywhere in the main text or the five endnotes. The chapter's Heidegger citations are instead drawn from the early Freiburg lecture courses (GA 1, 16, 58, 60, 61), *Being and Time* (SZ), the Nietzsche lectures (GA 6.1), "Letter on 'Humanism'" (GA 9), "What Is Called Thinking?" (GA 8), and private correspondence (GA 16, GA 75, GA 81, and the published letter-volumes with Arendt and Blochmann) — none of which is the 1929/30 boredom lecture course.

The single explicit use of the word "boredom" in the chapter (§3, p.240, discussed above under Constructs) is not a reading of FCM at all: it functions as an incidental foil-term within an argument about love's relation to absence and eternity, not as an engagement with Heidegger's own analysis of *Langeweile*. No fcm-0N bridge is claimed for this unit. Per the cluster's own instruction to check honestly rather than force a connection, this chapter should be read as confirming — by simple absence — that Heidegger's small but philologically real theory of love and his extended analysis of boredom belong to two largely non-intersecting regions of his corpus, joined (if at all) only at the very general level of a shared theory of mood/*Befindlichkeit* as disclosive, which this chapter touches only in passing (§2, discussed below) and never develops in boredom's direction.

## 5. Findings/Claims

**Epistemic aspect (§2).**
- C1. Heidegger's earliest writings (1912-1921) show a young, at-first fervent defender of pure logic who comes to doubt that logic alone can grasp truth, turning during his habilitation (1916) toward phenomenology, hermeneutics, axiology, and medieval mysticism, and toward "personal experience" as the ground of metaphysical inquiry (FE, p.230-231, citing GA 1: 191).
- C2. The last chapter of the 1916 habilitation marks Heidegger's transition from logic to a Pascalian "logic of the heart"; Heidegger describes medieval thinking as "absolute devotion" (*Hingabe*) and "immersion" into subject matter, achievable only through a "conforming open-mindedness of empathic understanding" (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.231, citing GA 1: 198, 408).
- C3. Giorgio Agamben was the first to identify Max Scheler's 1916 essay "*Liebe und Erkenntnis*" as the decisive influence on Heidegger's linking of love and cognition; Scheler held love to be the "uniting source of consciousness" underlying all cognition, perception, and willing, and Heidegger's 1921 Augustine lecture likewise treats love as a higher form of cognition set against the "dissociating sciences" (FE, p.231, citing Agamben 2008; Scheler 1963, 77-98, 94, 97).
- C4. In lecture notes from 1919-1920, Heidegger names love the "motivational foundation [*Motivgrund*]" of phenomenological understanding, given "in its enactmental sense [*Vollzugssinn*]," and elsewhere writes that "in love there is understanding" — devotion oriented not to facts but to meaning (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.231-232, quoting GA 58: 185, 168).
- C5. Heidegger criticizes Plato for treating ἔρως as a mere motivator toward philosophy rather than as philosophy's essence; the true philosophical attitude is not the "tyrannic stare" of logic but a "self-releasement into life," into its "originality" (*Ursprünglichkeit*), which Heidegger will later, with Eckhart, call *Gelassenheit* or "letting-be" (*Seinlassen*) — a position Tömmel argues he had already reached in 1916, well before his so-called "turn" (FE, p.232, citing GA 58: 263).
- C6. This early epistemic theory of love is, on Tömmel's reading, the direct "prologue" to *Being and Time*'s theory of moods as disclosive of world: Dasein's openness is constituted by the "attunement of a state-of-mind [*Befindlichkeit*]" (SZ 137), such that there is "no such thing as 'pure beholding'" (SZ 138) (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.232-233, quoting SZ 137-139).
- C7. Heidegger mentions "love" directly in a *Being and Time* footnote (SZ 139n1), quoting Pascal's maxim that the saints "enter into truth only by charity," and Augustine's parallel claim that truth is entered "except through charity" — thereby explicitly linking love and truth within his major published work, not only in private writing (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.233, quoting SZ 139n1, translating Pascal and Augustine).
- C8. This conviction persists into Heidegger's later work: in "What Is Called Thinking?" he plays on "*Vermögen*" (ability) and "*mögen*" (to like) to argue that thinking is only possible if one "likes" (loves) one's object of thought, Being; elsewhere he identifies thinking and loving outright and describes the relation between humanity and Being as one of mutual love (FE, p.233, citing GA 8: 5; GA 6.1: 197f; GA 9: 316; GA 75: 363).

**Existential aspect (§3).**
- C9. *Eigentlichkeit* (authenticity/being-oneself) is Heidegger's best-known Marburg-period achievement, but its pairing with existential isolation — authenticity reached by withdrawal from others, not encounter with them — creates an apparent conflict with *Being-with* (*Mitsein*), leading some (Großmann 1998) to doubt whether Heidegger's "methodological solipsism" leaves room for authentic sociality at all (FE, p.233-234).
- C10. Tömmel argues this doubt is unjustified, citing a 1925 wedding speech Heidegger gave for his brother Fritz: marriage is founded on love, and "true love" does not ground in "external properties, relations and circumstances" but grows out of "an inner truthfulness towards oneself," which alone makes truthfulness toward the other possible (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.234, quoting GA 16: 52) — a claim she reads as fully consistent with BT: because Dasein must first find itself in being-towards-death, it can then be with others in an entirely new way.
- C11. Analysis of Heidegger's private correspondence (chiefly with Hannah Arendt, 1925, and Elisabeth Blochmann, 1918-19) reveals an *alternative* path to authenticity, alongside BT's self-centered route: the encounter with the beloved as itself a source of true selfhood (FE, p.234).
- C12. Tömmel's central interpretive claim: being-in-love and being-towards-death share the same "kairologic structure," which Heidegger developed through his 1919 engagement with the messianism of St. Paul and subsequently transferred to his own philosophy (FE, p.234-235).
- C13. Love's beginning is described, in the letters, as a "true break-out" that transforms existence for good — decades before "event" (*Ereignis*) becomes central to Heidegger's thinking: the other's presence "suddenly breaks into our life," an intrusion "no soul can come to terms with" (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.235, quoting Arendt and Heidegger 2004, 5). This "moment of vision" (*Augenblick*) breaks with the linearity and homogeneity of time; Tömmel connects it to Paul Tillich's contemporaneous concept of *kairos* as the "irruption of eternity into time" (FE, p.235, quoting Tillich 1926, 35).
- C14. Love, like anxiety and death in BT, functions as a *principium individuationis*: "for everything else" — every other human undertaking — there are "conventions, help, and limitations," but love alone means being "forced into one's innermost existence" (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.236, quoting Arendt and Heidegger 2004, 21). Unlike BT's anxiety, however, this individuation happens through relation to another Dasein, not withdrawal from all others: "oneself becomes strong in love," in "a giving that is not a giving away" (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.236, quoting Heidegger 2005, 112).
- C15. Heidegger's later distinction (in the Nietzsche lectures) between "passion" (*Leidenschaft*) and "affect" (*Affekt*) clarifies why receptivity does not undermine authenticity: affects like joy or wrath "affect" us so that we lose ourselves, whereas a genuine passion like love or hate is the "break-out" of something already nurtured within, rooted in *pathos*/*paschein* — to suffer, endure, be determined by (FE, p.236-237, citing GA 6.1: 42-44; Heidegger 1963, 39f).
- C16. Reconciling love with BT's analysis of Dasein as burdened: love, like the moods BT describes as disclosing "the burdensome character of Dasein" (SZ 134), is a "sweet burden" the lover cannot divest herself of; the lover, in relating to what she is not, comes to herself for the first time, an intensification rather than an annihilation of identity (FE, p.237-238, quoting Arendt and Heidegger 2004, 4f).
- C17. Love's temporality is not lived duration together but repetition — via awaiting and "holding-out" (*Durchhalten*) — of the founding "moment of vision"; drawing on Kierkegaard's concept of repetition (which BT itself defines as authentic having-been, SZ 385), Tömmel argues love's repetition, unlike BT's, takes place entirely within the immanence of Dasein rather than as shared temporal life (FE, p.238-239, quoting Heidegger 2005, 99; citing SZ 385).
- C18. This temporal structure is revealed to be messianic: the lover's condition mirrors the believer's relation to Christ's return — the "returning unification" Heidegger speaks of does not lie ahead in future chronological time but beyond time altogether, in eternity (FE, p.239, quoting Arendt and Heidegger 2004, 18-19).
- C19. Presence-in-absence is achieved via "shyness" (*Scheu*), a "reserved, patient, astonished remembrance" that keeps the beloved near by "keeping something distant in its fullness" (FAITHFUL-Tömmel, p.240, quoting EHP 153); true unification occurs when this shyness "is heightened," not dissolved (FE, p.240, quoting Arendt and Heidegger 2004, 23).
- C20. Tömmel explicitly rebuts Thomas Rentsch's claim that "positive" moods like joy or love cannot disclose the finite structure of existence as well as "negative" moods can: her analysis shows Heidegger's love is not positive in any ordinary sense but "a radical loneliness," structurally producing the same individuating effect as anxiety (FE, p.241, citing Rentsch 2013, 73).

**Conclusion (§4).**
- C21. What unifies the epistemic and existential aspects, across all periods of Heidegger's thought, is a single underlying claim: love is essentially linked to truth — to cognition and understanding in the earliest texts, to *Eigentlichkeit* in the 1920s, and to the relation to Being in the later period (FE, p.241-242).
- C22. Against Karl Jaspers' verdict that Heidegger's philosophy was "without love," Tömmel judges this "philologically" wrong: the sources demonstrate a highly sophisticated concept of love, one on which Heidegger even identifies philosophy itself with love (FE, p.242, citing Jaspers 1978, 34).
- C23. Tömmel closes with two reservations. First, modeling intimate relationships on Pauline messianism is a "strange choice" that both sacralizes and marginalizes the beloved other, who becomes merely an occasion for the lover's return to self — evidence, again, of Heidegger's persistent inattentiveness to interaction, communication, and community. Second, Heidegger's repeated identification of thinking/philosophy with love reads as "almost uncanny" given the Black Notebooks: in that period his thinking was driven not by love but "by another genuine passion, by hate" (FE, p.242).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This chapter's relevance to Part III is genuinely distant — arguably the most distant unit built so far in this cluster — and should not be forced. It contains no boredom construct beyond a single incidental mention, no FCM engagement, and no empirical content of any kind. What it does offer is conceptual, at one remove.

First, Tömmel's reconstruction of love's *kairological* temporality (an intensified "moment of vision," repeated through awaiting rather than lived through as duration) gives Part III a precise philosophical vocabulary for the *opposite* pole of the boredom construct's characteristic time-drag: where boredom phenomenologically dilates and empties chronological time, Heidegger's love (on Tömmel's reading) collapses or suspends chronological time altogether in favor of a repeated, intensified instant *(anticipatory-application)*. If Part III's survey instrument or its qualitative interview protocol ever needs language for describing a participant's subjective experience of session-time as compressed, absorbing, or "outside the clock" — the affective opposite of the boredom items — this chapter's *Augenblick*/*chronos* contrast is a more precisely worked-out source than the more commonly cited flow literature, because it is argued from within the same Heideggerian mood-theoretic framework FCM itself uses *(anticipatory-application)*.

Second, the chapter's insistence that even a chapter-length treatment of one specific affect (love) requires distinguishing an epistemic register (what the affect discloses about the world/object) from an existential register (what the affect does to the discloser's own selfhood) is a useful general caution for how "engagement" or "immersion" get operationalized in VR-vs-2D survey items: a single self-report item asking whether a participant "felt engaged" risks collapsing exactly the two registers Tömmel spends this entire chapter insisting must be held apart *(anticipatory-application)*. This is not a claim about boredom specifically but about affect-construct validity generally — directly relevant to this cluster's stated purpose of capturing "affect" as a dissertation concept independent of boredom.

Third, and more cautionary than constructive: the chapter's own closing move — using Heidegger's account of a "positive" affect (love) to rebut Rentsch's claim that only "negative" moods disclose finitude — is a reminder, worth carrying into Part III's discussion of engagement/flow as boredom's contrast pole, that valence (positive/negative) and disclosive depth or individuating force are not the same axis; a phenomenologically "positive" state (love, and by extension flow or absorbed engagement) can be just as radically individuating, and just as poor a candidate for casual self-report capture, as a "negative" one like boredom or anxiety *(anticipatory-application)*.
