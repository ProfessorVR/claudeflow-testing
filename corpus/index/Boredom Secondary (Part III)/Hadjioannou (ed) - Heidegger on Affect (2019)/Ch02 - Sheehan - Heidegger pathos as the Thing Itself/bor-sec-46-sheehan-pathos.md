# bor-sec-46 — Sheehan, "Heidegger: πάθος as the Thing Itself" (2019) — Chapter 2 of 12 in Hadjioannou (ed.), *Heidegger on Affect*

**Type:** chapter in a 12-chapter, 13-author edited collection (Palgrave, "Philosophers in Depth" series) · **Discipline:** philosophy (Heideggerian phenomenology; ancient Greek philology) · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand G, first unit — this cluster now covers all 12 chapters of the volume for their own account of affect/mood/emotion, independent of boredom. This chapter is authored by **Thomas Sheehan** (Stanford University); Christos Hadjioannou is the volume's *editor* only (he separately authored chapter 4) and is never the source of this chapter's claims. Sheehan's chapter never mentions boredom (*Langeweile*) or Heidegger's *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (FCM) lecture course; it engages the shared *Stimmung*/*Befindlichkeit* architecture only at the most general level, so no FCM-specific bridge is claimed. Its chief interest for this dissertation lies elsewhere: it reconstructs, book-length-essay style, why Heidegger's entire project turns on πάθος — read not as emotion but as facticity — making it the single most sustained engagement with the Greek term *pathos* found in this cluster to date, and one whose Aristotelian sourcing (*De Interpretatione*, *De Anima*, *Metaphysics* IX) runs parallel to, but does not overlap with, the rhetorical *pathē* of *Rhetoric* II central to Part I.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Sheehan argues that πάθος — read correctly, i.e., de-psychologized — is *die Sache selbst*, "the thing itself," of Heidegger's whole philosophical project (FE, p.31). The argument runs in four steps, explicitly announced at the outset (FE, p.31): (1) fix the actual goal of Heidegger's *Seinsfrage*, which is not "being" but intelligibility (*Anwesen*) and its "whence" (*Herkunft*); (2) work out what "transcendence" and "intentionality" mean once intentionality is understood as transcendence-based "minding"; (3) an interlude sketching Heidegger's phenomenological transformation of Aristotle's first philosophy; (4) show that facticity, read as πάθος, is what Sheehan calls the "factum" — the single, unitary, always-already-operative bond of ex-sistence and intelligibility that is the ultimate presupposition of everything human. The chapter is simultaneously a terminological history: it tracks one and the same "factum" as it is renamed across Heidegger's career — *In-der-Welt-sein*, *Gegenschwung*, *Ereignis*, *Seyn*, *Bestimmt-sein*, *Bezug* — arguing that all of these are place-holders for what, in the 1955 lecture "What Is Philosophy?," Heidegger and Plato's Theaetetus jointly identify as πάθος.

## 2. Structure

The chapter (book pp.29–46) comprises an unnumbered introduction, four numbered sections, extensive endnotes, and a short reference list.

1. **Introduction** (pp.29–31) — sense-making (*Sinn*, *Bedeutsamkeit*) as an inescapable, constitutive feature of ex-sistence, illustrated via Sartre/Roquentin; the claim that Heidegger's "Sein" question is really a question about intelligibility; announcement of the four-step argument.
2. **§1 "The Goal"** (pp.31–33) — *Sein* as provisional stand-in for *Anwesen*; the "factum" as the single bond of ex-sistence and intelligibility; polemic against hypostasizing "Being Itself" into a self-giving, self-withdrawing Super-*Sein* (targeting, per n.13, Richard Capobianco).
3. **§2 "Transcendence and Intentionality"** (pp.33–36) — transcendence as *ratio essendi* of intentionality, intentionality as its *ratio cognoscendi*; intentionality renamed "minding"; rejection of mind-as-container ("digestive epistemology," via Sartre); Aristotle's *De Interpretatione* on the mind's *pathēmata*; *Sorge*'s double focus (attuned minding of the clearing / relating to things within it).
4. **§3 "Interlude: Phenomenology"** (pp.36–38) — Aristotle's two questions of *prōtē philosophia* (what counts as real; what counts as realness) transposed from a direct-realist to a phenomenological register: τὸ ὄν → τὸ παρόν, οὐσία → παρουσία.
5. **§4 "πάθος"** (pp.38–43) — the chapter's core: Heraclitus and Plato on πάθος as φιλοσοφία's ἀρχή; Heidegger's relocation of πάθος from psychological emotion to existential *Stimmung*; the risk that "tuning"/"call-and-response" imagery reintroduces a two-term relation; the resolution via *Bestimmt-sein*, *Bezug*, and *Brauch* as one-sided, non-relational facticity; the derivation of all this from Aristotle's δύναμις τοῦ παθεῖν in *Metaphysics* IX; the closing equation πάθος = facticity = the factum.
6. **Notes 1–41** (pp.43–46) — extensive German/Greek/Latin textual apparatus (GA volume-and-page citations, Grimm's dictionary, Aquinas) supporting nearly every claim in the main text.
7. **Reference** (p.46) — single entry (Gerassi 2009, the Sartre/crabs anecdote).

## 3. Constructs

This chapter is not about boredom in any register, and none of the cluster's eleven controlled boredom constructs is directly treated. Sheehan's subject is the single Greek term πάθος pursued through Heidegger's *entire* corpus (1919 lecture courses through the late Contributions-era and beyond) as the name for facticity-as-such, not through any analysis of a *particular* mood such as boredom, anxiety, or wonder-as-lived-affect. The one place an honest analogical link is available — flagged explicitly as *our* extension, not Sheehan's claim (P/anticipatory-application) — is **situational vs. trait/state boredom**: Sheehan's central methodological move is to distinguish sharply between (a) πάθος read psychologically/existentielly, as an occurrent, passing feeling, and (b) πάθος read existentially/structurally, as the standing facticity of ex-sistence (FE, p.40). That same distinction — between a state-level occurrent affective episode and a deeper structural/dispositional condition — is exactly the axis the boredom literature uses to separate situational boredom from trait boredom proneness, though Sheehan never applies it to boredom himself. A second, weaker analogical purchase on the same construct comes from *Sorge*'s double focus (FE, pp.35–36): holding open the field of meaning as such (structural) versus relating to particular things within that field (occasioned, situational). All other ten constructs — profound/deep boredom, being-bored-by, being-bored-with, boredom proneness (as a named construct), mind-wandering, attentional disengagement/meta-awareness, boredom-as-regulatory-signal, flow/engagement, monotony/meaninglessness, and temporality of boredom — receive no treatment whatsoever, direct or oblique; the chapter simply never reaches them.

## 4. Reading of FCM

This chapter does **not** engage Heidegger's boredom analysis in *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (GA 29/30) at all. There is no mention of *Langeweile*, the three forms of boredom, the station-scene, *Zeitvertreib*, or *Grundstimmung* anywhere in the text or its forty-one endnotes. Sheehan's argument operates one level up, at the general architecture of *Stimmung*/*Befindlichkeit* that FCM's boredom analysis presupposes and specifies: Sheehan explicitly notes that Heidegger's 1955 relocation of πάθος into *Stimmung* ("attunement to *Seyn*") repeats a move Heidegger "had previously done with *Befindlichkeit* (SZ §29)" (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, p.40). That is the *only* point of contact with the conceptual machinery FCM's three-form analysis puts to specific use on boredom; it is a general resonance (the same underlying doctrine of attunement-as-existential-structure), not a specific engagement with FCM's text or with boredom as a phenomenon. Accordingly, this unit claims **no fcm-0N bridge**: the honest and accurate statement is that Sheehan's chapter supplies further confirmation, from a different angle (a 1955 lecture rather than the 1929/30 lecture course), of the same general *Stimmung*-as-existential-structure doctrine that other units in this cluster (e.g., the Elpidorou & Freeman pieces) already connect directly to FCM's boredom chapters — but Sheehan himself draws no such connection, and it would be a fabrication to attribute one to him.

## 5. Findings/Claims

**Step 1 — The goal (pp.31–33).**
- C1. Heidegger used "*Sein*" only as a provisional stand-in for *Anwesen*, the intelligibility or meaningful presence of whatever is encountered; the real goal of his work lay beyond *Anwesen*, at its *Herkunft* ("whence") (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, p.31, citing GA 7:234 and GA 6.2:304).
- C2. Intelligibility occurs only in correlation with human being; the a priori, ineluctable togetherness of ex-sistence and intelligibility is named across Heidegger's career as *In-der-Welt-sein*, *Gegenschwung*, *Ereignis*, *Seyn*, and *Entsprechung* — all naming one and the same "factum," a "unitary phenomenon" (SZ 53) that must never be split into two independent terms subsequently brought into "relation" (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.31–33, citing GA 73.1:790).
- C3. The "worst parody" of Heidegger's thought hypostasizes "Being Itself" into a Metaphysical Something — a Super-*Sein* that occasionally "gives" or "sends" itself and has now mostly "withdrawn" or "hidden" itself, abandoning Dasein to metaphysics and technology; this is "crude but deadly serious mythologizing" that misses the irony of declaring "the end of philosophy" (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, p.33). Endnote 13 names Richard Capobianco as this position's "standard-bearer."

**Step 2 — Transcendence and intentionality (pp.33–36).**
- C4. Transcendence — our a priori "thrown," "stretched" openness as the field of intelligibility — is the *ratio essendi* of intentionality; intentionality is the *ratio cognoscendi* of transcendence (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, p.33, citing GA 24:91). Sheehan thereafter uses "intentionality" to mean only this transcendence-based sense, terming it "minding."
- C5. There is no "inside" to ex-sistence, for Heidegger as for Aristotle; we are always already "outside" (*Draußensein*, SZ 62), and paradoxically this outsideness is our very condition of being inside meaning — a point made against Sartre's parody of "digestive epistemology," the model on which knowing is a kind of eating and digesting (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.33–34).
- C6. Aristotle's *De Interpretatione* (1, 16a6–8) holds that the mind has *pathēmata* ("affections"/impressions) of things, which Heidegger renders *Erlebnisse* and glosses via νοήματα; these *pathēmata* are "likenesses" (*homoiōmata*) of encountered things, but not "inner images" locked inside a mental closet — they are the very mode by which we are assimilated to, made like unto (ὁμοιόω), what is meaningfully present "outside" (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.34–35, citing SZ 214).
- C7. *Sorge* ("care," better rendered "concern"/"interest") has a double focus: holding open the field of possible meaning through attuned minding (*befindliches Verstehen* / *verstehende Befindlichkeit*), and relating to things within that field (*Sein bei*). Existentielly this is *Besorgen* (things), *Fürsorge* (others), *Selbstsorge* (self); existentially it is *In-der-Welt-sein*, *Jemeinigkeit*/*Zu-sein*/*Seinkönnen*, with a penumbral awareness of mortality (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.35–36).

**Step 3 — Interlude on phenomenology (pp.36–38).**
- C8. Aristotle's *prōtē philosophia* poses two questions — what counts as real (τί τὸ ὂν ᾗ ὄν) and what counts as the realness of the real (τίς ἡ οὐσία) — in a direct-realist register where the inquirer is "outside of and apart from" (ἔξω καὶ χωριστόν) the matter at hand. Heidegger's phenomenological transposition makes the inquirer co-constitutive: τὸ ὄν becomes τὸ παρόν, οὐσία becomes παρουσία (the παρ- root indicating intelligible relatedness to ex-sistence) (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.36–38, citing *Metaphysics* IV.1 1003a21 and VII.1 1028b4). Sheehan stresses this is Heidegger rendering explicit Aristotle's own implicit phenomenology — via Aristotle's stated convertibility of τὸ ὄν and τὸ ἀληθές (*Metaphysics* I, 993b) — not imposing phenomenology on ontology from outside (FE, p.37).

**Step 4 — πάθος (pp.38–43), the culminating argument.**
- C9. Heidegger's 1955 Cérisy-la-Salle lecture "What Is Philosophy?" (GA 11:7–26) parallels his 1929 "What Is Metaphysics?": both direct the audience toward a personal experience of what is under discussion, so that what philosophy examines must matter to us personally, touching us at the very root of our own existence (paraphrase of GA 11:7, ≤12-word direct quotation avoided; FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.38–39). This raises — without settling — the worry that philosophy might then be a matter of feelings, tested against André Gide's remark that "fine sentiments make for bad literature" (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, p.38, quoting Gide).
- C10. Via Heraclitus (fragments 35, 50, 51), Heidegger reads the pre-Platonic φιλόσοφος not as one who strives (ὄρεξις/ἔρως) for σοφία but as one already attuned to, in harmony (ὁμολογεῖν, ἁρμονίη) with, the λόγος — a "tense harmony... like a tightly strung bow or lyre." By Plato and Aristotle's era, two centuries later, this harmonious attunement had devolved into a striving for wisdom (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.39–40).
- C11. In Plato's *Theaetetus* (155d2–4), Socrates identifies πάθος — wondering, marveling (τὸ θαυμάζειν) — as the sole ἀρχή of philosophy. Because ἀρχή names both origin *and* sustaining principle, wonder is not merely what starts philosophy but what maintains it throughout the philosophical life (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, p.40).
- C12. Heidegger relocates this πάθος out of the psychological/existentiel realm of passing emotion and into the existential structure he calls *Stimmung*: attunement to *Seyn*. This repeats, in 1955, the same interpretive move Heidegger had already made with *Befindlichkeit* at SZ §29 (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, p.40).
- C13. That very phrase — "attunement to..." — harbors a risk: it can imply dividing ex-sistence (the one) from the factum (the other), each independently subsisting and then brought into relation. Two of Heidegger's own images abet this risk: the "tuning" metaphor (*stimmen*, as in tuning an orchestra or striking a tuning fork) and the "call-and-response" metaphor (the *Stimme* and *Zuspruch des Seyns* — "the voice of Being that calls and claims" ex-sistence). Both risk reinstating the same two-term, subject-object structure — a crypto-metaphysical Super-*Seyn* — that Step 1 had already ruled out (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.40–41).
- C14. The resolution comes via *Bestimmt-sein*. Drawing on the Grimm brothers' etymology (*bestimmen* = Latin *constituere*, Greek καθίστημι, both causative: "to set in place, bring to a condition, establish"), Sheehan draws three conclusions: (i) ex-sistence's *Bestimmt-sein* is its condition of having been de-fined/de-termined, brought to its *finis*/τέλος — not cessation but the achieved, fulfilled state of ex-sistence; (ii) *Bestimmt-sein* is a reinscription of the same single factum earlier called *Geworfenheit* and later *Ereignet-sein*; (iii) crucially, there is no agent that "throws," "appropriates," or "attunes" ex-sistence *ab extra* — no efficient cause (ποιητικόν) acting on a patient (παθητικόν) — and yet πάθος remains at the heart of the project, now understood not as passing emotion but as another name for the existential structure of ex-sistence itself, equivalent to *Geworfenheit* and *Ereignetsein* (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.41–42).
- C15. Turning to the "Conversation with a Japanese Professor" (GA 12:119), Heidegger defines the hermeneutical *Bezug* ("relation") between ex-sistence and *Sprache* (which here means Heraclitean λόγος — the clearing as the possibility of intelligibility, not any system of communication). This *Bezug* is emphatically *not* a two-sided relation of the form "Dasein ←→ *Seyn*." Rather, the word names our fate of being "used" or "needed" (*gebraucht*), belonging to a *Brauch* that claims us — a one-sided existential fact: without us, there is no in-break (*Einbruch*) into things, no clearing, and therefore no possibility of meaning at all (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.41–42, citing GA 12:119).
- C16 (the thesis, culminating). Heidegger derives thrownness, appropriation, and *Bestimmtsein* from Aristotle's account of δύναμις τοῦ παθεῖν in *Metaphysics* IX (cf. V.12): πάσχω/πάθος name a thing's passivity (*pathēsis*) — its ability to undergo, receive, or "suffer" (bear up under) some action (ποίησις) visited upon it. Aristotle's own doctrine concerns the being of things generally; Heidegger radically adapts it to the structure of human being itself, where πάθος now names ex-sistence's fundamental facticity. We have always already "suffered" the burden of thrown-openness as the clearing, though no prior agent forced this upon us; to say facticity has been "done unto us" is just a way of saying it is the ultimate given, the presupposition behind which we cannot go. Therefore: πάθος is our facticity, and our facticity is itself the factum — *die Sache selbst* of Heidegger's entire project (FAITHFUL-Sheehan, pp.42–43, citing *Metaphysics* IX and V.12).

**On the rhetorical-pathos question (our own observation, P/anticipatory-application, not Sheehan's claim).** The Aristotle Sheehan engages throughout is not the Aristotle of the *Rhetoric*. Every Aristotelian citation in this chapter comes from *De Interpretatione* 1 (the mind's *pathēmata* as mental impressions/likenesses), *De Anima* III.8 (the soul as "somehow all things"), or *Metaphysics* IX and V.12 (δύναμις τοῦ παθεῖν, the general ontological doctrine of a thing's capacity to undergo an action). None of this is the *Rhetoric* II doctrine of the *pathē* — anger, fear, pity, shame, and the rest — as the audience-directed means of persuasion central to this dissertation's Part I. Sheehan's πάθος is metaphysical (a mode of being: receptivity/passivity as such) and psychological-cognitive (a mode of mental representation), not rhetorical-persuasive. The two lineages share a root (πάσχω, "to undergo, be acted upon") and a family resemblance — Aristotle's rhetorical *pathē* are, after all, a species of *pathein*, an audience *being made to undergo* something at the orator's hands — but Sheehan draws no such genealogical line himself, and it would misrepresent this chapter to claim he does. The honest finding is a productive *adjacency*, not an identity: this chapter substantially deepens the ontological and psychological registers of πάθος/πάσχειν that stand behind, but are not the same as, the rhetorical register the dissertation treats in Part I.

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This unit's primary dissertation relevance runs to Parts I and II, not Part III. For **Part I** (Aristotelian rhetorical *pathos*): this chapter is valuable precisely as a *contrast case* rather than a source of direct support. It demonstrates, at book length, a fully worked-out non-rhetorical use of πάθος/πάσχειν — grounded in *De Interpretatione*, *De Anima*, and *Metaphysics* IX/V.12 — that never touches the *Rhetoric*'s taxonomy of persuasive emotions. Citing this chapter alongside Part I's rhetorical-*pathē* material lets the dissertation state precisely, with a citable philosophical source, why the metaphysical/psychological and rhetorical-persuasive senses of πάθος must not be conflated even though both descend from πάσχω — and, more speculatively, why Aristotle's own general doctrine that a thing's πάθος is its capacity to be acted upon (δύναμις τοῦ παθεῖν) is a plausible deep structure underlying the *Rhetoric*'s own claim that persuasion works by making an audience *undergo* something *(anticipatory-application)*. For **Part II** (Heideggerian affect/metaphysics): Sheehan's culminating equation — πάθος = facticity = the factum = *die Sache selbst* — offers Part II a precise, well-sourced vocabulary for distinguishing existentiel/psychological affect from existential-structural attunement, directly bearing on the dissertation's locked terminology (genus virtual-object; phantasma as originary) by supplying an independent, rigorously argued precedent for treating a Greek affect-term as naming an ontological structure rather than a lived feeling *(anticipatory-application)*. Sheehan's explicit critique of hypostasized "Super-*Seyn*" readings (targeting Capobianco) is also a methodological caution worth carrying into any Part II discussion that risks treating Heideggerian *Ereignis*/*Seyn* as an agent that "gives" or "withdraws" *(anticipatory-application)*. For **Part III** (the VR/survey material): this chapter's relevance is minimal and should not be forced — it contains no boredom content, no measures, and no empirical claims of any kind; its sole indirect service to Part III is the general reminder, shared with other Strand-A/B units in this cluster, that any occurrent/state-level affective report (including student boredom) sits within a deeper existential-structural register that self-report alone cannot fully capture *(anticipatory-application)*.
