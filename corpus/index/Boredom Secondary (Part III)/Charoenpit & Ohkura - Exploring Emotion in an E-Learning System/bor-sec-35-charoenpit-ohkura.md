# bor-sec-35 — Charoenpit, Saromporn, and Michiko Ohkura, "Exploring Emotion in an E-learning System Using Eye Tracking" (2015)

**Type:** empirical HCI/affective-computing experiment (screen-based eye tracker, within-subjects counterbalanced design) · **Discipline:** affective engineering / e-learning HCI, correlating eye-metric indexes with self-reported learner emotion · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus role:** Strand E — the cluster's clearest e-learning-context eye-tracking unit; its Learning-Area/Out-of-Learning-Area (LA/OLA) apparatus and "interest vs. boredom group" questionnaire design are the strongest available template for `grounds-measure`-bridging to the eye-tracking/emotion channel of the not-yet-built Boredom Experiment (VR Attention Study) dataset entry, and its educational setting invites a non-citational thematic comparison with this cluster's Strand B pedagogy units.

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Charoenpit and Ohkura (*International Journal of Affective Engineering* 14.4, 2015, pp.309-316) address a gap they state plainly: "to date, few e-learning systems have derived emotions from eye tracking data" (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.309). Building on the authors' own earlier biological-signal-focused e-learning system [11], they design and test a prototype that records learners' eye movements while they study two units of introductory C-programming content, then ask whether eye-metric indexes — fixation counts, fixation durations, and pupil diameter, each computed inside versus outside a defined learning area — track two "emotion groups" the authors construct from a nine-item post-content questionnaire: an interest group and a boredom group (FE, p.309). The paper's own contribution claim, restated in the Conclusion, is narrower than the introduction's framing suggests: of the several eye-metric indexes tested, only the fixation-number ratio and the fixation-duration ratio are put forward as "useful" (FE, p.313/315), while the pupil-diameter ratio's relationship to the two emotion groups is reported but, as shown below, sits in some tension with the interpretive rule the authors themselves state for it.

## 3. Constructs

Mapped onto the controlled construct axis, the source treats:

- **situational vs. trait/state boredom** — treated only operationally, never named as such: "boredom" is the mean of three within-session questionnaire items (Table 2) answered once per content unit, implicitly state/situational, with no trait instrument (e.g., a Boredom Proneness Scale) administered or discussed (FE, p.311-312).
- **attentional disengagement / meta-awareness** — the paper's operational core. The Learning Area (LA) versus Out-of-Learning-Area (OLA) screen partition (Figure 5) operationalizes attention as fixation location, following the claim that eye data "indicate a person's interest level and the focus of her attention" (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.309, citing Hend & Remya [6]); disengagement is read off as elevated OLA fixation counts/durations relative to LA.
- **flow / engagement (contrast pole)** — the "interest" group is this paper's engagement pole, built from six of the nine questionnaire items and grounded in the same three-model apparatus (Russell's Circumplex, "Shen," "Kort's learning spiral") used to define both emotion groups (FE, p.310-311).
- **boredom proneness / mind-wandering / monotony-meaninglessness / temporality of boredom / profound-deep-boredom (FCM forms) / boredom-as-regulatory-signal** — not treated. No trait measurement; mind-wandering is never named (gaze drifting into OLA is read purely as disengagement, not directed thought); monotony/meaninglessness are never theorized beyond the boredom-group items themselves ("tiring," "anxiety-inducing," "frustrating" — Table 2, items 4/5/8); subjective time is never discussed; boredom is framed purely instrumentally, a negative state to detect and eventually help learners "deal with" (FE, p.309, 315), not an adaptive signal.

## 4. Measures & methods

Eight Brazilian students were divided into two groups following two counterbalanced content/style patterns (Table 1): Pattern I studied Content 1 (loops in C) in Style A (text-only slides) and Content 2 (decision-making statements in C, "more difficult than content 1") in Style B (text-plus-pictures slides); Pattern II reversed the pairing (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.311-312). The eleven-step protocol ran: calibration, registration, pre-test 1 (ten questions), Content 1, post-test 1, questionnaire 1, a five-minute break, pre-test 2, Content 2, post-test 2, questionnaire 2 (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.311-312). The nine-item, five-point Likert questionnaire (Table 2) was assembled from two external sources [15,16] and split into an interest group (six items) and a boredom group (three items: "tiring," "anxiety," "frustrating") (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.312).

Eye movements were recorded with **EyeTech Quick Glance hardware, version 5.0**, sampling at **50 Hz** (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.312). The display was partitioned into two areas of interest — Learning Area (LA) and Out-of-Learning-Area (OLA) — and three raw eye metrics were computed separately for each area, per user/content/style (Table 4): number of fixations, fixation duration (s), and pupil diameter (mm). From these, three LA/OLA ratios were derived (fixation-number ratio, fixation-duration ratio, pupil-diameter ratio), with stated interpretive rules: a high fixation-number or fixation-duration ratio "indicates that learner is interested," a high pupil-diameter ratio "indicates that learner is bored" (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.313). Pearson correlations were then computed between each metric/ratio and the questionnaire group means, by content and by style (Tables 5-6), and a single-participant follow-up (User 04) compares questionnaire and eye-metric evidence directly via an independent-samples t-test contrasting Content 1 vs. 2 within LA and OLA (Table 7).

## 5. Findings/Claims

- Post-test scores generally exceeded pre-test scores, with three users (03, 04, 08) as named exceptions; interest-group questionnaire means were generally higher than boredom-group means across users and conditions (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.312-313).
- Table 5 reports positive correlations with the interest-group mean: fixation-duration ratio in Content 1 (r=.831, p<.01), fixation-number ratio in Content 1 (r=.673, p<.05), and pupil-diameter ratio in both Content 1 (r=.694, p<.01) and Style A (r=.665, p<.01); OLA fixation count in Content 2 correlated negatively with interest (r=-.778, p<.05) (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.314).
- Table 6 reports negative correlations with the boredom-group mean: fixation-duration ratio (r=-.656, p<.01), fixation-number ratio (r=-.690, p<.01), and pupil-diameter ratio (r=-.790, p<.05) in Content 2, and pupil-diameter ratio in Style B (r=-.826, p<.05) (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.314). The pupil-diameter ratio's *negative* correlation with boredom here runs contrary to the stated rule that "a high pupil diameter ratio indicates that learner is bored" (p.313) — that rule predicts a *positive* correlation with boredom, not negative; the source does not flag or resolve this internal inconsistency (FE, our observation).
- User 04 shows a self-report/eye-metric mismatch: boredom-group mean was higher for Content 1 than Content 2, while fixation-plot evidence (Figures 8-9) shows the OLA-directed pattern the authors associate with boredom occurring in Content 2. They attribute this to questionnaire-timing lag and conclude they "focused on the eye metric results because they are more precise than the questionnaire results, which cannot measure learner emotions in real time" (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.314).
- Table 7's t-test (User 04, Content 1 vs. 2) found significant OLA differences — fixation number (p=.028), duration (p=.009), point (p=.020), length (p=.007) — with no significant LA differences (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.314).
- The Conclusion names the fixation-number and fixation-duration ratios (not the pupil-diameter ratio) as the "useful eye metric indexes" identified, and states future work toward an intervention experiment to sustain attention/motivation (FAITHFUL-Charoenpit & Ohkura, p.315).

## 7. Analytical role

*(anticipatory-application)* This source is a plausible `grounds-measure` candidate for the eye-tracking/emotion channel of the not-yet-built Boredom Experiment (VR Attention Study) dataset entry: its LA/OLA area-of-interest partition, its three raw eye metrics computed per-area, and its derived LA/OLA ratios together offer a concrete, low-cost precedent for classifying engagement versus boredom from eye-tracking data in an instructional setting. ****** UNVERIFIED: unconfirmable until the dataset entry specifies its own apparatus and area-of-interest scheme; a VR headset's integrated tracker would track 3D gaze vectors against headset-relative regions rather than 2D screen coordinates against a fixed LA/OLA split, so translation is not immediate (P/anticipatory-application).

*(anticipatory-application)* The paper's own unresolved tension — a stated interpretive rule ("high pupil-diameter ratio indicates boredom") not borne out in sign by its own correlation tables, alongside the User 04 case where self-report and eye-metric evidence diverge and the authors elect to trust the physiological channel — is a methodological caution for Part III: neither self-report/physiological convergence nor an intuitive sign-direction for a given eye metric should be assumed without checking the correlational evidence in hand, and post-hoc questionnaire timing may itself introduce recall-lag confounds eye-tracking channels do not share (P/anticipatory-application).

*(anticipatory-application)* Content 2 is described as "more difficult" than Content 1, yet it is also the condition most consistently associated with the boredom-correlated eye metrics — a difficulty/frustration confound the source never examines. Part III's own VLE materials, compared across units of differing difficulty, would need to disentangle boredom proper (monotony, low perceived value) from mere task difficulty before attributing eye-metric or self-report differences to boredom as such (P/anticipatory-application).

*(anticipatory-application)* Thematically, this source and Strand B's Feldges & Pieczenko (bor-sec-16, "Boredom in Educational Contexts: A Critical Review") both address boredom within institutionalized instruction from opposite methodological poles — empirical/correlational eye-tracking HCI here, Husserlian-phenomenological critical review there — with no citation relationship in either direction (Feldges & Pieczenko postdates this source by five years but does not cite it; this source's reference list contains no phenomenological literature). The parallel is worth naming precisely because it is non-citational: two traditions independently converge on "boredom in instruction" without engaging each other, itself a modest data point about how partitioned the empirical and phenomenological boredom literatures remain (P/anticipatory-application).
