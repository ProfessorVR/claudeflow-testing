# Elpidorou (2018) — *The Good of Boredom*

**Type:** journal article, *Philosophical Psychology* 31(3), 323–351 (published online 10 Jul 2017; print 2018) · **Discipline:** psychology of emotion / analytic value theory of well-being · **Depth:** deep (~29pp) · **Corpus-role:** Strand-C co-anchor (with bor-sec-25) for the debate axis *boredom-as-deficit ↔ boredom-as-functional/adaptive* and for the construct-axis row *boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)*.

## Thesis / Aim

Elpidorou argues that the "transitory and nonpathological experience of boredom" — state boredom, sharply distinguished from trait boredom/boredom proneness — functions as a regulatory mechanism that promotes well-being by contributing to personal growth and to the (re)construction of a meaningful life, describing it in the abstract as "a regulatory psychological state" (FAITHFUL-Elpidorou, p.323). The paper compresses its own argument into a syllogism: boredom promotes movement; movement is essential to well-being; therefore boredom promotes well-being (p.326). Elpidorou is explicit that this is not the first paper to link boredom to self-regulation, but claims two advances over "extant articles" (p.326): first, it specifies the mechanism by tying boredom to the *locomotion* dimension of Regulatory Mode Theory (Higgins, Kruglanski, & Pierro, 2003) rather than leaving "self-regulation" unanalyzed; second, and by the author's own account the paper's more novel contribution, it takes the further step of showing that boredom's capacity to keep us in motion is *beneficial to well-being* in the specific, eudaimonic sense developed by Ryff and Singer (1998) — a claim Elpidorou states, in the paper's closing section, "no other work on boredom has suggested" (p.340). As agents with projects, goals, and relationships, he concludes, we are "much better off having the capacity to be bored than lacking it" (p.326).

## Structure

1. **§1 Boredom: A tale of two constructs** (pp.323–326) — surveys the measurement literature (BPS, ZBS), the harmful correlates of boredom *proneness*, and establishes the conceptual priority and ubiquity of *state* boredom over trait boredom; closes by foreshadowing the regulatory-state proposal.
2. **§2 Caution!** (p.327) — explicitly scopes the thesis to occasional, non-chronic state boredom; introduces the paper's central analogy: pain is aversive yet adaptive (congenital analgesia is dangerous), and boredom is argued to work the same way.
3. **§3 Describing boredom** (pp.328–330) — a component-process account (affective, cognitive, physiological, volitional characters) that supplies the descriptive basis for the functional claim; includes the paper's review of ambiguous/contradictory physiological and neural correlates.
4. **§4 Boredom and self-regulation** (pp.330–334) — 4.1 imports Regulatory Mode Theory's locomotion/assessment distinction and argues boredom specifically recruits locomotion, with three supporting arguments and a dysfunction account of boredom proneness; 4.2 "The limits of boredom" (pp.334–337) qualifies the thesis via three complications (boredom can lead to more boredom; the "interesting" isn't always beneficial; the boring shouldn't always be escaped).
5. **§5 Boredom and well-being** (pp.337–340) — 5.1 stakes out the eudaimonic (Ryan & Deci; Ryff & Singer) side of the hedonic/eudaimonic well-being debate; 5.2 maps boredom's locomotion-function onto two of Ryff and Singer's (1998) six dimensions of psychological well-being: personal growth and purpose/meaning in life.
6. **§6 Connections and further directions** (pp.340–341) — situates the argument against the dominant pessimistic literary-philosophical tradition (Kierkegaard, Schopenhauer, Heidegger, et al.) and flags open empirical questions.
7. **§7 Conclusion: The value of negativity** (pp.341–342) — generalizes the point to negative affect as such.

## Constructs

- **Boredom-as-regulatory-signal (functional account)** is the paper's central construct, developed across §§1–5: boredom is "both an informative and motivational state" (p.330) that signals a mismatch between one's situation and one's interests/goals and motivates goal-directed movement out of it.
- **Situational vs. trait/state boredom** structures the whole paper from its title ("§1 Boredom: A tale of two constructs," p.323) onward; the thesis applies *only* to the state form, and this distinction does the entire argumentative work of §2's "Caution!" scoping move.
- **Boredom proneness** is treated extensively (pp.323–325) via its documented harms (anger, aggression, depression, poor grades, risky driving, gambling, and more) and then, in §4.1, reframed as the *dysfunction* or failure-mode of the same regulatory mechanism that, working properly, produces the paper's "good."
- **Monotony / meaninglessness** recurs throughout: boring situations are perceived as "non-optimal," "meaningless or trite" (p.328); boredom's function is described as restoring "the perception that one's activities are meaningful and congruent with one's overall projects" (p.326); §5.2 builds directly on Van Tilburg and Igou's (2011, 2012) meaning-regulation account.
- **Temporality of boredom (time-drag)** appears in the cognitive-character discussion (§3.2, p.328): bored individuals experience "an altered passage of time," judged empirically via Watt's (1991) time-perception study and Danckert and Allman's (2005) duration-judgment errors.
- **Mind-wandering / task-unrelated thought** is listed as a cognitive marker of boredom (p.328) and later reappears as one legitimate *route* by which "mental" (as opposed to physical) locomotion can alleviate boredom (p.331) — engaging in "a different mental activity, such as mind-wandering."
- **Attentional disengagement / meta-awareness** is central to the cognitive character of boredom (p.328, "difficulties in concentrating and maintaining attention") and reappears in the physiological review via Danckert and Merrifield's (2016) fMRI comparison of resting-state, boredom-induction, and sustained-attention conditions, where anti-correlated anterior-insular activity is read as a marker of *failed* engagement (p.329).
- **Flow / engagement (contrast pole)** receives only a light touch: engagement/interest is the implicit contrast state throughout (bored = disengaged and dissatisfied vs. moved toward what is "interesting, engaging, or meaningful," p.330), and Csikszentmihalyi's *Beyond Boredom and Anxiety* (1975) is cited in passing on challenge (p.328, n.3) and on self-regulatory processes (p.340), but flow itself is never developed as a rival or complementary construct.
- **Profound/deep boredom, being-bored-by, being-bored-with** do not appear as analytic categories anywhere in this paper — see Reading of FCM, below.

## Reading of FCM

This paper does **not** engage the *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* directly, and the omission is worth registering explicitly given that Elpidorou is, across his other Strand-A entries in this cluster (bor-sec-02 through bor-sec-06), this cluster's most Heidegger-literate author. Heidegger appears exactly twice, both in passing. First, in §6's historical survey of boredom's philosophical and literary reception, he is listed alongside "Dante, Pascal, Novalis, Schopenhauer, Kierkegaard, Dostoevsky, Pessoa, ... Russell, and Brodsky" as a figure in whose work boredom "figures" (p.340) — no exposition of the three forms, *Leergelassenheit*, *Hingehaltenheit*, or the *Augenblick* is offered or implied. Second, footnote 7 (pp.341–342) groups Heidegger with Russell (1996) and Brodsky (1997) as one of the tradition's "dissenting voices" against Kierkegaard's pronouncement that "boredom is the root of all evil" (Kierkegaard, 1843/1987, p.285) — a one-line gesture crediting Heidegger with recognizing *some* positive significance in boredom, without any further textual engagement. The reference list cites the 1983/2001 German/English edition of FCM (*Die Grundbegriffe der Metaphysik*) only to source this footnote. In short: this is a paper by Heidegger's most careful contemporary reader on boredom that deliberately brackets Heidegger entirely, building its case for boredom's value from analytic psychology and eudaimonic value theory alone. That bracketing is itself informative for the cluster — it shows Elpidorou running two distinct research programs (psychology-of-emotion functionalism and Heidegger scholarship) that touch only glancingly here, in contrast to bor-sec-04 (Elpidorou & Freeman, 2019), where the two are deliberately synthesized. No `bridges-to-fcm` edge is created for this unit; a bridge would misrepresent the source.

## Findings / Claims

1. **Boredom is conceptually and empirically distinct from trait boredom, and only the state form is at issue** (§1, pp.323–326). Boredom proneness (measured by the Boredom Proneness Scale; Farmer & Sundberg, 1986) is documented to correlate with anger, aggression, depression, anxiety, hostility, apathy, loneliness, poor grades, dropout, job dissatisfaction, accidents, risk-taking, binge eating, substance abuse, and problem gambling — a catalogue Elpidorou reproduces in full before setting it aside as *not* the target of his argument.
2. **The pain analogy licenses the paper's central move** (§2, p.327): the capacity to feel pain is valuable even though pain itself is unpleasant (congenital insensitivity to pain produces short, injury-prone lives); analogously, the *capacity* to be bored is valuable because it "protects us from certain situations" by informing us of, and motivating escape from, a mismatch between our situation and our interests — even though the experience itself remains aversive.
3. **The component-process description grounds the functional claim** (§3, pp.328–330): boredom is aversive (affective), marked by disengagement, dissatisfaction, attentional difficulty, and time-drag (cognitive), physiologically ambiguous (Elpidorou concedes explicitly that "there is no agreement as to whether boredom is a state of low or high arousal," p.329, citing studies for low arousal, high arousal, and both), and volitionally characterized by a "thicker" desire than mere "do something else" (p.331) — bored individuals want to do something *specifically* interesting or meaningful, which is what differentiates boredom from apathy.
4. **Boredom recruits locomotion, not assessment** (§4.1, pp.330–333). Elpidorou gives three arguments that boredom's function is *goal-directed* movement rather than movement as such: the volitional content of boredom is directional, not aimless (p.331); mere physical change does not relieve boredom (truck drivers on monotonous roads remain bored despite motion, p.332); and only a goal-directed-movement account makes sense of boredom proneness as a *dysfunction* — either a failure to move, or movement into situations that fail to satisfy (p.333). This dysfunction account is explicitly offered as reconciling, rather than contradicting, §1's catalogue of boredom-proneness harms, and generates a testable prediction later confirmed by cited evidence: locomotion is strongly negatively correlated with boredom proneness (Struk, Scholer, & Danckert, 2016, cited p.332).
5. **The thesis has three explicit limits** (§4.2, pp.334–337): boredom can motivate escape into another boring situation (self-knowledge is required to use it well); the "interesting" situation boredom moves us toward is not guaranteed to be *beneficial* (risky, exciting behavior is the paradigm counter-case); and some boring situations (waiting in line, over-familiar material) are appropriately boring and should not be escaped, while others (a student bored in an important class) should not simply be escaped either — though even here boredom can prompt beneficial creativity (Gasper & Middlewood, 2014; Mann & Cadman, 2014) rather than only disengagement.
6. **Boredom contributes to eudaimonic, not hedonic, well-being** (§5, pp.337–340). Building on Ryan and Deci's (2001) review and Nozick's (1974) experience-machine intuition (a happy simulated life is not a good life, p.338), Elpidorou maps boredom's locomotion-function onto two of Ryff and Singer's (1998) six dimensions of psychological well-being — *personal growth* (boredom moves us toward what we already find interesting, helping us "realize and practice our talents," p.339) and *purpose/meaning in life* (boredom signals lost meaning and motivates its reconstruction, p.340) — concluding that boredom "can help one to establish or reestablish a sense of meaningfulness and coherence" (p.340).
7. **The paper closes by generalizing to negative affect as such** (§7, pp.341–342): there is "a place for negative emotions in well-being," and articulating boredom's function is offered as a template for taking other unpleasant states seriously as adaptive rather than merely deficient.

## Analytical role (anticipatory-application)

*(P/anticipatory-application) — the following are our interpretive links to Part III, not claims Elpidorou makes himself.*

This unit, together with bor-sec-25 (*The Bored Mind Is a Guiding Mind*), is the primary grounding for the cluster's named debate axis, *boredom-as-deficit ↔ boredom-as-functional/adaptive*, and it is worth stating precisely what position it stakes out, because the paper's own move is more careful than a flat endorsement of "functional over deficit." Elpidorou does not deny the deficit literature; §1 reproduces it in full (pp.323–325) and §2 explicitly disclaims any suggestion "that our well-being is promoted by the chronic or frequent experience of boredom" (p.327). Instead, the paper *partitions* the debate along the state/trait line already established by the field's own measurement literature: *state* boredom, occasionally experienced, is a functional, adaptive regulatory signal (the "good" of the title), while *trait* boredom/boredom proneness remains genuinely harmful — but its harm is now explained *as* a dysfunction of the same mechanism (§4.1, p.333, and note 5, pp.341–342, where Elpidorou clarifies that "the mere disposition to experience boredom" is not itself dysfunctional; only its high-frequency, wide-scope form is). This is a reconciling rather than a partisan move: deficit accounts of chronic boredom and functional accounts of episodic boredom are shown to be compatible, with the former reframed as the pathology of the latter's normal operation. For Part III, this licenses a specific, guarded interpretive move: survey items or interview passages describing a *single, situational* episode of student or player disengagement (e.g., VLE boredom in a familiar or over-easy module) can be read, on Elpidorou's own terms, as evidence of a regulatory signal doing its job — informing the subject of a meaning/interest mismatch and motivating movement — rather than as raw evidence of design failure or deficient engagement. That reading does *not* extend to items measuring boredom-*proneness* (trait-level, BPS-style instruments), which this paper's own argument keeps on the deficit side of the ledger; Part III should not use bor-sec-26 to launder trait-level boredom findings into a "functional" gloss.

Second, this paper is best read as the value-theoretic companion to bor-sec-25's more mechanistic argument, and the relationship between the two is not merely thematic — it is a citation relationship internal to the source itself. The reference list here cites "Elpidorou, A. (forthcoming). The bored mind is a guiding mind: Toward a regulatory theory of boredom" (p.351), and the in-text citations to this "forthcoming" piece (pp.325, 328, 330, 338–339, 340, and footnote 2 on p.341) consistently *defer* technical work to it: the "systematic and exhaustive review of the literature on the state of boredom" (p.328), the detailed treatment of boredom's ambiguous physiological/arousal profile (p.328), and the expressive component of boredom (n.2, p.341) are all pointed at the companion piece rather than developed here. What *The Good of Boredom* does instead — and what it explicitly claims as its own distinct contribution (p.340, "no other work on boredom has suggested that boredom can play a role in our well-being") — is take the shared regulatory-theory mechanism (locomotion, self-regulation, informative-and-motivational function) established jointly across both papers and push it one step further into eudaimonic value theory via Ryff and Singer's (1998) framework. The two 2018 papers therefore divide labor along a mechanism/value-theory seam: bor-sec-25 is expected to carry the fuller "regulatory theory of boredom" apparatus (hence its subtitle), while bor-sec-26 carries the "why this matters for a good life" argument. This is a `cites-cluster-author` relationship (Good of Boredom forward-cites its own companion as a manuscript in production), not a case of two independent, merely-parallel arguments — though the division of labor means neither paper alone contains the complete regulatory-theory-plus-value-theory case; Part III should cite both together when invoking "Elpidorou's functional account" rather than treating either as self-sufficient.

Third, no citation relationship exists to bor-sec-02 or bor-sec-03 (Elpidorou & Freeman, *Affectivity in Heidegger I/II*, 2015) — neither "Freeman" nor either paper's content appears anywhere in this source's text or bibliography. This is a meaningful negative finding for the cluster's citation graph: Elpidorou's psychology-of-emotion program and his Heidegger-affectivity program, though contemporaneous and authored by the same person, are not cross-referenced at the level of this individual paper. Part III should not assume citation continuity across all Elpidorou entries in this cluster merely on grounds of shared authorship.

Fourth, the paper's own §4.2 "limits of boredom" (pp.334–337) — particularly the classroom example (p.336, a student bored by an "important" class, where boredom's motivational force may lead to harmful mind-wandering rather than beneficial creativity or re-engagement) — offers Part III a ready-made caution against over-applying the functional gloss to VLE contexts: Elpidorou's own view is that boredom "needs our guidance" (p.335) and "will not by itself solve our problems" (p.341) to be put to good use, which maps onto Part III's finding that spatial-proximity co-watching in the VLE (see `project-vle-proximity-voice-and-groups`) may supply exactly this needed direction/guidance, converting an otherwise unguided (and thus potentially harmful) episode of state boredom into a productively re-directed one — a mapping Part III should present as its own extension, not as a claim this source makes.
