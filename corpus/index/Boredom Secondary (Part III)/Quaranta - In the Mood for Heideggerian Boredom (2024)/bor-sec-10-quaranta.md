# bor-sec-10 — Quaranta, "In the Mood for Heideggerian Boredom? Film Viewership as Being-in-the-World" (2024)

**Type:** journal article (*Film-Philosophy* 28.1, pp.31–46) · **Discipline:** film-phenomenology / Heideggerian philosophy · **Depth:** deep · **Corpus-role:** Strand A — applies FCM's three-forms-of-boredom schema to film spectatorship via Shawn Loht's "viewing Dasein" argument; sibling reading to bor-sec-11 (cinema + boredom).

## 1. Thesis/Aim

Quaranta takes up Shawn Loht's *Phenomenology of Film: A Heideggerian Account of the Film Experience* (2017) — its claim that film viewing is an extension of being-in-the-world, structured through Mitsein and a bidirectional disclosedness between viewer and film — and argues that Loht's brief, undeveloped remarks on boredom can be substantially deepened by turning to Heidegger's *Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics* (FCM), where Loht himself does not go (he stays with *Being and Time* and, on mood, mainly with fear) (FE, p.32). Her aim is to show that boredom, and especially profound boredom, is a privileged mood for disclosing both characters' and spectators' being-in a film world, and to test this claim through a sustained reading of Ryusuke Hamaguchi's *Drive My Car* (2021), whose aesthetics of dead-time, silence, slowness and repetition she reads as deliberately arousing profound boredom in the viewer (FE, pp.31–32).

## 2. Structure

- **Abstract/Introduction** (pp.31–33) — frames the engagement with Loht, diagnoses the FCM-shaped gap in his book, previews the *Drive My Car* case.
- **"Worldhood, Moods, Understanding"** (pp.33–36) — rehearses Heidegger's being-in-the-world (attunement/understanding/discourse), mood and fundamental mood, then the three forms of boredom, closing on profound boredom as authentic disclosure of finitude.
- **"Film Viewership as Being-in-the-(Cinematic-)World"** (pp.36–38) — expounds Loht's bidirectional-disclosedness thesis and introduces "aesthetics of mood" (after Sinnerbrink 2012) as the mechanism by which a film arouses moods that disclose a meaningful film world.
- **"Temporal Entrancement in *Drive My Car*"** (pp.38–44) — the sustained close reading: silence/noise, fixed shots, the Hokkaido car-drive sequence, the self-disclosive dialogue it enables, and the climactic "two hands" shot read as cinematic disclosure of being.
- **"Heideggerian Film-Philosophy(?)"** (pp.41–44) — closes with reservations about a fully Heideggerian film-philosophy (rigidity, the excluded body, over-reliance on Malick), contrasting it favorably but incompletely with the embodied film-phenomenology tradition (Sobchack, Barker, Marks).

## 3. Constructs

Of the controlled-vocabulary CONSTRUCT rows, this humanities source treats a narrow, cinema-specific cluster and leaves the cognitive-science rows untouched — worth registering explicitly rather than leaving silent for the future concordance.

- **Profound/deep boredom (FCM 3rd form)** — the paper's organizing construct. Quaranta paraphrases Heidegger's *es ist einem langweilig* ("it is boring for one") as the impersonal, indeterminate form in which "time itself bores one" (FAITHFUL-Quaranta, p.34), and treats it as the mood capable of disclosing a film world's meaning as well as the characters' and viewers' shared finitude (pp.34–41).
- **Being-bored-by (1st form) / Being-bored-with (2nd form)** — both are defined only briefly, in German-tagged apposition (*Gelangweiltwerden von etwas* / *Sichlangweilen bei etwas*), purely to set up the third form as "the more profound" (FAITHFUL-Quaranta, p.34); neither receives independent cinematic application.
- **Temporality of boredom (time-drag)** — operative throughout the *Drive My Car* reading via her own coinage "temporal entrancement": silence, repetition and fixed shots are read as aesthetically producing the sense that "time itself" drags (pp.39–41).
- **Monotony/meaninglessness** — named at the essay's close as "the boresome nature of our everydayness," the busyness by which contemporary Dasein turns away from the burden of existence (FE, p.44).

## 4. Reading of FCM

Quaranta's engagement with FCM is real but selective, and precision here matters for how this unit should be used downstream. She anchors her third-form paraphrase directly in Heidegger's text — citing 1983/1995 pp.134, 138–141, and 147–150, 158 — which places her reading squarely inside **fcm-06-third-form** (FCM §§29–36, book pp.132–159): the definition of the impersonal "it is boring for one," the "telling refusal" of beings as a whole, and the thesis that "time itself (originary temporality) bores Dasein," opening the world "as radically meaningless and groundless" and thereby calling Dasein to authentically appropriate its finitude (FAITHFUL-Quaranta, pp.34–36). She also reaches back briefly to **fcm-04-first-form** (§§19–23, book pp.92–105), citing Heidegger's claim that the first form traps one in an "inauthentic past, present and future," and names **fcm-05-second-form** (§§24–28, book pp.106–131) definitionally without further citation (p.34).

What she does *not* do is walk through FCM's own structural apparatus for the third form — *Leergelassenheit*/*Hingehaltenheit* as transformed in profound boredom, the entrancement (*Gebanntheit*) of the threefold temporal horizon, or the *Augenblick* ("moment of vision") that the entrancement holds in suspense. None of that vocabulary appears in her text; her own section title, "Temporal Entrancement," is a gloss she supplies for cinematic purposes, not a citation of Heidegger's *Gebanntheit*. Her reading is therefore best described as an **extension by domain-application**: she takes FCM's third-form *conclusion* (profound boredom is the disclosure, via a crisis of everyday meaning, of Dasein's groundless finitude) and transplants it, largely intact and uncontested, onto the film-viewing situation via Loht's Mitsein-based bridge — rather than revising, contesting, or even fully rehearsing Heidegger's own argumentative machinery for reaching that conclusion. She does not contest Heidegger's account of boredom itself anywhere in the essay; her reservations (§5, "Heideggerian Film-Philosophy(?)") are about the scope of applying Heidegger's philosophy *as a whole* to cinema, not about the boredom analysis specifically.

## 5. Findings/Claims

1. Loht's viewing-Dasein argument establishes film viewing as a mutually disclosive relation — occasioning "the viewer's disclosedness of the film" and, reciprocally, the film's disclosure to the viewer (Loht 2017, p.108, qtd. p.36) — Quaranta's baseline, not her own finding (FAITHFUL-Quaranta, p.36).
2. Boredom, especially in its profound form, is a ground mood capable of eliciting "spectatorial experiences and disclosure of meaning" for viewers and of "allowing characters' being in the film world" (FE, p.32).
3. "Aesthetics of mood" — specific stylistic choices (dead-time, silence, slowness, repetition, fixed shots, monotonous ambient/engine noise) — can aesthetically arouse profound boredom in a viewer, given the viewer's own disposedness to be "assailed"; the film alone is insufficient, since moods are "neither subjective nor objective" (FAITHFUL-Quaranta, p.38).
4. The Hokkaido car-drive sequence in *Drive My Car* is read as literalizing FCM's temporal thesis: iterative shots and unbroken engine noise stretch time and produce indifference on the characters' faces, priming a subsequent self-disclosive dialogue (Kafuku's confession) as the moment where profound boredom's disclosive potential is realized narratively (FE, pp.39–41).
5. The climactic "two hands" shot is read, via Loht's discussion of Heidegger on artworks and the Rashomon hand-shot, as an image whose disclosive force "overflows representation," unconcealing both solitude and being-with (FE, pp.40–41).
6. Contemporary everydayness is itself "boresome" — profound boredom is disclosive precisely because it arises from and reveals "the exceedingly fast-changing, lonesome world" of the present (FE, p.41).
7. Quaranta remains "sceptical" of a fully Heideggerian film-philosophy: it risks excluding the ethical dimension, over-concentrating on Malick/Antonioni, ignoring Heidegger's own critique of film, and — most importantly — has no account of the body, a limit the embodied phenomenological tradition (Sobchack, Barker, Marks) addresses more successfully (FE, pp.43–44).
