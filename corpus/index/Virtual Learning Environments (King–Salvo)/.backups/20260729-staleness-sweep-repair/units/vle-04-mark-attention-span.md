## A. Catalog (ascending by book page)

### Attention constitutes experience (p. 30)
1. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 30]** "But also importantly, James believed that our choice of what we pay attention to is consequential, as we construct our life experience this way: 'Millions of items of the outward order are present to my senses which never properly enter into my experience. Why? Because they have no interest for me. My experience is what I agree to attend to. Only those items which I notice shape my mind—without selective interest, experience is an utter chaos.'" (Mark quoting William James; her n. 3)
2. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 30]** "In other words, James believed that what we decide to pay attention to becomes part of our lived experience. I might be walking in a beautiful garden and have my cell phone out. I am texting with a friend, and I'm trying to spell correctly and dodge autocorrect, which often guesses wrong. It is the texts that have entered into the record of my experience, and not the softness of the ground, the trill of the warbler or the scarlet of the azaleas."

### The attentional system (pp. 32–36)
3. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, p. 32]** Attention as networks in different brain regions composing an attentional system: **Alerting** (maintaining vigilance during a task), **Orienting** (prioritizing/selecting stimuli — the manager's email, the chime), **Executive control** (managing interference from irrelevant stimuli; restraining responses to distraction).
4. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, p. 33]** **Executive function** — focusing attention and pursuing goals; manages prioritizing and switching tasks, decision-making, sustaining and allocating attention, working memory, self-regulation.
5. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, p. 34]** **Limited pool of attentional/cognitive resources** (50+ years of research); performance suffers when required resources exceed those available.
6. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, pp. 35–36]** **Pupil diameter increases with cognitive load.** (Direct methodological warrant for P1/P2's pupil measures.)

### Framing and the rote trap (pp. 51–55)
7. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE (near-verbatim), p. 51]** Framing errors: "When you make a conscious decision to do something, you likely frame your choice, but you probably don't realize you are doing so…. Your situation, your emotional state and your mental energy all set the stage and will likely influence what you choose to do."
8. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE (near-verbatim), p. 55]** The rote attention trap: "The happiness itself becomes a reward and keeps you bound to the rote activity. This is why people spend so many hours watching TikTok." Rote activities = stimulus–response with immediate gratification (laughter, points, levels, winning); Thorndike (1911) law of effect; "Intermittent reinforcement strengthens the habit of engaging in the activity until it becomes ingrained."

### The attentional-states framework (pp. 70–73) — THE KEY THEORETICAL ARTIFACT
9. **[MARK-FIGURE, p. 70]** Quadrant diagram: **engagement (high/low) × challenge (high/low)** → **Focus** (high/high), **Rote** (high engagement / low challenge), **Bored** (low/low), **Frustrated** (low engagement / high challenge).
10. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE (near-verbatim), pp. 70–71]** **Focus** = "a temporal state when people feel absorbed in an activity and the activity requires some degree of challenge to a person's skill set. Being highly engaged and challenged in work is correlated with motivation, activation, concentration, creativity, and satisfaction."
11. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE (near-verbatim), p. 71]** **Rote** = "When people are highly engaged, but not at all challenged … Rote activity is mechanical and routine."
12. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE (near-verbatim), pp. 71–72]** **Bored** = "When people are not very engaged and not very challenged … Surfing the internet, flitting from site to site, and barely sticking around long enough to read a few sentences … boredom uses few cognitive resources, or put more aptly, it **underuses available resources**. Our dimensions of low challenge and low engagement are consistent with how boredom has been considered, as **a state of low arousal**: boring activities just don't provide much stimulation, making it difficult to concentrate on the activity."
13. **[MARK-VERBATIM (endnote screenshot)]** Her boredom sources: n. 17 Mikulas & Vodanovich, "The essence of boredom," *Psychological Record* 43.1 (1993): 3; n. 18 Fisher, "Boredom at work: a neglected concept," *Human Relations* 46.3 (1993): 395–417.
14. **[AUTHOR-NOTE]** "German word for boredom is *Langeweile*, which translates into 'long length of time'." (The notes already reach for the FCM etymology — *lange Weile*, the long while.)
15. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, pp. 72–73]** **Frustrated** = highly challenged but not engaged.

### Attentional state and distractibility (p. 80)
16. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE (near-verbatim), p. 80]** "A rote or bored attentional state provides an easy entry point for distractions…. The attentional state one is in makes one susceptible to distractions. … When we're bored, our attention is not goal-directed … Attention is driven by goals, and without a strong goal, like when you're bored or doing rote activity, then your attention is like **a reed in the wind**."

### Multitasking (p. 86)
17. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 86]** Multitasking predates screens: Monica Smith (*A Prehistory of Ordinary People*) — "multitasking has been around for over 1.5 million years, since our bipedal ancestors began to build tools. Humans relied on multitasking to survive as they had to continually monitor their environment…"

### The forty-seven seconds (pp. 93–101)
18. **[MARK-VERBATIM, pp. 93–94; typed lead at 93]** Screen attention duration declining: 2004 ≈ "one hundred fifty seconds (two and a half minutes)" before switching; 2012 ≈ 75 s; 2016–2021 steady "between forty-four and fifty seconds." Replications: Meyer et al. (Microsoft Research), 20 developers over 11 workdays ≈ 50 s; Fatema Akbar (dissertation), 50 office workers over 3–4 weeks ≈ 44 s. "…people switch their attention on computer screens **about every forty-seven seconds on average**."
19. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, p. 94]** 2016 **median** attention duration = 40 s (half of all observations shorter).
20. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, p. 97]** **Attention residue** (Sophie Leroy): what one looked at previously interferes with current viewing, "especially if the current content is not engaging"; attentional AND emotional residue impede focused attention.
21. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, p. 99]** More work mediated through computers → more switching opportunities; attention span on any screen "has reached a steady state of about forty-seven seconds."
22. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, pp. 100–101]** In a studied workplace, roughly **every 12 minutes** a significant disruption lasting ≥ 2 minutes.

### Development, young users (page unmarked — verify; her n. 40 nearby)
23. **[MARK-VERBATIM, page unmarked]** "One thing we should worry about with very young children using digital media so much is that self-control and executive function are not yet mature. These functions develop throughout childhood, reaching similar competencies to adults at around age ten."
24. **[AUTHOR-NOTE — the dissertation author's own design idea]** "Thus VR with eye tracking and data logging that analyzes attention span could run in the background and alert parents/drs when they begin to approach problematic focus behaviors." (→ §III.5 seed: ambient attention-span instrumentation as a VLE design feature; connects to FCM design-implication #1, the surface–depth disagreement index. Author voice — never attribute to Mark.)
25. **[AUTHOR-PARAPHRASE, page unmarked — verify]** College students' attention span averages ~48 s on computer/phone screens before switching; habitual switchers are more easily distracted even off-device.

### The attention-capture economy (pp. 154–165) — the dark-pattern counterpoint
26. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 154]** "In early psychometric studies, people's cognitive abilities were measured first with physical tests, and then with surveys, which is how IQ scores came about. But **surveys and tests are no longer needed when sensors can unobtrusively detect people's physiological signals** and tracking can detect digital traces of their online behaviors."
27. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 155]** Ad-testing physiology: "…tracking and facial expressions to determine how much they sweat, what exactly they look at on the ad, and what emotions the ad elicits. When a person sweats, it means they are aroused…"
28. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 155]** "The algorithms incorporate this information—who you are, how you feel, what you do when and where—and then use it to **capture your attention**."
29. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 157]** "**Digital phenotyping** refers to collecting data that people produce in their online behavior and using it to measure things about them, such as their mood and cognition. The Greek root *pheno* means 'to appear'…"
30. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 157]** CAS/Nanyang study: gender, age, population density, interactions, privacy settings, username length, linguistic patterns "correlated quite well with a person's emotional state and psychological well-being"; more first-person *I* → less positive affect.
31. **[MARK-VERBATIM, pp. 157–158]** Major depressive disorder predictable from public Twitter data, "or even from your Instagram photos."
32. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 158]** "…if the algorithm analyzes just three hundred of your likes, it knows your personality better than your significant other." And: "Algorithms can be engineered to affect our attention through leveraging knowledge about our personality…"
33. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 159]** Facebook patent: "Determining user personality characteristics from social networking system communications and characteristics"; personality-matched feeds → more attention → more time on platform.
34. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 159]** Social notifications tap "curiosity about others"; "While some notifications require controlled attention to respond to them, others leverage **involuntary or automatic attention**. Many algorithms are designed to capture our attention because they elicit integral emotions from us such as happiness…"
35. **[MARK-VERBATIM, pp. 159–160, unmarked tail]** "…surprise, fear or disgust. These types of basic emotional reactions occur spontaneously and automatically without cognitive processing and are called **lower-order emotions**. … If you receive targeted notifications going after your lower-order emotions **when you are low in attentional resources or in a bored or a rote state, when you are more susceptible to distraction, you can't help but respond to them**."
36. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 161]** Frances Haugen testimony: Facebook weighted angry-emoji posts to the top of feeds; "Facebook's AI program was tweaked to deliberately provide content to people that could be harmful"; later re-weighted toward "love"/"sad," untested outside the company.
37. **[MARK-VERBATIM, p. 165]** TikTok: "The laughter caused by watching the video triggers an endogenous opioid release in those parts of the brain that process rewards … within fifteen seconds, TikTok videos can reward us with laughter … the more time we spend on TikTok, the more we are bonded to the app to reap the rewards."

### Behaviorist coda (pp. 252–253)
38. **[MARK-VERBATIM, pp. 252–253]** "Strict behaviorist psychologists considered that human behavior is molded through exposure to stimuli in the environment… B. F. Skinner believed that human behaviors, or for that matter the 'minds and hearts of men and women,' are changed by the contingencies in the social and physical environment. **To Skinner, human cognition is a fallacy.** He argued that our environments condition us in how we behave in our daily lives."

## B. Integration digest — how this serves the Part III introduction

### Thread 1: justification for analyzing human engagement with digital artifacts
- **The James anchor (p. 30, #1–2):** attention *constitutes* lived experience — the phenomenological warrant, in Mark's own framing, for treating attention/engagement as the right unit of analysis for digital environments. Strong epigraph or opening-move candidate for §III.0; it also rhymes with the dissertation's A₀→A₄ premise (what enters the chain is what shapes the *hexis*).
- **The empirical mandate (pp. 93–101, #18–22):** attention on screens has collapsed to ~47 s average / 40 s median, with heavy switching costs (attention residue, #20) — digital artifacts are now the *default habitat* of attention, so studying engagement there is studying attention as such.
- **The methodological turn (pp. 154–157, #26, #29):** sensors and behavioral traces now measure what surveys once did — Mark herself narrates the field's move to unobtrusive physiological measurement. This is the *direct* justification for the King–Salvo program's EEG / eye-tracking / HMD-telemetry approach (and #6, pupil–load, warrants the pupil measures specifically).
- **The stakes (pp. 155–165, #27–37):** industry already instruments attention — asymmetrically, for capture. Academic analysis of engagement with digital artifacts is not optional curiosity; it is the counterweight.

### Thread 2: justification for improving attention span *using* digital artifacts
- **States, not a scalar (pp. 70–73, #9–15):** Mark's framework already refuses a single engagement axis — attention comes in *kinds* (focus/rote/bored/frustrated). This legitimates designing environments that produce a *kind* of attention, not merely "more engagement."
- **Design levers she names:** goal-strength governs distractibility ("a reed in the wind," p. 80, #16) → design for strong, present goals; reward structure governs the rote trap (p. 55, #8) → design rewards that serve the task, not the platform; residue and disruption cadence (pp. 97–101, #20–22) → design pacing and washout.
- **The author's own seed (#24):** ambient VR eye-tracking as background attention-span monitoring — a constructive, care-oriented use of exactly the instrumentation the capture economy uses adversarially. Belongs in §III.5 beside Wendt principle 10 (advocate preferable worlds; refuse dark patterns) and FCM design-implication #1 (the surface–depth disagreement index as the thing worth instrumenting).

### Hooks into the existing apparatus (use with provenance care)
1. **Rote ↔ the second form.** Mark's *rote* state — occupied, mechanical, low-challenge, "an easy entry point for distractions" — is the closest psychological cousin of Heidegger's being-bored-*with* (occupied surface, hollow depth). Her framework *sees* the surface occupation but reads it as engagement-without-challenge; the FCM apparatus reads the same profile as a boredom *form*. That contrast is productive, not contradictory: her quadrant classifies *levels* on two axes; the three forms classify *structures*. (Tag any such mapping PROJECTED.)
2. **"Bored = low arousal" (p. 72, #12) vs the eye-tracking windfall.** P2 empirically found **high-arousal boredom** (the searching gaze) alongside the low-arousal "zombie stare." Mark's low-arousal characterization matches only the latter. The King–Salvo data thus *complicates* the standard low-arousal account Mark inherits (her nn. 17–18) — a genuine, citable point of dissertation originality; FCM's first form (restless *Zeitvertreib*) is what the standard account misses.
3. ***Langeweile* (#14):** the notes' own etymological bullet is the door from Mark's psychology to FCM's temporality (the long while; *Hingehaltenheit*; felt-time dilation as P1's cleanest datum).
4. **Attention residue (#20) ↔ the carryover problem.** Leroy's residue is the psychological name for what P2 observed (boring-first exhaustion) and what FCM leaves untheorized (boredom's aftermath; design-implication #6, "the one place the traffic runs from the lab toward the philosophy"). Three-way triangulation available: Leroy/Mark (psych) + P2 order-effect (empirical) + FCM open problem (phenomenological).
5. **Capture economy ↔ phantasia-conscription and *bia*.** "Algorithms … capture your attention" via lower-order emotions in bored/rote states (#28, #35): in RO vocabulary, design that conscripts the *phantasia* and bypasses deliberation while the chain is stalled. §III.5's ethical line: the same knowledge that lets a platform capture a stalled chain lets a VLE *restart* one. (Wendt principle 10 is the norm; Mark supplies the adversary's playbook.)
6. **Skinner coda (#38) — the differentiation obligation.** Mark's behaviorist framing ("environments condition us") superficially resembles the dissertation's designed-*Umwelt* A₀ thesis. §III.0 or §III.1 should mark the difference explicitly: the RO account keeps the agent's efficient-final causality (the designer occasions, never causes — producing-art/using-art hinge); Skinner's "cognition is a fallacy" is precisely what the actualization chain denies. One paragraph of differentiation prevents a category-mistake reading of the whole Part.
