## Book Overview

Thomas Rickert's *Ambient Rhetoric: The Attunements of Rhetorical Being* (2013) argues that rhetoric is fundamentally ambient—that it is the always-ongoing disclosure of the world that shifts our manner of being in that world, rather than a discrete symbolic act performed by autonomous human agents upon passive audiences. Drawing primarily on Martin Heidegger's phenomenology (particularly his concepts of *Dasein*, attunement/*Stimmung*, dwelling, and the fourfold), contemporary cognitive science (embodied/embedded cognition), new materialisms (Latour, Harman, Barad), and twentieth-century ambient music, Rickert contends that rhetoric must be reconceived as ontological rather than merely epistemic. The book's central metaphor, *terroir*—the irreducible somewhereness of wine rooted in earth, climate, practice, and place—establishes from the outset that rhetoric, like winemaking, is grounded in material relations that exceed human intention and control.

The book divides into two parts. Part 1, "Diffractions of Ambience," performs genealogical and deconstructive work on classical rhetorical concepts—*chōra*, kairos, networks, and ambient music—to loosen their humanist/subjectivist encrustation and prepare them for ambient reframing. Part 2, "Dwelling with Ambience," constructs a positive account of ambient rhetoric through sustained engagement with Burke and Heidegger on language/attunement, a theory of the "rhetorical thing" (objective, subjective, ambient), Latour's fourfold/thing theory, and a practical case study on dwelling and ecological sufficiency. The Conclusion addresses Heidegger's silence, movement, and disclosure as the ongoing work of ambient rhetoric.

Rickert is explicit that he seeks neither to supersede nor to replace older understandings of rhetoric but to *attune* us to what they exclude: the material, the withdrawn, the nonhuman, and the ambient conditions from which rhetorical situations emerge. The book is primarily ontological and speculative rather than offering a ready-made methodology for rhetorical practice—though practice implications surface in the case-study chapters (Ch 3–4, 8).

## Provisional Claims (10 main theses)

1. **Rhetoric is ambient**: it is the always-ongoing disclosure of the world shifting our manner of being in that world, not a discrete symbolic act (Preface xii).
2. **Rhetoric is ontological**: it has to do with being and not just knowing; it is grounded in material relations from which it springs (Preface xii, xv).
3. **Agency is distributed**: across human and nonhuman assemblages; the idea of mind as engine of reason is dethroned in favor of complex feedback loops (Intro 10).
4. **"Environment" must be reconceived as ecology**: not a passive container/backdrop for human action but a relational, participatory, mutually constitutive field (Preface x, Intro 5–6).
5. **Attunement is ontologically prior**: it precedes cognition, volition, and intention; it is how the world is already disclosed before we reflectively attend to it (Intro 8–9, 14).
6. **Classical rhetorical concepts need recovery**: *chōra*, kairos, *periechon* have been narrowed by humanist tradition and need genealogical refiguration (Intro 11).
7. **Material/affective dimensions are integral, not supplementary**: materiality, embodiment, affect are not additions to rhetoric but constitutive of it (Preface xii–xiii).
8. **The "extraction model" of rhetoric is inadequate**: the comedian/rhetorician extracting salient material from situations misses the ambient conditions that generate those situations (Preface xiii).
9. **Rhetoric transforms dwelling**: not just subjective states but our fundamental disposition concerning how we are in and dwell in the world (Preface xiii).
10. **Cognitive science and Heidegger converge**: embodied/embedded cognition, when pushed beyond its Cartesian remnants, resonates with Heideggerian phenomenology on situatedness, worldliness, and the inadequacy of subject/object models (Intro 10, 16–20).

## Candidate Tensions (5 to watch for)

1. **Scope of nonhuman agency**: Rickert insists on distributed agency but also says humans are "particularly important" participants (Preface xviii). How far does nonhuman agency actually extend, and does it risk becoming undifferentiated?

2. **Critical distance within ambience**: If rhetoric is ambient and we are always already attuned, where does critical evaluation or resistance come from? Can one critique from *within* ambience, or does the framework absorb all opposition?

3. **Ontological claims vs. practical payoff**: The book is primarily ontological/speculative. Rickert acknowledges this ("Much will remain to be done," Preface xvi). Does the ontological reframing generate actionable insights for rhetorical practice, or does it remain at the level of philosophical revision?

4. **Heidegger's politics and the ambient project**: Rickert draws heavily on Heidegger (abbreviations list 17 Heidegger works). The later Heidegger's relationship to National Socialism and his "silence" (addressed in the Conclusion) create a tension: can ambient rhetoric selectively appropriate Heidegger's ontology without inheriting its political baggage?

5. **Terroir as metaphor vs. argument**: *Terroir* opens the book as a vivid illustration, but Rickert says "I adopt none of these positions" (Preface xi) about the terroir debate itself. Does *terroir* do genuine conceptual work throughout the book, or does it function primarily as a framing device that fades after the Preface?
