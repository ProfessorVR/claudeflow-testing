## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### ch3-s1: [Opening] (pp. 99--102)
- Rickert frames the chapter as bringing his discussions of *chora* and kairos into conversation with complexity theory and network culture, arguing that networks and complexity can augment a theory of ambient rhetoric (99--100).
- Complexity's importance for rhetoric and composition was first broached by Phelps, Cooper, and Lanham; this chapter continues that line of inquiry (100).
- The chapter plan: (1) connect ambient rhetoric to *chora* and kairos via complexity and networks; (2) illuminate ambience's practicality through concrete examples (Eno, ambientROOM); (3) show limitations of the network concept; (4) revisit kairos through new materialist perspectives (100--101).
- Networks rework but cannot escape place; "actuality" was always networked, and dwelling has always been incarnated and materialized---what we learn from digital networks is not so much new as differently disclosed (101--102).
- "House" is resonant with *chora*: a place where movement resides and generation sets forth; networks are not a structural epiphenomenon but an ontological way of being-in-relation-and-movement (102).

### ch3-s2: (Haunted) Houses in Motion: Networks as a Principle of Being (pp. 102--110)
- Language, like a house, gathers, bears up, and shelters relations; it is ambient, built from elements within the world as well as by human being (102--103). Heidegger's claim that "language is the house of Being" is reframed: language stems from world as a composite of meaning and matter, and the world comes to speaking in language (102--103).
- Mark Taylor's complexity-theoretic account of network culture: connectivity becomes overconnectivity at tipping points where "more is different"; writing is a complex adaptive system far from equilibrium (104).
- Taylor treats writing as incarnational networking: the writer is a "nodular subjectivity" caught in coadaptive threads; a subject emerges as a node *because* of the network, not prior to it (104).
- Rickert augments Taylor: while Taylor grasps that the network is ontological and not just technical, his account of *incarnational* networking undervalues materiality, embodiment, and the body's ambient environment---the physical world as abode (105).
- Rickert pushes beyond node/connection/web metaphors toward metaphors of meshing, osmosis, and blending; Being (all that has being) comes to us as the world-meaning surroundings that give rise to what brings itself forth (105).
- Ambience is less driven by *connection* and more resonant with *immersion* and *permeation*; it adds emphasis on the constitutive role of the blended environment that the network metaphor does not offer (106).
- Ambience attunes us to modalities of attention that are postconscious and auratic; individuation, singularity, objectness are ongoing achievements worked out of an ambient chorography that entangles and emplaces (106).
- Ambience furthers the collapse Taylor captures regarding the autonomous, willing subject; but a consideration of ambience suggests that writing is written more by the situation itself---composed of multiple entangled actants, human and nonhuman (107).
- The concept of ambience would begin from a newly materialist theoretical space that understands the world as its own best representation; ambience puts place, language, and body into coadaptive, vital, buoyant interaction (107).
- The *timeliness* of a given situation is crucial to ambience, manifesting a kairotic dimension; every decision is "worlded" and finds a tipping point that gives it over to being different---ambience seeks to include but go beyond the incarnational network logic Taylor describes (107).

### ch3-s3: Practices of Ambience: Brian Eno (pp. 110--117)
- Music is an entry point because the term *ambient* is most culturally associated with music; music's ambience encompasses production site, recording qualities, spatial environment, and reception context (108).
- Eno, a self-described nonmusician, began exploring "ambient music" in the mid-1970s; predecessors include Steve Reich, Stockhausen, Terry Riley, the Berlin school of electronica (108).
- The origin story of *Discreet Music* (1975): Eno, bedridden after an accident, heard a record at near-inaudibility merging with environmental sounds (rain, light), presenting "a new way of hearing music"---as part of the ambience of the environment (109).
- Eno's *Discreet Music* disrupts foreground/background binaries: the "music" cannot be separated from competing sounds of wind, rain, mood setters; discreteness dissolves into a holistic soundscape (110).
- Eno's compositional method---tape loops, chance, long-delay echo systems---produces music as an emergent phenomenon, a "moment of complexity" where disparate elements combine into discontinuous new order; the locus of creation is dispersed to include the environment (110).
- Systems and ambience conjoin: Eno designs systems that, once set into operation, create music with little intervention, scrambling the roles of composer and audience, actant and recipient; the music itself gains complexity through interaction with the environment (110--111).
- Eno's achievement fulfills what artists and writers desire: not homeostasis but "the creation of something 'one could never predict'"; feedback is not just communication between entities but an ontological manifestation of dynamic entanglement (111).
- Heidegger's I-situation: "The I does not need to be in view, it flows with the situation" (*TDP* 154); the submergence of "view" into flow is integral to the generation of the unexpected, of disclosure and production beyond any simple intent (111).
- Allen Scult connects the I-situation to Heidegger's "language is the house of being": the "isness" comes to us through a primordial rhetorical making-known of our situated being-in-the-world (111--112).
- Ambience is material and spatial, materially embedded in place not as a static description but as a plastic, open-ended, evolving event; place must be reconceived in accordance with *chora* so that distinctions between subjective/objective, nature/culture collapse into a more originary weddedness (112--113).
- The virtual is not ontologically lesser than the actual: "Anything that can be experienced at all is a possible something" (Heidegger, *TDP* 88); "virtuality" is simply a mode of being-in-networks, just different in affordances, hindrances, and intensities (113).
- Eno's albums *Ambient 1: Music for Airports* and *Ambient 4: On Land* create fictional landscapes that, when inhabited through listening, dissolve the distinction between artificially created and "real" aural spaces; Eno shifts from replicating spaces to inventing nonrepresentational spaces (113--114).

### ch3-s4: The ambientROOM Project (pp. 117--121)
- The MIT Tangible Media Group's ambientROOM project (Ishii, Wisneski et al.) applies ambience as a design paradigm for information distribution, reconsidering how we inhabit place in the information age (114).
- The ambientROOM is a personal interface *environment* that "displays information through subtle cues of sound, light, or motion easily relegated to the periphery of awareness," inspired by natural phenomena (wind, sunlight, rainforest sounds) (115).
- The ambientROOM is explicitly *not* a network: emphases on peripherality, auratics, and osmotics blur connective lines and nodes; instead we experience a rhetorical encounter, an ongoing event coaxing salience out of the holistic ambient environs (115).
- The transformative rhetorical aspect at work is inhabitation: a person in the ambientROOM *is* the situation; changes in the room result in changes in the person and vice versa---mood, affect, emotion are grounded in the design of the room (115).
- Ambient media have a learning effect analogous to "driving a car": perception changes based on familiarity with the environment, constituting a direct implementation of Heidegger's I-situation (115).
- Salience is not forgone but emerges from a holistic environment conceived on ambient principles; without ambience, without attunement, there is no salience (116).
- Eno and the ambientROOM together demonstrate that ambience connotes not just surroundings but a dynamic, immersive environment composed of many coadapting elements producing new levels of "order"---it is *emergent*, an ecology of elements and forces in holistic transformation (116).
- Two crucial aspects of ambient rhetoric invoked: (1) the focus on eventful generation in place conceived ambiently corresponds to *chora*; (2) kairos, a tipping point where change is an opportune moment---but this opportunity belongs to the situation catalyzing itself, not to an actant taking advantage (116--117).

### ch3-s5: Kairos and Network (pp. 121--125)
- Kinneavy's understanding of kairos as "the right or opportune time to do something" remains dominant but is largely Platonic (117).
- Bernard Miller, following Untersteiner and Heidegger, argues that kairos is an experience or encounter, not merely a concept; for Gorgias, a decision is "willed by *kairos*" (Untersteiner 181)---kairos wills the human in a situation (117).
- The Platonic kairos assumes an autonomous, self-willing subject; Miller's Heideggerian path sees kairos as linked with logos to the extent that we are within them, caught up in willing that wills us (118).
- Kairos is linked with logos insofar as we are taking part in them; ambience gives place for the intricate entwinement of logos and kairos; "language is a situation, a world, brought forth and disclosed in words" (Worsham 226) (118).
- Taylor on writing and complexity: the time of writing is not linear flow but "strange loops governed by nonlinear dynamics"; past, present, and future are knotted together, conditioning and transforming each other (118).
- For Taylor, kairos aligns with the tipping point of complexity theory: the moment at the edge of chaos when a system transforms to exhibit a new level of order; the writer is extended into the very dynamics of ambience (119).
- Writing as emergent phenomenon: writers are not "in control"; they are written in the act of writing; ideas emerge in the complexity of interaction beyond individual control; the writer contributes as catalyst and site of disclosure, not as sole producer and controller (119).
- For Eno, composing becomes a process whereby one is spoken or played in the speaking or playing; the kairotic moment is one of having been willed rather than of willing (119--120).
- Taylor grasps that the network is a principle of being, ontological and not just technical; networks metastasize and become networks all the way down (120).
- Mind, Taylor states, "is distributed throughout the world"; kairos renders agency as something distributed, worldly, transcending the individual human being (121).
- Rickert's critique: for all the talk of incarnation, Taylor's networks are impoverished in materiality---"dry; there are specters, ghosts, distributions, nodes, relays, and tipping points---plenty of electric juice but no wells. There are no thought springs. Connection is not thought deeply or vibrantly enough; the idea of nodes seems too stable. There is a dearth of ambience" (121).

### ch3-s6: Not at Home: Moving beyond the Network (pp. 125--129)
- Rickert prefers "the metaphor of the ambient" over the network: ambience is immersive, osmotic, peripheral; it suggests tactile connections beyond already established points or nodes (122).
- The link gives little leeway with ephemeral, auratic modalities of everyday life; the network is ultimately still invested in binaries (movement/node, line/point); ambience includes choric engagement and interaction beyond the link (122).
- The attunement to what is choric reminds us we cannot reduce the world to simple presence; ambience is better suited to gathering kairos and logos into a worldliness that includes but transcends the human (122).
- Barrett J. Mandel's "The Writer Writing Is Not at Home" (1980): Mandel contrasts with Taylor, emphasizing that consciousness in writerly production is emergent, arising out of sources and interactions not given to thought as it thinks and writes (124).
- Jasper Johns's artistic practice illustrates kairos conceived as ambient: Johns does not imagine or hypothesize changes; he does them materially; the world takes part in human doing, telling Johns what is fitting for the lithograph---a matter of rightness and fittingness, not conscious "better" (124).
- Eno on the creative process: "As soon as you externalize an idea, you see facets of it that weren't clear when it was just floating around in your head" (qtd. in Grant 28); error is not failure but one more move, emerging from what is choric in a kairotic moment (125).
- The ambient is not a call to passively hearken or submit but an uncanny, plastically amorphous space of generation; agency derives its bearings from the environment (127).
- Rodney Brooks's "subsumption architecture" robotics: robots dispensing with centralized processing and representation, instead connecting sensors directly to actuators---"The world is its own best model" (Brooks, qtd. 236); agency is a hybrid of coadapting material forces, not a subject choosing a path in an objective world (126--127).
- N. Katherine Hayles: "Modern humans are capable of more sophisticated cognition than cavemen not because moderns are smarter . . . but because they have constructed smarter environments in which to work" (289); cognition is material and ambient, not solely cognitive (127).
- The writer is not merely *in* a situation but *is* a situation, an I-situation; the writer is written by the environment---kairos and *chora*, when ambiently attuned, pick out two different ways for understanding the involvement of the environment in human being (128).
- The I-situation is *being* written, tempered and modulated by material, affective, temporal, and existential vectors (Heidegger, *TDP* 154--55); it condenses the fact that previous writing itself took place in response to an innumerable series of thoughts, sensations, affects, environments, words---most never reaching conscious awareness (128).
- Closing: writing is an art of ambient rhetoric; ambience brings together several strands of thought common with Taylor's network culture but extends them by stretching agency away from its traditional seat in subjects to a full consideration of action as *material, affective, ecological,* and *emergent* (129).

## 3. Key Concepts

### networks
Rickert treats networks as ontological, not merely technological: "networks are not a structural epiphenomenon but an ontological way of being-in-relation-and-movement" (102). Through Taylor's complexity theory, the network becomes a principle of being in which subjects emerge as nodular points within coadaptive threads. However, Rickert argues the network metaphor remains insufficient because it stays invested in binaries (node/link, foreground/background) and lacks the immersive, osmotic, material thickness that ambience requires.

### ambient / ambience
The chapter's central operation is to distinguish ambience from network. Ambience is "immersive, osmotic, peripheral" (122); it connotes not surroundings but a dynamic, emergent ecology of coadapting elements. Ambience is less driven by *connection* and more resonant with *immersion* and *permeation* (106). It attunes us to postconscious, auratic modalities of everyday life. Ambience gathers language, world, kairos, and logos differently than the network does---through blending, meshing, and osmosis rather than linking.

### practice
The chapter's subtitle ("the Practice of Everyday Life") signals that ambient rhetoric is not purely theoretical but manifests in concrete making. Eno's compositional method, the ambientROOM's design paradigm, and Johns's lithography all demonstrate how ambient rhetoric operates in actual creative and technological practice. Practice here is not instrumental application but the site where ontological dynamics become disclosable.

### Eno (Brian Eno's ambient music)
Eno serves as the chapter's primary exemplar of ambient practice. His *Discreet Music* origin story---bedridden, hearing music merge with environmental sounds at near-inaudibility---concretizes the dissolution of foreground/background. His compositional method (tape loops, chance, systems that run with minimal intervention) produces music as emergent phenomenon, dispersing the locus of creation to include environment, technology, and listener. Eno's music demonstrates that the composer becomes a "function" (in Foucault's sense), that feedback is ontological entanglement, and that "the work starts to define you rather than you define it" (qtd. in Prendergast 119).

### ambientROOM
The MIT Tangible Media Group's ambientROOM (Ishii, Wisneski et al.) translates ambient principles into information design. It displays information through subtle environmental cues (sound, light, airflow) relegated to the periphery of awareness, inspired by natural phenomena. Rickert stresses it is explicitly *not* a network: it operates through peripherality, auratics, and osmotics rather than connective lines and nodes. The ambientROOM exemplifies the rhetorical force of inhabitation---changes in the room change the person, and vice versa---and implements Heidegger's I-situation as a concrete design.

### kairos (networked / ambient)
Rickert recasts kairos through complexity theory and Heidegger. Against the Platonic/Kinneavy understanding of kairos as opportune time seized by an autonomous agent, Rickert follows Miller's Heideggerian reading: kairos *wills* the human, not the other way around. Through Taylor, kairos aligns with the "tipping point" of complexity theory---the moment at the edge of chaos where a system transforms. The kairotic moment belongs to the situation catalyzing itself as what will have happened, not to an actant capitalizing on opportunity. Kairos, linked with logos, is the intricate entwinement disclosed in ambience.

### dwelling / home
The chapter opens with the house metaphor (resonant with *chora*) and closes with "not at home." Dwelling has always been networked, incarnated, materialized (101--102). Language is the house of Being, but this house is haunted---uncanny, *unheimlich*. Mandel's essay title ("The Writer Writing Is Not at Home") encapsulates the chapter's arc: the writer is not securely at home in a controlled environment but is dispersed into the ambient situation, written by the I-situation's material, affective, temporal vectors.

### beyond-network
Rickert's critical move is to show that the network metaphor, while ontologically significant, must be supplemented and ultimately surpassed. Taylor's networks are "dry"---they lack materiality, wells, thought springs (121). The network remains invested in binaries; ambience, by contrast, is immersive, osmotic, choric. The chapter argues for metaphors of meshing, osmosis, and blending over node, connection, and web.

### I-situation (*das Situations-Ich*)
Heidegger's early locution for the situational-I, precursor to *Dasein*. Rickert adopts it because it retains a sense of I-ness while dissolving the traditional subject: the I does not need to be in view but flows with the situation (*TDP* 154). The I-situation is "being written" by the material, affective, temporal, and existential vectors of the ambient situation (128). It concretely appears in Eno's compositional practice and the ambientROOM's inhabitation.

### complexity theory
Drawn primarily from Mark Taylor's reading of complexity science: complex adaptive systems operate far from equilibrium; change is discontinuous, emergent, and unpredictable; tipping points produce new levels of order. Rickert finds complexity theory useful for explaining contemporary culture and the dynamics of writing but argues that Taylor's version undervalues materiality and the ambient, remaining too focused on the incarnational logic of the network.

### emergence
The ontological dynamic that connects networks, complexity, ambience, and kairos. Music, writing, art, and information environments all produce meaning and order as emergent phenomena---not planned or controlled by an autonomous agent but arising from the coadaptive interaction of multiple elements (human, nonhuman, material, technological, environmental). Emergence is what makes ambience irreducible to network logic: it is not merely the product of links but of immersive, choric blending.

## 4. Main Positions and Arguments

**P1: Networks are ontological, not merely technological.** Drawing on Taylor's complexity-theoretic account of network culture, Rickert argues that networks describe a mode of being-in-relation-and-movement, not just a technological infrastructure. Subjects emerge as nodular points *because of* the network, not prior to it. Dwelling has always been networked; what digital culture discloses differently is the degree of complexity and the acceleration toward tipping points (102, 104, 120).

**P2: The network metaphor is necessary but insufficient for ambient rhetoric.** While Taylor correctly grasps the network as a principle of being, his account of incarnational networking undervalues materiality, the body, and the physical environment as abode (105). Taylor's networks are "dry"---full of specters, ghosts, and tipping points but lacking wells and thought springs (121). The network remains invested in binaries (node/link, movement/point) that obscure the immersive, osmotic, choric character of ambience (122).

**P3: Ambience exceeds the network through immersion, permeation, and choric generation.** Ambience is less driven by connection and more resonant with immersion and permeation (106). It attunes us to postconscious, auratic modalities; it dissolves the foreground/background binary that even sophisticated network thinking preserves. Where the network offers connection, ambience offers blending, meshing, and osmosis. Ambience captures the "software" play of being and doing that *arises* from the network and also, *chora*-like, creates its condition of possibility (107).

**P4: Eno's ambient music concretely demonstrates ambient rhetoric in practice.** Eno's compositional method---systems that run with minimal intervention, chance operations, environmental incorporation---produces music as emergent phenomenon. The locus of creation is dispersed to include the environment; the composer becomes a function; feedback is ontological entanglement, not just communication (110--111). The *Discreet Music* origin story and *On Land*'s invented landscapes exemplify the dissolution of subject/object, natural/artificial, foreground/background.

**P5: The ambientROOM implements ambient rhetoric as information design.** The MIT project translates ambience into a concrete interface paradigm where information is conveyed through peripheral environmental cues rather than foregrounded displays. It is explicitly not a network; it operates through peripherality and osmotics. The ambientROOM instantiates the I-situation: a person in the room *is* the situation, and changes in room and person are inseparable (115).

**P6: Kairos must be reconceived as ambient---willed by the situation, not seized by the agent.** Against the Platonic/Kinneavy model of kairos as opportune time capitalized upon by an autonomous subject, Rickert follows Miller's Heideggerian reading: kairos wills us, not vice versa (117--118). Through complexity theory, kairos aligns with the tipping point where a system transforms; the kairotic moment belongs to the evolving situation, not to a subject who masters time. Kairos and logos are intricately entwined in ambience (118).

**P7: The writer is not at home but is an I-situation written by the ambient environment.** Through Mandel, Johns, Eno, and Taylor, Rickert argues that writing is an emergent phenomenon in which the writer is extended into the dynamics of ambience. The writer is not "in control" but is a semiaware nodal point, a conduit for a wide, complex array of forces (128). Consciousness in writing is emergent, arising from interactions not given to thought as it thinks and writes. Writing is an art of ambient rhetoric (129).

**P8: Agency is hybrid, distributed, and environmentally derived.** Brooks's subsumption architecture and Hayles's observation about "smarter environments" reinforce Rickert's claim that agency derives its bearings from the environment rather than from an interior subject choosing a path in an exterior world (126--127). Agency is a hybrid of coadapting material forces; to assign characteristics of intelligence and control to a creator-subject is to foreclose the ambient dimension.

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

**T1: The "dearth of ambience" critique of Taylor.** Rickert's claim that Taylor's networks lack materiality and ambience (121) is striking given that Taylor's *incarnational* logic is explicitly about embodiment and materiality. The tension hinges on what counts as sufficient material thickness: Rickert sees Taylor as ultimately retaining a representationalist worldview where subjects use language to represent an objective world (107). But Taylor's own language of incarnation, ghostwriting, and nonlinear dynamics seems already to push toward the material. The question is whether Rickert's critique identifies a genuine gap or a difference of emphasis.

**T2: The status of conscious agency within ambience.** Rickert repeatedly insists that ambient rhetoric does not call for passivity---we are not asked to "passively hearken or submit" (127). Yet the chapter's rhetoric consistently diminishes conscious control: the writer is "not at home," kairos wills us, error is generative, consciousness impedes creation (125). How, practically, does one distinguish the ambient dissolution of the autonomous subject from a call to passivity? The answer seems to lie in *attunement*---an active receptivity---but this remains more gestured at than theorized in the chapter.

**T3: Eno as paradigm case.** Eno's ambient music is a powerful illustration, but it is also an exceptional, highly specialized practice. The chapter's title references "the practice of everyday life" (echoing de Certeau), yet the practices examined---avant-garde composition, MIT research labs, lithography by Jasper Johns---are far from everyday. How ambient rhetoric operates in genuinely mundane practice (conversation, email, bureaucratic writing) remains an open question.

**T4: The ambientROOM's limited scope.** Rickert claims the ambientROOM is "not a network" (115), but the project's own literature describes it in terms of networked information flows, and its designers explicitly position it within ubiquitous computing---a field deeply invested in network architectures. The distinction Rickert draws between network and ambient may be cleaner conceptually than it is in the artifact itself.

**T5: Kairos as willed vs. kairos as tipping point.** The chapter juxtaposes two framings of the reconceived kairos: (1) the Heideggerian/Miller reading where kairos wills us, suggesting a quasi-fatalistic encounter, and (2) the complexity-theoretic tipping point, which is emergent and unpredictable but structurally describable. These two framings do not obviously cohere. The Heideggerian version implies ontological depth and withdrawal; the complexity version implies systemic dynamics that are, in principle, modelable. Whether these are genuinely complementary or merely analogous remains unresolved.
