## 1. Chapter Metadata

- **Title**: "Burke and Heidegger: Rhetoric and the Attunement of Language"
- **Part**: Part 2 --- Dwelling with Ambience, Chapter 5, Pages 159--190
- **One-sentence thesis**: Rickert argues that Burke's theory of symbolic action and Heidegger's ontological account of language, despite their significant differences regarding where meaning is seated (human symbolicity vs. worldly disclosure), converge on the insight that language is always already embedded in material, affective, and relational worldly conditions, and that this convergence grounds a theory of ambient rhetoric in which persuasion is prior to rhetoric's emergence as a discrete symbolic act.

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### ch5-s1: [Opening] (pp. 159--163)
- Rickert frames Chapter 5 as the transition into Part 2's constructive project, announcing that he will examine language's relation to world through Burke and Heidegger to build toward ambient rhetoric.
- The chapter's opening pages (continuing from pp. 159--162 of Part 2's framing) argue that rhetoric is not reducible to the symbolic realm, that affectability precedes and gives place to symbolic action, and that persuasion is prior to rhetoric --- rhetoric's "condition of possibility" (163).
- Diane Davis's work on presymbolic affectability is invoked: persuasion cannot be circumscribed within symbolicity because affectability comes before and gives place to symbolic action, distributed throughout infinitesimal encounters in the world (163).
- Central claim: rhetoric rests in the fact that persuasion is prior to rhetoric; worldly affect, modulated in persuasion, hollows out the space for rhetoric's emergence (163--164).

### ch5-s2: A World of Meaning: Getting at Burkean Things (pp. 164--170)
- Rickert examines Burke's *A Rhetoric of Motives*, beginning with Burke's argument that rhetoric is the use of language (symbolicity) as inducement to action (164, citing *RM* 42).
- Burke distinguishes two kinds of topics: commonplaces derived from opinion (epistemological content) and formal topics that earn their status by "abstracting some formal or procedural element" (*RM* 57), bringing language's orderings into rhetoric's persuasive work (164).
- The materiality of language is stressed: persuasion is locked into language's mechanics; the anticipatory pulsion toward participating in a formal pattern once it is set constitutes an affective, material dimension of rhetoric (165).
- Burke's concept of "fittingness" parallels Hawhee's account of language emerging from presymbolic gesture: bodily gesture grows into language but retains gestural forms in rhythm, tone, tic, image, and idea (Hawhee, *Moving* 107, 111) (165).
- Burke addresses nonverbal persuasive elements: equipment in a doctor's office "also appeals as imagery" (*RM* 171); material objects, practices, and discourses persuade by reason of their symbolic character (166).
- Rickert's key reading of Burke on *meaning*: fire is not merely a sensed phenomenon but a full rhetorical participant whose material integration into the human world necessitates ideas; things mean, and insofar as they mean, they bodily invoke and persuade (166--167).
- Burke's concept of "intuition" as *acting-with* (not passive perception): the intuition of a phoebe's song in spring is a response to the *idea* of spring, embedded in a wider orbit of meanings, where the inarticulate "tonal image" stands for a partly inarticulate act (*RM* 175) (167--168).
- Rickert connects Burke to Heidegger on the withdrawal of meaning: Burke acknowledges inarticulate intuition and will not relegate it to the dustbin, but insists that acting-with's orbit circles meaning, not intuition (168).
- Burke on the church spire: nonverbal things persuade because "the dialectical realm of ideas is seen to permeate the positive realm of concepts" (*RM* 186); Burke's world matters because it verbs, even if verbs matter because they are bound to bodies (169--170).

### ch5-s3: "Oh, Baby, Baby, It's a Wild World": Coping with Early Heidegger / The Back-and-Forth of Worldliness (pp. 170--178)
- Rickert turns to Heidegger's *Being and Time*: people are always already thrown into a world of involvements; we do not theoretically grasp the world but cope with it through practical engagement, know-how, and tacit background (170--171).
- Language for the early Heidegger emerges from this background: "Discoursing or talking is the way in which we articulate 'significantly' the intelligibility of Being-in-the-world" (*BT* 204); language as discoursing emerges from holistic meanings that already permeate the world (171).
- Heidegger on hearing: we never hear "pure noise" but always hear the creaking waggon, the column on the march; Dasein dwells *alongside* what is ready-to-hand, not leaping from sensations to a world (*BT* 207) (171).
- The early Heidegger of *Being and Time* offers a reading of Burke that is "less a rejoinder than an intensification": Burke pushes toward the idea that everything means; Heidegger closes the gap between intuition and meaning by dissolving the subject-object separation --- there is no bare intuition, only experience already immersed in the world (172).
- Heidegger and Burke share the insight that for both, the world is ultimately a human one (in different senses), and humans retain priority as the source or site for meaning; Dasein is the being through whom Being comes forth (173).
- Rickert then moves to Heidegger's "On the Essence of Truth" (*OET*): truth as correctness (correspondence) is reframed as *aletheia* (unconcealment); a comportment creates an open region (*das Offene*) within which beings come to presence (174--175).
- Key distinction between Burke and Heidegger: Burke's "a way of seeing is also a way of not seeing" (*PC* 70) concerns a *subject's* perspective; Heidegger's un/concealment concerns the *thing's* way of coming to presence --- a terminology works the same way as seeing, opening some possibilities and screening off others, but transcends the subject (175).
- For Burke, the play of seeing/not-seeing is seated in human doing; for Heidegger, the play of concealment/unconcealment is *not solely human doing* (175--176).
- Rickert's summation: rhetoric is not exclusively a symbolic art, nor does it issue solely from human being; rhetoric is fundamentally wedded to the world and emerges within it; rhetoric is a modality of the world's revealing itself, including human being (176).

### ch5-s4: "Into This House We're Born / Into This World We're Thrown": On Being Spoken (pp. 176--182)
- Rickert argues that Burke and Heidegger present overlapping but significantly different theories of language, both contributing to ambient rhetoric (176).
- Both push us to accept that meaning is already written into the world; Burke frequently leaves open a gap from sensorium to meaning, nature to culture, that Heidegger closes (176).
- The later Heidegger's views on language: neither human flourishing nor speaking results solely from human initiative; "language is the house of being" means not linguistic idealism but that we are enclosed by the language that developed with us and thereby foreclosed to what lies beyond it (177).
- Rickert corrects common misreadings of "language is the house of being": it is not that language creates reality (linguistic idealism) but that the house of being is built out of and within the world, as both shelter and opening to directions to be pursued (177, 182).
- Heidegger's discussion of Sputnik (*OWL* 62): a word is not a sign but rather the way things are given their being through language; "hurry" names a technological enframing that prepared Sputnik's possibility; language is the rich background of ordering relations within which symbolicity emerges (179).
- The Japanese dialogue from *On the Way to Language*: aesthetics as a Western conceptual apparatus has no prepared place in the Japanese chorographic background; this illustrates that language emerges within a relational background of land, people, culture, each drawing from and co-responsive to one another (180--181).
- Heidegger on words as "wellsprings" (*What Is Called Thinking?* 130): words are not buckets scooping content but wellsprings simultaneously producing meanings that express and withdraw; we are spoken by words in their saying (181).
- Key separation between Burke and Heidegger on language: Burke grounds signification in the body and includes social drama but at the cost of transubstantiating nonverbal things into symbolic essences; Heidegger furthers ambient rhetoric by providing an ecological theory of language attuned to the world's involvement in human doing and saying, where matter is neither spirited away nor given back to naive realism (182, 186).

### ch5-s5: Rhetoric as Attunement to the World (pp. 182--190)
- Rickert synthesizes: Burke and Heidegger share a view of language suggesting meaning inheres in the world, though Heidegger pushes harder on this than Burke; both see tremendous constitutive power in language (182).
- Burke goes far beyond Heidegger in excavating social struggle and action in the symbolic; Heidegger transforms our sense of what language *is* by (1) attuning us to aspects of language that withdraw from thinking when we define language as symbolicity, and (2) sundering language from humanist grounds (182).
- On the distinction between Burke's "Terministic Screens" and Heidegger's *differentiation*: Burke offers a social constructionist view where language mediates reality, while Heidegger's differentiation is profound --- reality is not "out there" screened by language but disclosed through the relational matrix of language, tools, body, and environment (183).
- Heidegger on Tolkien and *glossopoeia*: inventing a language requires inventing its development, its emergence from a rich ambient scramble of environment; language runs deeper than mediation (184).
- Language is enmeshed within a style of being; the nonverbal ground contains the potential for the verbal; Heidegger leads closer to ambient rhetoric despite Burke's attempts to retain the verbal as a distinct category (185).
- Heidegger's notion of freedom: not autonomous free will but an emergent cascade of action; all beings take part in how they are and what they are in the world; language's a priori weddedness to the world means truth is not propositional correspondence but "certain resonance" with the object's directedness (Heidegger, *OET* 122--23) (185).
- Advertising as a test case for ambient rhetoric: ads disclose the world and place human beings as resources for maximizing advantage; Heidegger's theory requires asking about the ambient background that gives advertising its place and bearing (186--189).
- Rickert's culminating claim: rhetoric emerges from our being-in-the-world; it is a response to the way the world attunes and speaks us; even if we keep rhetoric within language's orbit, language is never simply the doing of human beings; rhetoric is opened through an attunement to ambience (189).
- Final sentence: rhetoric receives its bearings from an attunement to all aspects of worldly existence, human and nonhuman, without mistaking any given presencing as ultimate being; rhetoric shares in a partiality that offers hope for further and enriching unfoldings in rhetorical being (190).

## 4. Main Positions

**P1**: Rhetoric cannot be confined to the symbolic realm; affectability (Davis) precedes and gives place to symbolic action, making persuasion prior to rhetoric as a discrete practice (159--163).

**P2**: Burke's theory of symbolic action, despite its humanist center, already gestures toward ambient rhetoric by recognizing that nonverbal, material elements persuade through their symbolic character --- fire, equipment, architecture all participate rhetorically by virtue of their integration into human meaning-making (164--170).

**P3**: Burke's concept of "intuition" as *acting-with* reveals that meaning is not imposed on the world by a subject but is encountered through embodied, affective engagement; however, Burke ultimately circles back to meaning (not intuition) as ascendant, preserving a gap between sensorium and symbolicity that limits his ambient potential (167--169).

**P4**: Heidegger's early phenomenology of being-in-the-world dissolves the subject-object split more thoroughly than Burke: there is no bare intuition or pure noise, only experience already immersed in a world of practical, meaningful involvements; this intensifies Burke's insights (170--173).

**P5**: Heidegger's concept of *aletheia* (truth as unconcealment) reframes the relation between language and world: a comportment opens a region within which beings come to presence, and every unconcealing is also a concealing. This differs from Burke's "way of seeing / way of not seeing" because for Heidegger the play of concealment is not solely seated in the human subject (174--176).

**P6**: The later Heidegger's claim that "language is the house of being" is not linguistic idealism but the insight that language emerges from and is enmeshed within the relational matrix of world, tools, body, culture, and environment; words are wellsprings, not signs, and we are spoken by language as much as we speak it (177--182).

**P7**: Burke and Heidegger converge in holding that meaning inheres in the world and that language has tremendous constitutive power, but they diverge on the locus: Burke grounds language in human symbolic action (body, social drama), while Heidegger locates language in the ecological disclosure of being, making matter and world co-constitutive rather than secondary (182, 186).

**P8**: Heidegger advances ambient rhetoric beyond Burke in two ways: (1) he attunes us to aspects of language that withdraw from thinking when language is defined as symbolicity, and (2) he sunders language from exclusively humanist grounds, opening rhetoric to nonhuman and material participation (182).

**P9**: Rhetoric, reconceived ambiently, is a response to the way the world attunes and speaks us; persuadability is not the result of rhetorical discourse being uttered but emerges from a materiality and relationality that is a priori --- we are persuaded because being in the world is such that the possibility for persuasion is already there (163, 186, 189).

**P10**: Advertising provides a concrete test case: ambient rhetoric requires asking not whether ads manipulate but what ambient background (hurry, profit, enframing) gives advertising its place, bearing, and speaking; a "sustainable" rhetoric would attend to language's saying rather than rhetoric's shrillest manipulative forms (187--189).

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

**T1 --- Burke's residual humanism vs. Heidegger's anti-humanism**: Rickert credits Burke with rich forward-thinking but repeatedly notes that Burke "swings too far" toward symbolicity, transubstantiating nonverbal things into symbolic essences (186). Yet Rickert also insists he is not simply preferring Heidegger over Burke (169). The tension: can the Burkean emphasis on social drama, identification, and the material body genuinely coexist with Heidegger's dissolution of human privilege, or must one yield?

**T2 --- "We are spoken" and agency**: Rickert approvingly cites Heidegger's claim that "we are spoken by words in their saying" (181) and that language "speaks us" in a chorographic sense. This raises the question of where rhetorical agency resides. If language speaks us, what becomes of the rhetor's capacity for critique, invention, or resistance? Rickert acknowledges the appearance of "mystical determinism" (182) but insists it is not --- though the argument for why it is not remains gestural rather than fully elaborated.

**T3 --- The status of nonverbal things**: Burke argues that nonverbal elements persuade "by reason of their symbolic character" (166); Rickert reads this as both insight and limitation. But Rickert's own account of how things persuade *ambiently* (without symbolic mediation) is primarily negative --- it is what Burke and Heidegger gesture toward --- rather than positively theorized in this chapter. The positive account is deferred to Ch 6 ("The Rhetorical Thing").

**T4 --- Advertising and sustainable rhetoric**: Rickert introduces advertising as a test case (187--189) and gestures toward a "sustainable" rhetoric that attends to language's saying rather than manipulation. But the criteria for distinguishing "sustainable" from "shrill" rhetoric remain undeveloped here; the evaluative framework is deferred to Chs 7--8. This raises the question of whether ambient rhetoric can provide normative grounds or whether it is purely descriptive.

**T5 --- Early vs. later Heidegger**: Rickert draws on both early Heidegger (*Being and Time*) and later Heidegger (*OWL*, *OET*, *PLT*), noting that the later Heidegger bears "a greater affinity to Burke than the later Heidegger does" (170 --- actually the reverse: early Heidegger bears greater affinity). The transition from early to later Heidegger within the chapter is somewhat compressed, and the precise mechanism by which the later Heidegger's language theory (Saying, wellsprings, the house of being) relates to the earlier phenomenology of being-in-the-world could be more explicitly worked through.
