## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### ch2-s1: [Opening] (pp. 74-76)

- Rickert frames the chapter's purpose: to rethink kairos not merely as temporal opportunism but as materially emplaced, thereby developing a richer understanding of environment and kairotic invention.
- He distinguishes his project from a historical reconstruction: he is not claiming ancient Greeks held modern materialist views but rather that materialist concerns surfaced in both eras, warranting comparative and inventive thought.
- The chapter thesis is stated directly: "without a more materialist understanding of emplacement, kairos is an empty concept" (76).
- Rickert positions this chapter as extending Ch. 1's argument on *chora*: both *chora* and kairos are concepts "sufficiently rich to help prepare new forms of disclosure" once freed from subjectivist encrustation (77).
- He connects kairos to the broader ambient project through networks, ecologies, and the role of place in rhetorical theory.

### ch2-s2: Early Greek Understandings of Kairos (pp. 76-80)

- The word *kairos* first appears in Homer's *Iliad* as an adjectival/noun form (*kairion*) referring to the body where penetration by an arrow is easiest — a spatial, bodily, material meaning older than the temporal sense of "opportune time" (77-78).
- Rickert draws on Richard Onians's philological work: *kairos* connotes a "penetrable opening," an aperture or nick (from the English cognate *nick*, "just in the nick of time"), linking spatial vulnerability to temporal opportunity (78-79).
- A weaving metaphor: *kairos* stems from the shuttle's passage through the warp of a loom — the opening is spatial, the shot must pass through a *place*, and the warp is brief, linking time and space (79).
- The three Moirai (Fates) connect *kairos* to fate, destiny, and cosmic apportionment — *moira* as "a portion of the whole" fuses kairos with measure, right proportion, and the fateful (79).
- Eric Charles White synthesizes these meanings: kairos as "a passing instant when an opening appears which must be driven through with force if success is to be achieved" (79). White foregrounds kairos as primarily inventional.
- The Sophists, especially Protagoras and Gorgias, are noted for making kairos a fundamental principle of rhetorical artistry; John Poulakos describes sophistic rhetoric as an art that "seeks to capture in opportune moments that which is appropriate" (79-80).

### ch2-s3: Contemporary Understandings of Kairos (pp. 80-84)

- Michael Carter attempts to harmonize kairos and *stasis* through a Pythagorean notion of "balance" — kairos as navigating differing positions to find workable truths in situations lacking certainty (81).
- Carter's emphasis on harmony risks domesticating kairos into a cognitive balancing act; Rickert notes that Carter's approach nevertheless "prepares a significant amount of ground for ambience to emerge and gain traction" (87).
- Susan Jarratt emphasizes temporality and decorum in sophistic kairos but also highlights the role of local *nomoi* — community-specific customs and laws — expanding kairos beyond mere timing (80).
- Janet Atwill's characterization of kairos as "mastery of the moment" (59) is called "fateful" by Rickert because destiny/fate is precisely what cannot be mastered; the notion of mastery reintroduces subjective control (80).
- Debra Hawhee's "Kairotic Encounters" marks a significant turn: she theorizes kairos as "invention-in-the-middle," a space-time that dissolves the a priori subject-object distinction, where "kairos mediates — or goes 'between' — the outside of the self" (82). Rickert sees Hawhee as proposing a "postmodern, dispersed form of subjectivity" (82).
- Muckelbauer similarly reaches toward a Deleuzian, ontological reading: deciding is not the rhetor's action but the demand's own indistinguishability from the rhetor, "one cannot choose to either accept or refuse the [kairotic] demand" (83).
- Rickert explicitly aligns Hawhee's and Muckelbauer's work with his own ambient arguments for a posthuman, ontological kairos (83).

### ch2-s4: Kairos beyond Subjectivity (pp. 84-88)

- Scott Consigny's *Gorgias: Sophist and Artist* organizes readings of Gorgias into subjectivist, empiricist, and antifoundationalist schools; Rickert argues that all three categories implicitly maintain the subject-object relationship (84-85).
- Consigny characterizes subjectivists (Untersteiner, Miller, White, Vitanza) as denying objectivity; Rickert counters that these theorists are "precisely *not* subjectivist" because their readings of kairos reveal spatial-material dimensions that dissolve the subject-object dichotomy (85).
- Untersteiner's account is pivotal: for Untersteiner, a rhetor's decision is "willed by *kairos*" (181), and the kairotic decision accords not with universals but with "the concrete form of concomitant circumstances, the specific situation" (178). Rickert reads this as showing the situation *gives back* — it is generative, not a passive backdrop (87).
- Bernard Miller differentiates the Platonic kairos (subjective mastery of opportunity) from the Gorgian kairos, which "fuses subject and object, creating a 'meditative correspondence' that slams shut the ontological difference" (88). For Miller, kairos is akin to Heidegger's *Augenblick* — the instant of concerned disclosure in being-in-the-world (88).
- Vitanza links kairos to Nietzsche's "will to power" and "dispersive power" — kairos as material, worldly definiteness that gives rise to dynamic, flowing understanding rather than rationalist logic (88-89).
- Rickert quotes Nietzsche's *Will to Power* (550) at length: the world as "a monster of energy... a sea of forces flowing and rushing together... *This world is the will to power — and nothing besides!*" — to illustrate Vitanza's materialist kairos (89).
- The section closes with Rickert's synthesis: kairos itself, as something ontological, is inseparable from new forms of becoming and disclosure; it is not a conceptual term or occurrent event but part of the ongoing emergence of the situation (90).

### ch2-s5: Kairos and Theories of Distributed Cognition (pp. 88-92)

- Rickert pivots from philological/theoretical recovery to cognitive science: distributed cognition theories support the ambient reading of kairos by showing that thought and invention are not contained within the autonomous subject.
- Gorgias's extemporary oratory before Athenian audiences exemplifies ambient invention: he trusted the moment (*toi kairoi*) rather than relying on pre-prepared mastery, coinventing with the crowd as "varying degrees offloads rhetorical work onto the crowd" (94).
- Andy Clark's work on extended cognition — the subject as a "spatio-temporally extended process not limited to the tenuous envelope of skin and skull" (*Being* 221) — is cited approvingly, though Rickert notes Clark "subscribes to a traditional empiricist view" and thus "does not extend this point as far as could be hoped" (92).
- Clark's account of the subject as a *process* resonates with Rickert's ambient claim that "everyday coping and practices are not a matter of individual, discrete, and precomposed bodies interacting with an exterior, precomposed world of things and discourses but rather at all times compose new brain-body-thing-world circuits" (92).
- Paul Dourish's ethnographic study of air traffic controllers demonstrates how place constitutively distributes cognition: the controllers "manage the airspace by managing the strips" (65), transforming monitoring into embodied physical performance arising around the specific details of the work site (92-93).
- Rickert draws from Dourish the conclusion that "the immediate environment radically distributes the activities of the subjectivities working within it" — no "subject controls" what occurs; rather, actions emerge as willed by the situation because there are no discrete subjects absent their relations and connections (93).
- The section ends by framing invention ambiently: "Viewing kairos with the autonomous, willing subject replaced by an emplaced and dispersed subjectivity suggests kinds of invention attuned less to seeking advantage over or success against an audience than to working with what an audience and a material situation bring forth" (91).

### ch2-s6: Kairos and Ambient Invention in *The Usual Suspects* (pp. 92-98)

- Rickert uses Bryan Singer's film *The Usual Suspects* (1995) as an extended case study to demonstrate ambient invention in practice, noting that the film is "explicitly about rhetoric as a social and verbal art" (Blakesley, 234).
- The character Verbal Kint (Kevin Spacey) constructs an elaborate fabricated narrative during police interrogation by drawing inventional material from the ambient environment — names from a bulletin board, a coffee cup (Kobayashi), office details — weaving environmental cues into his tale (96).
- A traditional reading would cast Verbal as a clever Socratic/kairotic inventor who capitalizes on misfortune through subjective skill. But Rickert argues this reading fails: "the kairotic situation cannot be adequately explained without taking account of such human interaction" but the film goes further, showing the office space itself as "a coinventor" (96).
- The film demonstrates that "the environment is always situating us in arrangements that simultaneously unleash some possibilities and foreclose on others" — ambient environs generate affordances that invent us in kairotic moments (96).
- Subjects do not exist as separate from world but as "a complex folding within other complex foldings of material and discursive force" (97). Choices in the film are "always orchestrated or otherwise influenced from afar... not predetermined, yet there is clearly little or no autonomy, no free will in the customary sense common to possessive individualism" (97).
- Verbal is "a kind of latter-day Sophist, one who invents through attunement to audience and place in much the same way Gorgias trusted to do" (97). His cleverness is "inventive passivity before the ambient environs in which he is immersed" (97).
- The chapter concludes with a redefinition: "Thinking kairotically and kairos spatially thus moves us from a subjectivity of semiautonomous, willing agents to something like subjectivity as condensations of probabilities realized in movement, materialized in space, and invented in place" (97).
- On rationality: kairos emplaced is not irrational but rather calls for "refining what rationality will have meant when it is made again ambient in a worldly sense" (98). Attunement is understood "not as a subjective state of mind or willed comportment but as an ambient catalysis within what is most material and concrete, a gathering that springs forward" (98).

## 3. Key Concepts

### kairos
The chapter's central term, traced from its earliest Greek usage as a spatial-material concept (a penetrable opening on the body, a gap in the warp of a loom) through its Sophistic elaboration as temporal opportunism and decorum, to Rickert's ambient reframing. Rickert contends that the dominant temporal-opportunistic reading is a *narrowing* of the richer originary meaning. Ambiently understood, kairos names the emergent disclosure of a situation in which rhetor, environment, and occasion co-constitute one another. It is neither purely subjective (something the rhetor seizes) nor purely objective (something the environment determines) but the dissolution of that dichotomy itself.

### invention
Rickert redefines invention away from the humanist model (a subject deploying heuristics, capitalizing on opportunities) toward an ambient model in which invention is "dispersed into the material environs" (95). Invention is something that happens *through* the coinvention of subject, place, and circumstance — not something a subject *does* to a situation. The case of Verbal Kint illustrates invention as attunement to ambient affordances rather than autonomous fabrication.

### ambient invention
The chapter's synthetic concept: invention understood as emerging from the emplaced, material, relational conditions of a situation rather than from autonomous subjective agency. Ambient invention names the process by which the environing world participates in generating rhetorical action. The air traffic controllers and Verbal Kint both exemplify ambient invention — cognition and creativity distributed across brain, body, tools, and place.

### distributed cognition
Drawn from Andy Clark and Paul Dourish, distributed cognition provides empirical-theoretical support for the ambient reframing. Rickert uses it to show that cognitive processes are not brain-bound but extend into the material environment. However, he pushes beyond Clark's empiricist framing: for Rickert, distribution is not just an explanatory model but an ontological condition — situations *are* the distribution of cognition, affect, and materiality.

### subjectivity
The chapter's primary polemical target in its conventional form: the autonomous, willing, self-present subject who seizes kairotic moments through skill and training. Rickert does not eliminate subjectivity but reconceives it as "condensations of probabilities realized in movement, materialized in space, and invented in place" (97). Subjectivity becomes emplaced, dispersed, and ambient rather than self-grounding.

### temporality
Rickert's key deconstructive move: showing that the dominant temporal reading of kairos (right time, opportune moment) occludes the originary spatial-material meaning. The temporal dimension is not rejected but reintegrated into a richer spatio-temporal-material understanding. Kairos is not just *when* but *where* and *how* — the concrete circumstances of a specific situation.

### emergence
The process by which kairotic situations come into being. Emergence names the way that elements of a situation — material, discursive, affective, spatial — compose into something that exceeds their individual contributions. Rickert's ambient kairos is fundamentally emergent: it cannot be predicted, controlled, or mastered but only attuned to. The concept links to complexity theory (previewed here, developed in Ch. 3).

### emplacement
The condition of being materially situated — not merely "located" in a container-environment but constitutively shaped by and shaping of place. Rickert argues that "without a more materialist understanding of emplacement, kairos is an empty concept" (76). Emplacement is the ontological ground from which kairotic situations emerge.

### attunement
The mode of being-in that precedes and enables kairotic invention. In the chapter's closing pages, attunement is defined as "not a subjective state of mind or willed comportment but an ambient catalysis within what is most material and concrete, a gathering that springs forward" (98). Attunement names the receptive, non-mastering disposition through which ambient invention occurs.

## 4. Main Positions and Arguments

### P1: Kairos is originally spatial-material, not temporal
Rickert's philological recovery, drawing on Onians, Race, and others, demonstrates that the earliest uses of *kairos* in Homer refer to a vulnerable place on the body (the point where an arrow penetrates) and a gap in the warp of a loom through which the shuttle passes. The temporal meaning ("opportune moment") is a later development. This spatial-material origin is not merely a historical curiosity but evidence that the concept always carried materialist implications that were subsequently suppressed by the rhetorical tradition's focus on subjective timing and opportunity (77-79).

### P2: Contemporary theorists of kairos inadvertently prepare the ground for an ambient reading
Rickert surveys Carter, Jarratt, Atwill, Hawhee, Muckelbauer, Consigny, Untersteiner, Miller, and Vitanza — not to refute them but to show that their diverse readings of kairos, despite being framed in terms of subjectivism, empiricism, or antifoundationalism, all point toward a dissolution of the subject-object dichotomy. Hawhee's "invention-in-the-middle" and Muckelbauer's Deleuzian reading come closest to the ambient position; Consigny's categories, despite his intentions, demonstrate that even the "subjectivist" theorists are exploring spatial-material dimensions (80-90).

### P3: Kairos discloses the inadequacy of the subject-object model for rhetoric
The chapter's central philosophical argument: if kairos is emplaced, emergent, and materially constituted, then the model of a subject seizing an objective opportunity collapses. "Neither the categories of subjective and objective nor even metaphors like poles and middles are adequate for thinking kairos. Rather, an ambient understanding of kairos illuminates the more originary, spatial usage of the notion" (85). This extends Ch. 1's argument about *chora* to the domain of invention and temporality.

### P4: Distributed cognition theories provide empirical support for ambient kairos
Clark's extended mind thesis and Dourish's ethnography of air traffic controllers demonstrate that cognitive processes are distributed across brain, body, tools, and environment. Rickert uses these accounts to ground the ambient reading of kairos in something more than philosophical speculation. However, he notes that Clark and Dourish remain within an empiricist framework that does not fully embrace the ontological implications. Rickert pushes their findings toward a Heideggerian conclusion: situations compose new "brain-body-thing-world circuits" at all times (92).

### P5: Invention must be reconceived as ambient — not something a subject does but something that emerges from emplaced conditions
The *Usual Suspects* analysis demonstrates that Verbal Kint's inventive brilliance is not autonomous fabrication but "inventive passivity before the ambient environs in which he is immersed" (97). The environment is a coinventor: names, objects, spatial configurations, and the interrogation situation itself generate the affordances from which Verbal's narrative emerges. Invention is therefore "attuned less to seeking advantage over or success against an audience than to working with what an audience and a material situation bring forth" (91).

### P6: Ambient kairos redefines rationality rather than abandoning it
Rickert explicitly rejects the characterization of kairos as irrational. Since kairos is emplaced, "it is the most rational of concepts" (97). What changes is the conception of rationality itself: no longer a faculty of the autonomous subject weighing alternatives, but a worldly, ambient process of attunement to the material conditions from which thought and action emerge. The chapter ends by calling for "refining what rationality will have meant when it is made again ambient in a worldly sense" (98).

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

### T1: How far does "inventive passivity" extend?
Rickert characterizes Verbal Kint's ambient invention as "inventive passivity" (97) — a receptive attunement to environmental affordances. But the film also shows Verbal as highly agentive: he weaves a complex narrative with apparent deliberation, deceives skilled interrogators, and escapes. The tension between passivity and the manifest cleverness of the performance is acknowledged but not resolved. Does ambient rhetoric need a more nuanced account of how passivity and activity interrelate, or does Rickert's framework absorb all activity into ambient emergence?

### T2: The empiricist limitation of distributed cognition
Rickert acknowledges that Clark "subscribes to a traditional empiricist view" (92) and that Dourish's phenomenological commitments may not go far enough. Yet he draws heavily on their work to support the ambient reading. The question is whether the empiricist framing of distributed cognition is genuinely compatible with the Heideggerian ontological claims Rickert wants to make, or whether the convergence is superficial — a problem he defers to later chapters (Ch. 5 on Burke and Heidegger).

### T3: Historical commensurability
Rickert explicitly disclaims any claim that ancient Greeks held materialist views in the modern sense: "such a claim would be highly dubious" (76). Yet his argument depends on showing that materialist concerns "showed up in both eras" and that "these disclosures warrant comparative and inventive thought" (76). The methodological question — how much conceptual continuity can be claimed across two millennia without anachronism — remains a productive tension throughout the chapter.

### T4: The status of subjectivity after dissolution
Rickert redefines subjectivity as "condensations of probabilities realized in movement, materialized in space, and invented in place" (97). This formulation is evocative but raises questions about ethical and political agency. If subjects are condensations rather than agents, how do we account for responsibility, deliberation, or resistance? The chapter does not address this directly, and it remains an open question for the book as a whole.

### T5: Mastery vs. attunement in pedagogical contexts
Rickert critiques Atwill's emphasis on "mastery of the moment" as reintroducing subjective control, and the chapter consistently favors attunement over mastery. But rhetorical pedagogy has historically depended on cultivating skills — training students to recognize and exploit kairotic moments. If kairos is ambient and cannot be mastered, what does rhetorical education look like? The chapter gestures toward this (via Gorgias's trust in the moment) but does not develop a pedagogical account.
