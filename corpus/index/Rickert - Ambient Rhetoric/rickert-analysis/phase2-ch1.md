## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### ch1-s1: [Opening] — pp. 41-47 (top)

**Focus**: Chora, invention, place, the separatist mind/body paradigm

- Rickert announces that the *chora* illuminates how rhetoric and invention rest on an interplay of revealing and concealing — a generative "infomaterial" matrix that is difficult to apprehend but out of which rhetorical production occurs (42-43).
- He challenges the separatist mind/body/environment paradigm: from the choric perspective, minds are at once *embodied* and *dispersed* into the environment, making them composites of intellect, body, information, and material scaffoldings (43). Andy Clark's work on extended cognition supports this claim.
- He argues that the topoi are themselves "cradled within the *chora*" — that is, the familiar inventional places (commonplaces) get their bearings and dynamics from a more fundamental choric ground (44).
- The *chora* furthers ambient rhetoric by showing transformations not only in thought but in material infrastructure: new media, ubiquitous computing, and ambient intelligence are material actants urging an ambient perspective (44-45).
- Rickert previews the chapter's three main interlocutors — Kristeva, Derrida, and Ulmer — who collectively transform our senses of beginning, creation, and invention by placing those activities within material environments, informational spaces, and affective/bodily registers (45).
- He frames the chapter's guiding questions: the locus of beginnings, the creation of boundaries between self and world, and the importance of place (45-46).

### ch1-s2: Dances with Chora — pp. 47-49

**Focus**: Greek contexts for *chora* in Plato's *Timaeus*; etymology; architecture and dance

- Rickert traces the etymology and pre-Platonic usage of *chora*: commonly used for space before the *Timaeus*, closely associated with land, city, region, and ground; older than *topos* in the written record (47). He distinguishes *chora* from *kenon* (void) and notes its overlap with *topos*.
- Drawing on Keimpe Algra, he notes *chora* carries both concrete (land, territory) and abstract (extension, social rank, proper positioning) senses — both remain important to the *Timaeus* (47).
- Via Indra McEwen's study of architectural origins, Rickert connects *chora* to *choron* and *choros* (dance, dance floor), arguing that place and making are conjoined from Homer and Hesiod onward: "any activity presupposes a place for it to occur, as dancing requires a dance floor" (48).
- He highlights the figure of Daedalus — architect, labyrinth-builder, maker of the dance floor at Knossos — as laying the groundwork for a notion of *chora* that connects place, activity, and *polis*, central to the *Timaeus* (48).
- McEwen's insight that the *polis* is "a surface woven by the activity of its inhabitants" in ritual processions establishes that the *chora* emerges from situated activity, much as a dance "weaves" a dance floor (48-49).
- Rickert draws the architectural-discursive parallel: both building and discourse require a sense of place for instantiation to occur, and instantiation itself results from spatial dynamics (49).

### ch1-s3: The Matter of Beginning — pp. 49-56

**Focus**: The *Timaeus* as a text about beginning; the *chora* as "third kind"; materiality, genesis, *periechon*

- Rickert analyzes the dialogic structure of the *Timaeus*: the *chora* has no real qualities itself but "designates a kind of beginning"; its odd passivity marks it as fundamentally indeterminate, "giving 'nothing' to or withdrawing from what emerges" (50).
- Plato weaves beginning and creation into the dialogue from the first line — the missing fourth person, the need to go backward (to the *Republic*) to go forward, the interweaving of beginnings with memory (50-51).
- A beginning is not autonomous or decontextualized but rather a "distribution of beginnings, or a positing of beginnings giving rise to new, or other, beginnings" — a rhetorical effect rather than a locatable founding moment (51).
- The *Timaeus* links *techne* and *physis*, fabrication and becoming: the *Republic*'s ideal city lacks *eros* — generative becoming — and the *chora* dissolves the distinction between the made and the born, between *techne* and *physis* (52).
- Rickert introduces *periechon* alongside *chora*: meaning "that which surrounds, encompasses, or makes room for," *periechon* appears at key moments in the *Timaeus* and connects ambience to the *chora* through a sense of surrounding, encompassing cosmos (53).
- Plato uses *chora* to open the theme that ambience can never be understood simply as presence: "Place is not simply an immediate environment; it includes the background by means of which things show up as what they are" — a background that "recedes, or withdraws, even as it generates" (55). Edward Casey's reading of the *chora* as "the matrix of all becoming" that "declines to be determined" supports this (55).

### ch1-s4: Kristeva: Invention Inventing Itself — pp. 57-61

**Focus**: The semiotic *chora*; preverbal realm; affect, embodiment, drives; invention as exceeding method

- Rickert reads Kristeva's *Revolution in Poetic Language* as theorizing a "semiotic" preverbal realm (the *chora*) prior to and distinct from the symbolic realm, one that can subvert the latter's masculine, overly rational character (57).
- The originary *chora* remains in a dialectical relation to the symbolic: it includes emotions, sensations, and traces of psychical and material experience; these choric experiences are transformed by the Idea under the logos but the *chora* persists as "an archaic origin" that "restores these repressed aspects of environmental, bodily, and relational experience" (57).
- Rickert highlights the mother-child relationship as illustrative of choric embedding: singsong intonation, gesture, play, and movement modulate affect prior to the symbolic, and "only the smallest amount of what occurs in this relation can be considered symbolic" (57-58).
- Kristeva's key point for Rickert: invention is "necessarily choric because it exceeds the subject and the range of symbolicity" — without the *chora*, sciences and humanities are "archivistic, archaeological, and necrophilic" (58-59).
- Rickert addresses Judith Butler's feminist critique that the *chora* is oddly static and gendered, ultimately arguing that the political and feminist points "cannot be said to explain the full import of the *chora*" — its strangeness as a third kind resists purely gendered readings (59).
- The section's key move for invention: Kristeva "invites us to see choric invention as a particular form of beginning, one we might describe as invention *inventing itself*" — invention takes place in and through affective situations, not through systematized method alone (61). Affect, habituation, sensation, intuition, environment, and accident are equally part of the genesis of ideas (60).

### ch1-s5: Derrida: Oh, Khora! — pp. 61-66

**Focus**: Deconstruction of *khora*; naming and aporia; *Chora L Works* and architectural invention; Socrates' choric withdrawal

- Rickert turns to Derrida's two general tracks: theoretical investigations of the *chora*'s place in thought/discourse, and practical deployments of *chora* as an inventive principle — the latter usage catalyzes Ulmer's work (61).
- In *On the Name*, Derrida treats *khora* as a third term outside the mythos/logos opposition — a *place* for what lies outside this opposition. The question of naming is central: "*khora*" functions as a name for a referent whose status is one of "uneasy oppositions, aporia, and conjecture," complicated by its self-reflexivity (62).
- The aporia: *khora* questions the distinction between the sensible and the intelligible upon which rhetoric is built; the metaphors used to describe it (womb, receptacle, matrix, nurse) do not truly describe it but rather give place for those metaphors to emerge (63).
- Rickert analyzes Derrida's insight that invention may inhabit "a paradoxical or impossible place within rhetoric precisely because of its always ongoing withdrawal" — rhetoric includes invention insofar as it initiates a discourse, but this "stops short of actually addressing or thinking invention" as such (63).
- *Chora L Works* (the Derrida-Eisenman architectural collaboration) demonstrates that bringing *khora* into concrete inventional practice confronts a "personal limit" — what the *chora* was for Plato is "precisely an impossibility" because that which gives rise to the world "cannot *be* in the world" (65). The project remains unfulfilled precisely because *khora* "inhabits an impossible place, one that governs, in a manner nearly metametaphysical" (65).
- Rickert reads Socrates' own withdrawal into silence during the *Timaeus* as itself choric — a *triton genos* like the *khora*, his silence allows the emergence of Timaeus's cosmology, paralleling how the *chora* withdraws to enable generation (64).

### ch1-s6: Ulmer: A Choric Inventio — pp. 67-73

**Focus**: Electracy; *heuretics*; chorography; invention as emplaced, performative, ambient; chapter conclusion

- Rickert positions Ulmer as offering "a more complete flowering of the *chora* as a rhetorical concept" and a more practical approach: Ulmer applies principles of choric invention developed from Plato, Derrida, and Kristeva to his own writerly/inventional aesthetics, with high self-reflexivity appropriate to the electronic age (67).
- Ulmer's key move: replacing *topos* with *chora* in the practice of invention — devising "a discourse on that which, similarly, is the other of method" (*Heuretics* 66). The *chora* is approachable "only through bastard discourses, or as if in a dream" (71).
- Ulmer's inventive rhetorical forms — the Mystory, the Popcycle, the CATTt — constitute alternatives to rationalistic print-culture methods; new media and technologies allow us "to inhabit place differently than we did before" (68). Hypermedia combines image, text, and sound in permutations that borrow techniques across media forms.
- Rickert emphasizes Ulmer's insight about externalization and dispersion: as environments become "smarter" through ambient intelligence, "material and informational scaffolding becomes part of that in which and out of which rhetorical and cognitive activity occur" (70). Invention's emplacement within ambient environs "displaces the priority traditionally assigned to subjective human activity for invention" (70).
- Technologies help disclose how we are in the world (connecting to Heidegger's *QCT*): in the age of computation, ecological understandings transform our ambient environs, and this transforms us in relation to them (70).
- The chapter's conclusion: "at the heart of invention is invention itself, conceived in a newly material and choric fashion" — the *chora* allows us to practice and theorize how the seeming paradox (what enables rhetoric also withdraws) is "actually productive." It is part of what gives rise to rhetoric, but it also withdraws, "which in turn necessitates nothing more than another beginning, another *inventio*" (73).

## 4. Main Positions and Arguments

### P1: Rhetoric and invention rest on a generative "infomaterial" matrix (the *chora*) that reveals and conceals simultaneously

- **Premise 1**: The *chora* in Plato's *Timaeus* is a "third kind" that has no qualities of its own but serves as the generative matrix from which all becoming emerges (50, 55).
- **Premise 2**: This matrix operates through an interplay of revealing (giving place for things to appear) and concealing (withdrawing, declining determination) (55, 73).
- **Premise 3**: Rhetoric and invention work within and out of this matrix — they are not autonomous acts of rational agents but are constituted by material, affective, and environmental relations (42-43).
- **Conclusion**: Invention is fundamentally emplaced and choric, arising from conditions it can never fully represent or capture.
- **Key Interlocutors**: Plato, Casey, Clark
- **Status**: core book thesis (foundational claim for ambient rhetoric)

### P2: The separatist mind/body/environment paradigm is inadequate for understanding rhetorical invention

- **Premise 1**: Much rhetorical theory relies on a demarcation between mind and body, body and environment, with a valuation of method, idea, and logic (43).
- **Premise 2**: From the choric perspective, minds are "at once *embodied* and *dispersed* into the environment" — composites of intellect, body, information, and material artifacts (43).
- **Premise 3**: Clark's work on extended cognition shows mind enters "deep and complex relationships with nonbiological constructs, props, and aids" (43).
- **Conclusion**: Rhetorical theory must reconceive its basic subject as emplaced, distributed, and materially constituted rather than as autonomous rational agency.
- **Key Interlocutors**: Clark, Edbauer, Hayles
- **Status**: local support (grounds the chapter's reframing within cognitive science)

### P3: The topoi are cradled within the *chora* — topical invention presupposes choric ground

- **Premise 1**: The Greek history of topical invention shows the topoi providing a *place* where thoughts begin and grow (44).
- **Premise 2**: The topoi get their bearings and maintain their dynamics "as cradled within the *chora*" (44).
- **Premise 3**: The *chora* furthers ambient rhetoric because it shows transformation not only in thought but in material infrastructure (44).
- **Conclusion**: Topical invention is not abandoned but reframed — "topic place is reattuned to what gives it place" (67).
- **Key Interlocutors**: Plato, Ulmer, Vitanza
- **Status**: local support (explains the relationship between existing rhetorical invention theory and the new choric framework)

### P4: Kristeva's semiotic *chora* shows that invention exceeds rational method and the range of symbolicity

- **Premise 1**: Kristeva theorizes a semiotic realm prior to and distinct from the symbolic that includes drives, affects, bodily traces (57).
- **Premise 2**: Invention is "necessarily choric because it exceeds the subject and the range of symbolicity" (58).
- **Premise 3**: Method, rational ideas, and systematic approaches are "only part of what occurs in an inventional procedure, the other parts being choric" (60).
- **Conclusion**: Affect, habituation, sensation, intuition, environment, and accident are equally part of rhetorical invention, not supplements to rational method.
- **Key Interlocutors**: Kristeva, Butler (as critic), Greenspan and Shanker
- **Status**: local support (establishes the affective/embodied dimension of choric invention)

### P5: Derrida's *khora* reveals that invention inhabits a paradoxical or impossible place — it works through withdrawal

- **Premise 1**: *Khora* questions the sensible/intelligible distinction upon which rhetoric is built (63).
- **Premise 2**: Rhetoric includes invention insofar as invention initiates discourse, but this "stops short of actually addressing or thinking invention" (63).
- **Premise 3**: The *Chora L Works* project demonstrates that bringing *khora* into concrete practice confronts impossibility: what gives rise to the world "cannot *be* in the world" (65).
- **Conclusion**: Invention may inhabit "a paradoxical or impossible place within rhetoric precisely because of its always ongoing withdrawal" (63).
- **Key Interlocutors**: Derrida, Eisenman, Vernant
- **Status**: core book thesis (the withdrawal/impossibility structure is fundamental to ambient rhetoric's ontology)

### P6: Ulmer's chorography demonstrates that choric invention can be practically enacted through performative, media-specific methods

- **Premise 1**: Ulmer replaces *topos* with *chora* in inventional practice, creating "a discourse on that which is the other of method" (71).
- **Premise 2**: Chorographic forms (Mystory, Popcycle, CATTt) are performative — they enact choric invention rather than representing it (71-72).
- **Premise 3**: New media and ambient intelligence make choric invention's emplaced, distributed character newly visible and practically available (68, 70).
- **Conclusion**: Choric invention is not merely philosophical but can be practiced through media forms that attend to the conditions of possibility generating discourse and place.
- **Key Interlocutors**: Ulmer, Rice, Vitanza, Hawk
- **Status**: positive proposal (the most practice-oriented claim of the chapter)

### P7: Place is not a container but a generative, dynamic, withdrawing ground

- **Premise 1**: The *chora*'s archaic sense as territory, land, and city shows place was always understood as woven by the activity of its inhabitants (48, McEwen).
- **Premise 2**: In the *Timaeus*, the *chora* dissolves the dichotomy between nature and artifice, between the ideal city (*atopos*, placeless) and the emplaced city (54).
- **Premise 3**: "Place is not simply an immediate environment; it includes the background by means of which things show up as what they are" — a background that "recedes, or withdraws, even as it generates" (55).
- **Conclusion**: Our conceptions of space are changing in accord with the *chora*: "becoming more malleable and dispersed" as computation becomes worldly (72-73).
- **Key Interlocutors**: McEwen, Casey, Algra, Edbauer
- **Status**: core book thesis (grounds the entire ambient project's reconception of environment/place)

### P8: The *Timaeus* establishes that a beginning is not autonomous but is a distribution of beginnings

- **Premise 1**: The dialogue's structure — requiring return to the *Republic*, raising the issue of memory, counting to three with a missing fourth — demonstrates that a beginning already entails a past, and "what emerges instead is a distribution of beginnings" (51).
- **Premise 2**: The *chora* itself designates a kind of beginning that is fundamentally indeterminate, "giving 'nothing' to or withdrawing from what emerges" (50).
- **Conclusion**: Beginning is a rhetorical effect, not an autonomous founding moment — a key structural feature of invention that the *chora* names.
- **Key Interlocutors**: Plato, Sallis, Derrida
- **Status**: local support (establishes the non-foundational character of choric invention)

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

### T1: The accessibility paradox of the *chora*

The *chora* is described as approachable "only through bastard discourses, or as if in a dream" (71, via Plato/Ulmer), and its defining characteristic is withdrawal — it "cannot strictly speaking be *represented*" (71). Yet Rickert, Ulmer, and Derrida all write extensive theoretical treatments of it. **Tension with P5 and C4**: if the *chora* fundamentally withdraws from representation, what is the epistemic status of the very accounts Rickert reconstructs here? He acknowledges this via the "impossibility" theme (P5), but the chapter does not resolve whether choric discourse is itself always in some sense a failure or whether the performative dimension (P6) sidesteps the problem.

### T2: The relationship between topical and choric invention

P3 claims that the topoi are "cradled within the *chora*" and that topical invention is "not abandoned but reattuned." Yet P4 and P6 suggest that choric invention *exceeds* method, rationality, and the systematic — the very features that make topical invention pedagogically available. **Tension between C2 and C10**: does choric invention supplement topical invention (adding affect, materiality, environment) or does it displace it by locating invention's real ground somewhere method cannot reach? Rickert leaves this partially open, noting that "while some phenomena fall out of a systematized inventional method, they nevertheless remain integral to rhetorical production" (60).

### T3: The gendering of the *chora*

Rickert engages Butler's critique that characterizing the *chora* as feminine/maternal is "oddly static" and performs exclusions (59). He partially deflects the critique by noting Plato's nonfeminine metaphors and Vitanza's "third genders," but concedes that "the political and feminist points that Butler raises cannot be said to explain the full import of the *chora*" (59). **Tension with C5**: the receptacle/womb/matrix metaphorics persist throughout the *Timaeus* and in Kristeva's mother-child paradigm (C9). Rickert does not fully resolve whether a post-gendered reading of the *chora* is achievable or whether the maternal figuration is constitutive of the concept.

### T4: Nature/artifice after the *chora*

Rickert argues the *chora* "powerfully destabilizing[s]" the dichotomy between nature and artifice that Plato "originally assumed" (56). Yet the chapter also relies on a distinction between "material" and "informational" scaffolding (C6) and between human and nonhuman actants. **Tension with P7**: if the nature/artifice distinction is dissolved, what grounds the distinction between the material and the digital/informational that Rickert invokes when discussing ambient intelligence and computation (72-73)? This may be clarified in later chapters, but within Chapter 1 it remains unresolved.

### T5: The scope of "withdrawal" across the three interlocutors

Kristeva's *chora* is dialectically related to the symbolic — repressed but recoverable through avant-garde practice (C9). Derrida's *khora* is radically aporetic — it "cannot *be* in the world" (P5). Ulmer's *chora* is practically enacted through chorographic methods (P6). These three versions of withdrawal appear to operate at different ontological levels. **Tension across P4, P5, P6**: Rickert presents them as cumulative perspectives on the same concept, but the degree to which the *chora* is recoverable (Kristeva), impossible (Derrida), or performatively available (Ulmer) varies significantly. The chapter does not fully adjudicate among these positions but rather harvests each for its contribution to the ambient project.
