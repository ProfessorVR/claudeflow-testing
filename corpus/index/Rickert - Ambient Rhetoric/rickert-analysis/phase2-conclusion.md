## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### conc-s1: [Opening: Return to Terroir] pp. 271–272

- Rickert returns to *terroir* as the book's governing figure: not simply ground or soil but the close-knit relation among grapevines, earth, cultivation techniques, and constituencies (grapes, wines, consumers, chefs, farmers, producers, lawmakers) that imparts a unique quality—*le gout du terroir*, the taste of place (271).
- This ensemble constitutes a "material-social-hermeneutic ecology": a way of life spoken through evolving sensibility to the mutuality of meaning and materiality (272; cf. Trubek 18–44).
- The most dynamic practices emerge from "profound respect and even reverence for what we do not know and cannot predict," the Heideggerian mantra of shepherding and caring that appears in deep ecology and in the book's Ch 8 discussion of automobiles (272).
- Rickert acknowledges a countercurrent: elevating terroir and rootedness risks advocating immobility, conservativism, even the "politics of blood and soil" implicit in fascism (272).

### conc-s2: Movement and Place pp. 272–274

- The phylloxera crisis of the late nineteenth century serves as Rickert's counter-example: a tiny insect devastated two-thirds of European vineyards, and the solution required *movement*—grafting European vines onto resistant North American rootstocks (272–273).
- Lesson: "even the strongest commitment to place requires movement beyond its boundaries" (273). Place is formed through *chora* in two senses: (1) crossing limits and boundaries so a polis can thrive, and (2) *chora* as matrix of becoming, bringing place into being through movement, activity, and disclosure (273).
- Plato locates movement at the heart of *chora* even while shielding himself from it; the Sophists embody the movement Plato fears (*Timaeus* 19e; *Euthyphro* 11d–e) (273).
- Commitment to place is not static or isolating: "movement, activity, and building are what disclose and create a place, alongside the attunements to place that bring it into such disclosure and creation" (273–274).
- Rootedness discloses place as a "place for sharing practices and discourses related to growing vines and making wine" (274).
- Daniel Gross is cited for characterizing the capacity for being-moved as common to all beings, human and nonhuman ("Being-Moved" 13, 21), though Heidegger's later work still "privileges the rustic" and "centrally focuses on movement in human being" (274).

### conc-s3: Heidegger's Deafness pp. 274–276

- Rickert turns to "certain aspects of Heidegger that I have not yet brought up, particularly his problematic politics and what we might call his deafness" (274).
- Berel Lang (*Heidegger's Silence*) charges that Heidegger was "deaf to the Jewish question"; Lang sees the silence as inseparable from Heidegger the person—"if a bad person then bad philosophy" (97)—yet remains wary of sweeping judgment, since "a silence can mean many things" (274–275).
- Allen Scult (*Being Jewish/Reading Heidegger*) reworks Heidegger in terms of this limit: as a Jew reading Heidegger, he acknowledges "active and passive complicity" with National Socialism (1) yet finds a subversive parallel—reading as a Jew (being Jewish) how Heidegger read the ancient Greeks (reading Heidegger) (275).
- Scult writes: "This is Heidegger's 'gift' to me—a communication that brings me, in my being-in-the-world as Being Jewish, to a *Sichverstehen* [mutual understanding], evoked by his words and enabled by the space he leaves" (275, qtg. Scult 8).
- Scult's position: the sacred texts still emerge through hermeneutic practice; Heidegger's Greek texts remain "available for further disclosures" despite and because of his intensive relation to them (276).
- Rickert's takeaway: Heidegger's thought has "limitations and dangers," but these lapses "are available for further disclosive work"; Heidegger "cannot be given the power to so bind. The texts still light up, as Scult says. They still call and claim" (276).

### conc-s4: Ungrounding Ground pp. 276–279

- Heidegger's *The Principle of Reason* (1955–56 lectures): reason (*Grund*) and principle (*Satz*) share an essence—both are forms of grounding that give rise to the overpowering sway of technological life (277).
- The principle of reason is "a principle of being from which the reach of technology makes itself the ground to which we hearken" (277).
- Yet Heidegger ungrounds this ground: "In the heart of the principle of reason itself, Heidegger finds an unground, an abyss" (277).
- Angelus Silesius: "The rose is without why: it blooms because it blooms" (qtd in Heidegger, *PR* 35). Heidegger reads the "because" as simultaneously mimicking and denying the form of a reasonable proposition—the "reason" being that there is no reason (277).
- The world is not substantiated on reasons or propositions as posited *objectively*; "Reason, human and otherwise, does not ground being" (277). The power of Silesius's verse shows "how 'these grounds are abysmal'" (Heidegger, *PR* 37).
- Cynthia Haynes ("Writing Offshore") on the rose: "things do not depend on human justification, that they emerge before us on their own" (679); Norwegian saying: "nothing grows in the moonlight"—beauty and value exist regardless of purpose or ground (277–278).
- The principle of reason is equally a principle of being, "open to further disclosure" particularly as reread through Leibniz; reason and principle "take part in each other, making the principle of reason a statement about being itself" (278).
- The abyss at the end of *Principle of Reason* connects to *play*: "the abyss that opens up in reason is connected to play" (279). Play is not frivolity but the "whirl of being-in-the-world, the emergent and disclosive activities to which the world gives rise that make the world what it is" (279).
- Boomtown Rats' "I Don't Like Mondays" (based on Brenda Ann Spencer's 1979 school shooting): Rickert juxtaposes "the rose is without why" with "there are no reasons [for shooting people]" and "I don't like Mondays" to illustrate how irrationalism and mysticism must be kept in their ambient dimension (278–279).
- Key formulation: "We are seeking to understand rationality in an ambient key, one that is yoked to neither irrationalism nor mysticism. If we understand the world to be integral not just for basic human growth and survival, craft and activity, but also for the emergence, disclosure, and practice of ethics, then we must take care to understand abyssal grounds" (279).

### conc-s5: [Conservative Reading Dangers] pp. 279–280

- Wrathall's example of returning lawns of the American Southwest to desert (European-derived turf replaced by hearkening to the land in accordance with the fourfold) is "only half right": it risks "binding our contemporary disclosure of the desert... into what increasingly appears to be an unsustainable way of life, into a new *physis*-based foundation: nature as ultimate guide" (279–280).
- Toronto Island (from Ch 8): blocking autos can be read as inherently conservative, naturalizing the island as an actant rejecting automotive technology. Rickert insists: the material environs must "be coactive in the form of dwelling that emerges there"; ethical bearing and suasion "would emerge from that attunement" (280).
- Key methodological caution: "An ambient rhetoric suggests that overly focusing on or otherwise elevating what is present and taking that for all there is, or finding our sole bearings for ethics and action in whatever is present, is just as unsustainable as overemphasizing future possibility or the promise of transformative becoming" (280).
- Disclosure must be understood "as something always ongoing and transforming in accordance with the play of being-in-the-world, attuned simultaneously to what matters to us now while mindful that we cannot take reality for the simple presence of beings as they seem" (280).
- Ambience conveys how the world "takes part in what human being is and does, but never as a simple assemblage of 'that's just what's there' givens" (280).

### conc-s6: [Barad and Disclosure] pp. 281–283

- George Kennedy: animals practice rhetoric; crows "speak" to Kennedy insofar as affectability transcends human doing; rhetoric "is itself set up for further disclosure" (281).
- Central formulation: "Rhetoric is ambience as affective disclosure; or perhaps we could put it conversely, saying that it is disclosure itself becoming affective, suasive" (281).
- Attunement "remains always at issue, inseparable from the strife of differentiation and affiliation that never releases us" (281).
- Karen Barad's *Meeting the Universe Halfway*: "I unfortunately came to it too late to make it more essential to my project's DNA," but Rickert sees "productive resonance with Heidegger on the issue of disclosure" (281).
- The Stern-Gerlach experiment (1922): Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach set out to demonstrate space quantization. The experiment initially failed but led to a successful variant because Stern, a low-salary assistant professor, could only afford cheap cigars whose sulfurous smoke proved crucial in bringing out results on the detector plate (282).
- Lesson via Barad: class, nationalism, gender, and the cheap cigar are all "integral to the experiment proper... regardless of intention, part of the experiment" (Barad 165–67) (282).
- Barad elaborates (via Niels Bohr): "what we call physical reality is inseparable from the measuring or observational apparatus that renders something as what it is. Disclosure is itself ontological" (283); "there is no unambiguous way to differentiate between the object and the agencies of observation" (Barad 118) (283).
- Heidegger's notion of play resonates with Barad: "both our actions (or what Barad, reworking Judith Butler, will call performativity) to be inseparable from that with which we interact, including what we build" (283). But Heidegger "emphasizes that withdrawal works equally potently": the coming to presence of something does not exhaust all that it is (283).
- Withdrawal has "significant consequences, particularly for ecological thought": sustainability has much to gain from engagement with this issue; disclosure "is never simply given; it is an ongoing achievement" (283).

### conc-s7: [Final Synthesis] pp. 283–285

- Worldly affect "hollows out in advance what rhetoric will have come to be for us as performance, as meaning, and as knowledge" (284).
- Barad on ethics via performativity: "Just as the human subject is not the locus of knowing, neither is it the locus of ethicality" (393). Ethics becomes "not of othering, about the creation of radical exteriority, but of 'making connections and commitments'" (392) (284).
- "This 'ethics of worlding' performs as an 'agentive' trajectory that de-cisions, or cuts, from among complexly entangled pathways" (Barad 392) (284).
- Responsibility "is disclosed differently": the exclusions, exploitations, and devaluations we are "always privy" to remain "up for contestation and redress" (284).
- Ambient rhetoric "brings to disclosure the cradle of affectability to which we are endlessly receptive, a sending that has been long in the wind" (285).
- Sustainability as key contemporary application: "Perhaps the issue of sustainability that currently grips us... can aid us in pursuing this worldly affectability" (285).
- Fittingness: "increased awareness of the importance of *world* in our being in the world. In terms of rhetoric, it suggests that rhetoric stems from and is not imposed on the world. Rhetoric, then, is ambient and cannot be confined to a human orbit" (285).
- Final claim: "The claim that rhetoric is ambient fits rhetorical studies moving forward inclusive of its past by making the world essential to rhetoricity, and perhaps coming to a renewed sensibility to what the ancient Greeks called *periechon*" (285).
- Closing: "Ambience, then, brings us forward, just as it awaits us, not as the new, not as a marker distinguishing us from what has come before, but as an attunement disclosing what has been sent forward and continues in its sending. And I think of this as promise, challenge, and, ultimately, hope" (285).

## 4. Main Positions

**P1 — Movement is constitutive of place, not opposed to it.** Even the strongest commitment to rootedness and terroir requires movement beyond boundaries; the phylloxera case and *chora* both demonstrate that place comes into being through crossing, grafting, and disclosive activity, not through static preservation (272–274).

**P2 — Heidegger's political failures do not invalidate his thought for ambient rhetoric, but they impose a permanent critical obligation.** Lang and Scult show that Heidegger's silence/deafness on the Jewish question is a limit within his thought, not simply external to it; yet the texts remain "available for further disclosive work" precisely because disclosure exceeds authorial intention (274–276).

**P3 — The principle of reason, pushed to its limit, reveals an abyssal ground that is neither irrational nor mystical but ambient.** Heidegger's reading of Silesius and the principle of reason shows that the world is not substantiated on objectively posited reasons; the abyss opens onto play as the emergent, disclosive whirl of being-in-the-world. Rationality must be understood "in an ambient key" (276–279).

**P4 — Conservative and naturalistic readings of ambient rhetoric are misreadings.** Wrathall's desert example and the Toronto Island case both risk binding contemporary disclosure into a new *physis*-based foundation; ambient rhetoric requires attending to what withdraws and to future possibilities, not only to what is presently disclosed (279–280).

**P5 — Rhetoric is ambience as affective disclosure.** The central concluding formulation: rhetoric is "disclosure itself becoming affective, suasive"; it precedes symbolicity, is not confined to a human orbit, and stems from dwelling as the ongoing emergence of world (281, 285).

**P6 — Barad's agential realism productively resonates with Heidegger on disclosure.** The Stern-Gerlach experiment demonstrates that boundaries and agencies of observation are not given in advance but are themselves part of disclosive work; Barad and Heidegger converge on the inseparability of performativity from world, though Heidegger adds the crucial emphasis on withdrawal (281–283).

**P7 — Ethics in ambient rhetoric is an ethics of worlding, not of subjective moral calculation.** Following Barad, the human subject is not the locus of ethicality; ethics performs as an "agentive trajectory" that cuts from among entangled pathways; responsibility is not abdicated but disclosed differently, remaining always open to contestation (284).

**P8 — Ambient rhetoric culminates in a practical orientation toward sustainability and fittingness.** The ecological approach brings "increased awareness of the importance of *world* in our being in the world"; rhetoric is ambient, not imposed on the world, and the issue of sustainability is the most pressing contemporary horizon for this insight (285).

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

**T1 — Movement vs. rootedness as genuine dialectic or ad hoc resolution.** Rickert argues that movement is constitutive of place (via phylloxera, *chora*), but the mechanism by which the two are held together remains largely illustrative rather than formally articulated. When does movement become uprooting? The conclusion gestures toward balance without specifying criteria.

**T2 — The Heidegger problem as limit vs. as structural flaw.** Rickert follows Scult in treating Heidegger's political failures as a *limit* that does not contaminate the ontological insights. But the argument depends on the claim that texts exceed their authors—a hermeneutic premise that Rickert asserts rather than fully defends. Critics (e.g., Faye, Trawny on the *Black Notebooks*) might argue the politics are structurally entangled with the ontology of dwelling, Volk, and earth.

**T3 — Play and the abyss: from ontology to ethics.** The transition from the ontological claim (reason is abyssal, play is the whirl of being-in-the-world) to the ethical claim (we must take care to understand abyssal grounds) is asserted but under-argued. How does the recognition of abyssal grounds generate normative commitments rather than quietism or aestheticism?

**T4 — Barad as supplement vs. alternative framework.** Rickert admits Barad came "too late" to be essential to his project's DNA (281). The resonance with Heidegger is productive but the differences (Barad's emphasis on performativity and cuts vs. Heidegger's emphasis on withdrawal and gathering) are noted without resolution. Whether agential realism and Heideggerian disclosure are ultimately compatible ontologies remains an open question.

**T5 — Sustainability as concrete horizon vs. rhetorical gesture.** The conclusion invokes sustainability as the pressing practical application of ambient rhetoric (285), but the chapter offers no sustained engagement with sustainability discourse, policy, or practice. The term functions more as a promissory note than as a worked-out consequence of the ontological framework.

**T6 — Anti-conservative caveat vs. the book's own tendencies.** Rickert explicitly warns against conservative readings (279–280), yet the book's persistent privileging of dwelling, terroir, rootedness, the fourfold, and Heidegger's later thought creates a gravitational pull toward exactly the conservativism he disavows. The countercurrent (movement, phylloxera, becoming) occupies far less textual space than the rootedness motif.
