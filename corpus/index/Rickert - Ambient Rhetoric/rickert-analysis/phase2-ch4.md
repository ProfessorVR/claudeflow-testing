- **Title**: Ambient Music: Worlding Sound
- **Part**: Part 1 — Diffractions of Ambience
- **Chapter**: 4
- **Pages**: 130-155 (PDF 150-175)
- **One-sentence thesis**: Microsoft Windows startup music — from Eno's Windows 95 composition to Fripp's Windows Vista theme — demonstrates that music functions not as ornamental or merely emotional supplement to rhetoric but as an ambient, worlding force that, through mood (*Stimmung*), musical worlding, and soundmarks, attunes users to their material and technological environments in ways that exceed intentional design and necessitate an ontological rather than epistemological understanding of rhetoric.

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### ch4-s1: [Opening] (pp. 130-133)
- Rickert frames the chapter's argument: a functionalist view of Windows startup music as "frippery" replicates age-old arguments against rhetoric, reducing the richness of human being (130-131).
- Three dominant historical responses to music identified: (1) music affects us emotionally and must therefore be controlled (Plato/Aristotle); (2) music is indeterminate and semantically empty, making symbolic arts superior; (3) music is ornamental but inessential (131).
- The distrust these positions share parallels suspicions historically directed at rhetoric itself — both music and rhetoric are powerful, indeterminate, and inessential in the eyes of their critics (131).
- Secondary argument announced: examining music-technology relations reconfigures grounding assumptions about interactive media and rhetoric, moving us beyond discourse alone toward compositional, ambient understanding (132-133).
- Rhetoric redefined compositionally: "the synthesis of multiple content threads of varying intensities, including discourses, symbols, colors, graphics, musics, sounds, haptic elements, and more, all as gathered within, conditioned by, and expressing a material and affective environment" (133).

### ch4-s2: Windows 95: Boong-bliiing-tink-tink-tink (pp. 133-137)
- Historical context: Windows 3.1 had no startup music; its rudimentary sounds drew user backlash, prompting Microsoft to address the aural experience with Windows 95 (133).
- Microsoft contracted Brian Eno to compose startup music; Eno's ambient music credentials (quiet, atmospheric, eschewing melody, integrating technology as cocomposer) made him attractive to Microsoft (133-134).
- Constraints on Eno's composition: extremely short (3-4 seconds), must withstand countless replayings, must be pleasant but not demanding — "this idea was itself a maxim of the ambient music Eno developed" (134).
- Eno was given a list of specific moods, feelings, and attitudes the music was supposed to invoke; he developed eighty-four pieces, and the one chosen became the Microsoft Sound (134).
- Cave art at Lascaux and elsewhere placed in acoustically resonant chambers — Blesser and Salter argue prehistoric "multisensory art" was integrated into Stone Age life, evoking sense of the world including the divine; sound, vision, and environment have always been compositional concerns (136-137).
- Parallel drawn between prehistoric cave art's multisensory ambient environment and contemporary computer operating systems: "sound, vision, and environment have always been of compositional concern" (137).

### ch4-s3: Boot Up Once More, with Feeling (pp. 137-142)
- Sound as cave-like environment: deployment of sound within caves must be understood as nonsymbolic communicative technology; Eno's startup music similarly provides "semantically laden listening cues" forming an ambient environment with GUI and tactile elements (138).
- Critique of GUI-centric design: the focus on the *graphical* and *cognitive* in creating GUIs mirrors the archaeological focus on visuals; "perhaps 'multisensory user interaction space' (MUIS) would have been a more accurate term" (138).
- MIT's ambientROOM (Tangible Media Group) cited as design exemplar: moves information "into the physical environment, manifesting [it] as subtle changes in form, movement, sound, color, smell, temperature, or light" (Wisneski et al.), extending ambient music's promise (138).
- Information-as-framing critiqued: describing environmental flows solely as "information" is too narrow; "information's semantic specificity is not broad enough to account for, among other things, nonsemantic emotional modulation" (138).
- Music attunes us to an environment in ways that are "emotional, meaningful, and physiological"; music is "inseparable from the ambient environs in which it emerges" and listeners are "immersed in the unfolding of music in place" (139).
- Anechoic chamber example (Blesser and Salter): a chamber absorbing 99.9% of sound produces extreme placelessness, demonstrating that background sounds emplace us and ground our affective comportment in terms of "privacy, intimacy, security, warmth, encapsulation, socialization, and territoriality" (139).

### ch4-s4: Windows Vista: Emotional Coloring in Green and Blue (pp. 142-146)
- Robert Fripp (King Crimson, Eno collaborator) chosen for Vista's startup music; Jim Allchin reports Vista required different sounds than XP's "'western' and literal" ones — a telling marker that professional sound design had "become ambient and ecological" (140).
- Vista's sound has two components: an ambient bed of auditory "color" (looped guitar electronically processed) and a "three, four, five note theme" over the top; Fripp made over four hours of music for the four-second theme (140).
- Visual-aural integration: colors (green and blue) correspond to specific chords and key signatures; auditory mood describes visual texture and vice versa — "in discussions of sound, color can be used to describe ambient tone; similarly, in discussions of visuals, auditory mood can be used to describe visual texture" (141).
- Rickert's key move: affect understood as "a worldly disposition and not a subjective emotional state"; cannot posit simple cause-effect between sonic element and intended emotional result; tonal centers of D and E "become a variable contributing to the larger environs" (145).
- Aural architecture "exists regardless of how the acoustic attributes of a space came into existence: naturally, incidentally, unwittingly, or intentionally" — the aural architect "is most often not an actual person" (Blesser and Salter 5); designed sound cannot be reduced to anyone's intentions (145).
- Transition announced: the final part of the chapter will explore rhetoric ontologically through three concepts — *fundamental mood*, *musical worlding*, and *soundmarks* (142).

### ch4-s5: The Rhythm of Being: Mood (pp. 146-150)
- Emotion commonly reduced to subjective coloring of an objective world; Rickert argues this scientific worldview presupposes emotion is secondary and controllable, privileging logic over feeling (144).
- Heidegger's *Stimmung* (mood/attunement) introduced: mood is ontological, not psychological; it is "the pre-intelligible background necessary for us to make sense of, experience, and interact in the world" (146).
- Key Heidegger passages: "a mood [*Stimmung*] assails us. It comes neither from the 'outside' nor from the 'inside', but arises out of Being-in-the-world, as a way of such Being" (*BT* 176); attunement is "like an atmosphere in which we first immerse ourselves in each case and which then attunes us through and through" (*FCM* 67) (146).
- Depression as example: mood as attunement "permeates the entire situation, not simply the interior mental state of the person"; depression transforms a person's *world* so that the world comes to take part in the depression (146).
- Damasio's neuroscientific work offers empirical convergence: his third level of emotion, "background feeling," is "comparable to Heidegger's *Stimmung*" — "minimalist in tone and beat, the feeling of life itself, the sense of being" (*Descartes' Error* 150) (146-147).
- Greenspan and Shanker: emotional development is prerequisite for learning language; affect is "in some sense prior to language or symbolicity" and "strongly embodied, situated, and emotional trajectory" (Ratcliffe 296-97) (147).
- Norman's *Emotional Design*: three modalities of emotional response — visceral, behavioral, reflective; Norman claims emotional design "interweave[s] both emotions and cognition" (Norman 5, 21); but Rickert argues Norman's framework still risks instrumentalizing emotion (147-148).
- Rickert's synthesis: music and emotion are "connected through their emergence within an ambient situation, conceived ecologically and materially as well as socially"; if music is "worldly in this sense, then it generates cognitive and physiological as well as emotional responses, and these are in turn woven into our material environment" (148).

### ch4-s6: Aural Architecture: Musical Worlding and Soundmarks (pp. 150-154)
- John Covach's concept of *musical worlding* introduced: music's meaning making through situating a piece "in the richest possible network of relationships in hopes of coming to terms with how a piece creates meaning" (Covach 180) (148-149).
- Covach's case study: Foreigner (corporate rock) vs. the Cars (new wave) — differences break down over time as material elements (instruments, tonal qualities, studio equipment, production values, shared song structures) converge; "these tracks mean differently because of the ways in which they 'world'" (Covach 194) (149-150).
- Rickert augments Covach with greater emphasis on materiality: "the equipment that generates the sounds and the space within which it unfolds make up part of what we designate by the term *music*; they are not simply antecedents required for music's production" (150).
- R. Murray Schafer's *soundmarks* introduced: sonic counterpart to landmarks; soundmarks "have particular histories, values, and status that distinguish them" and are "powerful agents for defining and organizing communities" (Schafer 9-10; Blesser and Salter 29) (150).
- *Keynotes* vs. *signals* vs. *soundmarks* distinguished: keynotes are ambient background sounds (air conditioning, traffic hum) that "compose our world" and bind us to place; signals are meaningful sounds we attend to (warnings, alerts); soundmarks have cultural value and status (151).
- Schafer's critique of modern soundscape: laments transformation "from an ambience of discrete sounds to a continuous contour of speed and hum" (79); advocates "ear cleaning" — cultivating the ability to listen (151-152).
- Eno's Microsoft Sound analyzed as soundmark: chosen from eighty-three alternatives, it carves out "a unique and user-friendly space against a corporate, mass-manufactured backdrop" by building through electronic buzzes/whooshes to discrete piano notes, "a movement from a cold field of electronic susurration to an individualized sprinkle of analog warmth" (152-153).
- Prangstgrip video prank as demonstration: student boots laptop at high volume in library, disrupting everyone's study — demonstrates that "the everyday reliance on sound — not its lack but its strict moderation — to foster this attunement becomes apparent precisely when it is threatened"; the boot-up music functions as *dissoi* to Microsoft's sculpted *logoi* (153).
- Musical worlding, soundmarks, and mood "ultimately interrelated": "something as brief and seemingly inconsequential as startup music opens us onto the ambient dimensions inherent in a computer-user's environment" (153).

### ch4-s7: The Composition of Ambience (pp. 154-155)
- Musical worlding and soundmarks "necessitate ontological understanding since they are not simply phenomena that a preexisting, subjective entity experiences; rather, they are woven into the fabric of the world and are themselves complex agencies furthering ways of life" (154).
- Musical worlding and soundmarks are "integral to the way the world shows up and what our sense of it will be like, and they attune us in ways that bear up how we comport ourselves for work, play, interaction, or whatever else" (154).
- Microsoft Windows startup music shapes "not so much as to inflect and sustain a small part of our world, including some aspect of the background intelligibility and sensibility that attunes us in the world" — transcends Norman's "I like/I don't like" emotional framing (154).
- Ambient rhetoric reconceived: "we must not simply turn our sights to sound and ask how we can now use sound to generate desired affective responses"; instead attend to "the ambience of design" holistically — functional, cognitive, and affective dimensions together, "complexly generating an overarching ambient environment that cradles, without forcing, particular affective responses or that fosters an attunement" (154).
- Ambient music's political dimension via Eno: "ambient music is inherently political, evoking new senses of place and how human beings inhabit it; this includes, more pointedly, a reduced role for human agency reflecting a more distributed, ecological approach to will, action, and consequence" (155).
- Final note: startup music is only "a small element amid these far larger concerns (not to mention the larger expanse of sonic space and its rhetorical-architectural possibilities)," but "it nevertheless can open us onto a deeper reflection concerning our relation to technology, space, and sonics" (155).

## 4. Main Positions

### P1: Music is rhetorical-ontological, not merely emotional or ornamental
- **Premise 1**: Three historical arguments against music (emotional danger, semantic indeterminacy, ornamentality) parallel historical suspicions directed at rhetoric (131).
- **Premise 2**: The distrust characterizing these positions "resonates with the ire rhetoric too has continually inspired through the millennia" (131).
- **Premise 3**: If music genuinely affects us deeply (as even critics admit), then it "involves rhetoric" in the ambient sense (131).
- **Conclusion**: Music-rhetoric must be theorized in an "ambient modality" that attends to the ontological conditions of their shared affective power (131-132).
- **Interlocutors**: Plato, Aristotle (as historical positions Rickert overturns)
- **Status**: Framing thesis; argued throughout the chapter via case studies.

### P2: Windows startup music demonstrates ambient rhetoric in practice
- **Premise 1**: Microsoft deliberately sought to shape "the user's attunement to the Windows workspace" through sound, not just convey branding (135-139).
- **Premise 2**: The resulting ambient dimensions — "the mutually conditioning (and not determining) confluence of sound, image, material environment, bodies, and mood" — exceed any single party's intentions (135).
- **Premise 3**: Neither music nor its ability to affect us "emerges in isolation. Sound becomes part of our material, spatial environs" (135).
- **Conclusion**: The startup music exemplifies rhetoric as ecologically distributed among human and nonhuman elements, not as agent-initiated persuasion (142).
- **Interlocutors**: Eno, Fripp, Blesser and Salter, Microsoft design teams (Torre, Allchin, Ball)
- **Status**: Central empirical case; supported by detailed compositional history.

### P3: Affect/mood is ontological, not subjective — Stimmung as ground of rhetoric
- **Premise 1**: Heidegger argues mood is "only ever a change of attunement [*Stimmung*]" (*FCM* 68) — ontological, not interior (146).
- **Premise 2**: Mood "comes neither from the 'outside' nor from the 'inside', but arises out of Being-in-the-world" (*BT* 176) (146).
- **Premise 3**: Neurocognitive research (Damasio, Greenspan/Shanker, Ratcliffe) corroborates that background feeling/affect is prior to language and symbolicity (146-147).
- **Premise 4**: Norman's *Emotional Design* makes progress toward affect-sensitive design but risks instrumentalizing emotion within a subjective/objective framework (147-148).
- **Conclusion**: Mood as *Stimmung* provides the ontological ground for understanding how music (and rhetoric generally) operates — not through discrete emotional causation but through worldly attunement (148).
- **Interlocutors**: Heidegger (*BT*, *FCM*), Damasio, Greenspan and Shanker, Ratcliffe, Norman
- **Status**: Strongest philosophical argument in the chapter; mediates between case study and ontological claim.

### P4: Musical worlding and soundmarks reveal rhetoric's material-ecological character
- **Premise 1**: Covach's musical worlding shows that a piece's meaning derives from its position in a "web of relationships" — instruments, production values, studio equipment, cultural context — not from autonomous compositional intent (149-150).
- **Premise 2**: Schafer's soundmarks demonstrate that sonic elements have cultural value, organize communities, and shape place — functioning as "powerful agents for defining and organizing communities" (150).
- **Premise 3**: The material stratum (instruments, technologies, spaces) is "integral to the composition of the Windows Vista startup music" and cannot be excluded from what we designate as "music" (150).
- **Conclusion**: Musical worlding and soundmarks "necessitate ontological understanding since they are not simply phenomena that a preexisting, subjective entity experiences; rather, they are woven into the fabric of the world" (154).
- **Interlocutors**: Covach, R. Murray Schafer, Blesser and Salter
- **Status**: Supports the ontological pivot of ch4-s7; augmented by Rickert beyond Covach's original usage.

### P5: Rhetoric must be reconceived as compositional and ecological
- **Premise 1**: An ambient rhetoric treats composition as "the synthesis of multiple content threads of varying intensities" (133), not primarily linguistic/symbolic production.
- **Premise 2**: Multimodality as a framework is insufficient because it "indicates various, discrete modes that are then combined," whereas ambient rhetoric begins "from the idea that such 'modes' derive from an a priori ambient situation in which they are interactively combined" (143).
- **Premise 3**: Attending only to functional, cognitive, or affective dimensions individually misses how they "complexly generat[e] an overarching ambient environment" (154).
- **Conclusion**: Rhetorical design should attend to the full ambience — the ecological interplay of sound, image, materiality, space, and mood — rather than isolating individual modal channels or pursuing cause-effect emotional manipulation (154-155).
- **Interlocutors**: Robin Williams (CRAP principles, as limit case), Norman, Wisneski et al. (ambientROOM)
- **Status**: Culminating practical/methodological claim; extends to pedagogy ("teachers, writers, and rhetoricians," 154).

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

### T1: Intentional design vs. ambient emergence
Rickert insists that aural architecture "is most often not an actual person" (Blesser and Salter 5) and that designed sound cannot be reduced to intentions. Yet the chapter's case studies foreground highly intentional design processes — Eno composing eighty-four pieces to specification, Fripp laboring over four hours of material for a four-second snippet, Microsoft issuing lists of desired moods. The tension between design intention and ambient emergence is acknowledged but not fully resolved: how do we theorize the design process itself as ambient without collapsing the difference between deliberate composition and naturally occurring sonic environments?

### T2: Music's semantic indeterminacy and rhetorical specificity
Rickert wants music to be rhetorical in a robust sense, yet he also insists it exceeds semantic specificity ("information's semantic specificity is not broad enough," 138). If rhetoric is ontological attunement rather than symbolic persuasion, what distinguishes a rhetorically *successful* ambient composition from an unsuccessful one? Microsoft's design brief demanded specific qualities ("clear, confident, connected"), but Rickert argues these cannot be directly caused. The criteria for evaluating ambient rhetoric remain underspecified.

### T3: Heidegger's Stimmung and empirical affect science
Rickert draws on both Heidegger's phenomenological *Stimmung* and empirical neuroscience (Damasio, Greenspan/Shanker). These converge on the priority of affect, but their ontological commitments diverge sharply: Heidegger's *Stimmung* is a structure of Dasein's Being-in-the-world, while Damasio's "background feeling" remains neurobiological. Rickert notes the convergence is "fortuitous" (147) but does not adjudicate the philosophical tensions between phenomenological and naturalistic accounts of emotion.

### T4: Scope of the case study
The chapter treats Microsoft Windows startup sounds — compositions lasting 3-6 seconds — as exemplary of ambient rhetoric. Rickert acknowledges this is "a small element amid these far larger concerns" (155). Whether the analysis scales to more complex ambient situations (full soundscapes, architectural acoustics, long-duration musical environments) remains gestural rather than demonstrated in this chapter.

### T5: Multimodality critique vs. compositional practice
Rickert rejects "multimodality" because it treats modes as discrete units to be combined (143). But his alternative — modes deriving "from an a priori ambient situation" — raises the methodological question of how practitioners (teachers, designers, writers) are to work with ambient composition if they cannot analytically isolate modal channels. The chapter gestures toward ecological holism but provides limited practical guidance for ambient compositional practice.
