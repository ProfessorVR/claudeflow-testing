## 3A: Canonical Node List

### Core Nodes

**1. Ambience / Ambient**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: The ontological condition in which the surrounding environment is not a passive container or backdrop but an active, generative, immersive ground from which things, meanings, and rhetorical situations emerge. Ambience is less driven by connection (as in networks) and more resonant with immersion, permeation, and osmosis. It names the dynamic, emergent ecology of coadapting elements -- human, nonhuman, material, technological, environmental -- that compose the world prior to and exceeding any subject-object division.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch1, Ch2, Ch3, Ch4, Ch5, Ch6, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: ambient, the ambient, ambient environs, ambient situation
- **Centrality**: core

**2. Ambient Rhetoric**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Rhetoric reconceived as the always-ongoing disclosure of the world that shifts our manner of being in that world, rather than a discrete symbolic act performed by autonomous human agents upon passive audiences. Ambient rhetoric encompasses material things, nonhuman agencies, ecological assemblages, attunement, and the withdrawal of objects from relation. It is "disclosure itself becoming affective, suasive" (Conclusion, 281) and is fundamentally preparatory rather than paradigmatic.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch1, Ch2, Ch3, Ch4, Ch5, Ch6, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: rhetoric conceived ambiently, rhetoric as ambient
- **Centrality**: core

**3. Attunement / Stimmung**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Heidegger's ontological concept of mood (*Stimmung*) reconceived as the fundamental disclosure of the world that precedes cognition, volition, and intention. Attunement is not a subjective state of mind or willed comportment but "an ambient catalysis within what is most material and concrete, a gathering that springs forward" (Ch2, 98). It is "like an atmosphere in which we first immerse ourselves in each case and which then attunes us through and through" (*FCM* 67). Attunement grounds ambient rhetoric as the condition under which things, situations, and persuasive forces show up at all.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch2, Ch3, Ch4, Ch5, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: Stimmung, mood, fundamental mood, tuning, being-attuned
- **Centrality**: core

**4. Disclosure / Aletheia**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: The Heideggerian concept of truth as unconcealment: things come to presence in a particular way, always also concealing other aspects. Disclosure is always ongoing, never exhaustive, and never solely the doing of human beings. In Rickert's culminating formulation, "rhetoric is ambience as affective disclosure" (Conclusion, 281). Every act of unconcealing is simultaneously a concealing, which means rhetoric must attend to what withdraws as well as what appears.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch1, Ch5, Ch6, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: aletheia, unconcealment, presencing, coming-to-presence
- **Centrality**: core

**5. Dwelling**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Not mere habitation but an ontological, ecological relationship between humans, things, and environments. Dwelling is "activity conditioned by the land and the things of the world" (Ch7, 243) -- the ongoing process of building, disclosure, and attunement through which humans and nonhumans coexist. "Dwelling built the farmhouse, not the peasants per se" (Ch7, 243). Dwelling is the mode through which affectability becomes rhetoric conceived ambiently, and it is inherently ethical insofar as it gives rise to values through practice rather than human projection.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch3, Ch5, Ch6, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: dwelling-with, inhabitation, homemaking, being-at-home
- **Centrality**: core

**6. Ecology**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: The relational, mutually constitutive field of human and nonhuman elements, opposed to "environment" understood as a passive container. Derived from Greek *oikos* (household/dwelling place), ecology names the condition in which things, organisms, materials, and practices condition and support one another. Rhetoric is ecological insofar as its action is "material, affective, ecological, and emergent" (Ch3, 129).
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch3, Ch4, Ch6, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: ecological, material ecology, material-social-hermeneutic ecology
- **Centrality**: core

**7. Fourfold (das Geviert)**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Heidegger's gathering of earth, sky, divinities, and mortals in things. Not four separate categories but a "onefold" in which each element mirrors and rings in the others, constituting an ongoing interplay of presence and withdrawal. The fourfold is lived first and philosophized second; it is the framework through which dwelling takes its bearings. Things "gather" the fourfold, as in the jug example where wine, water, spring, earth, and sky are all gathered in the poured gift.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: das Geviert, the four, earth-sky-divinities-mortals
- **Centrality**: core

**8. Rhetorical Being**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: The condition of being-in-the-world as inherently rhetorical -- not a rhetorical agent who deploys persuasive techniques but a being whose very existence is constituted by the ambient disclosure of world. Rhetoric is not something one does but something one is immersed in. Rhetorical being names "the synthesis of multiple content threads of varying intensities" (Ch4, 133) gathered within material and affective environments.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch2, Ch5, Ch6, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: being-rhetorical, rhetoricity, rhetorical existence
- **Centrality**: core

**9. Thing / Gathering**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: A thing is not a mere object but a gathering of concerns, relations, and situations. Etymologically, Old English *thing*/*ting* = assembly for deliberation; Latin *res* = matter of discourse; the Icelandic *Althing* = parliament. Things gather the fourfold and participate in rhetoric as material agents of disclosure. The "rhetorical thing" (Ch6) names material participants in the ambient disclosure of world -- not merely objects of persuasion but co-constituents of rhetorical situations.
- **Chapters**: Ch5, Ch6, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: rhetorical thing, Ding, gathering, res, althing
- **Centrality**: core

### Important Nodes

**10. Chora**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: The Platonic "third kind" (*triton genos*) from the *Timaeus* -- neither Form nor material copy but a generative, withdrawing matrix or receptacle from which all becoming, beginning, and creation emerge. Rickert recovers *chora* through Kristeva (semiotic/affective), Derrida (deconstructive/aporetic), and Ulmer (inventional/performative) to show that rhetoric and invention rest on an "infomaterial" ground that reveals and conceals simultaneously. The *chora* is the ancient precursor to ambience: "Place is not simply an immediate environment; it includes the background by means of which things show up" (Ch1, 55).
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch1, Ch2, Ch3, Ch7, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: khora, choric, choric ground, third kind, triton genos
- **Centrality**: important

**11. Kairos**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Traced from its earliest Greek usage as a spatial-material concept (a penetrable opening on the body, a gap in the warp of a loom) through its Sophistic elaboration as temporal opportunism, to Rickert's ambient reframing. Ambiently understood, kairos names the emergent disclosure of a situation in which rhetor, environment, and occasion co-constitute one another. Kairos "wills" the human (via Miller/Untersteiner/Heidegger), not the other way around; it aligns with complexity theory's "tipping point" where a system transforms.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch1, Ch2, Ch3, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: kairotic, kairotic moment, tipping point (complexity analog)
- **Centrality**: important

**12. Nonhuman Agency**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Agency distributed across human and nonhuman actants -- not a projection of human agency onto objects but an acknowledgment of their performative operationality emergent from material, contextual, and relational conditions. Theorized through Latour's actant concept, Harman's tool-being, Bennett's vital materialism, and Brooks's subsumption architecture. Material things exercise persuasion irreducible to symbolic mediation (speed bumps, Berlin Key, electrical grid).
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch2, Ch3, Ch6, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: distributed agency, material agency, actant, nonhuman actant
- **Centrality**: important

**13. Embodiment / Embodied Cognition**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: The condition of being bodily situated in and constituted by a material environment. Drawing on Clark's extended cognition, Dourish's embodied interaction, and Heidegger's being-in-the-world, Rickert argues that minds are "at once embodied and dispersed into the environment" -- composites of intellect, body, information, and material scaffoldings. Embodiment grounds the ambient claim that rhetoric is material, not merely symbolic.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch1, Ch2, Ch3, Ch4, Ch5
- **Aliases**: embodied/embedded cognition (EEC), extended cognition, distributed cognition, emplacement
- **Centrality**: important

**14. Invention (Ambient/Emplaced)**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Rhetorical invention reconceived as fundamentally emplaced -- arising not from autonomous rational method but from the generative matrix of material, affective, and environmental relations. Invention is something that happens *through* the coinvention of subject, place, and circumstance. Kristeva's *chora* shows invention exceeds the range of symbolicity; Ulmer's chorography practically enacts choric invention; Verbal Kint demonstrates "inventive passivity before the ambient environs."
- **Chapters**: Ch1, Ch2, Ch3
- **Aliases**: ambient invention, choric invention, emplaced invention, inventive passivity
- **Centrality**: important

**15. Worldliness / Being-in-the-World**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Heidegger's foundational claim that Dasein is always already thrown into a world of practical involvements -- not a subject confronting objects but a being immersed in meaningful situations. From this ground, language, rhetoric, and invention emerge. Worldliness means that the world is simultaneously material and meaningful, never exhausted by the presence of material things, and rhetoric is "a response to the way the world attunes and speaks us" (Ch5, 189).
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch2, Ch3, Ch5, Ch6, Ch7, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: being-in-the-world, Dasein, thrownness, situatedness
- **Centrality**: important

**16. Withdrawal**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: The ontological condition whereby objects, meanings, and the world itself are never fully present to any relation. Things always reserve depths that exceed human knowledge, use, and attribution. Withdrawal is not absence but "a form of supporting" (Ch7, 244) -- the condition of possibility for ongoing disclosure. It ensures that the world is always more than what we make of it. Theorized through Harman's tool-being, Heidegger's concealment, and the *chora*'s generative recession.
- **Chapters**: Ch1, Ch6, Ch7, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: concealment, recession, reserve, the withdrawn, hiding
- **Centrality**: important

**17. Networks**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Treated as ontological rather than merely technological -- "a principle of being" in which subjects emerge as nodular points within coadaptive threads (via Taylor's complexity theory). However, Rickert argues the network metaphor is necessary but insufficient for ambient rhetoric because it remains invested in binaries (node/link, foreground/background) and lacks the immersive, osmotic, material thickness that ambience requires. Ambience supplements and exceeds the network.
- **Chapters**: Ch3, Ch6
- **Aliases**: network culture, actor-network, incarnational networking
- **Centrality**: important

**18. Periechon**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Greek term meaning "that which surrounds, encompasses, or makes room for." Appears alongside *chora* in Plato's *Timaeus* and connects to the sense of a surrounding cosmos that is itself alive. Rickert identifies it as the ancient Greek precursor to the concept of ambience and returns to it in the book's final sentence as what ambient rhetoric recovers: "a renewed sensibility to what the ancient Greeks called *periechon*" (Conclusion, 285).
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch1, Ch6, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: that which surrounds, the encompassing
- **Centrality**: important

**19. Technics / Technology**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Technology understood not merely as instrumental tool but as disclosive -- it reveals how we are in the world (connecting to Heidegger's *Question Concerning Technology*). Rickert maintains a dual stance: technology makes ambient conditions newly visible (Eno, ambientROOM, EV1), yet technological solutions to technologically produced problems risk entrenching us deeper in technological thinking (enframing). The *chora* dissolves the *techne*/*physis* distinction.
- **Chapters**: Ch1, Ch3, Ch4, Ch6, Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: technics, techne, enframing (Gestell), technological disclosure
- **Centrality**: important

### Peripheral Nodes

**20. Terroir**
- **Type**: example
- **Definition**: The irreducible somewhereness of wine rooted in earth, climate, practice, and place. Serves as the book's governing figure from the Preface onward, returned to in the Conclusion as a "material-social-hermeneutic ecology." Terroir grounds the claim that rhetoric, like winemaking, is constituted by material relations that exceed human intention and control.
- **Chapters**: Preface, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: le gout du terroir, taste of place
- **Centrality**: peripheral

**21. Sufficiency**
- **Type**: position
- **Definition**: Not a countervalue to be adopted but an ontological attunement materialized through dwelling practices. Opposed to the ambient logic of efficiency that pervades modern life. Sufficiency "emerges as a principle only insofar as we can abstract it from already ongoing dwelling" (Ch8, 257). Demonstrated through Toronto Island's resistance to automobility.
- **Chapters**: Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: the logic of sufficiency, sustainable dwelling
- **Centrality**: peripheral

**22. Electracy**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Ulmer's term for the emerging cultural epoch shaped by electronic media, analogous to literacy's relation to print. Electracy is itself choric -- "a third kind, following but being neither progress from nor a hybrid of orality and literacy" (Ch1, 68). New media and ambient intelligence make choric invention's emplaced, distributed character newly visible.
- **Chapters**: Ch1
- **Aliases**: electronic epoch, post-literacy
- **Centrality**: peripheral

**23. Soundmarks**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: R. Murray Schafer's term for the sonic counterpart to landmarks -- culturally valued sounds that define and organize communities. Distinguished from keynotes (ambient background sounds) and signals (meaningful foreground sounds). Used in Ch4 to demonstrate that sound is woven into the fabric of dwelling and rhetoric, not ornamental addition.
- **Chapters**: Ch4
- **Aliases**: keynotes, aural architecture, sonic landmarks
- **Centrality**: peripheral

**24. Assemblage**
- **Type**: concept
- **Definition**: Bennett's term (via Deleuze): a historical, contingent grouping of disparate actants generating power along a continuum, vitally inserted into larger assemblages that exceed and conflict with constituent actants. The university, the electrical grid, Toronto Island are all assemblages. Extends Latour's actant concept by emphasizing nested complexity and uneven power distribution.
- **Chapters**: Ch6, Ch8
- **Aliases**: vital materialism, material assemblage
- **Centrality**: peripheral

**25. Preparatory Rhetoric**
- **Type**: position
- **Definition**: Rhetoric conceived not as direct persuasion but as groundwork that lays conditions for disclosure and transformed dwelling. The book itself is "ultimately only preparatory" (Ch8, 269). Ambient rhetoric "cannot short-circuit itself into being a new paradigm" but can only attune, lay groundwork, and open conditions of possibility.
- **Chapters**: Ch8, Conclusion
- **Aliases**: festive rhetoric, enlivening
- **Centrality**: peripheral

## 3C: Tension Layer

### Tension 1: Withdrawal vs. Disclosure
- **Node A**: Withdrawal
- **Node B**: Disclosure
- **Dependency edge**: depends_on -- Withdrawal is the condition of possibility for ongoing disclosure; every unconcealing is also a concealing (Ch5, Ch7, Ch8, Conc)
- **Conflict edge**: contrasts_with -- If what matters most withdraws from presence, how can rhetoric -- which operates through some form of disclosure -- access the withdrawn? (Ch8, T5; Conc)
- **Synthesis**: This is Rickert's most productive tension: withdrawal is not the enemy of disclosure but its enabling condition. The world's inexhaustibility (its always-exceeding what we make of it) is what ensures rhetoric is never finished, always preparatory, always calling for further attunement. Withdrawal grounds rhetoric's inexhaustibility.
- **Chapters**: Ch1, Ch6, Ch7, Ch8, Conc

### Tension 2: Nonhuman Agency vs. Human Particularity
- **Node A**: Nonhuman agency
- **Node B**: Rhetorical being (subjectivity)
- **Dependency edge**: supports -- Distributed agency across human and nonhuman actants is constitutive of ambient rhetoric; agency derives its bearings from the environment (Ch2, Ch3, Ch6)
- **Conflict edge**: contrasts_with -- Rickert insists humans are "particularly important" participants (Preface xviii); if subjects are merely "condensations of probabilities," how do we account for responsibility, deliberation, resistance? (Ch2, T4; Ch6, T2)
- **Synthesis**: Rickert does not eliminate subjectivity but reconceives it as emplaced and distributed. The tension is generative because it prevents ambient rhetoric from collapsing into undifferentiated flat ontology while insisting that agency is never solely human. Humans are "particularly important" without being sovereign.
- **Chapters**: Intro, Ch2, Ch3, Ch6, Ch7, Conc

### Tension 3: Attunement vs. Mastery
- **Node A**: Attunement
- **Node B**: Invention (as traditionally conceived)
- **Dependency edge**: operationalizes -- Kairos ambiently understood demands attunement as the proper disposition; attunement enables kairotic invention (Ch2, Ch3)
- **Conflict edge**: contrasts_with -- If kairos cannot be mastered and invention is "inventive passivity," what becomes of rhetorical pedagogy and deliberate craft? Mastery is critiqued but never fully replaced (Ch2, T5; Ch1, T2)
- **Synthesis**: Attunement redefines rather than eliminates skilled practice. The rhetor does not seize the moment but is seized by it -- yet this requires a cultivated receptivity (Schafer's "ear cleaning," Gorgias's trust in the moment) that is itself a form of expertise, albeit non-sovereign.
- **Chapters**: Ch2, Ch3, Ch4, Ch8

### Tension 4: Networks vs. Ambience
- **Node A**: Networks
- **Node B**: Ambience
- **Dependency edge**: refines -- Ambience supplements and exceeds networks; networks prepare the ground for ambient thinking (Ch3)
- **Conflict edge**: contrasts_with -- Networks remain invested in binaries (node/link, foreground/background) that ambience dissolves; Taylor's networks are "dry" -- lacking materiality and thought springs (Ch3, 121)
- **Synthesis**: Networks are necessary but insufficient. They correctly grasp the ontological (not merely technical) character of relationality, but ambience adds the immersive, osmotic, choric thickness that the network metaphor cannot provide. The tension drives the book's move from Part 1's deconstructive work to Part 2's constructive dwelling.
- **Chapters**: Ch3, Ch6

### Tension 5: Burke vs. Heidegger on Language
- **Node A**: Burke (symbolic action)
- **Node B**: Heidegger (language as house of being)
- **Dependency edge**: supports -- Burke's recognition of nonverbal persuasion gestures toward ambient rhetoric; Heidegger intensifies Burke's insights (Ch5)
- **Conflict edge**: contrasts_with -- Burke grounds language in human symbolicity (body, social drama); Heidegger locates language in worldly disclosure, making matter co-constitutive. Burke "swings too far" toward symbolicity (Ch5, 186)
- **Synthesis**: Rickert harvests Burke for the richness of social drama and embodied rhetoric while correcting him with Heidegger's more radical decentering of the human. Together they establish that meaning inheres in the world, but they disagree on whether humans remain the gravitational center of language.
- **Chapters**: Ch5

### Tension 6: Dwelling vs. Technology (Sufficiency vs. Efficiency)
- **Node A**: Dwelling (sufficiency)
- **Node B**: Technics (efficiency)
- **Dependency edge**: depends_on -- Technology discloses ambient conditions and makes choric/ambient dimensions visible (Ch1, Ch3, Ch4)
- **Conflict edge**: contrasts_with -- Technological solutions to technologically produced problems entrench enframing; the EV1 "in the end never arrives at the larger problem" (Ch8, 264)
- **Synthesis**: Technology is both the condition under which ambient rhetoric becomes visible and the principal obstacle to genuine dwelling. The EV1 and Toronto Island together show that ambient rhetoric requires not anti-technology but a transformed relation to technology grounded in dwelling rather than efficiency.
- **Chapters**: Ch4, Ch8, Conc

### Tension 7: Latour vs. Heidegger on Things
- **Node A**: Latour (Dingpolitik / representationalist inclusion)
- **Node B**: Heidegger (fourfold / ontological attunement)
- **Dependency edge**: supports -- Latour's recovery of the etymological link between Ding and political assembly and his insistence on nonhuman actants advances ambient rhetoric (Ch6, Ch7)
- **Conflict edge**: contrasts_with -- Latour's representationalist parliament "sneaks humanism back in"; the simple fact of inclusion is insufficient to grant things existential weight (Ch7, 239)
- **Synthesis**: Rickert needs both: Latour's concrete demonstrations of material persuasion (speed bump, Berlin Key) and Heidegger's deeper ontological framework. Dingpolitik does not come first through extending politics but emerges from dwelling as attunement -- the political is grounded in the ontological.
- **Chapters**: Ch6, Ch7, Ch8

| Node | Chapters | Nature of Shift | Resolution |
|------|----------|----------------|------------|
| **Chora** | Ch1, Ch3, Ch7, Conc | In Ch1, *chora* is the central concept -- a generative matrix examined through three interlocutors (Kristeva, Derrida, Ulmer) with full philosophical weight. By Ch3, it becomes one resource among several for ambient thinking. In Ch7, it reappears as a historical precursor to the fourfold. In the Conclusion, it names the principle of movement constitutive of place. | The *chora*'s shift from protagonist to supporting concept reflects the book's own movement from genealogical recovery (Part 1) to constructive dwelling (Part 2). The fourfold effectively absorbs and extends what *chora* initiated. |
| **Kairos** | Ch2, Ch3 | In Ch2, kairos receives full genealogical treatment as a spatial-material concept. In Ch3, it is reconceived through complexity theory as a "tipping point." The Heideggerian reading (kairos wills us) and the complexity reading (emergent, structurally describable) create internal instability. | Rickert treats them as complementary rather than competing: both dissolve the autonomous subject, but the Heideggerian version adds ontological depth (withdrawal) that the complexity version lacks. The concept stabilizes as "ambient kairos" after Ch3. |
| **Invention** | Ch1, Ch2, Ch3, Ch5 | Begins as choric invention (Ch1: generative, withdrawing, exceeding method), becomes ambient invention (Ch2: emplaced, coinvented with environment), then folds into writing-as-ambient (Ch3). What Ch1 calls "invention inventing itself" becomes what the later chapters call "disclosure" and "dwelling." |

In Ch6, Latour's actor-network returns as a resource for theorizing material persuasion. | The instability reflects Rickert's ambivalence: networks are the right intuition (ontological relationality) with the wrong metaphorics (node/link binaries). | **Dwelling** | Ch3, Ch5, Ch7, Ch8, Conc | In Ch3, dwelling appears as "the house of being" that is haunted and uncanny -- "the writer writing is not at home." ## 3G: Macro-Trajectory

Rickert's *Ambient Rhetoric* executes a two-part movement from deconstructive genealogy to constructive ontology, with the pivot occurring at the boundary between Part 1 ("Diffractions of Ambience," Chapters 1-4) and Part 2 ("Dwelling with Ambience," Chapters 5-8).

Part 1 performs genealogical and deconstructive work on classical rhetorical concepts to loosen their humanist and subjectivist encrustation. Chapter 1 recovers the Platonic *chora* as a generative, withdrawing matrix that grounds invention in material-affective relations rather than autonomous rationality. Chapter 2 extends this recovery to kairos, tracing its originary spatial-material meaning beneath the dominant temporal-opportunistic reading and dissolving the subject-object dichotomy through distributed cognition and Heidegger's *Augenblick*. Chapter 3 tests these recovered concepts against network culture and complexity theory, finding networks ontologically significant but metaphorically insufficient -- ambience requires immersion, osmosis, and choric engagement beyond the link. Chapter 4 translates the argument into sonic practice through Eno's ambient music and Microsoft Windows startup sounds, demonstrating that mood (*Stimmung*), musical worlding, and soundmarks reveal rhetoric's material-ecological character. Throughout Part 1, the mode is primarily *diffractive*: each chapter takes a concept (chora, kairos, networks, music) and refracts it through ambient thinking to reveal suppressed material dimensions.

The pivot point occurs at the opening of Chapter 5, where Rickert announces the transition to Part 2's constructive project. The key turn is from asking "what has been suppressed?" to asking "what can be built?" Chapter 5 stages the Burke-Heidegger encounter on language and attunement, establishing that meaning inheres in the world and that persuasion is prior to rhetoric's emergence as symbolic act. This is the book's philosophical hinge: Burke provides social drama and embodied rhetoric, Heidegger provides the ontological dissolution of human privilege in language, and together they ground the constructive account that follows. Chapter 6 builds the theory of the "rhetorical thing" through Harman, Latour, and Bennett, culminating in the *Earthrise* photograph as paradigmatic ambient rhetoric. Chapter 7 constructs the fullest positive account of dwelling through Heidegger's fourfold and a critical engagement with Latour's Dingpolitik, arguing that withdrawal is itself a form of rhetorical supporting. Chapter 8 applies the framework to ecological practice through the Toronto Island community and the EV1, arriving at the claim that ambient rhetoric is "ultimately only preparatory" -- it lays groundwork for transformed dwelling rather than offering a new paradigm.

The Conclusion completes the trajectory by returning to *terroir* and addressing Heidegger's political silence, the abyssal ground of reason, and the dangers of conservative readings. Barad's agential realism provides a final resonance with Heidegger on disclosure, and the book ends where it began -- with the *periechon*, the encompassing, the ambient. The macro-trajectory thus moves from loosening (Part 1) to building (Part 2), from classical concepts recovered to contemporary dwelling practiced, with the consistent thread being that rhetoric is ambient: not imposed on the world but emerging from the world's own self-disclosure.
