## 1. Chapter Metadata

- **Title**: "Ambient Dwelling: Heidegger, Latour, and the Fourfold Thing"
- **Part**: Part 2 -- Dwelling with Ambience
- **Chapter**: 7
- **Pages**: 220-245
- **One-sentence thesis**: Rickert argues that dwelling, understood through Heidegger's fourfold (*das Geviert*) and critically supplemented by Latour's *Dingpolitik*, is not a subjective human achievement but a distributed, relational attunement to things in their gathering of earth, sky, divinities, and mortals -- and that the fourfold's constitutive withdrawal is itself a form of rhetorical supporting that discloses the conditions of possibility for ambient rhetoric.

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### ch7-s1: [Opening] (pp. 220-222)
- Rickert recapitulates the previous chapter's argument that ambient rhetoric reconceives rhetorical invention through attunement, ecology, and the material conditions of world-disclosure, and positions Chapter 7 as advancing the concept of *dwelling* as the chapter's central concern.
- Dwelling is introduced as something that "shifts our manner of being in the world" -- not merely habitation but an ontological relationship between humans, things, and environment.
- Signals that Heidegger and Latour will be the chapter's two principal interlocutors, with Heidegger providing the fourfold framework and Latour the political-materialist supplement.

### ch7-s2: Ethics/Ethos as Dwelling (pp. 222-223)
- Draws on Michael Hyde's work to connect *ethos* to *dwelling*: ethos is not simply rhetorical credibility but the making of a dwelling place, a home, an abode.
- Heidegger, as Hyde makes plain, argues that things are "knit into our way of making a home," so that things are integral to ethos -- how we make an abode (p. 225).
- Homemaking on this account is not humanist; it is distributed, ecological, and attuned: "we do not just gather things in our existence; in addition, we are gathered by things: 'we are the be-thinged, the conditioned ones'" (Heidegger, *PLT* 181).
- Utility alone cannot define the worth and importance of things; values emerge through ecological, distributed doing rather than human attribution alone.

### ch7-s3: All Things Considered (pp. 223-225)
- Rickert performs a sustained etymological and conceptual analysis of the word *thing*: Old English *thing* (as well as *dinc*, *ting*) refers to a gathering where matters of concern are deliberated, evoking *chora* from earlier chapters.
- Iceland's *Althing* presented as paradigmatic example: a parliament held at a specific, sublime location -- a *thing* is simultaneously a gathering, a place, and the matters gathered (p. 226).
- The Romans' *res* referred to matters of discourse and *res publica* to that which is deliberated in public (*PLT* 174); the Latin *causa* (case, cause, contested matter) becomes French *la chose* via Romance *la cosa*, all carrying the double meaning of material substance and matters of concern (*PLT* 175).
- Foucault's *Les Mots et les choses* (*The Order of Things*) invoked: the connection between "that which is at all" and "that which is in some way or other" has persisted across cultures and ages (*PLT* 176).
- *Matter* and *stuff* likewise carry double meanings -- material substance and significance/concern -- reinforcing that deliberation has always been conducted through and amid things.
- Latour uses Heidegger to connect things to assemblages; his *Making Things Public* collection thematizes the public thing as *Dingpolitik* (p. 227).

### ch7-s4: The Thing as Gathering: Heidegger's Fourfold (pp. 225-235)
- **The fourfold defined**: Heidegger's fourfold (*das Geviert*) comprises earth, sky, divinities, and mortals -- "admittedly a rather oblique statement" (p. 224). Dwelling takes its bearings from, manifests, and cultivates an attunement to the fourfold.
- **Earth**: the building bearer, nourishing with fruits, tending water and rock, plant and animal. Earth is also the hidden building bearer, held to be what withdraws, harboring secrets and complex interactions only partially revealed (*PLT* 178).
- **Sky**: the sun's path, the course of the moon, the glitter of stars, the year's seasons, light and dusk, the weather, drifting clouds, blue depth of the ether.
- **Divinities**: the beckoning messengers of the godhead; out of the hidden sway of the divinities, the god emerges as what removes him from comparison with beings that are present.
- **Mortals**: human beings, called mortals because they can die -- "To die means to be capable of death as death. Only man dies" (*PLT* 178).
- **The jug example**: Heidegger's jug in "The Thing" gathers the fourfold. The jug is not a jug because language presents it as such or because humans made it; rather, a jug is already a thing whose space is carved out by material affordances (clay, need for holding liquids). The "poured gift" of the jug consists of wine, water, milk -- outpourings that sustain ritual, communality, conviviality, caretaking. What is outpoured was already gathered, cultivated, prepared in dwelling places -- water from the spring, wine from the grape (*PLT* 172-173).
- **Historical legacy**: Rickert traces the fourfold back to Empedocles's four elements (air, earth, fire, water), noting that roots (*rhizomata*) were characterized by moisture, the open sea, and also in terms of the gods. Empedocles's four elements are possibility as well as material actuality, carrying archetypal, cosmic, and psychic resonances. Connection drawn to Neolithic "elemental" shapes (Platonic solids), *chora*, and Critchlow on geometric symbolism (pp. 230-231).
- **Against abstraction (Harman critique)**: Rickert argues against Graham Harman's reading, which reduces the fourfold to a metaphor for two axes (presence/withdrawal and unity/plurality). Harman's neglect of the everyday and the concrete makes his understanding "strangely arid" (p. 234). Rickert sides with Mark Wrathall: the fourfold "knits things and the world of human practice and meaning together into existential importance" (p. 234). The fourfold is not simply a description of presence but "an ongoing process of disclosure that never fully wrests being from the world and brings it into associated with good beer and morning coffee" (pp. 234-235).
- **David Kolb**: Reducing the fourfold to more precise terminology ("object, times and moods, values, and individual subjects") eliminates the tension between presence and withdrawal that is central to the fourfold (p. 233).
- **Each element rings in the others**: The fourfold is really a onefold; its elements ring out in one another. Earth may be the hidden building bearer, but earth is also brought to presence. None of the elements can be assigned to only a single category. "The four are always modulating between concealment and unconcealment" (pp. 235-236).

### ch7-s5: Latour's Parliament of Things (pp. 235-240)
- Latour's *Making Things Public* collection and *Dingpolitik* introduced: Latour resurrects Husserl's slogan "Back to Things!" to argue that the *Ding* designates both those who assemble and what causes their concerns and divisions (p. 228).
- Latour delves into etymology (like Heidegger) but acknowledges that numerous institutions already deal variously with things -- scientific laboratories, technical institutions, marketplaces, churches, temples, boardrooms, digital forums (p. 228).
- Latour invokes Heidegger's fourfold (*das Geviert*): "those sites able to assemble mortals and gods, humans and nonhumans" (Latour 13), but Rickert argues Latour's emphasis on presence and the known is problematic when compared to Heidegger's understanding of the fourfold (p. 238).
- **Rickert's critique of Latour**: Latour's representationalist parliament "sneaks humanism back in precisely where it seemed to have been evacuated" (p. 239). The simple fact of inclusion is insufficient to grant things existential weight and import -- there can be "no simple revaluing without deeper transformations in our lived relations to the world" (p. 239).
- Latour's parliament depends on an a priori separation between humans and nonhumans, which is an odd position for Latour, who wants to dissolve clear boundaries (p. 238).
- Karen Barad's critique invoked: the political field is not limited to the statehouse but is the ongoing activity separating human and nonhuman, those represented and those who act, and those who do not (Barad 59) (p. 238).
- Things are not simply present and relational, awaiting representation; they constitute "the impossibly convoluted and interactive stitchwork of being to which we have only partial access" (p. 239). A new attunement is necessary, not merely a new representational scheme.
- Affectability must be worldly; it must go beyond human doing. Latour's representationalist parliament is insufficient because "the simple fact of inclusion is insufficient to grant things existential weight" (p. 239).

### ch7-s6: Withdrawal as Supporting: Place and the Fourfold (pp. 240-245)
- **Black Forest farmhouse**: Heidegger's famous description (*PLT* 160) analyzed at length. The farmhouse is richly interwoven into its sustaining environs -- built from material given by the land, placed just so as regards the land's features. It does not force itself over the land or master it but achieves "a sustainable coexistence, a mutual flourishing" (p. 240).
- **Urban dwelling (against Heidegger's rural privileging)**: Rickert counters Heidegger's apparent privileging of the rural by invoking Foghat's "Fool for the City" and War's "The World Is a Ghetto." The city man returning home feels dwelling in urban traffic, crowd, and bustle just as the peasant does in the Black Forest. War's song traces the fourfold in the ghetto -- earth, sky, mortals, calling and searching, even some sense of the divine -- while providing "no easy harmony" but strife, poverty, and divisiveness (pp. 240-242).
- **Dwelling is not the work of humans alone**: "Dwelling built the farmhouse, not the peasants per se" (p. 243). Dwelling includes building, conditioned by land, things, weather, mortality -- "the environment also takes part in the doing, and dwelling is Heidegger's distillation for this dispersion of agency" (p. 243).
- **The ghetto as fourfold**: A ghetto is not simply a social problem but a complex assemblage of humans and nonhumans, "caught up in forms of mutual if asymmetrical disclosure" (p. 243). War's ghetto is simultaneously indictment and call -- the world is ghetto, meaning social and material divisiveness pervade everywhere, not just one place.
- **Withdrawal as supporting**: "This withdrawing is also a form of supporting; it is the repletion of all that is, which in turn ensures that the world is always more than what we make of it" (p. 244). Withdrawal is not absence but the condition of possibility for disclosure: dwelling makes being's concealment an occasion for joy, humbleness, thought and insight, guidance and action.
- **Place is never given in its entirety**: The fourfold cannot be reduced to direct presence; it is also what remains concealed. The productive "acknowledgment of what is not human in the context of what is" (Thiele, *Timely* 179) means that a location is inseparable from its ecological connectedness (p. 244).
- **Affectability as rhetoric**: "Affectability is cushioned by a leeway that emerges in our ways of dwelling; in this manner affectability becomes rhetoric conceived ambiently" (p. 245). Disclosure cannot be grounded in naturalism; it becomes a new permutation of the *physis*/*nomos* split.
- **Co-responsiveness over attitude shift**: Heidegger warns that the shift from representationalist thinking to fourfold attunement cannot be a "mere shift of attitude" (*PLT* 181). It requires *co-responsiveness* -- an acknowledgment (*bedingt*) that we are the be-thinged and conditioned ones, requiring a change in the kinds of dwelling that make continual disclosure possible (p. 245).

## 4. Main Positions

**P1 (Dwelling as ontological, not humanist)**: Dwelling is not the subjective achievement of human agents imposing culture upon nature. Rather, "dwelling built the farmhouse, not the peasants per se" (p. 243). Dwelling is activity conditioned by the land, things, weather, mortality, and the environment -- a distributed, ecological process in which human agency is one participant among many. Heidegger grounds human flourishing in constructive activity: "the attunement to the world that characterizes dwelling is not passivity" but rather "activity that works through and with things as they show up in the world alongside us" (p. 224).

**P2 (The fourfold as ongoing disclosure, not abstract metaphor)**: Against Harman's reduction of the fourfold to two abstract axes (presence/withdrawal, unity/plurality) and Kolb's reduction to precise terminology, Rickert insists that the fourfold is "not simply a description of presence, of directly revealed things and their lived relations, but an ongoing process of disclosure that never fully wrests being from the world" (p. 233-234). The fourfold is lived first and philosophized second; its use of weddings, songs, and dances is not obscurantism but points to uniquely disclosive everyday practices.

**P3 (Withdrawal is itself a form of rhetorical supporting)**: The fourfold's constitutive withdrawal -- the fact that place is never given in its entirety, that things always exceed what we make of them -- is not a limit but a positive condition. "This withdrawing is also a form of supporting" (p. 244). It makes dwelling an occasion for joy, humbleness, insight, and ongoing attunement. This is the chapter's most original contribution to the ambient rhetoric project: withdrawal grounds rhetoric's inexhaustibility.

**P4 (Latour's Dingpolitik is necessary but insufficient)**: Rickert appreciates Latour's recovery of the etymological link between *Ding* and political assembly and his insistence on including nonhuman actants. However, Latour's representationalist framework "sneaks humanism back in precisely where it seemed to have been evacuated" (p. 239). Simply granting nonhumans representation within a human-designed parliament does not transform the ontological relations between humans and things. What is needed is not representation but a deeper attunement to the fourfold.

**P5 (Ethos is dwelling; things are integral to ethos)**: Following Hyde, Rickert argues that ethos is fundamentally the making of a dwelling place. Things are "knit into our way of making a home" (p. 225). Homemaking is not humanist but distributed and ecological: "we are the be-thinged, the conditioned ones" (Heidegger, *PLT* 181). This reconnects rhetoric's ancient concept of ethos to the ontological register of dwelling.

**P6 (Urban dwelling is as valid as rural dwelling)**: Against the common reading of Heidegger as privileging rural, provincial life, Rickert uses Foghat's "Fool for the City" and War's "The World Is a Ghetto" to argue that dwelling is possible anywhere -- city, ghetto, desert. "Despite much of what Heidegger himself might say, dwelling is possible anywhere" (p. 248, anticipating Ch. 8). The ghetto example is particularly powerful: the fourfold remains present even in poverty and divisiveness, and War's charge that "the world is a ghetto" encompasses all the world, not one confined place.

**P7 (Affectability becomes rhetoric when conceived ambiently)**: The chapter concludes by arguing that "affectability is cushioned by a leeway that emerges in our ways of dwelling; in this manner affectability becomes rhetoric conceived ambiently" (p. 245). The shift to fourfold attunement requires not a mere change of attitude but *co-responsiveness* -- an acknowledgment that we are be-thinged and conditioned, requiring new kinds of dwelling that make continual disclosure possible.

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

**T1 (The status of the divinities)**: Rickert acknowledges that Heidegger's terminology is "overly rustic" and that "the later Heidegger is murky and mystical" (p. 230). The divinities remain the most opaque element of the fourfold. Rickert gestures toward a secularized reading (the dimension of the sacred, the withdrawn, the uncanny) but does not fully theorize how divinities function in contemporary, secular, or urban contexts. War's song invokes "some sense of the divine" in the ghetto, but this remains underspecified.

**T2 (Harman critique and concreteness)**: Rickert's charge that Harman's reading is "strangely arid" and neglects the everyday raises the question of whether Rickert himself provides sufficiently concrete accounts of what fourfold dwelling looks like in practice. The farmhouse, the jug, and the song lyrics are suggestive, but the chapter remains primarily philosophical rather than practical. (This tension is partly addressed by Ch. 8's case study on sufficiency and automobility.)

**T3 (Latour's dissolution of boundaries vs. Rickert's critique)**: Rickert critiques Latour for maintaining an a priori human/nonhuman separation, but Latour's entire project (ANT, *We Have Never Been Modern*) aims to dissolve such separations. The question is whether Rickert's critique is internal to Latour's own logic or whether Rickert imports a Heideggerian standard that Latour would not accept. Rickert invokes Barad's critique to strengthen the point, but the tension between representationalist inclusion and ontological transformation remains a live question.

**T4 (Heidegger's politics and the rural)**: Rickert addresses the conservative, provincial, even xenophobic resonances of dwelling discourse (discussed more fully at the start of Ch. 8, pp. 247-248) and counters with urban examples. But the tension between Heidegger's known political commitments and the ambient rhetoric project's progressive, ecological aspirations is not fully resolved -- it is flagged as an ongoing concern.

**T5 (Withdrawal as supporting: rhetorical cash value)**: The claim that withdrawal is "a form of supporting" is philosophically rich but raises the question of what it means for rhetorical practice. If the world always exceeds our grasp, what follows for rhetoric beyond a general posture of humility and openness? The chapter gestures toward co-responsiveness and "new kinds of dwelling" but defers concrete elaboration to Ch. 8.
