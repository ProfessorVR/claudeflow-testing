## 1. Chapter Metadata

- **Title**: "The Rhetorical Thing"
- **Part**: Part 2 — Dwelling with Ambience
- **Chapter**: 6
- **Pages**: 191-219 (PDF 211-239)
- **One-sentence thesis**: Rickert argues that rhetoric must be understood as encompassing material things and nonhuman agencies—via Harman's object-oriented ontology, Latour's actor-network theory, and Bennett's vital materialism—so that the "rhetorical thing" names not merely an object of persuasion but a participant in the ambient disclosure of world, as exemplified by the *Earthrise* photograph's capacity to transform dwelling.

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### ch6-s1: [Opening] — pp. 191-193

- Opens with Michael Kochin's claim that materiality poses a challenge for rhetorical theory; Rickert frames the chapter as addressing how "things" participate in rhetoric beyond symbolic mediation.
- Announces the chapter's guiding question: how to account for the material dimensions of rhetoric without collapsing into naive realism, material determinism, or a new permutation of idealism.
- Previews engagement with Harman's speculative realism and object-oriented philosophy as a path that integrates the ambient perspective, treating objects as both relating to and withdrawing from their environments.
- Argues that human being requires an "ontological weddedness to things and environs" that affords rhetoricity as a modality of being-together, not relegable to human symbolicity alone (198).
- Distinguishes his position from Judith Butler's postmodern constructivism: the bodying of the body is a further formation of being-in-the-world conditioned by material world, not a discursive construction that trumps materiality (198).

### ch6-s2: Realism or Idealism? No Thanks! — pp. 193-198

- Surveys the realism/idealism debate via Hikins's naive realism and Lyne's pragmatic idealism; shows that both share a subject/object split that ambient rhetoric must overcome.
- Hikins's realism: confidence in the world's reality, epistemological access to "at least some aspects" of it (22); but waffling on nuance opens the door for idealist critique.
- Lyne's idealism: experience cannot be divorced from ideas, meaning is always in the world; but a turn to the physical risks "Nowheresville" for the rhetorician (171-72).
- Argues that the binary opposition between realism and idealism "sets up in advance what can be argued and how" (197), excluding precisely the territory Rickert wants to occupy.
- Introduces Heidegger's nutshell statement from *Parmenides*: "only when man becomes the subject do non-human beings become objects" (165); language emerges because we are already entangled with the material realm.
- Previews Harman and speculative realism as a way to understand what a material object is or can be without the subject/object dichotomy.

### ch6-s3: How to Have Fun with Hammers: Graham Harman's Tool-being — pp. 199-202

- Extends Heidegger's tool analysis via Harman's *Tool-Being*: objects cannot be positioned solely within the horizon of human intention or awareness; they exceed every relation they enter (TB 2).
- Harman's "grand, dark abundance" (TB 125): even totaling all contexts, roles, and social goals in which an object is embedded would not give us its being; objects always withdraw.
- Dreyfus reads Heidegger as a "robust realist" avoiding naive realism and the Charybdis of idealism; the world is a *participant* in the ongoing event of being-in-the-world, not a stage or backdrop.
- The bridge example: a bridge is a nexus of relationality contributing to the making of a place even as it simultaneously withdraws from all relation—the key ontological insight is that objects act without anthropomorphism.
- Withdrawal as the other key issue: unintended consequences (blowback) illustrate that whatever we think or design about an object, it always exceeds such assignments; objects wend their way through their own capacities.
- Harman articulates a position in neither realism nor idealism: objects are like icebergs, simultaneously as real as can be and largely undiscovered, bearing a "strange vitality" that is neither anthropomorphism nor idealist projection.

### ch6-s4: What Can Automation Tell Us about Agency? — pp. 202-207

- Engages Carolyn Miller's essay "What Can Automation Tell Us about Agency?" on the AutoSpeech-Easy scoring machine: the question is whether and how machines exercise agency in rhetorical terms.
- Miller's two levels of agency attribution: (1) agency is "posited" via the Eliza effect (humans project agency onto machines); (2) the machine is an "agent function" (derived from Foucault's "author function") referring to performative operationality through material, contextual, and ideological conditions (C. Miller 151-152).
- Rickert pushes beyond Miller: Harman's argument takes us in a "quite different direction"—if the machine were built, it would transform how people perform, technologize humans, and redistribute labor between humans and nonhumans (203).
- The AutoSpeech-Easy's "agentive" or "performative" push resists specificity and anthropomorphism, yet leaves traces on human behavior like a black hole leaving traces on surrounding matter (203).
- Burke's argument (from Ch 5) that nonverbal things take on the nature of words (*RM* 186): Miller rightly underscores the performative nature of agency, but locating agency in teachers' reactions shores up anthropocentricity.
- Transition to Latour: Harman details the object's withdrawal; Latour will demonstrate that objects show their "agency" more concretely through material persuasion.

### ch6-s5: [Latour: Material Persuasion] — pp. 207-209

- Introduces Bruno Latour's work on nonhuman agents and material persuasion via *Pandora's Hope*: human activity proceeds via engagements and entanglements with material things; Latour uses terms like *actants*, *objects*, and *nonhumans* to escape the strictures of objectivity (PH 185).
- The Shane example (gun debate): Latour argues humans and guns as actants are ontologically equal and irreducible; their jointure yields a new entity (citizen-gun or gun-citizen) with new capabilities (PH 179). Rhetoric enters because assembly and relation do not just *happen*; persuasion is necessary.
- The speed bump (*gendarme couche*): a paradigmatic instance of material persuasion. The bump translates the driver's goals (soft inducement not to hurt pedestrians) into a hard inducement (protect car from damage), manifesting expression materially rather than symbolically (PH 186-187).
- Contrast with Burke: Burke's church spire example operates in a world of symbolic meaning; the speed bump's force "cannot be reduced to meaning"—it exemplifies material suasion through tumult, damage, and affective encounter.
- The Berlin Key essay: Latour shows that disciplinary relations are "incarnated" in material form; the key is not a singular material force but a composite of key-inhabitant-supervisor-schedule-door (207). Latour's point: critical social relations reside in material things.
- Latour augments Burke: social drama plays through material things; dropping the exclusive focus on the social and symbolic, Latour shows dramatics are no longer the province of humans alone.

### ch6-s6: Thing One and Thing Two: From Latour to Bennett — pp. 209-213

- Identifies a remaining conflict: for Heidegger and Harman, an object is never fully brought to presence; Latour's strong relationalism seems to imply things are entirely what their encounters bring them to be. Harman "rides the Heideggerian line on withdrawal harder" (TB 2).
- Transition from Latour to Bennett: the speed bump assemblage (human-car-motion-sensitivity-bump-tumult-fragility-discomfort-damage) illustrates that encounters necessarily involve something else; withdrawal means the persuasive force of material objects can slip away.
- Jane Bennett's vital materialism: rather than actants in a network, Bennett proposes assemblages as historical, contingent groupings vitally inserted into larger assemblages that exceed and conflict with constituent actants ("Agency" 445).
- The university as assemblage: a grouping of disparate loci of power along a continuum from human actors (students, faculty) to forces and things (methodologies, texts, funding, technologies, buildings), all embedded in shifting terrains of other assemblages.
- The 2003 North American blackout: Bennett argues the grid is a complex assemblage of "coal, sweat, electromagnetic fields, computer programs, electron streams, profit motives, heat, lifestyles, nuclear fuel, plastic..." ("Agency" 448). The blackout cannot be explained by human activity alone; electricity itself is a wild actant prone to "loop flows."
- Bennett's grid "speaks": rhetorically, it speaks in two senses—(1) in its particular idiom, letting us know mistakes were made and mastery is incomplete, and (2) through its withdrawal, laying out an infinite conditionality with unfathomable depths (213).
- Rickert's synthesis: "the partiality and emergence of rhetoric is a modality of our dwelling in the world" (209). Inclusion and selection (as Burke reminds us) are rhetorical because they demonstrate motivation through an admixture of humans and nonhumans.

### ch6-s7: Earthrise — pp. 213-219

- Heidegger on Sputnik: the word *Sputnik* was already spoken before the object was wrought; spoken in advance by the word *hurry*, which gleaned the ambient situation from which the desire for such technology emerged.
- Rickert agrees but pushes further: Sputnik itself, as a technological object with unique powers and affordances, also already speaks in *Earthrise*. Sputnik hollows out a space for *Earthrise*, illuminating an originary material affectability (217).
- *Earthrise* (Apollo 8, December 1968): the photograph is transformative as an instance of ambient rhetoric. It differs from all earlier images in making available a new sense of world and our belongingness to it; "the earth is now an ecological world, including not just humans but animals, plants, stones, water, soil, clouds, and more, *all of which need and are affected by one another*" (215).
- The image's power cannot be accounted for by human attribution alone: the materiality of the earth—"the fragile, sheltering beauty of the 'blue marble'"—is more than a meaningful image; it bears up the meanings we reach for (216).
- Materiality speaks in a twofold sense: (1) meaning never culls all that there is of something, including its affective capacity; (2) meaning and language result from human and nonhuman composites, emerging from the world (216).
- *Earthrise* calls and attunes: it cultivates directives without demanding or coercing; bringing them into play requires collective work including humans and nonhumans. Rhetoricity cannot be rendered solely as human doing (216).
- Nietzsche's "death of God" passage (*Gay Science* 181-82) invoked: the cosmological groundlessness Nietzsche describes is what *Earthrise* both evokes and counters—the earth as *periechon*, sheltering and encompassing, rooted in the Greek sense of ecology (*oikos*) as "dwelling place" (215).
- *Earthrise* germinates modalities of dwelling: attending to, shepherding, cultivating active awareness of cohabitation and mutual conditioning by nonhumans (215). Not holistic overoptimism but a fragility and value in what is assumed mundane and masterable.
- Responds to Heidegger's alarm about photographs from space (*HR* 325): if *Earthrise* merely filled the void offered by science, Heidegger would be correct. But its power comes from the way the image emerges from science and calls for something beyond enframing—a new engagement with the nonhuman and the earth as material thing (218).
- Closing: the word *thing* (*althing*) meant precisely a gathering of people deliberating about matters of concern, an assembly gathered by things. *Earthrise* beckons toward an ethic not fully captured by human-supplied meanings; things are profoundly integrated into human belonging and offer the possibility of new relations to technology (218-219).

## 4. Main Positions

**P1**: The realism/idealism binary is inadequate for rhetorical theory because both positions share a subject/object split that forecloses the ambient materiality Rickert seeks to theorize. The binary "sets up in advance what can be argued and how" (197). [pp. 193-198]

**P2**: Objects withdraw from every relation they enter (Harman), meaning that rhetoric must attend not only to what is present and disclosed but to the "grand, dark abundance" (TB 125) that exceeds human access, use, and meaning-making. Withdrawal is not absence but the reserving of the material world's potential for all future relationality. [pp. 199-202, 204, 212]

**P3**: Nonhuman things exercise a form of agency that is neither anthropomorphic projection nor simple mechanical causation but a performative operationality emergent from material, contextual, and relational conditions. Miller's AutoSpeech-Easy, Latour's speed bumps and Berlin Key, and Bennett's electrical grid all demonstrate this distributed agency. [pp. 202-213]

**P4**: Material persuasion (Latour) names a form of rhetoric irreducible to symbolic mediation: the speed bump persuades materially through tumult and damage, not through meaning; its force "cannot be reduced to meaning" (207). Rhetoric thus encompasses material organization, pursuit, translation, and negotiation complexly enmeshed in assemblages of humans and nonhumans. [pp. 206-209]

**P5**: Assemblage theory (Bennett) extends Latour by emphasizing that agency is never an isolable event but a composite achievement of alliances and compositions stemming from interplay of discrete and collective agencies. The 2003 blackout demonstrates that human activity alone cannot account for what happened; electricity itself is a wild, agentive actant. [pp. 210-212]

**P6**: *Earthrise* is a paradigmatic instance of ambient rhetoric: a material thing (photographic image + technological apparatus + earth-as-object) that transforms dwelling by disclosing the earth as an ecological world of mutual need and affectability among humans and nonhumans. Its power exceeds human attribution because the materiality of the earth itself bears up the meanings we reach for. [pp. 213-219]

**P7**: "The partiality and emergence of rhetoric is a modality of our dwelling in the world" (209). Rhetoric is ambient insofar as it acknowledges that things are part of rhetoric's condition of possibility, always already material in a double sense. Rhetoric's becoming ambient is a modality of how we dwell in the world. [pp. 209, 213]

**P8**: Even ethics cannot be considered solely within the human domain: *Earthrise* beckons toward an ethic that is not fully captured by human-supplied meanings, since things are profoundly integrated into human belonging and interacting, offering new relations to technology. The word *thing* (*althing*) itself meant a gathering for deliberation—an assembly gathered by things. [pp. 218-219]

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

**T1: Withdrawal vs. Relationalism**: Rickert acknowledges a "conflict" between Harman's insistence on withdrawal (objects are never fully present to any encounter) and Latour's strong relationalism (things are constituted by their relations). Rickert tries to hold both, but the tension is never fully resolved: if objects always withdraw, how can Latour's relational accounts of material persuasion be complete? Rickert gestures toward this via the speed bump assemblage (209) but leaves the synthesis largely implicit.

**T2: Anthropomorphism and the language of agency**: Rickert repeatedly cautions against anthropomorphism (202, 203, 208) yet uses language like "the grid speaks" (213), *Earthrise* "calls and attunes" (216), and the bridge "acts" (202). The chapter oscillates between insisting that nonhuman agency is genuinely nonhuman and using idioms that inevitably import human-like intentionality. The question of whether there is a non-metaphorical way to describe nonhuman rhetoric remains open.

**T3: Material persuasion vs. symbolic persuasion**: The speed bump example establishes that some persuasion is irreducibly material. But Rickert also notes that the speed bump is "a complex of human and nonhuman relations with a deep history" (208), which seems to reintroduce the symbolic. The boundary between material and symbolic persuasion is productively blurred but never clearly drawn.

**T4: *Earthrise* as ambient rhetoric vs. human artifact**: Rickert argues *Earthrise* exceeds human attribution, yet the photograph is a thoroughly technological human product (camera, mission, circulation, cultural reception). Rickert addresses Heidegger's alarm about planetary photographs (217-218) and argues the image calls for something beyond enframing, but the question persists: does the "ambient" dimension of *Earthrise* reside in the earth's materiality, the photograph's technological apparatus, or the cultural ecology of its reception? Rickert seems to want all three simultaneously.

**T5: Scope of "rhetoric"**: By the end of the chapter, rhetoric encompasses material things, nonhuman agencies, ecological assemblages, and the withdrawal of objects from relation. This expansiveness risks rendering "rhetoric" coextensive with "relationality" or "world" itself. Rickert signals awareness of this ("My point is not that everything here is rhetoric," 208) but the delimiting principle remains unclear.
