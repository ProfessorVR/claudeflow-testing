## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### intro-s1: [Opening] (pp. 1-5)

- Opens with three epigraphs (Yes, Atom Heart/Deleuze, Karen Barad) signaling the book's investment in perception, materiality, and relational ontology.
- Sets the scene: digital technologies permeate everyday environments, dissolving boundaries between subject/object, human/nonhuman, information/matter -- "we are entering an age of ambience" (1).
- Introduces two popular culture examples -- the film *Minority Report* (database-driven ads as distributed rhetorical agents) and M. T. Anderson's novel *Feed* (digitally connective "wetware") -- to demonstrate that rhetoric circulates through human and nonhuman elements, making it impossible to confine rhetorical agency to human subjects alone.
- Poses the book's central question: "how can rhetoric be understood to suffuse the entire situation if its traditional definition largely confines it to the persuasive (and symbolic) activity of human subjects?" (3).
- States the main claim: rhetoric must be understood as ambient; it "can no longer remain centered on its theoretical commonplaces, such as rhetor/subject, audience, language, image, technique, situation, and the appeals accomplishing persuasive work" but must "diffuse outward to include the material environment, things (including the technological), our own embodiment, and a complex understanding of ecological relationality as participating in rhetorical practices" (3).
- Uses the prehistoric cave paintings at Lascaux and Altamira to illustrate that humans have always been ambient, even if only now gaining self-reflexive access to that insight; the cave art was not recognized as art until the background of intelligibility shifted (4-5).

### intro-s2: The Concept of Ambience (pp. 5-8)

- Articulates ambience as having a twofold sense drawn from Heidegger: (a) the background of intelligibility and practical coping from which things are disclosed, and (b) the complex give-and-take with material surroundings, including the materiality of what we dwell in and from (5).
- Traces the etymology: *ambience* from Latin *ambientem* (present participle of *ambire*, "to go about/around"), meaning surrounding, encompassing, environing (5).
- Notes that French refinement added an aesthetic dimension (*ambiance* as arrangement of accessories supporting a work's primary effect), and that music adopted the term to describe acoustic qualities of particular environments (6).
- Recovers the Greek *periechon* ("that which surrounds, encompasses") via Leo Spitzer's historical study, showing that the concept was already in play with Anaximenes, Hippocrates, Empedocles, and others -- indexing awareness that spirit/atmosphere constitutes the human condition both materially and spiritually (6).
- Invokes Cicero's *De natura deorum* and Spitzer on *periechon*'s connotations of warmth, protection, and embracement to argue that the concept carries a "striking sense of attunement" that Romans and later Latin/medieval writers could not fully capture (7).
- Cites Jean-Paul Thibaud on ambience as putting "us in immediate contact with a situation in its entirety" (7), a "diffuse, disseminated presence" irreducible to interpretive acts -- ambience as ecological, transubjective, active and reactive among human and nonhuman elements.

### intro-s3: Ambience and Attunement (pp. 8-10)

- Connects ambience to Heidegger's concept of *attunement* (*Stimmung*): ambience involves "the whole person"; there is no person separable from the environment who can be "tacked onto" it (8).
- Defines attunement as not additive but as "a fundamental entanglement, with the individuation of particular facets being an achieved disclosure" -- wakefulness to ambience is not a subjective achievement but an ambient occurrence (8).
- Explains Heidegger's *Stimmung* as disposition in the world, usually translated "attunement" or "mood," indicating how one finds oneself embedded in a situation; it is "neither inside nor outside" but arises from "the way of our being there with one another" (*FCM* 66) (9).
- Argues that attunement is ontologically prior: it opens us to the ambience of all, not just human existence, implicitly changing our view of human being and jeopardizing modernist conceptions of the autonomous subject (9).
- Points to salience, the public sphere, and Gerald Hauser's "rhetorically salient meanings" to argue that what is public is as ambient as it is salient -- rhetoric already reaches for and works within what is ambient (9-10).
- Previews the four theoretical strands brought together: contemporary cognitive science, Heidegger's hermeneutic phenomenology, new materialisms/thing theory, and twentieth-century ambient music (10).

### intro-s4: Heidegger and the World Round-About (pp. 11-16)

- Identifies Heidegger as providing the "latticework" for the concept of ambience, permeating the use of cognitive science and speculative realism throughout (11).
- Clarifies that Rickert seeks not to upgrade the conceptual apparatus but to "further the scope, reach, and power of our explanations for rhetoric's operations" and to obtain "fresh insights on rhetoric's past" and "practicing it in the everyday world" (11).
- Explains Heidegger's problematization of the subject-object dichotomy: the modern world, built on a dichotomy between subject and object constituting the very essence of things, forces subjects to temper their partiality in apprehending an objective world; Heidegger finds the alternative grounding in the "from which" where thought emerges -- *chora*, attunement, situatedness (*Befindlichkeit*) (12).
- Introduces key Heideggerian terminology: *Dasein* as "being there" (being situated in a world); *Umwelt* as "environing world" and "world round-about" where the preposition *um* ("about/around") carries spatial encompassingness -- the world is simultaneously material and meaningful, never exhausted by presence of material (13).
- Discusses Heidegger's verbs and middle-voice syntax (via John van Buren and Bret Davis) as linguistically performing the dissolution of subject-object grammar: "the world is what en-counters (us)" rather than "what we encounter" (13).
- Elaborates *Befindlichkeit* (situatedness) and *Stimmung* (attunement/mood) from *Being and Time*: mood "assails us" and "has already disclosed, in every case, Being-in-the-world as a whole, and makes it possible first of all to direct itself towards something" (*BT* 176); affect is a modality of world-body entanglement, not interior psychology (14).
- Connects to Diane Davis on presymbolic affectability and rhetoric as prior to symbolicity; Daniel Gross on pathos as ground of logos (not vice versa); and Heidegger's own 1924 seminar linking *pathe* to "the basic possibilities in which being-there [Dasein] itself is primarily oriented toward itself" (*BCAP* 176) (14-15).
- Summarizes three themes of ambience drawn from Heidegger: (a) ambience as material, spatial, environmental surroundingness; (b) ambience as affective investment and emplacement; (c) ambience as dispersal and diffusion of agency beyond exclusively human attribution (16).
- Notes the later Heidegger's ethical dimension: dwelling as "letting be" that spares, preserves, and cultivates; agency reimagined as caretaking and shepherding rather than mastery; things and world supply "directives" to which we cultivate attunement (*PLT* 147-158) (15-16).

### intro-s5: Cognitive Science and Embodied-Embedded Cognition (pp. 16-20)

- Frames cognitive science as extending the Heideggerian insight into material situatedness through empirical research on embodied/embedded cognition (EEC): Clark, Varela, Thompson, Rosch, Dourish, Wheeler (16).
- Critiques Cartesian dualism (mind as transcending base materiality) and argues for extended mind: mind as bodily, dispersed into the environment, with body and world as integral to cognitive processing rather than passive stage (10, 16).
- Discusses Andy Clark's *Being There* and *Natural-Born Cyborgs*: tools as equipment (Heideggerian *Zuhandenheit*), the "leaky" mind "forever escaping its 'natural' confines and mingling shamelessly with body and world" (*Being* 53); environment as necessary complement to human being (17-18).
- Introduces Paul Dourish on embodied interaction: software "depends inevitably on our ideas about representation and reality" (*Dourish* viii); forehaving structures our relation to tools and technologies (18).
- Presents the AI/robotics debate: Wheeler on classical AI (Deep Blue) as disembodied Cartesian cognition vs. Rodney Brooks's situated robots (Genghis) that respond directly to local environment without centralized planning -- evidence that cognition requires a body and a world (19-20).
- Argues that rhetorical theory harbors the same Cartesian assumptions: "our constellation of concepts, our underlying notions of persuasion and its operations, our epistemological stance on truth, our sense of credibility -- all this and more indicate a preexisting way of understanding the world and human being" from which rhetoric springs (20).

### intro-s6: It's a Thing: New Materialisms and Nonhuman Agency (pp. 21-27)

- Argues that humanities understandings of materialism (Marxist, cultural-critical) have not adequately confronted the vitality of matter itself; materiality in rhetorical studies tends to refer to "the materialization of human ideas" as secondary to a primary thought (21-22).
- Introduces the "speculative realism" / "object-oriented ontology" / "thing theory" / "new materialism" cluster (Harman, Latour, Brown, Plotz, Barad) as granting ontological and agentive weight to things and world (22).
- Discusses John Plotz on thing theory: "not theory about the cultural significance of objects" but "about the power of objects themselves" (22); the thing has a material thrust that coexists with and emerges within ambient culture.
- Presents Graham Harman's object-oriented ontology via Heidegger's tool-being: objects "withdraw from human view into a dark subterranean reality that never becomes present to practical action" (Harman, *TB* 1); things harbor a hidden surplus beyond their relational reduction to "caricatures" (23).
- Extends to Latour's actor-network theory: actants (neither purely human nor nonhuman but "quasi-objects" and "quasi-subjects") proliferate in laboratories, social life, deliberation; agency is no longer the sole preserve of humans but is distributed through networks (24).
- Discusses Latour's *Dingpolitik* exhibit ("Making Things Public: Atmospheres of Democracy," 2005): the thing (*das Ding*) as gathering political concerns, assembling publics, binding people and objects in ways that "map out a public space profoundly different from what is usually recognized under the label of 'the political'" (25).
- Cites Jane Bennett on "vital materialism" and the agential powers of natural and artifactual things, leading to "a more cautious, intelligent approach to our interventions in that ecology" (27).
- Connects dwelling to intelligence: "Dwelling is an attunement that can generate various kinds of knowledge, in particular a knowledge of how the world gives back, as it were, or how the world transcendent of human thought and power is integral to how life takes shape" (27).

### intro-s7: The World Waking Up: Ambiences of Music, Intelligence, and Place (pp. 28-33)

- Frames ambient music (twentieth-century development) as exemplifying how disclosive practice transforms rhetoric: new forms of music entail new ways of listening, which reshape our relation to place and environment (28).
- Introduces Brian Eno's two senses of "ambient": (a) music facilitating listening at various levels of attention, shifting between background and foreground; (b) music creating "a sense of place that complements or alters your environment" (28).
- Argues that ambient music's creation of "impossible" places (via echo, delay, timbre manipulation, found sound layering) demonstrates that the local environs are not mere background but "an active player in their own right" -- from sourcing of found sound to acoustic properties of recording environments to equipment used (29).
- Draws the analogy: "Ambient rhetoric, analogically, brings the world to us but in doing so transforms the disposition of our inhabitancy" -- rhetoric from an ambient perspective "can no longer be situated solely in human subjective performance" (29).
- Develops the importance of place: Heidegger (via Edward Casey) on place as "habitation, a material, unique location wherein people and things are situated"; the ambient environs as "not at all defined by a being-located-side-by-side-and-around-each-other and by geometrical relations, but rather it is the round-about of worldly dealings" (*O* 79) (30).
- Connects to Marshall McLuhan ("the medium is the message") but reframes: place "affects us -- it is an occasion for the world's revealing -- and is not best understood in terms of *messaging*" (31).
- Discusses ubiquitous computing / ambient intelligence (Greenfield, Aarts and Marzano): technology as invisible scaffolding (paralleling Heidegger's ready-to-hand), becoming noticeable only when it ceases to function; the "dark penumbral backdrop" of equipment coming to wakefulness (31).
- Presents the rhetorical stakes: ambient intelligence disperses agency so that "there is no *specific* sense of locus or agency here"; material things, forces, agencies, discourses, and people "all disperse, circulating through a dynamic material-informatic ecology" (32).
- Notes Eno's political claim: ambient music's invitation to "stop seeing yourself as the centre of the world" is "political theory: and it's where the music grows from" (33).

### intro-s8: Rhetoric, Ambience, Dwelling (pp. 33-37)

- States the practical imperative: "We need conceptions of rhetoric that keep pace with these transformations and their attendant opportunities and dangers" (33).
- Defines rhetoric as "doing as revealing and revealing as doing -- and hence integral to our dwelling in the world"; it "can no longer be understood solely as a subjective, verbal, visual, or even performative art" but must be "an embodied and embedded practice" emerging from "environmentally situated and interactive engagements, redolent of a world that affects us, that persuades us prior to symbolicity" (33-34).
- Introduces Heidegger's *dwelling* (Wohnen): active comportments toward the world enacted less as mastery/control than as "letting be" that spares, preserves, and cultivates; building linked to dwelling, and rhetorical production linked to dwelling (34).
- Provides a critical test case: Thomas Cole's definition of rhetoric as "a speaker's or writer's self-conscious manipulation of his medium with a view to ensuring his message as favorable a reception as possible on the part of the particular audience being addressed" (ix) -- Rickert does not refute Cole but shows this definition's narrowness, particularly its dependence on intent and self-consciousness (35).
- Critiques intent-based rhetoric: intent may be "causally irrelevant to the effects produced in the audience"; Arabella Lyon argues intent has been "woefully undertheorized in rhetoric precisely because it is always assumed" (35-36); intent is "only one element in a large array of things, feelings, peoples, and forces all complexly interacting" (36).
- Critiques self-consciousness as definitional boundary: tethering rhetoric to self-consciousness "cut[s] off large swaths of human activity from rhetorical practice" and tethers theory to "humanist notions of willing" (36).
- Closing argument: "I hope this book helps us to see such narrowing as a way to impoverish rhetoric when we should be opening it up, not to make it bigger, but to attune us, in rhetoric and via rhetoric, to its fullest flowering" (36).
- Final sentences invoke sustainability, materialist ethics (non-Marxist), and co-dwelling: "Ambient rhetoric opens us onto these insights and thus is less an answer in itself than an invitation to disclose anew, to build further, and so begin to dwell" (37).

## 4. Main Positions and Arguments

### P1: Rhetoric is ambient

- **Claim**: Rhetoric must be reconceived as ambient -- as diffusing outward beyond the symbolic activity of human subjects to include the material environment, things, embodiment, and ecological relationality as constitutive participants in rhetorical practice.
- **Premises**: (a) Digital technologies, ubiquitous computing, and ambient intelligence dissolve boundaries between subject/object, human/nonhuman, information/matter (1-2). (b) Rhetorical situations like *Minority Report* ads and *Feed* wetware demonstrate that rhetoric circulates through human and nonhuman elements alike (2-3). (c) The traditional definition confining rhetoric to symbolic persuasion by human subjects cannot suffuse the entire situation (3). Therefore, rhetoric must be understood as ambient.
- **Key interlocutors**: Thibaud, Heidegger, Latour, Bennett
- **Status**: core book thesis

### P2: Attunement is ontologically prior to cognition, volition, and intention

- **Claim**: Attunement (*Stimmung*) precedes and conditions all cognition, intention, and deliberate action; it is the way the world is already disclosed before reflective attention, and it is not a subjective achievement but an ambient occurrence.
- **Premises**: (a) Heidegger: "the mood has already disclosed, in every case, Being-in-the-world as a whole, and makes it possible first of all to direct itself towards something" (*BT* 176). (b) Attunement is neither inside nor outside but arises from "the way of our being there with one another" (*FCM* 66). (c) Mood is not reducible to psychology but is constitutively entangled with environment (*BT* 176; Ratcliffe 289). Therefore, attunement is the ontological ground from which rhetorical situations emerge.
- **Key interlocutors**: Heidegger (*BT*, *FCM*), Ratcliffe, Davis, Gross
- **Status**: core book thesis (ontological foundation)

### P3: The subject-object dichotomy is inadequate for rhetorical theory

- **Claim**: The Cartesian split between autonomous subject and objective world, which grounds traditional rhetorical theory's emphasis on the intentional agent wielding symbolic power, must be overcome. Rhetoric's "constellation of concepts, our underlying notions of persuasion and its operations, our epistemological stance on truth, our sense of credibility -- all this and more indicate a preexisting way of understanding the world and human being" that is Cartesian and must be confronted (20).
- **Premises**: (a) Heidegger shows that Dasein is always already "being there" in a world, not a subject confronting an object (12-13). (b) Cognitive science (EEC) demonstrates that mind is embodied and extended, not encapsulated (16-20). (c) New materialisms show that agency is distributed, not centered in human subjects (21-27). Therefore, rhetorical theory based on autonomous subjective agency is reductive.
- **Key interlocutors**: Heidegger, Clark, Dourish, Wheeler, Brooks, Harman, Latour
- **Status**: core book thesis (critical/deconstructive ground-clearing)

### P4: Cognitive science and Heidegger converge on situated, embodied being

- **Claim**: Embodied-embedded cognition, when pushed beyond its residual Cartesian assumptions, resonates with Heideggerian phenomenology on the inseparability of mind, body, and world and the inadequacy of subject-object models.
- **Premises**: (a) Clark: the mind is "a leaky organ, forever escaping its 'natural' confines and mingling shamelessly with body and world" (*Being* 53). (b) Brooks's robots achieve complex behavior by responding directly to the local environment without centralized planning (19-20). (c) Wheeler: Brooks's work presupposes fundamental assumptions about human being and the world that align with (German) philosophy (20). (d) Heidegger's tool analysis (*Zuhandenheit*) and Dourish's embodied interaction both show that forehaving structures our relation to technologies. Therefore, these converge.
- **Key interlocutors**: Clark, Dourish, Wheeler, Brooks, Heidegger
- **Status**: local support (for P1 and P3)

### P5: Things have ontological and agentive weight irreducible to human meaning-making

- **Claim**: Objects/things are not inert recipients of human meaning but harbor a withdrawn dimension, exercise material agency, and participate as actants in rhetorical situations; rhetoric must therefore become "object-oriented" enough to account for the power of things.
- **Premises**: (a) Harman: objects "withdraw from human view into a dark subterranean reality" (23); things reduce each other to "caricatures" in encounters (23). (b) Latour: actants (quasi-objects, quasi-subjects) proliferate through networks; agency is no longer solely human (24). (c) Plotz: thing theory is "about the power of objects themselves" (22). (d) The *Challenger*/*Columbia* wreckage example: the shuttle "is not an 'objective object' -- it is threaded through and assembled across human and nonhuman participation" (26). Therefore, rhetoric must account for things as participants.
- **Key interlocutors**: Harman, Latour, Plotz, Bennett, Brown
- **Status**: local support (for P1 and P3; preview of Ch 6-7)

### P6: Intent and self-consciousness are insufficient to define rhetoric

- **Claim**: Defining rhetoric through intentional, self-conscious manipulation of a medium (as Cole does) impoverishes rhetoric by cutting off the embodied, embedded, material, and ambient dimensions; intent is "only one element in a large array of things, feelings, peoples, and forces all complexly interacting" (36).
- **Premises**: (a) Cole defines rhetoric as "a speaker's or writer's self-conscious manipulation of his medium" (35). (b) Intent may be causally irrelevant to effects; Lyon argues intent is "woefully undertheorized" because "always assumed" (35-36). (c) Tethering rhetoric to self-consciousness cuts off what is "denied to self-consciousness" from rhetorical inquiry (36). (d) The four strands (Heidegger, EEC, new materialism, music) each demonstrate dimensions of rhetoric that exceed intentional control. Therefore, intent and self-consciousness cannot be definitional boundaries.
- **Key interlocutors**: Cole, Lyon, Davis, Lundberg and Gunn
- **Status**: local support (critical case study demonstrating P1 and P3)

### P7: Ambient rhetoric is linked to dwelling and an ethics of sustainability

- **Claim**: Rhetoric, reconceived as ambient, is integral to dwelling -- the Heideggerian mode of being enacted as sparing, preserving, cultivating rather than mastery -- and opens onto an ethics of ecological sustainability and co-dwelling.
- **Premises**: (a) Heidegger: dwelling as "letting be," linking building/production to caretaking (*PLT* 147-149). (b) Rhetoric "reveals and constitutes the informational environment within which we flourish" (34). (c) Ambient rhetoric "marshals energies toward sustainability" and "grants a new, materialist edge, one generated from non-Marxist sources, that brings forth something akin to an ethics" (37). Therefore, ambient rhetoric entails an ethical dimension oriented toward dwelling.
- **Key interlocutors**: Heidegger (*PLT*), Bennett
- **Status**: core book thesis (ethical/practical horizon, developed in Chs 7-8)

### P8: Ambient music exemplifies the rhetoricity of world-disclosure

- **Claim**: Ambient music's transformation of listening, place, and attention demonstrates in practice how rhetoric works ambiently -- generating its own landscape, transforming the disposition of inhabitancy, and dispersing agency across sonic, spatial, technological, and human elements.
- **Premises**: (a) Eno's two senses of "ambient" (attention-shifting, place-creating) parallel the structure of ambient rhetoric (28). (b) Recording captures not just musicians but rooms, environments, equipment -- the environs are "an active player in their own right" (29). (c) Ambient music evokes "the concreteness of place" through experiential registers, constituting what Rickert means by *situation* (*Befindlichkeit*) (29). Therefore, ambient music is an exemplary instance of ambient rhetoric.
- **Key interlocutors**: Eno, Fuller, Hannett, Hassell
- **Status**: illustrative (preview of Ch 4)

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

### T1: Scope and differentiation of nonhuman agency

Rickert insists that agency is dispersed across human and nonhuman assemblages (P5), yet he also notes that "we must grant such agencies different weights and values" (16) and that his project is "of a magnitude and scope to challenge more traditional notions of human agency" without eliminating human distinctiveness. The tension: if agency is truly distributed and there is "no *specific* sense of locus or agency" (32), how are different degrees of agency differentiated? What prevents the framework from collapsing all agency into an undifferentiated field? This connects to P1 and P5.

### T2: Critical distance within ambience

If attunement is ontologically prior (P2) and we are always already ambient, from what position can one critically evaluate or resist particular ambient conditions? The Introduction presents ambience as encompassing -- "culturally we are inundated by ambience" (4) -- and warns against relying "solely on salience, attention, and consciousness to guide us" (33). But it does not specify the mechanism by which one might achieve critical purchase within a framework that renders all experience ambient. This connects to P2 and P7.

### T3: Ontological project vs. practical methodology

Rickert explicitly frames his project as a "reconception" and "attuning" rather than a methodology: "Much will remain to be done" (Preface). The Introduction performs extensive philosophical ground-clearing (P3, P4, P5) but the practical implications for rhetorical practice -- how one would *do* ambient rhetorical analysis or *produce* ambient rhetoric -- remain largely promissory, deferred to the case-study chapters (Chs 3-4, 7-8). This connects to P1 and P7.

### T4: Heidegger's nostalgia vs. Rickert's technological present

Rickert notes that Harman critiques Heidegger for "fall[ing] prey to this tendency" of restricting authentic "things" to nostalgic examples (jugs, farmhouses, peasant shoes, the bridge at Heidelberg) while treating modern objects as "mere *objects*" (23). Rickert explicitly departs from Harman on this point but also depends on the later Heidegger's dwelling/fourfold framework. The tension: can Heidegger's account of dwelling, conceived in relation to pre-technological examples, adequately ground an ambient rhetoric of digital technologies, ubiquitous computing, and networked assemblages? Rickert addresses this partly through Greenfield/Aarts on ambient intelligence (31-32) but the fit remains unsettled. This connects to P7 and the concept of dwelling.

### T5: Ambience as both ancient and distinctly contemporary

Rickert argues that "humans have always attended to the ambient" (4) -- Lascaux, *periechon*, cave acoustics -- yet simultaneously claims that "we are entering an age of ambience" (1) driven by digital technologies and ubiquitous computing. The tension: is ambience a permanent ontological feature of human being newly recognized, or a historically emergent condition produced by specific technological developments? The Introduction holds both simultaneously without fully reconciling them. This connects to P1 and the concept of ambience.
