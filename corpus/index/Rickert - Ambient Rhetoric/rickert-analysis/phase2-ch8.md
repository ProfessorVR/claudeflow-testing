## 1. Chapter Metadata

- **Title**: "Attuning to Sufficiency: A Preparatory Study in Learning How to Dwell"
- **Part**: Part 2 — Dwelling with Ambience
- **Chapter**: 8
- **Pages**: 246-269

**One-sentence thesis**: Rickert argues that the ecological crisis demands a shift from the logic of efficiency to a principle of sufficiency understood not as a rhetorical counterargument but as an ambient attunement materialized through dwelling practices, demonstrated through the Toronto Island community's resistance to automobility and the EV1 documentary's disclosure of the car-as-thing, both of which reveal that rhetoric must be reconceived as preparatory work for transforming how we dwell in and through our material environs.

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### ch8-s1: [Opening] (pp. 246-247)

- Opens with Michael Hyde's rethinking of ethos as dwelling and the claim that "how we dwell is not simply a question of where we are but of how we are" (246).
- Introduces Thomas Princen's *The Logic of Sufficiency* as the chapter's primary interlocutor: sufficiency challenges the dominant logic of efficiency that pervades modern life.
- Frames automobility as the central test case: the car materializes efficiency so thoroughly that questioning it exposes how deeply efficiency structures dwelling.
- Announces the chapter's aim: to show that ambient rhetoric is preparatory work that lays the groundwork for transformed ways of dwelling.

### ch8-s2: Dwelling and Homesickness (pp. 247-248)

- Invokes Foghat's "Slow Ride" and the rock-and-roll fantasy of dwelling "anywhere" to illustrate modern homelessness — the restless mobility that forecloses genuine dwelling.
- Connects Heidegger's notion that dwelling is not merely inhabiting a location but a manner of being-in-the-world, where the fourfold (earth, sky, divinities, mortals) gathers.
- Argues that the modern condition involves a "homesickness" (after Novalis via Heidegger): we have lost the capacity to dwell because technology brings the world to us as a resource, as masterable, and as always available for efficiency-driven extraction.

### ch8-s3: Dwelling in/as Sufficiency (pp. 248-253)

- Lays out Princen's critique of efficiency: efficiency has become "a profoundly persuasive discourse" (250), an ambient logic knit into political, cultural, and economic organizations so deeply that it "brooks no argument" (250).
- Distinguishes efficiency (maximize throughput, always faster/cheaper/more) from sufficiency (restraint, enough, long-term ecological viability). Efficiency's success is precisely the problem: its benefits make it appear unassailable.
- Critiques Princen's own rhetorical strategies: Princen's solutions — "buy green," "vote green," "use resources more efficiently" — remain trapped within the humanist/subjectivist rhetorical framework they claim to oppose, presupposing individual choice played out against an objective world (251-252).
- Argues that sufficiency cannot be adopted as a mere countervalue or abstract principle; it must achieve "an ontological grip on us" through ambient attunement rather than a relation of exteriority (252-253).
- Introduces Andrew Rudin's insight (via Princen) that sustainability is "never just one given problem" but threaded through "an entire array of knowledges, practices, discourses, organizations, and *things*" (253) — the problem is not social but materialized in practices and things, requiring a conception of rhetoric with "the materiality of dwelling built in" (253).
- Connects back to Heidegger's argument (Ch. 5) that what appears to us is an ensemble of interlocking elements immersed in an environment that surrounds, affords, and sustains (253-254).

### ch8-s4: Toronto Island — Sufficiency in Practice (pp. 254-260)

- Presents Toronto Island as a case study: a small island in Lake Ontario, annexed by Toronto in 1867, with a history of tension between city authorities (who view the island as a resource to maximize) and island residents (who dwell there in a mode of sufficiency).
- The bridge/tunnel debate: in the 1950s, Toronto proposed connecting the island to the mainland by bridge or tunnel, which would introduce automobiles and radically transform island life — roads, parking, gas stations, hotels, suburban sprawl (255).
- Islanders resisted not merely to preserve "uses" but because their way of life was at stake; the automobile would reweave the entire fabric of island existence (255-256).
- Distinguishes vacationing/touring (resource consumption, spectacle, escape) from residing/dwelling: the tourist arrives, enjoys, departs, with no deep ties to the surrounding environs; the resident's commitment is attuned to worldly affectability (256).
- Princen's extended discussion of the automobile as actant: dependence on cars is "a kind of physical addiction," with decentralization and dispersion becoming key developmental vectors because automobiles materially afford quick travel over distance (258).
- Sufficiency emerges as a *principle* only insofar as we can abstract it from already ongoing *dwelling* — it is living in a way attuned to worldly affectability that gives sufficiency its hold, not top-down adoption of a principle (257).
- The challenge for the islanders: they must move beyond arguing from "uses" (which mirrors the opposition's efficiency logic) toward *enlivening* — making their way of life real, palpable, and valuable to others — and simultaneously making withdrawal from presence part of their rhetorical work (259-260).

### ch8-s5: Ambient Dwelling and the EV1 (pp. 261-265)

- Transitions from Toronto Island to a second case: the 2006 documentary *Who Killed the Electric Car?* about the General Motors EV1, the first mass-produced electric car, developed in response to California's Zero Emission Vehicle mandate (262).
- The film is rhetorically interesting because it disrupts the standard humanist rhetorical framework: it includes car-creation narratives, fan testimonials, and a "suspects on trial" format that grants agentive powers to material objects, evoking Latour's parliament of things (262-263).
- The EV1 is itself inherently suasive: the car calls its owner through environmentally friendly appeals combined with the "sexiness of well-performing technology," opening the possibility that the electric car can be a *thing* that calls us to forms of care beyond just the car, as part of a larger ecology (263).
- The EV1 materially practices sufficiency: it creates a vehicle conditioned by other things, sketching a relation different from efficiency-driven maximum performance and marketplace profitability (263).
- Limitation: the EV1 remains ultimately a technological solution to technologically produced problems — it reinforces rather than challenges the fundamental precepts of automobility. As Heidegger argues, the problem with technology is that it fosters technological solutions that entrench us deeper in technological thinking (264-265).
- The Toronto Island and EV1 examples work as counterparts: the EV1 illuminates infrastructure's power and transformation's possibility; Toronto Island shows how changes in infrastructure transform dwelling. Both reveal that material persuasion inheres in environments, not just in attitudes (265).

### ch8-s6: Dis/Closures — The Thing between Latour and Heidegger (pp. 265-267)

- Argues that the Toronto Island case reveals a "sufficient form of life tethered to a material and worldly affectability" — the real issue is not the bridge/tunnel but the entire ambient situation of dwelling (265).
- Critiques the reduction of things to presence: what is present is certainly important, but "the reduction to presence — to what is revealed and the manner in which it is revealed — is problematic if we go no further" (266). Future forms of disclosure will reveal new variables, not isolated but emerging from deep relations to the whole.
- Dwelling on Toronto Island discloses the environment as a "complex relational stitchwork of things individuating themselves, emerging from the as-a-whole into mutually conditioning and supporting aspects of being" (266). The island is *not nature* — it is not separate from the human, not in need of preservation from human ravages, but integral to the unique character of dwelling there.
- Engages Latour's *Dingpolitik*: Latour would bring all elements of the island — land, beaches, plants, animals, roads, buildings — into political representation. But Latour is "less able to attend to the conditions of possibility for remaking the political into *Dingpolitik*" because his framework remains a matter of human willing and pursuit, with forums "ready-made and available" (267).
- Heidegger's rejoinder: such politics ultimately reinforces the priority of the human. Latour offers no fundamental tempering of will. Reconceiving nonhumans as agents affecting humans "constitutes an advance, but Latour offers no larger sense of the 'as a whole'" (267).
- The key distinction: a *Dingpolitik* does not come first through extending political practices to nonhumans; rather, dwelling as attunement to conditionality, disclosed in and through the fourfold, already gives rise to what can emerge as *Dingpolitik* (267).

### ch8-s7: Festivity, Celebration, and Preparatory Rhetoric (pp. 268-269)

- Argues that values are sacralized through everyday practices in a fashion beyond the human: if values transcend human projection, then Latour's *Dingpolitik* must already be spoken in a way of dwelling (268).
- Performing rhetoric in an ambient key is less a matter of *adding* religion, art, and other forms of knowledge *back in* than of reattuning ourselves to their station and to what their station may yet be (268).
- Introduces the festive as a form of preparatory rhetoric: festivity need not be happy or cheerful; it attunes us to the gifting of the world and a sense of value that transcends human being, manifesting through moods of contemplation, reverence, joy, or other possibilities (268).
- Celebration discloses world differently and opens the possibility of dwelling anew — art, religion, and other ways of being with things show up as indispensable for our way of life, "radiant in such fashion that they are graced to us and so lay claims on us we should listen to them" (268).
- Final declaration: the book itself is "ultimately only preparatory" (269). Ambient rhetoric cannot short-circuit itself into being a new paradigm; it can only attune and, by such attunements, conjoin us in worldly affectability, giving place and bearing to everything we are and everything we do (269).

## 4. Main Positions

**P1. Efficiency is ambient, not merely economic.** Efficiency is not simply a metric or policy preference but "a profoundly persuasive discourse" (250) that has become ambient — woven into the fabric of our political, cultural, and economic organizations such that it "brooks no argument" (250). This means challenging efficiency is not a matter of counter-argument but of transforming the material conditions of dwelling.

**P2. Sufficiency cannot be adopted as a principle; it must emerge from dwelling.** Princen's call for a "principle of sufficiency" falls into the same trap as the efficiency-logic it opposes: it treats the issue as a matter of individual or collective choice played out against an objective world. Sufficiency "emerges as a *principle* only insofar as we can abstract it from already ongoing *dwelling*" (257), meaning it must achieve an ontological grip through ambient attunement rather than discursive advocacy.

**P3. The Toronto Island case demonstrates ambient rhetoric in practice.** The islanders' decades-long resistance to the bridge/tunnel succeeds not through conventional rhetorical strategies (which mirror the opposition's efficiency logic) but through the accumulated force of *dwelling* — their way of life generates a sensibility attuned to island life that gives their arguments their root (257-258). This is the closest the book comes to a practical demonstration of ambient rhetoric.

**P4. The EV1 reveals the car as a rhetorical thing.** The electric car is itself inherently suasive: it calls its owner, opens the possibility that a car can be a *thing* that calls us to forms of care as part of a larger ecology, and materially practices sufficiency (263). Yet the EV1 remains "ultimately a technological solution to technologically produced problems" (264-265), illustrating Heidegger's claim that technology fosters technological solutions that close off other approaches.

**P5. Latour's Dingpolitik is valuable but insufficient.** Latour's extension of political representation to nonhuman actants constitutes an advance, but it "offers no larger sense of the 'as a whole'" (267) and remains a matter of human willing and pursuit. Dwelling as attunement to conditionality through the fourfold *already gives rise to* what can emerge as Dingpolitik — the political does not come first (267).

**P6. Ambient rhetoric is preparatory, not paradigmatic.** The book's concluding gesture: ambient rhetoric "cannot short-circuit itself into being a new paradigm" (269). It can only attune, lay groundwork, and open conditions of possibility for future forms of disclosure and dwelling. Even the book itself is "ultimately only preparatory" (269).

**P7. Values are sacralized through dwelling, not projected by subjects.** The world, "thought as material presence, as meaning, and as that which withdraws, is sacralized through everyday practices in such a fashion as to ground meaning and worth beyond the human" (268). Values emerge from practices, not from human projection onto a neutral world.

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

**T1. The gap between ontological diagnosis and practical rhetorical guidance.** Rickert's critique of Princen's strategies (buy green, vote green) is sharp, but his own alternative — "enlivening," "preparatory rhetoric," "attunement" — remains frustratingly abstract. If the Toronto Islanders' success stems from their having already been dwelling in a mode of sufficiency, what guidance does ambient rhetoric offer to communities that have *not* been so dwelling? The answer seems to be: none yet, since the project is "only preparatory."

**T2. Princen simultaneously embraced and overridden.** Rickert agrees with Princen on sufficiency and praises his argument's sophistication, yet charges that Princen's rhetorical framework "falls into a trap" by remaining humanist and subjectivist (252). The tension is productive but underexamined: Rickert relies on Princen's empirical case studies (Toronto Island, the automobile as actant, Rudin's insight) while dismissing the rhetorical theory Princen uses to frame them.

**T3. Technological solutions vs. ambient transformation.** The EV1 section argues that technological solutions entrench technological thinking (264-265), but Rickert does not account for the possibility that incremental technological change might itself shift ambient conditions over time. Is there no role for technological reform within an ambient framework, or is all such reform merely "preparatory" in a lesser sense?

**T4. The Latour critique and political agency.** Rickert charges that Latour "offers no fundamental tempering of will" and that his Dingpolitik remains humanist (267). Yet Rickert's own account of how dwelling "already gives rise to" Dingpolitik risks quietism: if the political emerges from attunement rather than deliberate extension of rights to nonhumans, what happens when the existing attunement is itself structured by efficiency? The chapter acknowledges but does not resolve this circularity.

**T5. Withdrawal and rhetorical persuasion.** Rickert's invocation of Heideggerian withdrawal (266) introduces a fundamental tension with any rhetorical project: if what matters most withdraws from presence, and if ambient rhetoric must attend to what is "not manifest" (262), how can rhetoric — which operates through some form of disclosure — access the withdrawn? The chapter gestures toward festivity and celebration (268) but does not develop a mechanism.
