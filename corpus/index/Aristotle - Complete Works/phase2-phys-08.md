## 1. Unit Metadata

| Field | Value |

| Book | Physics VIII (10 chapters) |
| Scope | 250b10--267b26 |
| Density | Very High (architectonic capstone of the entire Physics; proves the necessity of an eternal unmoved mover through interlocking arguments about the eternity of motion, the impossibility of infinite causal regress, the primacy of locomotion, the superiority of circular motion, and the partlessness of the first mover) |

**One-sentence thesis**: Physics VIII argues that motion must be eternal (since time is eternal and time is an affection of motion), that every chain of movers must terminate in something that moves itself, that within every self-mover there is an unmoved component that is the primary cause, that locomotion is the primary and only continuous kind of motion, that circular locomotion alone is continuous and infinite, and therefore that there must exist a first mover that is eternal, unmoved, without parts and without magnitude, situated at the circumference and imparting a single, uniform, eternal rotation.

    ├─ Dilemma: did motion begin at some time, or has it always existed?
    ├─ Time is either created or eternal
    │   ├─ If time is eternal, motion is eternal (time = affection of motion, 251b11)
    │   ├─ "The now" is always a middle-point, uniting past and future
    │   └─ Therefore there must always be time, therefore always motion
    ├─ Argument from imperishability: if motion could begin, there would be
    │   a prior change; if it could perish, there would be a posterior change
    ├─ Predecessors: Democritus (time is uncreated), Plato (time simultaneous
    │   with world-creation), Anaxagoras/Empedocles (Love and Strife alternate)
    └─ All natural philosophers except Plato implicitly agree motion is eternal

    │   └─ Reply: some part of the animal is always in motion (respiration,

    │   at rest and sometimes in motion

## 4. Main Positions

### PHYS-08-P1: Motion is eternal --- it had no beginning and will have no end

Aristotle's opening argument (Ch. 1, 251a8--252b5) proceeds from the eternity of time. Time cannot have a beginning, because the "now" (τὸ νῦν) is always a middle-point uniting a before and an after; therefore there was always time before any supposed first now, and there will always be time after any supposed last now. Since "time is the number of motion" or an "affection of motion" (251b11), if time is eternal, motion must be eternal. A second, independent argument proceeds from the impossibility of a first change: if motion began, there would have been a prior change that brought about the conditions for motion; if motion could perish, there would be a subsequent change --- in either case motion is presupposed by its own supposed origination or destruction. This conclusion is contrasted with Plato's position (who "alone asserts the creation of time" in the *Timaeus*) and aligned with Democritus, who holds time to be uncreated. 250b--252b (PDF 108--110).

### PHYS-08-P2: Every chain of moved movers terminates in a self-mover

In Ch. 5 (256a4--258b9), Aristotle establishes that everything in motion is moved by something. If A is moved by B, and B by C, the series cannot go on to infinity, because in an infinite series there would be no first mover, and without a first mover the intermediate links have no source of motion. Therefore the chain must terminate in something that moves itself. The self-mover is analysed as containing two components: a part that is moved and a part that imparts motion without itself being moved. This structure is illustrated through the example of the stick moved by the hand moved by the man: "the first mover is moved but not by anything else, it must be moved by itself" (256a1). 256a--258b (PDF 116--120).

### PHYS-08-P3: There must be an eternal, unmoved first mover

Ch. 6 (258b10--260a19) extends the argument: perishable self-movers (animals) come to be and cease to be, so they cannot be the ultimate ground of motion's eternity. There must therefore be "something eternal, whether one or many, that first imparts motion, and this first mover must be unmoved" (258b11--12). The unmoved mover must be "exempt from all change, both unqualified and accidental" (259a14). Being simple and invariable, it produces simple and continuous motion. Aristotle also argues for parsimony: "we ought to suppose that there is one rather than many, and a finite rather than an infinite number" of unmoved movers (259a9--10). 258b--260a (PDF 120--122).

### PHYS-08-P4: Locomotion is the primary kind of motion

Ch. 7 (260a20--261a27) argues that among the three kinds of motion --- in respect of magnitude (growth/decrease), in respect of affection (alteration), and in respect of place (locomotion) --- locomotion is primary. It is primary in several senses: (1) ontologically, because alteration presupposes locomotion (the alterer must approach the altered), and growth presupposes alteration which presupposes locomotion; (2) temporally, because locomotion is the last capacity living things acquire but the first in the order of nature, since "the degree in which things possess locomotion corresponds to the degree in which they have realized their natural development" (261a15); (3) essentially, because locomotion alone "does not involve a change of being" in the subject (261a20); (4) causally, because "it is the self-mover that we declare to be the principle of things that are moved and impart motion and the primary source for things that are in motion" (261a24), and the self-mover moves in respect of place. 260a--261a (PDF 122--123).

### PHYS-08-P5: Only circular motion is continuous and infinite

Chs. 8--9 (261a28--267a20) contain the decisive kinematic argument. Rectilinear motion cannot be continuous and infinite because: (a) a thing traversing a finite straight line must turn back, and the return involves contrary motions (up/down, forward/backward); (b) at the turning point the thing must come to a stand (proved by the "now" argument: A cannot simultaneously have arrived at B and be departing from B, so there is an instantaneous rest at B, 262a18--262b8); (c) between contraries there is always an interval. Circular motion, by contrast, involves no contraries: the start and end of the arc coincide, "the centre is alike beginning, middle, and end" (265a31--b2), so "there is no point on the circular line at which that which is in process of locomotion must come to a stand" (265b5). Moreover, rotation is the *measure* of all other motions and "the only motion that admits of being regular" (265b12). 261a--265b (PDF 123--131).

### PHYS-08-P6: The first mover is without parts and without magnitude

Ch. 10 (267a21--267b26) completes the argument. Nothing finite can cause motion during an infinite time: if A is the infinite force and AB the finite force, the finite will be exhausted in finite time and can never equal the infinite. Therefore the first mover's power must be infinite. But an infinite force cannot reside in a finite magnitude (proved by a reductio: adding finite increments to a finite force will at some point exceed any assigned limit, but will never equal the infinite), nor can there be an infinite magnitude (already proved in Physics III). Since the first mover causes eternal motion, it must have infinite power, and since infinite power cannot inhere in any magnitude, the first mover "is indivisible and is without parts and without magnitude" (267b25--26). It occupies the circumference of the cosmos because "the things nearest the mover are those whose motion is quickest, and in this case it is the motion of the circumference that is the quickest; therefore the mover occupies the circumference" (267b7--9). 267a--267b (PDF 133--134).

- **Movement of Animals (De Motu)**: The practical syllogism and the need for an unmoved point in animal locomotion (698a--701b) directly applies the Physics VIII framework to biological motion.

## 5. Tensions

### T1: Self-motion vs. the unmoved mover

Aristotle argues in Ch. 5 that the causal chain terminates in a self-mover, but in Ch. 6 he argues that the self-mover itself contains an unmoved component that is the true principle. The relation between self-motion and unmoved motion is thus ambiguous: is the self-mover genuinely self-moving, or is it moved by its own unmoved part? If the latter, "self-motion" is a misnomer --- it is really the motion of one part by another. Aristotle resolves this structurally (the self-mover is a composite: AB, where A moves and B is moved), but the question of whether genuine self-motion is ontologically possible remains. This tension connects to the De Anima's claim that the soul is not itself in motion (DA I.3--4) while being the principle of an animal's motion.

### T2: The eternity of motion vs. the finitude of individual motions

Individual motions clearly begin and end: a stone falls, a fire rises, an animal walks and stops. Yet Aristotle insists motion as such is eternal. The reconciliation lies in the distinction between individual motions (which are finite and between contraries) and the continuous circular motion of the heavens (which has no contraries and no termini). But this means "the eternity of motion" rests entirely on the heavens; sublunary motion is not itself eternal but is continuously *caused* by the eternal celestial motion. The weight of the argument therefore depends on the cosmological claim that the heavens actually rotate eternally --- a claim that is observational, not demonstrative.

### T3: Circular motion as continuous vs. the "now" argument

The proof that rectilinear motion is not continuous turns on the claim that at the turning point the moving thing must be at rest at a "now" (262a--b). But in circular motion the thing also passes through every point on the circumference. Why does the same argument not apply? Aristotle's answer is that in circular motion the start/end coincide and no point is *privileged* as a terminus (265a31--b2), so there are no contrary phases. This is persuasive for uniform rotation but creates a difficulty for any circular motion that is non-uniform --- a case Aristotle does not explicitly address.

### T4: The location of the unmoved mover

At 267b7--9, Aristotle places the unmoved mover "at the circumference" on the ground that things nearest the mover move fastest and the circumference is the fastest-moving part. But the unmoved mover is "without parts and without magnitude" (267b25). How can something without spatial extension "occupy" the circumference? The tension is between the physical/cosmological placement (at the outermost sphere) and the ontological characterization (partless, immaterial). This same tension resurfaces in Metaphysics Lambda, where the unmoved mover is characterized as pure thought, apparently immaterial, yet is said to cause motion as an object of desire (kinei hos eromenon, 1072b3).

## 8. Dissertation Relevance

### 8.1 Why PHYS-08 is Dissertation-Critical

Physics VIII provides three indispensable foundations for the dissertation's treatment of rhetorical ontology:

1. **The ἐνέργεια/δύναμις framework in its fullest kinematic articulation**. The dissertation's argument that phantasia operates as an ἐνέργεια --- an actualization of a perceptual capacity --- depends on the Physics VIII account of how potentiality passes into actuality when a hindrance is removed (255b1--256a3). This is not merely a metaphysical doctrine but a physical one: the transition from δύναμις to ἐνέργεια is always a motion (κίνησις), and phantasia is precisely such a motion (De Anima III.3, 429a1: "phantasia is a kind of motion"). Physics VIII's proof that all motion reduces to locomotion, and that locomotion is grounded in the activity of the unmoved mover, therefore places phantasia within the broader cosmological order of motion.

2. **The continuity argument and its implications for temporal experience**. The proof that only circular motion is truly continuous (261a28--265b11) has consequences for the analysis of time-consciousness. If perception and phantasia involve temporal synthesis (the apprehension of before and after), and if time is an "affection of motion" (251b11), then the continuity conditions established in Physics VIII constrain what kinds of perceptual experience can be temporally unified. The "now" argument (262a18) is particularly relevant: the now as a dividing/uniting point of time reappears in De Anima III.2 and in the analysis of the "common sense" (koine aisthesis) that perceives time.

3. **The unmoved mover as the ultimate principle of all natural motion**. The dissertation's claim that rhetorical action (persuasion, pathos, the "setting before the eyes" of Rhetoric III) is intelligible only within the broader framework of Aristotelian natural motion requires that this framework have a determinate structure. Physics VIII provides that structure: all sublunary motion is ultimately caused by the eternal circular motion of the heavens, which is caused by the unmoved mover. Rhetoric, as a *techne* that works through motion (the motion of the soul in pathos, the motion of phantasia in visualization), is situated within this order.

## 9. Structural Notes

### 9.1 Argumentative Architecture

Book VIII has a remarkably systematic architecture, proceeding through five nested arguments that progressively narrow the characterization of the first mover:

1. **Eternity of motion** (Chs. 1--3): Motion is eternal. Some things are always in motion, some always at rest, some sometimes each.
2. **Regress to self-mover** (Chs. 4--5): Everything moved is moved by something; the chain terminates in a self-mover.
3. **Self-mover to unmoved mover** (Ch. 6): The self-mover contains an unmoved component; the ultimate mover is eternal and unmoved.
4. **Primacy of locomotion and circular motion** (Chs. 7--9): Locomotion is primary among kinds of motion; circular locomotion is the only continuous and infinite locomotion.
5. **Partlessness and location** (Ch. 10): The unmoved mover has no magnitude; it occupies the circumference.

Each stage depends logically on its predecessors, and the conclusion (267b25--26) synthesizes all five.

### 9.2 Relationship to the Physics as a Whole

Book VIII is the architectonic capstone of the Physics. Books I--II establish the principles and causes of natural things; Books III--IV define motion, the infinite, place, void, and time; Books V--VI classify the kinds of motion and establish continuity; Book VII provides preliminary arguments about mover-moved relations. Book VIII draws on all of these --- the definition of motion from III, the analysis of time from IV, the continuity proofs from VI, the mover-moved framework from VII --- to prove the existence and nature of the first mover. It thereby connects the Physics to the Metaphysics (the theological characterization of the unmoved mover in Book Lambda) and to the De Anima (the analysis of soul as a principle of motion).
