- **unit thesis**: This treatise identifies the common cause of all animal locomotion by situating it within a cosmological framework of moved-and-unmoved, then demonstrates that desire (*orexis*) is the proximate efficient cause of animal movement, that phantasia and intellect move only by arousing desire, and that the mechanism of action follows the structure of the practical syllogism, whereby a universal major premise and a particular minor premise yield action as conclusion.

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 The cosmological preconditions of animal motion (chs. 1--5)

#### 2.1.1 The immovable point of rest (ch. 1, 698a1--698b4)
- All animal movement requires a part at rest: joints function as origins, one bone moves while another remains fixed.
- The joint is both one and two---"potentially and actually" (698a20--22, Barnes p. 1087)---a centre from which bending and extension originate.
- Each animal as a whole must have "within itself a point at rest, whence will be the origin of that which is moved, and supporting itself upon which it will be moved both as a complete whole and in its members" (698b1--5, Barnes p. 1087).

#### 2.1.2 The external immovable (ch. 2, 698b5--699a14)
- The internal point of rest is "quite ineffectual unless there is something outside it which is absolutely at rest and immovable" (698b8--9, Barnes p. 1087).
- Just as one cannot push a boat from inside it---"the man who is himself in the boat, if he pushes, fixing himself against the boat, does not move the boat" (699a5--7, Barnes p. 1088)---so external ground or medium is necessary.
- This extends beyond animals to the cosmos: the earth serves as the immovable ground for all moving things.

#### 2.1.3 The unmoved mover and the heavens (ch. 3, 699a14--699b11)
- The mover of the whole heavens must be immovable and no part of the heavens (699a15--17, Barnes p. 1088).
- Refutes the hypothesis that poles can account for cosmic motion: poles "have no magnitude, but are merely termini or points" (699a27, Barnes p. 1088).
- The earth stands to the whole of nature as the immovable ground stands to animals.

#### 2.1.4 Heavenly motions and the dissolution problem (ch. 4, 699b11--700a6)
- Addresses the difficulty of whether the earth could be displaced by sufficient force, and whether the heavens are of necessity indestructible.
- References *Physics* VIII and *On the Heavens* I for full treatment of the immovable origin.
- Poses whether the soul is moved or at rest, referring to *De Anima* I 3--4.

#### 2.1.5 Internal movers in animals and generation (ch. 5, 700a6--700b4)
- Returns to animals: within each animal there must be both a moved part and an immovable origin (700a7--9, Barnes p. 1090).
- Distinguishes self-movers (animals) from things moved by external forces: animals "move themselves are all moved supporting themselves on what is outside them" (700a23--25, Barnes p. 1090).
- Distinguishes locomotion from growth and generation: "the question of original generation and decay is different" (700a29, Barnes p. 1090); the point at rest is not necessary for generation.

### 2.2 The soul as mover: desire, phantasia, and intellect (chs. 6--7)

#### 2.2.1 The soul moves the body; desire as proximate cause (ch. 6, 700b4--701a1)
- Inquiry shifts to how the soul (*psyche*) originates movement in the living creature (700b8--10, Barnes p. 1091).
- "All living things both move and are moved for the sake of something, so that this is the limit of all their movement---that for the sake of which" (700b15--17, Barnes p. 1091).
- "Now we see that the living creature is moved by intellect, imagination, purpose, wish, and appetite. And all these are reducible to thought and desire" (700b17--19, Barnes p. 1091).
- Both imagination (*phantasia*) and sensation are on common ground with thought, since "all three are faculties of discrimination" (700b19--21, Barnes p. 1091).
- "Wish, however, impulse, and appetite, are all three forms of desire, while purpose belongs both to intellect and to desire" (700b22--23, Barnes p. 1091).
- "Therefore the object of desire or of intellect first initiates movement" (700b24, Barnes p. 1091)---not every intelligible object, "but only the end in the domain of conduct" (700b25, Barnes p. 1091).
- The good (*to agathon*) or the apparent good initiates movement: "we must suppose that a seeming good may take the room of actual good, and so may the pleasant, which is itself a seeming good" (700b28--29, Barnes p. 1091).
- Desire and its faculty are "the last in the chain of things moved to move something else" (701a1, Barnes p. 1091); the living creature moves because "some alteration has been set going on the occasion of sensation or imagination" (701a4--5, Barnes p. 1091).

#### 2.2.2 The practical syllogism (ch. 7, 701a7--701b1)
- The transition from thought to action is explained via the practical syllogism: "the end is the truth seen" (701a9--10, Barnes p. 1091).
- Major premise (universal): "every man ought to walk"; minor premise (particular): "I am a man"; conclusion: action---"straightaway one walks" (701a13--14, Barnes p. 1091).
- Production follows the same structure: "I ought to create a good, a house is a good: straightaway he makes a house. I need a covering, a coat is a covering: I need a coat. What I need I ought to make, I need a coat: I make a coat" (701a18--20, Barnes p. 1092).
- "And the conclusion 'I must make a coat' is an action" (701a20, Barnes p. 1092).
- "The action goes back to a starting-point" (701a21, Barnes p. 1092): if there is to be a coat, there must first be this, and if this, then this---"and straightaway he does this. Now that the action is the conclusion is clear" (701a23, Barnes p. 1092).
- "The premisses of action are of two kinds, of the good and of the possible" (701a24, Barnes p. 1092).
- Rapid or unreflective action: "when a man is actually using perception or imagination or thought in relation to that for the sake of which, what he desires he does at once" (701a29--31, Barnes p. 1092).
- "For the actualizing of desire is a substitute for inquiry or thinking. I want to drink, says appetite; this is drink, says sense or imagination or thought: straightaway I drink" (701a32--33, Barnes p. 1092).
- "In this way living creatures are impelled to move and to act, and desire is the last cause of movement, and desire arises through perception or through imagination and thought" (701a34--36, Barnes p. 1092).
- Desire to act may arise "sometimes from appetite or impulse and sometimes from wish" (701b1, Barnes p. 1092).

### 2.3 The physiological mechanism and involuntary movement (chs. 8--11)

#### 2.3.1 The puppet analogy and bodily change (ch. 7 cont. + ch. 8, 701b1--702a21)
- "The movements of animals may be compared with those of automatic puppets" (701b2, Barnes p. 1092): a tiny occasion sets off cascading movement.
- Animal organs, sinews, bones are analogous to pegs, iron, strings of puppets (701b6--9, Barnes p. 1092).
- Qualitative change in the body is "caused by imaginations and sensations and by ideas" (701b16, Barnes p. 1092); imaginations and thinking "have the same power as the objects" (701b19--20, Barnes p. 1092).
- A small change near the heart produces vast bodily differences: "blushing, let us say, or turning white, and tremblings and shivers and their opposites" (701b30--32, Barnes p. 1092).
- The object of pursuit or avoidance triggers heating or chilling (702a2--3, Barnes p. 1093); "what is painful we avoid, what is pleasing we pursue" (702a3, Barnes p. 1093).
- Affections are all "accompanied by heating or chilling, some in a particular member, others in the body generally" (702a5--7, Barnes p. 1093).

#### 2.3.2 The organic mechanism: joints, imagination, and simultaneity (ch. 8 cont., 702a7--702b11)
- "The organic parts are suitably prepared by the affections, these again by desire, and desire by imagination" (702a17--19, Barnes p. 1093).
- "Imagination in its turn depends either upon thinking or upon sense-perception" (702a19--20, Barnes p. 1093).
- "The simultaneity and speed are due to the natural correspondence of the active and passive" (702a20--21, Barnes p. 1093).
- The origin of movement in the animal must be in a definite joint; the joint is "in potency one, but which becomes two in actual exercise" (702a28--30, Barnes p. 1093).

#### 2.3.3 The soul's location: the centre (ch. 9, 702b20--703a9)
- Left and right sides are symmetrical and moved simultaneously; "the origin of the moving soul must be in the middle" (702b22--23, Barnes p. 1094).
- "The sensorium is in our opinion in the centre too" (702b24, Barnes p. 1094): alteration at the origin through sense-perception extends and contracts adjacent parts, producing locomotion.
- The middle of the body is "in potency one but in actuality more than one" (702b26, Barnes p. 1094); the soul is "distinct from the magnitude described and yet located therein" (703a3, Barnes p. 1094).

#### 2.3.4 The connate spirit (*pneuma*) (ch. 10, 703a4--703b2)
- Desire moves being itself moved; "that which is moved, but whose nature is not to initiate movement, is capable of being passive to an external power, while that which initiates movement must needs possess a kind of force and power" (703a9--11, Barnes p. 1094).
- "Animals do both possess connatural spirit and derive force from this" (703a11--12, Barnes p. 1094).
- The *pneuma* (connate spirit) "appears to stand to the soul-origin in a relation analogous to that between the point in a joint which moves being moved and the unmoved" (703a13--14, Barnes p. 1094).
- The organ of movement must expand and contract: "this is precisely the characteristic of spirit. It contracts without constraint, and so is able to pull and to thrust from one and the same cause" (703a21--23, Barnes p. 1095).
- The animal organism is likened to "a well-governed commonwealth" (703a30, Barnes p. 1095): once order is established, "each part naturally doing its own work as nature has composed it" (703a34--35, Barnes p. 1095).

#### 2.3.5 Voluntary and involuntary movements (ch. 11, 703b2--704b4)
- Voluntary movements involve desire and phantasia: "the heart and of the penis; for often upon an image arising and without express mandate of the intellect these parts are moved" (703b6--8, Barnes p. 1095).
- Non-voluntary (*akousion*): sleep, waking, respiration---"neither imagination nor desire is properly mistress of any of these" (703b9--10, Barnes p. 1095).
- Involuntary movements result from qualitative bodily changes (heating, chilling) that occur despite reason: "the movements which occur in spite of reason in the aforesaid parts occur when a change of quality supervenes" (703b17--18, Barnes p. 1095).
- "Imagination, as we said above, produce that which brings about the affections, since they produce the forms which bring them about" (703b20--21, Barnes p. 1095).
- The treatise concludes: "And so we have finished our account of the reasons for the parts of each kind of animal, of the soul, and further of sense-perception, of sleep, of memory, and of movement in general; it remains to speak of generation" (704b1--4, Barnes p. 1096).

## Section 7: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **Intellect vs. desire as mover (ch. 6)**: Aristotle claims "the object of desire or of intellect first initiates movement" (700b24) but also insists desire is the proximate mover and intellect moves only through desire. The phrase "object of intellect" retains a residual role for *nous* that sits uneasily with desire's monopoly. Whether *nous praktikos* has any independent motive force or is entirely dependent on desire for causal efficacy remains in tension. [INTERP-low] --- Linked to: MA-01-P3.

2. **Phantasia's scope: cognitive or sub-rational? (chs. 6--7, 11)**: Phantasia is listed as a "faculty of discrimination" on "common ground" with thought (700b19--21), yet it also produces involuntary movements of the heart and genitals "without express mandate of the intellect" (703b6--8). Whether phantasia in MA operates as a unified faculty or divides into rational and sub-rational varieties (as *De Anima* III.11 suggests with *phantasia bouleutike* vs. *aistheke*) is not resolved here. [INTERP-med] --- Linked to: MA-01-P3, MA-01-P5, MA-01-P7.

3. **The practical syllogism: logical form or causal sequence? (ch. 7)**: The practical syllogism is presented with logical vocabulary (premises, conclusion) yet the "conclusion" is an action, not a proposition. Whether this is a genuine syllogism with a truth-evaluable conclusion, or a psychological causal sequence dressed in logical language, is a persistent interpretive problem. The rapid-action cases ("I want to drink... straightaway I drink," 701a32--33) seem to compress any deliberative structure to vanishing point. [INTERP-med] --- Linked to: MA-01-P4.

4. **Pneuma's ontological status (ch. 10)**: The connate spirit is described as standing to the soul "in a relation analogous to that between the point in a joint which moves being moved and the unmoved" (703a13--14), yet its material characteristics (expanding, contracting, exhibiting "weight compared with the fiery element, and lightness by comparison with the opposites of fire," 703a25--27) suggest a quasi-elemental body. Whether pneuma is soul's instrument, a fifth element, or simply heated air is not settled here. [INTERP-low] --- Linked to: MA-01-P6.

5. **The cosmological-zoological analogy (chs. 1--5 vs. 6--11)**: The opening five chapters establish that animal motion requires an external immovable (the earth, ultimately the cosmic unmoved mover), but the internal analysis of desire-phantasia-syllogism in chs. 6--11 never reconnects to this cosmological framework. How exactly the cosmic unmoved mover relates to the soul's desiderative function remains structurally open. [INTERP-low] --- Linked to: MA-01-P1, MA-01-P2, MA-01-P3.

## Section 10: Dissertation Integration Notes

### 10.1 Dissertation-Critical Passages

1. **The desire-phantasia-action chain (700b15--701a5)**: This is the core passage for the dissertation's argument that phantasia is not a mere cognitive faculty but a motive force that operates through desire. The claim that "the living creature is moved by intellect, imagination, purpose, wish, and appetite" (700b17--19) and that all reduce to "thought and desire" establishes the motivational architecture that the dissertation must reconstruct.

2. **The practical syllogism (701a7--701b1)**: The drinking example --- "I want to drink, says appetite; this is drink, says sense or imagination or thought: straightaway I drink" (701a32--33) --- is the locus classicus for phantasia's role in practical reasoning. It shows phantasia functioning as the faculty that supplies the particular premise, identifying *this* present thing as an instance of the desired kind.

3. **Phantasia's involuntary motive power (703b3--21)**: The claim that phantasia can move bodily parts "without express mandate of the intellect" is critical for any account of phantasia as sub-rational or pre-deliberative motivator, which bears on the dissertation's treatment of rhetorical phantasia as operating below the threshold of explicit argumentation.

### 10.2 Key Claims for Dissertation Argument

- **Phantasia as practical mover**: MA confirms that phantasia is not merely representational but causally efficacious in producing action, mediating between perception and desire.
- **The sub-rational dimension**: Involuntary movements caused by phantasia (ch. 11) show that phantasia can bypass deliberation entirely, supporting accounts of rhetorical persuasion that operate through images and affect rather than argument.
- **Practical syllogism as rhetorical structure**: The practical syllogism's structure (universal good + particular identification = action) maps onto rhetorical situations where the orator presents an image (*phantasma*) of a particular as falling under a desirable universal, thus moving the audience to action.
- **Continuity with *De Anima***: MA's treatment presupposes and extends DA III.9--11; the dissertation must read them as a single account.

### 10.3 BCAP Integration Points

- Heidegger in BCAP (GA 18) treats *orexis* and the practical syllogism in the context of *Rhetoric* and *Nicomachean Ethics*; MA provides the zoological-physiological foundation that Heidegger's existential appropriation leaves aside.
- The puppet analogy (701b2--9) and the commonwealth analogy (703a29--35) are relevant to Heidegger's reading of *praxis* as the being-in-the-world of the living creature.
