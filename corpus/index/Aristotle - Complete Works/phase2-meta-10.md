## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 The problem of mathematical objects: do they exist in sensibles or separate? (XIII.1–2, 1076a8–1077a14)

- Aristotle resumes the inquiry into whether there are, besides sensible substances, immovable and eternal substances such as the Forms or mathematical objects.
- **Three candidate non-sensible substances**: (1) the objects of mathematics, (2) the Ideas/Forms, (3) a combination of these.
- If mathematical objects exist *in* sensible things, absurdities follow: two solids cannot occupy the same place, and the objects cannot be divided independently of sensible bodies.
- If they exist *separately*, there must be separate planes, lines, and points corresponding to the mathematical solids — and then further planes, lines, and points prior to *those*, leading to an infinite regress of mathematical entities (1076b12–35, PDF 153).
- Mathematical theorems of a universal character extend beyond particular substances, requiring an intermediate substance — but this intermediate would be neither number nor magnitude nor spatial, and its separate existence is impossible (1077a1–10, PDF 153).

### 2.2 Mathematical objects exist neither separately nor in sensibles, but are studied by abstraction (XIII.3, 1077b–1078a30)

- Mathematics deals with magnitudes and numbers not *qua* separate entities but *qua* possessing certain definite qualities.
- The mathematician treats things by "supposing separate what is not separate" — this introduces no error, just as drawing a line and calling it a foot long when it is not introduces no error in the proof (1078a17–21, PDF 155).
- Sciences gain in accuracy by abstracting: geometry abstracts from movement, arithmetic from magnitude, etc. (1078a9–13, PDF 155).
- Mathematical objects exist not *in* sensibles nor *apart from* them, but as attributes studied by abstraction; they are prior in formula but posterior in substance to physical bodies.

### 2.3 The beautiful as a mathematical property (XIII.3 end, 1078a31–1078b5)

- Order (τάξις), symmetry (συμμετρία), and definiteness (τὸ ὡρισμένον) are chief forms of beauty, and the mathematical sciences demonstrate them in a special degree.
- These mathematical properties are causes of many things, so mathematics must treat beauty — "we shall speak more plainly elsewhere about these matters" (1078b5, PDF 156).

### 2.4 Critique of the theory of Ideas/Forms (XIII.4–5, 1078b7–1080a8)

- The supporters of the ideal theory were led to it because they accepted the Heraclitean doctrine of flux and Socrates' search for universal definitions (1078b12–30, PDF 156).
- Socrates did not make the universals or definitions exist apart; his successors gave them separate existence and called them Ideas/Forms.
- **Arguments against the Forms** (1079a4–1080a8, PDF 157–158):
  - The proofs for Forms prove too much: Forms of negations, of perishable things, of relations.
  - The "third man" argument: if the Idea and the particular share a common character, there must be a further Form over both (1079a13, PDF 157).
  - Forms cannot be causes of sensible things: they cause neither movement nor change.
  - Participation is "empty words and poetical metaphors" — what is it that works looking to the Ideas? (1079b25, PDF 158).
  - In the *Phaedo*, the Forms are said to be causes of both being and becoming, yet things come into being even without participation; many like things are produced without Forms (1080a2–8, PDF 158).

### 2.5 Problems about numbers and their principles (XIII.6–9, 1080a12–1086a21)

- Detailed examination of whether numbers are separable substances with the One and the indefinite dyad as principles.
- **Three views on number** (1080a15–b36, PDF 159):
  - (1) Ideal number only (units non-comparable).
  - (2) Mathematical number only (units all comparable).
  - (3) Ideal and mathematical number are the same.
  - The Pythagoreans hold mathematical number but give it spatial magnitude (1080b16–21, PDF 159).
- **The problem of comparable vs. non-comparable units** (XIII.7, 1081a5–1082a25, PDF 160–161): If all units are comparable, we get only mathematical number and the Ideas cannot be numbers. If units are non-comparable, ideal number faces absurdities about the generation of 2, 3, etc. from the One and the indefinite dyad.
- **Difficulties with the ideal 2, 3, 10** (XIII.7–8, 1082a26–1083b23, PDF 161–163): The units in the ideal 2 and ideal 3 cannot be properly combined; differentiating units is "an absurd fiction"; if ideal and mathematical number are the same, this is "the worst" view.
- **The Pythagorean doctrine** compared: fewer difficulties by not making number separable, but bodies cannot be composed of numbers, and indivisible magnitudes are impossible (1083b8–19, PDF 163).
- **Number and magnitude cannot exist separately** (XIII.8 end–9, 1083b23–1085a3, PDF 163–165): Number is either infinite or finite; if finite, the Forms will run short (e.g., if 3 is man-in-himself, what is the horse?). Difficulties about lines, planes, and solids posterior to number. The One as starting-point vs. element is confused (1084b2–1085a3, PDF 165).

### 2.6 The aporia of universal vs. individual principles (XIII.10, 1086b14–1087a25)

- Whether principles are universal or individual.
- If individual, they will be as numerous as the elements; knowledge requires universals but substance is individual — both horns are problematic (1086b33–1087a10, PDF 168).
- The statement that knowledge is universal "is in a sense true, although in a sense it is not" — knowledge is of universals potentially, of individuals actually (1087a15–22, PDF 169).

### 2.7 First principles cannot be contraries; critique of the One and the indefinite dyad (XIV.1, 1087a29–1088a14)

- All thinkers make first principles contraries (1087a29, PDF 169).
- But since nothing is contrary to substance, and the principle cannot be "something else" (i.e., an attribute), the principle cannot be a contrariety applied to a subject.
- Contraries are always predicable of a subject and cannot exist apart.
- **The One as measure** (1088a1–14, PDF 170): "One" evidently means a measure — the measure of some plurality appropriate to the kind of thing measured (in horses the measure is horse, in rhythms a beat, etc.).

### 2.8 The relative is not a substance; critique of the great and the small (XIV.1 end–2, 1088a15–1089b15)

- The unequal / the great and the small / plurality — all these are *relatives*, not substances (1088a22–28, PDF 170).
- The relative is "least of all things a real thing or substance" — it alone has no proper generation or destruction (1088a29–31, PDF 170).
- No eternal substance can have elements or consist of elements (1088b14–1089a6, PDF 171): if it consists of elements, it has matter; anything with matter can be or not be; eternal things cannot not-be.
- **Being and non-being** (1089a7–b15, PDF 172): Parmenides' challenge reformulated. "Being" has many senses; non-being likewise has many senses. The generation of many things from being is explained through the multiple senses of being (substance, quality, quantity, etc.), not through positing non-being as a co-principle.

### 2.9 Numbers are not causes and are not separate; the good as first principle (XIV.3–5, 1090a2–1092a17)

- The Pythagoreans supposed real things *to be* numbers because they found numerical attributes in nature — but they meant numbers of real things, not separable numbers (1090a20–25, PDF 173).
- Mathematical number alone exists, but it is not separable and is not a first principle (1090a28–35, PDF 173).
- If numbers are in no way present in sensible things, then they are causally idle; the theorems of mathematics will be true of sensible things anyway (1090b1–5, PDF 174).
- Points, lines, and extremes are mere limits, not substances (1090b5–13, PDF 174).
- Regarding the good: to say that the first principle is the One or a number *and* that it is good leads to absurdities — all Forms become species of good; the contrary element (the dyad / plurality / unequal) becomes the bad-itself (1091b30–1092a5, PDF 176).

### 2.10 Numbers are not formal, efficient, or final causes of things (XIV.5 end–6, 1092a11–1093b29)

- It has not been determined in which way numbers are causes: as limits (like points), as ratios (like harmony), as matter, or as formal causes (1092b8–15, PDF 177).
- **Eurytus' pebble method** (1092b10, PDF 177): fitting pebble-numbers to the outlines of man or horse to determine "the number of man" — this shows the absurdity of identifying things with numbers.
- The good cannot come from numbers because number is "neither the cause as agent, nor the matter, nor the formula and form of things" (1092b25–26, PDF 177).
- **Number mysticism demolished** (XIV.6, 1093a1–b27, PDF 178–179): seven vowels, seven strings, seven Pleiades, seven champions against Thebes — these are coincidences, not explanations. The analogies between numbers and nature are "like coincidences, appropriate to one another by analogy" (1093b18, PDF 179).
- **Concluding verdict**: The objects of mathematics are not separable from sensible things, and they are not the first principles (1093b27–29, PDF 179).

## 6. Tensions

| tension | node_a | node_b | type | description |
|---------|--------|--------|------|-------------|
| T1: Abstraction vs. real mathematical structure | Mathematical objects as mere abstractions | Order, symmetry, and definiteness as genuine causes of beauty | internal | XIII.3 argues mathematics treats attributes by abstraction (not separate entities), yet the same chapter claims order and symmetry are "causes of many things" (1078b1, PDF 156). | T2: Rejection of Forms vs. need for universal knowledge | Individual substances as primary realities | Knowledge requires universals | internal | XIII.10, 1086b33–1087a25 (PDF 168): if principles are individual, elements will be only as many as individual things, and there will be no knowledge (which is of universals). | T3: Anti-Platonist polemic vs. positive ontology | Extensive negative critique (XIII.4–9, XIV.1–6) | Sparse positive account | structural | Over 80% of these two books is devoted to refuting Platonist theories of Forms and numbers.
