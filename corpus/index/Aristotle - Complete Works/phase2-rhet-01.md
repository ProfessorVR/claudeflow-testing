## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Rhetoric as counterpart of dialectic and critique of predecessors (I.1, 1354a1–1355b25)
- Rhetoric is the counterpart (*antistrophos*) of dialectic: both deal with matters within the general ken of all, both belong to no definite science, both provide arguments.
- Current handbook writers neglect the proper modes of persuasion and focus instead on arousing prejudice, pity, and anger — non-essential appeals to emotion that circumvent the facts. 1354a11–16 (p. 3).
- In well-governed states, strict rules would render such emotional appeals unnecessary; the ideal judge decides only facts. 1354a31–1354b16 (pp. 3–4).
- Well-drawn laws should define as many matters as possible, leaving as few as possible to judicial discretion, because legislation is prospective and general, whereas judicial decisions are particular and susceptible to bias. 1354a31–1354b11 (pp. 3–4).
- Previous writers confined themselves to forensic oratory and said nothing about political oratory, which is "nobler" and less given to unscrupulous tricks. 1354b22–1355a3 (pp. 4–5).
- The technical study of rhetoric is concerned with the modes of persuasion (*pisteis*). 1355a3–7 (p. 5).

### 2.2 Rhetoric defined: its nature, usefulness, and relation to dialectic (I.1, 1355a7–1355b34)
- Persuasion is a sort of demonstration; the orator's demonstration is the enthymeme. 1355a5–8 (p. 5).
- The enthymeme is a sort of deduction (*sullogismos*); skill in deduction belongs to dialectic. He who is best at dialectic will also be best at enthymemes. 1355a8–14 (p. 5).
- Rhetoric differs from dialectic: dialectic proceeds from true or reputable premises; rhetoric also uses apparent proofs and apparent enthymemes. 1355a14–18 (p. 5).
- What makes a man a sophist is not his ability but his *choice* (moral purpose); in rhetoric the term "rhetorician" may describe either the speaker's knowledge or his choices. 1355b15–21 (p. 6).
- Rhetoric is useful for four reasons: (1) truth and justice are naturally stronger, so a failure to persuade is the speaker's fault; (2) some audiences cannot be reached by exact knowledge alone; (3) one must be able to argue on both sides to see the facts clearly and to refute unfair arguments; (4) it would be absurd if inability to defend oneself with speech were shameful when rational speech is more distinctive of a human being than physical strength. 1355a20–1355b7 (pp. 5–6).
- Rhetoric is not bound to a single definite class of subjects but, like dialectic, is universal in scope. 1355b8–10 (p. 6).
- Its function is not simply to succeed in persuading but rather to discover the available means of persuasion in each case, analogous to medicine's function being treatment rather than guaranteed cure. 1355b10–14 (p. 6).
- It is the function of one and the same art to discern the real and the apparent means of persuasion, just as dialectic discerns the real and the apparent syllogism. 1355b15–17 (p. 6).

### 2.3 The formal definition and the three technical proofs (I.2, 1355b25–1356b5)
- **Definition**: "Rhetoric may be defined as the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion." 1355b25–26 (p. 7).
- This is not a function of any other art; every other art instructs or persuades about its own subject-matter, but rhetoric observes the means of persuasion on almost any subject. 1355b26–34 (p. 7).
- Modes of persuasion: some are *atechnoi* (non-technical — witnesses, contracts, evidence given under torture) and some are *entechnoi* (technical — what we construct by the principles of rhetoric). 1355b35–1356a1 (p. 7).
- Three kinds of technical proof furnished by the spoken word:
  - (1) **Ethos**: persuasion through the speaker's personal character — "we believe good men more fully and more readily." Character must be produced by the speech itself, not by prior reputation. 1356a1–13 (p. 7).
  - (2) **Pathos**: persuasion through putting the audience into a certain emotional frame — "our judgements when we are pleased and friendly are not the same as when we are pained and hostile." 1356a14–19 (p. 7).
  - (3) **Logos**: persuasion through proof or apparent proof furnished by the words of the speech itself — "when we have proved a truth or an apparent truth." 1356a19–20 (p. 7).
- The master of persuasion must be able to reason logically, understand human character and excellences, and understand the emotions. 1356a20–25 (pp. 7–8).
- Rhetoric is thus an offshoot of dialectic and of ethical studies; ethical studies may fairly be called "political." 1356a25–27 (p. 8).

### 2.4 Enthymeme and example as rhetorical deduction and induction (I.2, 1356a35–1357a7)
- Just as in dialectic there is induction and deduction (both real and apparent), so in rhetoric: the example is an induction, the enthymeme is a deduction, the apparent enthymeme an apparent deduction. 1356a35–1356b5 (p. 8).
- Everyone who effects persuasion uses either enthymemes or examples: there is no other way. 1356b5–7 (p. 8).
- The difference between example and enthymeme mirrors the difference between induction and deduction as discussed in the *Topics* and *Analytics*. 1356b8–18 (p. 8).
- Speeches relying on examples are as persuasive as those relying on enthymemes, but enthymemes excite louder applause. 1356b20–25 (p. 8).
- What is persuasive is persuasive to someone, either because it is directly self-evident or because it appears proved from other statements that are so. But rhetoric concerns classes and types, not individuals. 1356b28–1357a1 (pp. 8–9).
- Rhetoric draws on materials that call for discussion — the regular subjects of debate — about things that could be otherwise (contingent matters). 1357a1–7 (p. 9).

### 2.5 The materials of the enthymeme: probabilities, signs, evidences (I.2, 1357a7–1357b25)
- It is possible to form deductions either from previous deductions or from premises not yet proved but reputable; the former are hard to follow at length, the latter fail if based on premises that are not generally admitted. 1357a7–16 (p. 9).
- The enthymeme deals with what is for the most part, the example being an induction and the enthymeme a deduction. The enthymeme must consist of few propositions, fewer than a primary deduction; if any premise is familiar, it need not be stated. 1357a16–22 (p. 9).
- There are few facts of the necessary type that can form the basis of rhetorical deductions; most matters of deliberation and action are contingent. 1357a22–30 (p. 9).
- The propositions forming the basis of enthymemes are **probabilities** (*eikota*) and **signs** (*semeia*). 1357a30–32 (p. 9).
  - A probability is what happens for the most part. 1357a34–b1 (p. 9).
  - Of signs, one kind bears the relation of particular to universal, the other of universal to particular. 1357b1–3 (p. 9).
  - A necessary sign is an **evidence** (*tekmerion*): it constitutes irrefutable proof. 1357b4–10 (pp. 9–10).
  - A non-necessary sign: refutable (e.g., "he has a fever, therefore he is ill" — refutable because one can have a fever without being ill). 1357b10–21 (p. 10).

### 2.6 The example as rhetorical induction (I.2, 1357b25–1358a2)
- The example has already been described as one kind of induction, distinguished by the special nature of the subject-matter. 1357b25–28 (p. 10).
- Its relation is not whole to part or part to whole, but part to part, like to like. 1357b28–30 (p. 10).
- Illustrative example: Dionysius asking for a bodyguard is scheming for tyranny, because Peisistratus and Theagenes both asked for bodyguards and became tyrants — reasoning from particular instances to a general principle. 1357b30–1358a2 (p. 10).

### 2.7 Two sorts of enthymeme: common and special (I.2, 1358a2–1358a32)
- An important distinction between two sorts of enthymemes, overlooked by almost everybody. 1358a2–4 (p. 10).
- One sort belongs to rhetoric proper; the other belongs to other arts and faculties. 1358a4–7 (p. 10).
- The common *topoi* apply to questions of right conduct, natural science, politics, and many other disconnected subjects (e.g., the commonplace of "the more or less"). 1358a10–17 (p. 11).
- Special *topoi* are based on propositions that apply only to particular groups or classes. 1358a17–21 (p. 11).
- The nearer one comes to setting up a science distinct from dialectic or rhetoric, the more one unconsciously crosses into another discipline. Most enthymemes are in fact based on special rather than common topics. 1358a21–28 (p. 11).

### 2.8 Three species of rhetoric: deliberative, forensic, epideictic (I.3, 1358a36–1358b20)
- Rhetoric falls into three divisions determined by the three classes of listeners: the hearer as judge of future things (assembly member), as judge of past things (juryman), or as observer of the speaker's skill. 1358a36–1358b4 (p. 11).
- Therefore three divisions of oratory: **deliberative** (*sumbouleutikon*), **forensic/judicial** (*dikanikon*), **epideictic/display** (*epideiktikon*). 1358b4–8 (p. 11).
- Each refers to a different kind of time: deliberative to the future, forensic to the past, epideictic to the present (though it may also recall the past or anticipate the future). 1358b13–17 (p. 11).

### 2.9 The ends of the three species (I.3, 1358b20–1359a5)
- Rhetoric has three distinct ends, one for each of its three kinds. 1358b20–22 (p. 12).
- **Deliberative**: the expedient (*sumpheron*) and the harmful. 1358b22–24 (p. 12).
- **Forensic**: the just (*dikaion*) and the unjust. 1358b24–26 (p. 12).
- **Epideictic**: the noble/honourable (*kalon*) and the base/disgraceful (*aischron*). 1358b27–28 (p. 12).
- Each speaker brings in subsidiary considerations (e.g., the deliberative speaker may also address justice, the forensic speaker may invoke expediency). 1358b24–29 (p. 12).
- Each kind aims at its proper end: the litigant never denies having done harm but always denies injustice; the deliberative orator may concede inexpediency but never injustice. 1358b29–38 (p. 12).
- The propositions of rhetoric are evidences (*tekmeria*), probabilities (*eikota*), and signs (*semeia*). Every kind of deduction is composed of propositions, and the enthymeme is a deduction composed of these types. 1359a5–10 (p. 12).

### 2.10 Transition to the topoi of deliberation (I.4, 1359a11–29)
- The orator must have propositions at his command about the possible and impossible, and about whether something has or has not occurred, will or will not occur. 1359a11–16 (p. 12).
- The orator must also have propositions about greatness and smallness (the greater and lesser good, the greater or lesser act of justice). 1359a20–25 (p. 12).
- Such are the subjects regarding which we are inevitably bound to master the propositions relevant to them. 1359a26–29 (p. 12).

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **Rhetoric's moral status (I.1, 1355a29–b7)**: Aristotle concedes that rhetoric can be misused, then defends it by analogy to all good things except virtue. The tension between rhetoric as morally neutral *techne* and rhetoric as inherently directed at truth and justice is never fully resolved in these chapters. The argument that "truth and justice are naturally stronger" (1355a21) coexists with the admission that "one who uses such power of speech unjustly might do great harm" (1355b2–4). [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: RHET-01-P5.

2. **Ethos in the speech vs. ethos from reputation (I.2, 1356a1–13)**: Aristotle insists character must be "achieved by what the speaker says, not by what people think of his character before he begins to speak" (1356a8–10). This is a polemical position against Isocrates and common practice. Whether ethos can truly be divorced from the audience's prior knowledge of the speaker is a persistent interpretive question. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: RHET-01-P4.

3. **Relation of rhetoric to ethics and politics (I.2, 1356a25–27)**: Aristotle says rhetoric is "an offshoot of dialectic and also of ethical studies" and that ethics may "fairly be called political." This dual parentage creates a tension: is rhetoric fundamentally logical (dialectic) or fundamentally practical-political? The claim that rhetoric "masquerades as political science" (1356a27–28) is left ambiguous. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: RHET-01-P1, RHET-01-P3.

4. **Common vs. special topoi and disciplinary boundaries (I.2, 1358a17–28)**: The more one selects special topoi, the nearer one comes to a science distinct from rhetoric. This boundary problem — where does rhetoric end and a specialized science begin? — is flagged but not resolved. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: RHET-01-P7.

5. **The tripartite division and its exhaustiveness (I.3, 1358a36–b8)**: The division into deliberative, forensic, and epideictic rests on three audience roles. Whether these three exhaust the possible functions of rhetoric, or whether they are simply the institutional settings available in 4th-century Athens, is an open question that later rhetorical theory (e.g., letter-writing, homiletics) has repeatedly pressed. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: RHET-01-P6.

## Section 9: Dissertation Relevance

### 9.1 Why this unit is critical
RHET-01 provides the foundational apparatus for any dissertation treating Aristotelian rhetoric:
- The *definition* of rhetoric (1355b25–26) is the single most cited sentence in rhetorical theory.
- The *antistrophos* relation positions rhetoric within the broader organon and relative to dialectic — essential for any argument about rhetoric's epistemic or ontological status.
- The *three pisteis* (ethos, pathos, logos) structure nearly all subsequent accounts of persuasion.
- The *enthymeme* as the "body of persuasion" establishes the logical core of rhetoric and its dependence on contingent matter — critical for accounts of rhetoric's relation to truth, demonstration, and practical reasoning.
- The *tripartite division* (deliberative, forensic, epideictic) governs the classification of rhetorical situations and their ends.

### 9.2 Key passages for citation
- **1354a1** (p. 3): "Rhetoric is the counterpart of dialectic" — the opening sentence; defines rhetoric relationally.
- **1355a5–8** (p. 5): "Persuasion is a sort of demonstration... the orator's demonstration is the enthymeme" — enthymeme as the core of rhetoric.
- **1355b25–26** (p. 7): "Rhetoric may be defined as the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion" — the formal definition.
- **1356a1–20** (p. 7): The three technical proofs (ethos, pathos, logos) — foundational taxonomy.
- **1356a25–27** (p. 8): "rhetoric is an offshoot of dialectic and also of ethical studies" — rhetoric's dual parentage.
- **1357a34–b10** (pp. 9–10): Probability, sign, evidence (tekmerion) — the material of enthymemes.
- **1358a36–b8** (p. 11): Three species of rhetoric — the tripartite classification.

### 9.3 Cross-text connections
- **Heidegger BCAP (GA 18)**: Heidegger's 1924 lectures take the *Rhetoric* as a systematic hermeneutic of everyday Dasein. BCAP units on *pathos* and *ethos* directly interpret I.2's three pisteis as modes of Being-with-one-another. The enthymeme's reliance on the probable connects to Heidegger's analysis of *doxa* and everyday understanding.
- **Heidegger B&T**: The Being and Time analysis of *Befindlichkeit* (state-of-mind/attunement) draws on the *Rhetoric*'s treatment of the *pathe* (II.1–11). The I.1–I.3 framework — rhetoric as concerned with contingent matters and popular audiences — underlies Heidegger's claim that rhetoric is "the first systematic hermeneutic of the everydayness of Being-with-one-another" (B&T §29, H.138).
- **Rickert, Ambient Rhetoric**: Rickert's re-definition of rhetoric as "ambient" challenges the speaker-centered, intentional model that Aristotle's three pisteis presuppose. The RHET-01 definition (faculty of *observing* means of persuasion) may be more hospitable to ambient readings than the pisteis taxonomy.
