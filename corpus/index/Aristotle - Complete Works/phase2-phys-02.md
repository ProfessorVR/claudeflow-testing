## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Ch. 1 — Definition of φύσις: nature as internal principle (192b8–193b21)

- Some things exist by nature (animals, plants, simple bodies), others from other causes (beds, coats); natural things have an innate impulse to change, artifacts do not — 192b8–15 (PDF 17).
- Nature is "a principle or cause of being moved and of being at rest in that to which it belongs primarily, in virtue of itself and not accidentally" — 192b20–23 (PDF 17).
- The doctor healing himself illustrates accidental vs. per se causation: the medical art is in him accidentally, not qua patient — 192b23–27 (PDF 17).
- Artifacts have no innate impulse to change; insofar as they are composed of natural materials (stone, earth), they have nature, but only incidentally — 192b28–33 (PDF 17).
- Nature is always in a subject: it is a substance, for "things have a nature which have a principle of this kind" — 192b33–193a2 (PDF 17).
- First account of nature: the primary underlying matter of things which have in themselves a principle of motion or change — 193a28–30 (PDF 18).
- Second account: nature is the shape or form specified in the definition — 193a30–b5 (PDF 18).
- Antiphon's bed argument: if you planted a bed and the wood sprouted, what came up would be wood, not a bed — showing that the arrangement (form) is merely accidental to the persisting material — 193a12–17 (PDF 17).
- Form is nature rather than matter: "a thing is more properly said to be what it is when it exists in actuality than when it exists potentially" — 193b6–8 (PDF 18).
- Nature in the sense of a coming-to-be proceeds toward nature (form), not away from it: "doctoring leads not to the art of doctoring but to health" — 193b12–18 (PDF 18).
- Shape and nature are used in two ways; privation is also a kind of form — 193b18–21 (PDF 18).

### 2.2 Ch. 2 — The physicist vs. the mathematician; form and matter (193b22–194b15)

- The physicist differs from the mathematician: both study surfaces, volumes, lines, points, but the mathematician treats them in abstraction from natural bodies, the physicist treats them as limits of natural bodies — 193b22–31 (PDF 19).
- Astronomy is a department of natural science, not pure mathematics — 193b25–28 (PDF 19).
- The holders of the theory of Forms separate the objects of natural science from their matter, though these objects are less separable than mathematical objects — 193b35–194a1 (PDF 19).
- Since nature is twofold (matter and form), the physicist must investigate both: "how far must the student of nature know the form or essence?" — 194a12–15 (PDF 20).
- The arts use matter and know form: the helmsman knows the form of the helm, the builder knows both form and matter — 194a1–7 (PDF 20).
- Matter is relative: different matter for different forms — 194a8–12 (PDF 20).
- The mode of existence of forms is not separable from matter (except in statement); "it is the business of first philosophy to define" the separable mode — 194a14–15 (PDF 20).

### 2.3 Ch. 3 — The four causes (194b16–195b30)

- "Knowledge is the object of our inquiry, and men do not think they know a thing till they have grasped the 'why' of it (which is to grasp its primary cause)" — 194b17–20 (PDF 20).
- **Material cause**: "that out of which a thing comes to be and which persists," e.g. the bronze of the statue, the silver of the bowl — 194b23–26 (PDF 20).
- **Formal cause**: "the form or the archetype, i.e. the definition of the essence, and its genera," e.g. the octave relation 2:1 — 194b26–28 (PDF 20).
- **Efficient cause**: "the primary source of the change or rest," e.g. the father is cause of the child, the man who deliberated is a cause, generally "what makes of what is made and what changes of what is changed" — 194b29–32 (PDF 20).
- **Final cause**: "in the sense of end or that for the sake of which a thing is done," e.g. health is the cause of walking about — 194b32–35 (PDF 20).
- All intermediate steps between the agent and the end are "for the sake of" the end: "reduction of flesh, purging, drugs, surgical instruments are means towards health" — 195a3–8 (PDF 21).
- Causes are called causes in many ways; within the same kind, one may be prior to another — 195a27–b3 (PDF 21).
- All causes may be spoken of as either potential or actual: "the cause of a house being built is either a house-builder or a house-builder building" — 195b4–6 (PDF 22).
- In investigating causes, one must seek the most precise cause: "a man builds because he is a builder, and a builder builds in virtue of his art of building. This last cause then is prior" — 195b21–25 (PDF 22).

### 2.4 Chs. 4–6 — Chance (τύχη) and spontaneity (τὸ αὐτόματον) (195b31–198a13)

- Chance and spontaneity are reckoned among causes; the question is how they fit into the fourfold division — 195b31–36 (PDF 22).
- Some deny chance exists, claiming everything has a determinate cause — 196a1–7 (PDF 22).
- The early physicists (Empedocles, Democritus) found no place for chance among their causes — 196a17–24 (PDF 23).
- Others ascribe the heavenly sphere and the universe to spontaneity (the vortex arose spontaneously) — 196a24–28 (PDF 23).
- Ch. 5: Chance and spontaneity belong to the class of things that happen "for the sake of something" but happen incidentally — 196b10–197a5 (PDF 23).
- Chance is an incidental cause "in the sphere of those actions for the sake of something which involve choice" — thought, then, and chance are in the same sphere, "for choice implies thought" — 197a5–8 (PDF 24).
- Chance is indefinite and inscrutable to man; it is an accidental cause — 197a8–12 (PDF 24).
- Chance is called good when the result is good, evil when evil; "good fortune" and "ill fortune" apply when results are of considerable magnitude — 197a25–30 (PDF 24).
- Ch. 6: Spontaneity (τὸ αὐτόματον) is wider than chance (τύχη): every result of chance is from what is spontaneous, but not everything spontaneous is from chance — 197a36–b1 (PDF 25).
- Chance is restricted to agents capable of choice (thus of action and good fortune); children, animals, and inanimate things act "from spontaneity" but not "by chance" — 197b1–8 (PDF 25).
- Spontaneous events are those which, belonging to the general class of things that happen for the sake of something, have an external cause and do not come to pass for the sake of what actually results — 197b18–22 (PDF 25).
- The spontaneous is indicated by the phrase "in vain" (μάτην): "the stone did not fall for the sake of striking him" — 197b25–30 (PDF 25).
- Both are accidental causes "in the sphere of things which are capable of coming to pass not simply, nor for the most part" — 197b33–198a1 (PDF 26).

### 2.5 Ch. 7 — The four causes restated; the "why" question (198a14–198b9)

- There are causes, and the number of them is four — 198a14–15 (PDF 26).
- The "why" is referred either to the "what" (the formal cause), or to "what initiated a motion" (the efficient cause), or to "for the sake of what" (the final cause), or to the matter (the material cause) — 198a15–21 (PDF 26).
- The last three often coincide: "the what and that for the sake of which are one, while the primary source of motion is the same in species as these" — 198a24–27 (PDF 26).
- The question "why" is answered with reference to matter, form, mover, and end; "What comes to be after what? What was the primary agent or patient?" — 198b5–9 (PDF 26).
- The unmoved mover principle: things which cause motion "not being themselves moved" are not the province of natural science but of first philosophy — 198a35–b4 (PDF 26).

### 2.6 Ch. 8 — Nature acts for an end; teleology vs. Empedocles (198b10–199b33)

- Nature belongs to the class of causes which act for the sake of something — 198b10–11 (PDF 27).
- The difficulty: "why should not nature work, not for the sake of something, nor because it is better so, but just as the sky rains, not in order to make the corn grow, but of necessity?" — 198b16–23 (PDF 27).
- The Empedoclean objection: teeth come up sharp in front and broad at the back "of necessity," and those which happened to be fitted for use survived, "being organized spontaneously in a fitting way; whereas those which grew otherwise perished" — a proto-selectionist argument — 198b23–32 (PDF 27).
- Aristotle's reply: "it is impossible that this should be the true view. For teeth and all other natural things either invariably or for the most part come about in a given way" — regularity excludes chance — 199a1–5 (PDF 27).
- Teleological argument from analogy with art: "if a house had been a thing made by nature, it would have been made in the same way as it is now by art" — 199a12–15 (PDF 28).
- "If, therefore, artificial products are for the sake of an end, so clearly also are natural products" — 199a17–18 (PDF 28).
- The spider, the ant, the swallow build by nature, not after inquiry or deliberation: "it is plain that this kind of cause is operative in things which come to be and are by nature" — 199b26–30 (PDF 28).
- Mistakes in art (the literate man's misspelling, the doctor's wrong dose) show that purpose can misfire; analogously, "clearly mistakes are possible in the operations of nature also" — 199a33–b4 (PDF 28).
- The "ox-progeny" (man-headed ox-progeny) of Empedocles: if the original combinations failed to reach a determinate end, this was due to corruption of a principle, "as happens now when the seed is defective" — 199b5–9 (PDF 28).
- Natural things arrive at their ends "by a continuous movement originated from an internal principle" — 199b15–18 (PDF 28).
- **Summary**: "It is plain then that nature is a cause, a cause that operates for a purpose" — 199b32–33 (PDF 29).

### 2.7 Ch. 9 — Necessity in nature: hypothetical vs. absolute (199b34–200b8)

- Is necessity in nature hypothetical or absolute (simple)? — 199b34–200a1 (PDF 29).
- The current (mistaken) view treats necessity as if the wall of a house necessarily comes to be because heavy things fall and light things rise — this is to invert the order of explanation — 200a1–5 (PDF 29).
- The wall does not come to be "without these, but it is not due to these, except as its material cause: it comes to be for the sake of sheltering and guarding certain things" — 200a5–7 (PDF 29).
- Necessity is "on a hypothesis, not as an end": "the saw must be of iron, if we are to have a saw and perform the operation of sawing" — 200a10–13 (PDF 29).
- "Necessity is in the matter, while that for the sake of which is in the definition" — 200a14–15 (PDF 29).
- Analogy with mathematics: "since a straight line is what it is, it is necessary that the angles of a triangle should equal two right angles" — the conclusion is necessary given the premises, but the necessity runs from conclusion to premises, not vice versa — 200a15–19 (PDF 29).
- In nature, the end (form) is the principle of reasoning, while necessity is in the matter — 200a34–b4 (PDF 30).
- "The necessary in nature, then, is plainly what we call by the name of matter, and the changes in it" — 200b7–8 (PDF 30).
- Both causes must be stated by the student of nature, "but especially the end; for that is the cause of the matter, not vice versa; and the end is that for the sake of which, and the principle starts from the definition or essence" — 200b4–7 (PDF 30).
- The definition includes the matter: "the definition too there are some parts that stand as matter" — 200b7–8 (PDF 30).

## Section 7: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **Matter vs. form as nature (Ch. 1)**: Aristotle presents two accounts of nature — matter and form — and argues that form is nature "more properly." Yet he never eliminates the material account entirely; matter remains a genuine sense of "nature." The relationship between the two is described as complementary ("the physicist must study both" — 194a12), but the priority of form raises the question of whether matter qua matter has any independent explanatory role in natural science or is merely the hypothetically necessary substrate. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: PHYS-02-P1, PHYS-02-P2, PHYS-02-P6.

2. **The coincidence of three causes (Ch. 7)**: Aristotle claims that "the what and that for the sake of which are one, while the primary source of motion is the same in species as these" (198a24–27). If formal, final, and efficient causes coincide, the four-cause doctrine effectively reduces to two (matter + form/end/mover). Whether this convergence is universal or restricted to natural generation (where "man begets man") is a significant open question. In artifacts, the convergence clearly does not hold (the builder is not the same in form as the house). [INTERP-low] — Linked to: PHYS-02-P3.

3. **Chance and the "for the sake of" (Chs. 4–6)**: Chance is defined as an incidental cause "in the sphere of things done for the sake of something." This makes teleology logically prior to chance — chance is parasitic on purposive processes. But the account raises a circularity concern: if chance is defined relative to "for the sake of something," and "for the sake of something" is established partly by excluding chance (Ch. 8), the two doctrines are mutually supporting rather than independently grounded. [INTERP-medium] — Linked to: PHYS-02-P4, PHYS-02-P5.

4. **Teleology without deliberation (Ch. 8)**: Aristotle insists that nature acts for an end but "does not deliberate" (199b26). Spiders, ants, and plants act purposively without inquiry. The analogy with art presupposes a craftsman who *does* deliberate; Aristotle then strips deliberation away while retaining purposiveness. Whether this move is legitimate — whether "purpose" without any cognitive agent is a genuine explanatory category or a metaphorical extension from the domain of art — is the central contested issue in the interpretation of Aristotelian teleology. [INTERP-high] — Linked to: PHYS-02-P5.

5. **Empedocles' "natural selection" (Ch. 8)**: Aristotle's refutation of Empedocles depends on the claim that natural things come about "always or for the most part" and hence cannot be the result of chance. But Empedocles' argument could be read as explaining *why* regularity obtains (only the fit survived), not as denying regularity. Aristotle treats the Empedoclean account as positing chance-origin, but a more charitable reading might see it as a rival *explanation* of regularity. This tension has been central to modern reassessments (notably by David Sedley and Monte Ransome Johnson). [INTERP-high] — Linked to: PHYS-02-P5.

6. **Hypothetical necessity and the explanatory direction (Ch. 9)**: Aristotle argues that in nature, necessity runs from end to matter ("if there is to be a saw, it must be of iron"), not from matter to end. But the mathematical analogy (200a15–19) suggests that necessary truths run from premises to conclusions — yet Aristotle says in natural things the end is the "principle of reasoning." The analogy with mathematical proof appears to work differently from natural explanation, and whether "hypothetical necessity" truly parallels mathematical demonstration or merely borrows its language is unclear. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: PHYS-02-P6.

## Section 9: Dissertation Relevance

### 9.1 Why PHYS-02 Is Critical

Physics Book II provides the foundational causal and teleological framework that undergirds the entire Aristotelian system. For a dissertation on rhetorical ontology, this unit is indispensable for three reasons:

1. **The four causes structure all subsequent Aristotelian explanation.** Every analysis of φαντασία, perception, and rhetorical effect in the De Anima and Rhetoric presupposes the fourfold causal scheme established here. The formal and final causes, in particular, are what allow Aristotle to treat perception as "receiving the form without the matter" (De Anima II.12) and rhetoric as an art (τέχνη) directed toward persuasion as its end.

2. **The φύσις / τέχνη distinction** defines the boundary between natural and artificial production. Rhetoric is explicitly a τέχνη in the Rhetoric; understanding what this means requires grasping how Aristotle distinguishes art from nature in Physics II.1. The analogy between art and nature in II.8 (art completes what nature cannot or imitates it) is directly relevant to how rhetorical art relates to the natural processes of perception and phantasia.

3. **Teleology without deliberation** (II.8) provides the model for how natural processes — including perception, phantasia, and emotional response — can be purposive without being consciously directed. This is critical for the dissertation's account of how phantasia mediates between perception and action in ways that are goal-directed but sub-deliberative.

### 9.2 Key Passages for the Dissertation

- **192b20–23**: Definition of φύσις — internal principle of motion/rest. Foundation for the De Anima's treatment of soul as a principle of life.
- **193b6–8**: Form is nature more than matter; actuality prior to potentiality. Connects to De Anima II.1 (soul as first actuality).
- **194b16–195b30**: The four causes. Required for understanding the causal structure of perception, phantasia, and persuasion.
- **199a12–18**: Art imitates nature / nature imitates art. Central to the τέχνη/φύσις relation in the Rhetoric.
- **199b26–30**: Purposive action without deliberation (spider, ant, swallow). Model for sub-deliberative phantasia in De Anima III.3 and Movement of Animals.
- **200a14–15**: "Necessity is in the matter, while that for the sake of which is in the definition." Grounds the hylomorphic analysis of perception as form-reception.
