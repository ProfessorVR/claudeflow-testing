| Density | Very high (three major investigations, each with aporetic-constructive structure) |

**One-sentence thesis**: Aristotle demonstrates that place (topos) is the innermost motionless boundary of what contains a body, that the void (kenon) does not exist as a separate extension because motion is explicable without it and its postulation entails absurdities regarding speed and natural locomotion, and that time (chronos) is "the number of motion in respect of before and after" -- a numbering made continuous and divided by the 'now' (nun), which functions as the boundary uniting past and future while differing in being at each moment, and whose apprehension is inseparable from the soul's capacity to count.

## 4. Main Positions

### PHYS-04-P1: Place is the innermost motionless boundary of what contains

Aristotle establishes four preliminary characteristics of place: it contains but is no part of the thing, it is neither less nor greater than the thing, it can be left behind, and it admits of the distinction of up and down (211a1--6, PDF p.47). He then eliminates three candidates: place is not form (because form is not separable from the thing and moves with it), not matter (because matter is not separable from the thing in the relevant way), and not the extension between the containing extremities (because such an extension would be portable, multiply instantiated, and of indeterminate quantity: 211b5--212a7, PDF pp.47--48). The remaining option yields the definition: "the place of a thing is the innermost motionless boundary of what contains it" (to tou periechoutas peras akineton proton, 212a20, PDF p.48). The qualifier "motionless" is critical: when the river flows past a boat, the place of the boat is the river qua motionless, since place must be capable of being left behind. This definition resolves Zeno's infinite regress (the universe is not in a place, since nothing contains it) and explains natural locomotion (elements move to their proper natural places, which have determinate powers).

### PHYS-04-P2: The void does not exist as a separate extension

The core of the refutation (chs. 8--9) proceeds through four arguments. (1) Natural locomotion requires qualitative differentiation of place (up, down); in a void all locations are undifferentiated, so natural motion is impossible (214b12--215a1, PDF p.53). (2) Speed varies with resistance of the medium; in void there is no resistance, yielding infinite or zero-time traversal -- "there is no ratio in which the void is exceeded by body, as there is no ratio of nought to a number" (215b12, PDF p.54). (3) In a void all bodies regardless of weight would move at equal speed, since only the medium differentiates speeds (216a12, PDF p.55). (4) The phenomenon of compression and rarefaction, which void-theorists cite as evidence, is better explained by qualitative transformation of a single matter (dense <-> rare as the same matter in different states), requiring no separate void (217a10, PDF p.56). Aristotle acknowledges the void-theorists' strongest case (Democritus, Leucippus) but insists their explanatory desiderata are met without positing a separate extension.

### PHYS-04-P3: Time is the number of motion in respect of before and after

The definition is reached through a careful derivation. Aristotle first shows that time is neither identical with movement nor independent of it: change is local (in the thing changed), while time is universal; yet "we perceive time and movement together" (219a3--10, PDF p.58). The key move is to identify a structural parallel: the before-and-after exists first in magnitude (spatial extension), then in movement (as the body traverses the magnitude), and finally in time (as the before-and-after of movement in so far as it is countable). Hence: "time is the number of movement in respect of the before and after" (arithmos kineseos kata to proteron kai husteron, 219b1--2, PDF p.59). Time is not movement simpliciter but movement qua admitting enumeration. This definition makes time a dependent measure: it is number in the sense of what is counted (not the number with which we count), and it requires motion as its substrate.

### PHYS-04-P4: The 'now' (nun) is always the same in substratum but different in being

The 'now' is the central structural element of time. Aristotle argues that it cannot be always the same (for then all events would be simultaneous: 218a25--30, PDF p.57) nor simply always different (for the previous 'now' cannot perish either in itself or in another: 218a10--20, PDF p.57). The resolution: the 'now' is like a point traversing a line or a body being carried along in motion -- "the 'now' is always the same in substratum but different in being" (219b12--15, PDF p.59). As substratum it is what makes time continuous (the 'now' connects past and future); as differing in its logos it is what makes time divisible and countable. "Time is both made continuous by the 'now' and divided at it" (220a5, PDF p.59). This dual character -- same qua connecting, different qua dividing -- is the temporal analogue of the geometrical point that is both the end of one line-segment and the beginning of another. [INTERP-low: This structural parallel with the point is explicitly drawn by Aristotle at 220a10.]

### PHYS-04-P5: Time and the soul -- if there is no counter, there is no countable

In a striking passage (223a21--26, PDF p.65), Aristotle raises the question whether time would exist without soul: "Whether if soul did not exist time would exist or not, is a question that may fairly be asked; for if there cannot be some one to count there cannot be anything that can be counted...if nothing but soul, or in soul reason, is qualified to count, it is impossible for there to be time unless there is soul." The qualification is significant: movement can exist without soul (as the substrate of time), but time as *number* -- that is, as counted -- requires a counter. Aristotle does not unequivocally resolve the question, leaving open whether the countable exists independently of the counter. The dissertation-critical implication: the connection between time and psyche foreshadows the De Anima's account of perception and phantasia as temporal faculties. [INTERP-medium: The link to De Anima's temporal perception is not drawn explicitly by Aristotle in this passage but is strongly implied by the shared problematic.]

## 4b. Cross-Work Connections (Dissertation-Critical)

### Physics IV <-> De Anima (Perception of Time)

The Physics IV account of time as dependent on a counting soul (223a21--26) provides the ontological framework for De Anima's treatment of the common sense (koine aisthesis) as capable of discriminating temporal succession. If time is "the number of motion in respect of before and after," and numbering requires soul, then the perceptual faculty that apprehends temporal order is not merely registering an external fact but is constitutive of the temporal ordering itself. This connects directly to De Anima III.2 (the common sense perceives that we perceive) and to On Memory's account of time-awareness as a condition of recollection.

### Physics IV <-> Rhetoric III (pro ommaton poiein)

The analysis of the 'now' as the point at which time is both continuous and divided is structurally parallel to rhetoric's capacity to make things appear "before the eyes" (pro ommaton poiein, Rhetoric III). The rhetorical 'now' -- the moment of vivid presentation -- depends on the perceiver's capacity to apprehend temporal presence, which Physics IV grounds in the structure of the nun as always-present-yet-always-different. [INTERP-high: This structural parallel is not drawn by Aristotle but is central to the dissertation's framework.]

### Physics IV <-> Physics III (Definition of Motion)

The definition of time presupposes the definition of motion from Physics III.1 (201a10): motion as "the actuality of the potential qua potential." Time is the number of this actuality in respect of before and after. The before-and-after in motion derives from the before-and-after in magnitude (spatial extension), which itself presupposes the continuity established in Physics III.

## 5. Tensions

### T1: Place has "power" (dunamis) vs. place as mere boundary

At 208b10 (PDF p.43), Aristotle asserts that "the power of place" is remarkable: each element is "carried to its own place" (fire up, earth down), suggesting place has a causal or attracting power. Yet the final definition (212a20) makes place a mere geometrical boundary -- "the innermost motionless boundary of what contains." A boundary as such is not an efficient cause. The tension between place-as-powerful (with active dunamis) and place-as-boundary (a geometrical limit of a container) is never fully resolved. Commentators since Simplicius have debated whether the "power" belongs to place itself or to the containing body whose limit place is.

### T2: Void arguments presuppose medium-dependent motion

The refutation of void relies heavily on the principle that speed is proportional to medium density (215b1--216a7, PDF pp.54--55). This is an empirical generalization that Aristotle treats as necessary. If one denies the proportionality principle (as post-Galilean physics does), the void-refutation loses much of its force. The tension is between the conceptual/logical arguments against void (the impossibility of natural directions in an undifferentiated space) and the empirical/physical arguments (speed-proportionality), which rest on contestable premises.

### T3: Time as objective (in motion) vs. time as dependent on soul

The definition of time as "the number of motion" seems to ground time in an objective physical reality. Yet 223a21--26 (PDF p.65) raises the possibility that time cannot exist without soul. Aristotle hedges: movement (the substrate of time) can exist without soul, but time *as numbered* cannot. This leaves time's ontological status ambiguous -- is it a mind-independent feature of the natural world or a product of the counting activity of psyche? The tension runs through the entire medieval and modern reception of Aristotle's theory.

### T4: The 'now' as indivisible boundary vs. the 'now' as extended present

Aristotle insists the 'now' is a boundary, not a part of time (218a6, PDF p.57) -- it is indivisible like a point. Yet temporal experience involves an apprehension of duration in the 'now' (the "heroes in Sardinia" passage at 218b21--27, PDF p.58, suggests we notice time only when we perceive change within what seemed a single 'now'). The indivisible mathematical 'now' sits in tension with the phenomenological 'now' of temporal awareness. This tension becomes critical in the dissertation's treatment of phantasia, where the temporal horizon of the perceptual present exceeds the mathematical instant.

## 9. Dissertation Integration Notes

### Why PHYS-04 is critical

Physics IV provides three elements indispensable to the dissertation:

1. **The ontology of time**. The definition of time as "number of motion in respect of before and after" establishes the framework within which all subsequent accounts of temporal experience -- perception, memory, phantasia -- must operate. The dissertation's claim that phantasia has a temporal horizon depends on this Aristotelian analysis of the 'now' as simultaneously connecting and dividing.

2. **Time and the soul**. The qualified dependence of time on soul (223a21--26) opens the conceptual space in which the De Anima's perceptual faculties become constitutive of temporal order rather than mere passive registrars of it. This is the bridge-point between physics and psychology in Aristotle's system, and it is precisely the juncture at which the dissertation's argument about rhetorical temporality intervenes.

3. **Place and the body**. The account of place as the boundary of a containing body provides the spatial complement to the temporal analysis. Together, place and time constitute the coordinates within which embodied perception and rhetorical action occur. The refutation of the void ensures that there is no empty, homogeneous space: every location is qualitatively differentiated, which has implications for the rhetorical concept of situatedness.

### Key passages for direct quotation

- "The place of a thing is the innermost motionless boundary of what contains it" (212a20, PDF p.48)
- "Time is the number of movement in respect of the before and after" (219b1--2, PDF p.59)
- "It is evident, then, that time is neither movement nor independent of movement" (219a1, PDF p.58)
- "Time is both made continuous by the 'now' and divided at it" (220a5, PDF p.59)
- "Whether if soul did not exist time would exist or not, is a question that may fairly be asked" (223a21, PDF p.65)
- "Not only do we measure the movement by the time, but also the time by the movement, because they define each other" (220b15, PDF p.61)
- "Time wastes things away...time is by its nature the cause rather of decay" (221a30--b1, PDF p.62)
