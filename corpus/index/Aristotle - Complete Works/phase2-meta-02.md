- **Unit thesis**: Book α (II) is a short methodological preface establishing three results: (1) truth is accessible collectively even if no individual grasps it fully, (2) the causes cannot be infinite in any of the four kinds, and (3) the method of inquiry must be suited to the subject matter. Book Β (III) is the "book of aporiai" — a systematic enumeration and preliminary discussion of fourteen (or fifteen) difficulties that the science of first philosophy must resolve, including: whether one or many sciences investigate the causes; whether the science treats substance alone or also its attributes; whether there are non-sensible substances; whether genera or constituents are principles; whether being and unity are substances or predicates; and whether principles are universal or individual. These aporiai set the agenda for the central books of the Metaphysics.

## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### Book α (II)

#### 2.1 Ch. 1 — Truth is collective; no one grasps it fully (993a30–993b19, PDF 20–21)

- The investigation of truth is in one way hard, in another easy: no one attains it adequately, but no one fails entirely — 993a30–b4 (PDF 20).
- Each thinker contributes something; the union of all contributions yields a considerable result — 993b2–4 (PDF 20–21).
- We should be grateful not only to those with whom we agree but also to those who expressed superficial views, for they developed our powers of thought — 993b11–19 (PDF 21).
- Philosophy should be called knowledge of the truth; the end of theoretical knowledge is truth, of practical knowledge, action — 993b19–23 (PDF 21).

#### 2.2 Ch. 2 — The causes are not infinite in series or in kind (994a1–995a20, PDF 21–22)

- The causes of things are neither infinite in series nor infinitely various in kind — 994a1–2 (PDF 21).
- **Material cause**: one thing cannot proceed from another *ad infinitum* (flesh from earth, earth from air, etc.); the series must stop — 994a1–b6 (PDF 21).
- **Final cause**: the series of "for the sake of which" cannot go on without limit; there must be a last end — 994a8–b9 (PDF 21).
- **Formal cause**: the definition cannot be referred to another definition infinitely; the original definition is always more of a definition — 994b16–20 (PDF 22).
- **Efficient cause**: the series of movers has a first member; without a first there is no cause at all — 994a5–8 (PDF 21).
- If the kinds of causes were infinite in number, knowledge would be impossible — 994b27–995a1 (PDF 22).

#### 2.3 Ch. 3 — Method must suit the subject (995a1–995b4, PDF 22–23)

- The effect lectures produce depends on the hearer's habits; some demand mathematical precision everywhere, others want examples or poetic authority — 995a1–14 (PDF 22).
- Mathematical precision is not to be demanded in all cases but only where the subject has no matter; natural science has matter, so its method differs — 995a15–20 (PDF 22–23).
- We must first inquire what nature is and what natural science studies — 995b1–4 (PDF 23).

### Book Β (III)

#### 2.4 Ch. 1 — Catalogue of aporiai (995b4–996a17, PDF 23–24)

- We must first recount the difficulties (*aporiai*) that must be discussed; surveying them in advance is like untying knots — one must see the knot before one can undo it — 995b4–18 (PDF 23).
- A person who does not know the difficulties does not know whether he has found what he is looking for — 995b14–18 (PDF 23).
- Enumeration of the principal aporiai:
  1. Does it belong to one or more sciences to investigate all the kinds of causes? (995b5–6)
  2. Does the science of substance also deal with the axioms? (995b7–10)
  3. If the science deals with substance, does one science deal with all substances? (996a1–2)
  4. Does the science treat only substances or also their attributes (same, other, similar, contrary, prior, posterior)? (995b18–27)
  5. Are there only sensible substances or also others (Forms, intermediates)? (995b14–18)
  6. Are the principles and elements the genera or the constituent parts? (998a20)
  7. If genera, is it the highest genera or the lowest? (998b14)
  8. Is there anything besides matter, i.e. a cause in itself? (999a24)
  9. Are the principles limited in number or in kind? (999b24)
  10. Are the principles of perishable and imperishable things the same or different? (1000a5)
  11. Are being and unity substances or attributes? (1001a4)
  12. Are numbers, bodies, surfaces, and points substances? (1001b26)
  13. Why must we look for Forms besides perceptible things and intermediates? (1002b12)
  14. Do the principles exist potentially or actually? (1002b32)
  15. Are the principles universals or individuals? (1003a5)

#### 2.5 Ch. 2 — Aporiai 1–5: Scope and unity of the science (996a18–997b34, PDF 24–26)

- **Aporia 1**: Does one science investigate all four causes? Arguments on both sides — the efficient cause is contrary to the final, yet knowledge of a house requires knowing all four causes — 996a18–996b1 (PDF 24–25).
- **Aporia 2**: Whether the axioms (e.g., PNC) belong to the same science as substance — 996b26–997a15 (PDF 25).
- **Aporia 3**: Whether one science treats all substances or several treat different kinds — 997a15–25 (PDF 25).
- **Aporia 4**: Whether the science investigates substances alone or also their per se attributes — 997a25–34 (PDF 25–26).
- **Aporia 5**: Whether there are non-sensible substances (Forms, mathematical intermediates); extended discussion of mathematical objects and the absurdity of intermediate heavens — 997a34–998a19 (PDF 26–27).

#### 2.6 Ch. 3 — Aporiai 6–7: Genera vs. elements as principles (998a20–999a23, PDF 28–29)

- **Aporia 6**: Whether principles are the genera under which things fall or the constituent parts (elements) into which they are divided — 998a20–b14 (PDF 28).
- **Aporia 7**: If genera are principles, whether the highest genera or the infimae species are more truly principles — 998b14–999a23 (PDF 28–29).
- Arguments for highest genera: being and unity are most universal, predicated of everything. Arguments against: neither being nor unity can be a genus (since the differentiae of any genus must have being and unity) — 998b17–999a1 (PDF 29).
- Species closer to individuals seem to have more unity; where prior and posterior exist, no genus exists apart from the species — 999a1–23 (PDF 29).

#### 2.7 Ch. 4 — Aporiai 8–9: Cause apart from matter; number of principles (999a24–1000a4, PDF 29–31)

- **Aporia 8**: Is there something apart from the concrete individual (form apart from matter)? If not, there will be no object of thought, everything will be sensible, and knowledge will be impossible. If there is something eternal, matter must exist, and form or shape must also exist — but in which cases? — 999a24–b24 (PDF 29–30).
- **Aporia 9**: Are the principles one in number or one in kind? If one in number, nothing else will exist; if one in kind, perceptible things may differ from one another in kind — 999b24–1000a4 (PDF 30–31).

#### 2.8 Ch. 4–5 — Aporiai 10–11: Perishable vs. imperishable; being and unity (1000a5–1001b25, PDF 31–33)

- **Aporia 10**: Are the principles of perishable and imperishable things the same? If the same, how do some things perish and others not? Empedocles' elements are eternal, yet strife and friendship cause destruction and generation — 1000a5–1001a3 (PDF 31–32).
- **Aporia 11**: Are being and unity substances of things (as Plato and the Pythagoreans held) or is there some other underlying nature? — 1001a4–b25 (PDF 32–33). If being or unity is a substance, then nothing else besides the One can exist (Parmenides). If they are not substances, then no universals will be substances either, since being and unity are the most universal.

#### 2.9 Chs. 5–6 — Aporiai 12–15: Mathematical objects, Forms, potentiality, universality (1001b26–1003a17, PDF 35–38)

- **Aporia 12**: Are numbers, lines, surfaces, and points substances? If not, we are baffled about what being and substance are. If they are, then the generation and destruction of bodies presents paradoxes about boundaries (when bodies come into contact their boundaries become one; when separated, they become two) — 1001b26–1002b11 (PDF 35–36).
- **Aporia 13**: Why look for Forms in addition to sensible things and intermediates? If the Forms exist and principles are one in number, nothing besides the elements will exist — 1002b12–32 (PDF 36–37).
- **Aporia 14**: Do elements exist potentially or actually? If potentially, then that which is not yet may be, and it is possible that nothing exists; if actually, the elements must be prior to the things — 1002b32–1003a5 (PDF 37).
- **Aporia 15**: Are the principles universals or individuals? If universals, they are not substances (substance is always a "this," not a "such"). If individuals, they are not knowable (all knowledge is of universals). Further, if principles are not universals, they will not be knowable, and knowledge of principles will require other universals prior to them — 1003a5–17 (PDF 37–38).

## 6. Tensions

| Tension | Node A | Node B | Type | Description |
|---------|--------|--------|------|-------------|
| One science or many for the causes? | Each cause-type seems to belong to a different science (aporia 1) | Yet the wise man must know all causes (from Book Α) | methodological | The aporiai open with a tension between the apparent specialisation of causal inquiry and the programmatic claim that wisdom is universal knowledge of causes. | Genera vs. elements as principles | Genera are the principles of definitions and of knowledge (998a20) | Elements are the constituent parts into which things are resolved (998a23) | ontological | If genera are principles, the most universal terms should be most principled; if elements, the simplest constituents. These pull in opposite directions and are not resolved until Z–H. |
| Being and unity: substance or predicate? | Plato and Pythagoreans treat being/unity as substance (1001a9) | If substance, nothing besides the One exists (Parmenides); if not, no universal is substance (1001a19) | ontological | This aporia is arguably the deepest in the book. Aristotle's solution in Γ.2 (focal meaning) attempts to preserve the universal scope of being without making it a genus. |
| Universals: knowable but not substantial | All knowledge is of universals (1003a13) | Substance is always a "this," i.e. individual (1003a9) | epistemological/ontological | Aporia 15 generates the fundamental tension between the conditions for knowledge and the conditions for substance. | Method: precision vs. appropriateness | Some demand mathematical precision in all subjects (995a3) | Natural science has matter, so its method cannot be mathematical (995a15) | methodological | Book α warns against misplaced precision but does not explain what the appropriate method for first philosophy is.
