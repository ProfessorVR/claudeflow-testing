# Phase 2 Analysis: META-05 | Being and Substance (intro)

## Section 1: Unit Metadata

- **unit_id**: META-05
- **work**: Metaphysics (*Metaphysica*)
- **books**: E (VI) + Z (VII) i-iii
- **bekker_range**: 1025a-1029b
- **pdf_pages**: 70-77 (confirmed: 1025a visible as end of Book V/Delta ch. 30 transitioning to Book VI ch. 1 on PDF p.70; 1029b visible on PDF p.77 within Book VII ch. 3)
- **translator**: W. D. Ross
- **tier**: critical
- **dissertation_critical**: Yes -- substance, essence, the division of theoretical sciences
- **cluster**: first_philosophy
- **unit_thesis**: This unit accomplishes two preparatory tasks for the central substance books. First, Book VI (E) classifies the theoretical sciences into physics, mathematics, and theology/first philosophy, identifies the multiple senses of "being" (accidental, as truth/falsity, the categories, and potentiality/actuality), and dismisses accidental being and being-as-truth as not proper subjects of first philosophy. Second, Book VII (Z) chapters 1-3 open the investigation of substance proper by establishing its primacy among the categories (in formula, in order of knowledge, and in time), surveying candidate substances (sensible bodies, Platonic Forms, mathematical objects), and distinguishing four candidates for what constitutes substance: essence (to ti en einai), universal, genus, and substratum -- with substratum further analyzed into matter, shape (morphe), and the compound of both. The unit thus frames the central question of the Metaphysics: what is substance, and what is the substance of sensible things?

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 The theoretical sciences and first philosophy (VI.1, 1025b-1026a)

- Every science which is ratiocinative or involves reasoning deals with causes and principles, exact or indeterminate; but all such sciences mark off some particular being and inquire into its essence, without discussing being simply *qua* being (1025b3-10, PDF p.70).
- Natural science deals with that sort of substance which has the principle of its movement and rest present in itself; it is neither practical nor productive but theoretical (1025b18-22, PDF p.71).
- Since natural science deals with things inseparable from matter and not immovable, and mathematics deals with things immovable but probably not separable (embodied in matter), there must be a science prior to both if there is something eternal, immovable, and separable -- this is theology or first philosophy (1026a10-23, PDF p.71).
- The three theoretical philosophies are: mathematics, natural science, and theology; the highest science must deal with the highest genus (1026a18-22, PDF p.71).
- First philosophy is universal *because* it is first: if there is an immovable substance, the science of this must be prior, first, and universal in this way (1026a23-32, PDF p.71).

### 2.2 The senses of "being" and dismissal of accidental being (VI.2-3, 1026a33-1027b14)

- "Being" has several meanings: (1) the accidental, (2) being as truth (non-being as falsity), (3) the figures of predication (the categories: what, quality, quantity, place, time, etc.), and (4) potentiality and actuality (1026a33-1026b4, PDF p.71).
- Accidental being must be dismissed first: there is no science of the accidental, for the accidental is practically a mere name (1026b3-5, PDF p.72).
- Plato was in a sense not wrong in saying that sophistic deals with the accidental and what is not (1026b15, PDF p.72).
- Among things that are, some are always and of necessity, some for the most part; what is neither always nor for the most part is the accidental (1026b27-33, PDF p.72).
- The cause of the accidental is matter -- that which is capable of being otherwise than as it is for the most part (1027a13-15, PDF p.73).
- There are principles and causes which are generable and destructible without being generated or destroyed; otherwise all things will be of necessity (1027a29-32, PDF p.73).

### 2.3 Dismissal of being as truth/falsity (VI.4, 1027b17-1028a6)

- That which *is* in the sense of being true, or *is not* in the sense of being false, depends on combination and separation; truth and falsehood together are concerned with the apportionment of a contradiction (1027b18-23, PDF p.73).
- Falsity and truth are not in things (the good is not true, the bad false) but in thought; with regard to simple things and essences, falsity and truth do not exist even in thought (1027b25-28, PDF p.74).
- Since the combination and the separation are in thought and not in the things, being-as-truth is a different sort of being from being in the full sense; it must be dismissed as not indicating any separate class of being (1028a1-4, PDF p.74).
- We must therefore consider the causes and principles of being itself, *qua* being (1028a4-6, PDF p.74).

### 2.4 The primacy of substance among the categories (VII.1, 1028a10-1028b7)

- "Being" is said in several senses: it means what a thing is (the "this"), or quality, quantity, and the other categories. Of all these senses, that which primarily *is* -- not "is" in a qualified sense but *is* simply -- is the "what," which indicates substance (1028a10-18, PDF p.74).
- All other things are said to be because they are quantities, qualities, affections, or other determinations of substance; none of the other categories can exist independently, but only substance (1028a18-31, PDF p.74-75).
- Substance is primary in every sense: in formula (the formula of substance must be present in the formula of each of the other categories), in order of knowledge (we know a thing most fully when we know *what* it is), and in time (none of the other categories is separable, only substance) (1028a31-1028b2, PDF p.75).
- The question what being is reduces to the question what substance is (1028b2-7, PDF p.75).

### 2.5 Survey of candidate substances (VII.2, 1028b8-1029a33)

- Substance is thought to belong most obviously to bodies: animals, plants, their parts, natural bodies (fire, water, earth), the heavens and their parts (stars, moon, sun) (1028b8-13, PDF p.75).
- Whether these alone are substances, or there are also others, or only some of these, or some of these and some others, must be considered (1028b13-15, PDF p.75).
- Some think the limits of body (surface, line, point, unit) are substances, and more so than body or the solid (1028b15-18, PDF p.75).
- Others think there are eternal substances besides sensible things which are more in number and more real: Plato posited two kinds (Forms and mathematical objects), as well as the substance of sensible bodies as a third kind; Speusippus made still more kinds, beginning with the One (1028b18-27, PDF p.75).
- The questions: which of the common statements are right, what things are substances, whether there are any besides sensible substances, and whether there is a separable substance (1028b27-32, PDF p.75).
- We must first sketch the nature of substance (1028b33, PDF p.75).

### 2.6 Four candidates for substance: essence, universal, genus, substratum (VII.3, 1028b33-1029b12)

- "Substance" is applied in at least four main senses: (1) the essence (to ti en einai), (2) the universal, (3) the genus, and (4) the substratum (to hupokeimenon) (1028b33-1029a2, PDF p.75-76).
- The substratum is that of which other things are predicated, while it is itself not predicated of anything else; it is thought to be in the truest sense substance (1029a2-5, PDF p.76).
- Matter is said to be the substratum in one sense, shape (morphe) in another, and the compound of these in a third (1029a2-7, PDF p.76).
- If matter alone is substance, then all else is stripped away and nothing remains but matter -- neither a particular thing nor positively characterized; it is neither a particular quantity nor assigned to any of the categories by which being is determined (1029a10-24, PDF p.76).
- But separability and "this-ness" (tode ti) are thought to belong chiefly to substance; therefore form and the compound of form and matter would be thought to be substance rather than matter (1029a27-30, PDF p.76).
- The compound of matter and shape may be dismissed as posterior and obvious; matter also is in a sense manifest. We must inquire into the third kind -- form -- for this is the most difficult (1029a30-34, PDF p.76).
- There are some substances among sensible things; we must look first among these, advancing from what is more intelligible to us toward what is more intelligible by nature (1029b1-8, PDF p.76-77).
- We must investigate essence, since the essence was thought to be substance (1029b1-12, PDF p.77).

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **First philosophy as both particular and universal (VI.1)**: Aristotle claims that first philosophy is universal *because* it is first -- it deals with one particular kind of substance (the immovable, eternal) and this priority makes it universal. But this raises the tension between particular and universal science: how can a science that investigates one genus (immovable substance) simultaneously investigate being qua being across all genera? The relationship between theology and ontology is left programmatic. [INTERP-high] -- Linked to: META-05-P1, META-03 (being qua being in Book Gamma).

2. **The dismissal of being-as-truth and the status of simple essences (VI.4)**: Aristotle dismisses being-as-truth because truth is in thought, not in things. But he notes that "with regard to simple things and essences, falsity and truth do not exist even in thought" (1027b27). This cryptic remark raises the question of how we apprehend simple essences if not by combination/separation. The doctrine of nous as direct intellectual apprehension (cf. De Anima III.6) is relevant but not invoked here. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: META-05-P3, DA-04.

3. **Matter as cause of the accidental vs. matter as substratum of substance (VI.2 vs. VII.3)**: In Book VI, matter is identified as the cause of the accidental -- "that which is capable of being otherwise" (1027a13). In Book VII.3, matter is the ultimate substratum reached by stripping away all predicates. These two roles pull in different directions: matter-as-accident-cause suggests indeterminacy and contingency, while matter-as-substratum suggests a persisting substrate. Whether these are the same concept of matter, or whether "matter" is said in several senses, is a question the unit does not resolve. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: META-05-P2, META-05-P5.

4. **Separability and tode ti as criteria for substance (VII.3)**: Aristotle argues that separability and "this-ness" (tode ti) belong chiefly to substance, and this disqualifies matter. But form as discussed in this unit has not yet been shown to be separable or to be a "this." Whether form satisfies these criteria -- or whether only the compound does -- is precisely what the subsequent chapters of Zeta must establish. The criteria are invoked before being grounded. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: META-05-P5, META-06 (Zeta iv-xvii).

5. **The compound dismissed as "posterior" (VII.3)**: Aristotle dismisses the compound of matter and form as posterior and obvious (1029a30-31), choosing to investigate form as the most difficult candidate. But the concrete composite substance (this man, this horse) is the paradigm of substance in the Categories. The relationship between the priority of form (Metaphysics VII) and the priority of the individual composite (Categories) is a notorious interpretive problem. [INTERP-high] -- Linked to: META-05-P5, META-01 (Categories tradition).

## Section 10: Dissertation Relevance

This unit is critical for the dissertation's ontological foundation in three respects:

1. **The division of the senses of being** (VI.2) provides the map within which the dissertation's account of substance, actuality/potentiality, and the categories must be situated. The dismissal of accidental being and being-as-truth narrows the field to categorial being and potentiality/actuality as the proper subjects of ontological inquiry.

2. **The primacy of substance** (VII.1) establishes the thesis that all other categories depend on substance. For the dissertation's treatment of rhetorical ontology, this means that any account of how rhetoric engages with being must ultimately be grounded in an account of substance -- not quality, not quantity, not relation, but the "what" of things.

3. **The fourfold analysis of substance candidates** (VII.3) -- essence, universal, genus, substratum -- frames the central question that the dissertation must engage: what makes something the thing that it is? The privileging of form over matter as the primary candidate for substance, and the criteria of separability and tode ti, will be essential for understanding how Aristotle's ontology connects to his psychology of perception (De Anima) and his rhetoric of "setting before the eyes" (Rhetoric III). The form/matter distinction in substance theory maps onto the form/matter distinction in perception (receiving the form without the matter) and in rhetoric (making the audience "see" the form of what is described).
