# Phase 2 Analysis: META-08 — Metaphysics Ι (X) + Κ (XI)

## Section 1: Unit Metadata

- **unit_id**: META-08
- **tier**: Standard
- **work**: Metaphysics (*Metaphysica*)
- **books**: Ι (X) + Κ (XI)
- **bekker_range**: 1052a–1069a
- **PDF pages**: ~116–142 (formula: (Bekker−980)×1.566+3; verified against visible Bekker margins)
- **translator**: W. D. Ross (rev. J. Barnes)
- **cluster**: first_philosophy
- **unit_thesis**: Book X (Ι) investigates unity (*to hen*) and its correlative plurality (*to polla*), arguing that unity is not a substance but is predicated in as many ways as being, that the one and the many are opposed as indivisible to divisible, that contrariety is the greatest difference within a genus and is equivalent to complete privation, that intermediates are composed of contraries, that things other in species are contraries within the same genus differentiated by formula rather than matter, and that perishable and imperishable things must differ in kind (not merely by accident), thereby ruling out Platonic Forms as common to both. Book XI (Κ) is a compressed recapitulation of arguments from Books III (Β), IV (Γ), and VI (Ε) of the Metaphysics together with Physics II–V, rehearsing the scope of first philosophy as the science of being *qua* being, defending the principle of non-contradiction against Protagorean relativism, distinguishing the three theoretical sciences (natural science, mathematics, theology), defining movement as the actuality of the potential *qua* potential, and treating the infinite, change, and the continuous.

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Book X (Ι), chs. 1–2: The senses of unity and the one as measure (1052a15–1054a19)

- Unity is said in many ways; the primary sense is the measure, especially in quantity. The measure is that by which quantity is known; in number it is the unit, in length the foot, etc. The measure is always homogeneous with the measured. 1052a15–1052b19 (PDF 116–117)
- The one is not itself a substance: in colours the one is a colour (e.g., white), in music a tone; the one in each genus is a definite thing, and in no case is its nature just to be one. As in colours the one is sought among the colours themselves, so in substance the one is a substance. 1052b19–1053a30 (PDF 117–118)
- Unity means the same as being in a sense: it follows the categories in as many ways, is not comprised within any category, and is related to them as being is. To be one is to be a particular thing. 1053b9–1054a19 (PDF 118–119)

### 2.2 Ι.3: The one and the many; sameness, likeness, otherness (1054a20–1055a2)

- The one and the many are opposed in several ways: the one as indivisible, plurality as divisible. That which is indivisible or not divided is called one. 1054a20–32 (PDF 119)
- "The same" has several meanings: numerically, formally, materially. "Like" = same in form but not absolutely the same; "other" and "unlike" are their opposites. The other is not the same as the different: for the other, there must be a common genus or species in respect of which things differ. 1054a32–1055a2 (PDF 119–120)

### 2.3 Ι.4: Contrariety as the greatest difference (1055a3–1055b29)

- Contrariety is the greatest difference. Things that differ in genus have no way to one another and are too distant to be comparable; contrariety is the greatest difference within a genus. 1055a3–16 (PDF 120)
- That which is greatest in each class is complete; contrariety is complete difference, and complete difference is the greatest. The primary contrariety is between state (*hexis*) and privation (*sterēsis*) — not every privation, but complete privation. 1055a16–b29 (PDF 120–121)

### 2.4 Ι.5–6: The one vs. the many, the equal vs. the great and the small (1055b30–1057a17)

- How the one is opposed to the many and the equal to the great and the small. The equal is contrary to the *unequal*; the equal is intermediate between the great and the small, being a privative negation of both (neither great nor small, yet naturally fitted to be either). 1055b30–1056b2 (PDF 121–122)
- One and plurality are opposed as measure to thing measurable. These are opposed as relatives, not as contraries. Number is the first plurality; plurality is measurable by one. One and number are in a sense opposed, not as contrary but as relative terms. 1056b3–1057a17 (PDF 122–123)

### 2.5 Ι.7–8: Intermediates and contrariety in species (1057a18–1058b25)

- Intermediates are in the same genus as the contraries between which they stand; they are composed of the contraries. For all intermediates must be between opposites of some kind, and only contraries (not contradictories or relatives) admit of a middle term. 1057a18–b34 (PDF 123–124)
- Contraries do not involve one another in their composition; intermediates are all incomposite or all composite. Something is compounded out of the contraries, so there is a change from contrary to intermediate sooner than to the other contrary. All intermediates are compounded out of the primary contraries. 1057a18–1058a7 (PDF 123–125)
- That which is other in species is other in something belonging to both, i.e., the genus. Difference in species is a contrariety: "I give the name of 'difference in the genus' to an otherness which makes the genus itself other" — i.e., contrariety *is* complete difference, and this belongs only to things in the same genus. 1058a7–b25 (PDF 125–126)

### 2.6 Ι.9–10: Matter, formula, and the perishable vs. the imperishable (1058a29–1059a14)

- Why woman does not differ from man in species though female and male are contrary: the contrariety is in the matter, not in the formula. Contrarieties in the formula make a difference in species; those in the compound material thing do not. Whiteness or blackness does not make a species-difference; male and female are modifications peculiar to the animal, but they belong to the matter, not to the substance. 1058a29–1058b26 (PDF 126–127)
- The perishable and the imperishable must be different in kind: perishableness and imperishableness are attributes of necessity (not by accident), so the characteristics in virtue of which one thing is perishable and another imperishable are opposite. Therefore there cannot be Forms if Forms are the same in form with the individuals; things which differ in kind are further apart than those which differ in form. 1058b26–1059a14 (PDF 127)

### 2.7 Book XI (Κ), chs. 1–6: Recapitulation of aporiai, being *qua* being, non-contradiction, Protagoras (1059a18–1063b35)

- Whether Wisdom is one science or several; whether it deals with substances only or also with their accidents; whether being and unity are genera or principles; whether there is a separable substance besides the sensible. 1059a18–1060b30 (PDF 127–129)
- The science of the philosopher: being *qua* being. "Being" has many senses but is used in virtue of one common nature (the *pros hen* structure). The philosopher studies the first differences and contrarieties of being: plurality, unity, likeness, unlikeness, etc. One science covers all contraries that fall under being and unity. 1060b31–1061b17 (PDF 129–130)
- The mathematician and the philosopher: the mathematician studies *qua* quantitative and continuous, the philosopher studies each subject in relation to being *qua* being. Both natural science and mathematics are *parts* of Wisdom. 1061b17–1061b33 (PDF 130–131)
- The principle of non-contradiction: the same thing cannot be and not be at the same time in the same respect. This cannot be demonstrated from a more certain principle, but there is proof *ad hominem*: whoever signifies something must signify one definite thing, and cannot truly assert the contradictory. 1061b34–1062b11 (PDF 131–132)
- Refutation of Protagoras: "man is the measure of all things" reduces to the claim that what appears to each person is true. But the same thing appears sweet to some and bitter to others; the difficulty arises from the natural philosophers' doctrine that everything comes from what is not. Aristotle replies: coming-to-be is from what is not *actually* but is *potentially*; heavenly bodies do not change, so they supply a fixed basis for truth; contradictory statements cannot be true of the same subject at the same time. 1062b12–1063b35 (PDF 132–134)
- **Motion-as-passage formulation** (1063a19–21): Within the Protagoras-refutation, Aristotle articulates the topological "from-something-into-something" structure of motion: "if there is movement, there is also something moved, and everything is moved out of something and into something; it follows that that which is moved must first be in that out of which it is to be moved, and then not be in it, and move into the other and come to be in it" (Ross 1908 trans., verified at MIT Classics Archive `classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/metaphysics.11.xi.html`). This passage is the Kappa-internal anchor for the "motion-as-medium-of-passage" thesis the dissertation §1.3 develops; the verbatim is distinct from (and complementary to) the *energeia-of-potential-qua-potential* recapitulation at Κ.9, 1065b16–23 listed at §2.8 below. ### 2.8 Κ.7–12: The three theoretical sciences, accidental being, movement, the infinite, change, the continuous (1063b36–1069a14)

- Three theoretical sciences: natural science (deals with things having a principle of movement in themselves), mathematics (theoretical, subjects cannot exist apart), theology (deals with what is separable and unmovable; this is the first and most important principle, and theology is the best of the theoretical sciences). 1064a10–b14 (PDF 134–135)
- Accidental being: there is no science of the accidental; the accidental is what occurs neither always nor for the most part; nothing is by accident necessary; chance and spontaneity are causes by accident. 1064b15–1065b4 (PDF 135–136)
- Movement (*kinēsis*) defined: the actuality of the potential as such. Movement is in the movable; it is the fulfillment of that which is potentially, when it is fulfilled and actual, not *qua* itself but *qua* movable. Movement is evidently in the movable, for it is the fulfillment of this by that which is capable of causing movement. The actuality of both mover and moved is one (the same interval from one to two and from two to one). 1065b5–1066a34 (PDF 136–137)
- The infinite: not a separate, independent thing; neither a spatial magnitude nor a plurality; the infinite cannot be among sensible things (if the formula of body is "that which is bounded by planes," there cannot be an infinite body); the infinite is not an actual magnitude but exists only potentially, by successive division or addition. 1066a35–1067a37 (PDF 137–139)
- Change: three kinds of movement (in quality, quantity, and place); there is no movement of substance (because there is nothing contrary to substance), nor of relation, nor of acting and being acted on. That which changes goes from subject to subject, from subject to non-subject (destruction), or from non-subject to subject (generation); change from non-subject to non-subject is not a change. Every movement is from something and to something; the thing that is moved is different from both. 1067b1–1068b25 (PDF 139–141)
- The continuous: the successive, the contiguous, and the continuous defined. The successive has nothing of the same class between; the contiguous = successive things whose extremes touch; the continuous = whose extremes are one. Since all change is between opposites, and change between contradictories has no intermediate, the between must be between contraries. 1068b26–1069a14 (PDF 141–142)

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **The authenticity of Book XI (Κ)**: The near-verbatim overlap between Κ.1–6 and Books Β/Γ, and between Κ.7–12 and Physics II–V, has led most scholars since Jaeger to question whether Κ is an authentic Aristotelian text or a student compilation. Ross himself notes the inferior quality of the recapitulation. If Κ is spurious (or an early draft), its doctrinal authority for the definition of movement and the scope of first philosophy is diminished relative to the parallel passages in Physics III and Metaphysics Γ. [INTERP-medium]

2. **Unity and being: identical or merely coextensive?** Book Ι argues that unity follows the categories in as many ways as being, and that "to be one is just to be a particular thing" (1054a13–19). But Aristotle does not straightforwardly assert that unity and being are the same notion; he says they "mean the same" in a sense. Whether the convertibility thesis (every being is one and every one is a being) amounts to strict identity or only extensional equivalence remains contested. This parallels the Γ.2 (1003b22–34) discussion. [INTERP-medium] — Linked to: META-08-P1.

3. **Contrariety and privation: which is prior?** Aristotle argues that the primary contrariety is state vs. privation (1055a33), yet he also treats contrariety as a kind of difference (1055a3–16) and privation as a kind of contradiction (1055b9–16). The relationships among contradiction, privation, and contrariety are not fully linearized: contrariety is said to be *complete* privation, but privation itself comes in degrees and kinds. The precise ordering remains underspecified. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: META-08-P2.

4. **The perishable/imperishable argument and hylomorphism**: The Ι.10 argument that perishable and imperishable must differ in kind (1058b26–1059a14) seems to commit Aristotle to a strong claim that there can be no common genus for corruptible and incorruptible substances. But Metaphysics Λ.1 (1069a30) classifies all three kinds of substance (eternal sensible, perishable sensible, immovable) under the single inquiry of first philosophy. Whether these share a genus "analogically" or whether the Ι.10 argument applies only to Forms is a point of tension. [INTERP-medium] — Linked to: META-08-P4, META-09 (Λ).

5. **The recapitulated definition of movement and Θ.6**: Κ.9 (1065b5–1066a34) reproduces the Physics III definition of movement as "the actuality of the potential *qua* potential," but the Θ.6 analysis of the kinēsis/energeia distinction (META-07) refines what "actuality" means and distinguishes complete actualities from incomplete movements. Κ never integrates the Θ.6 refinement, using the Physics III formula without the tense-test criterion. This reinforces the suspicion that Κ draws on an earlier stratum of Aristotle's thought that predates the full Θ analysis. [INTERP-low]
