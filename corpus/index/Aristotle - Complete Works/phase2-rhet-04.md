**One-sentence thesis**: Aristotle completes his account of the means of investing speeches with moral character by cataloguing the traits of youth, old age, the prime of life, and the effects of birth, wealth, and power on character, then transitions to the argumentative resources common to all oratory -- the topics of possibility and impossibility, past and future fact, and greatness and smallness -- before providing a comprehensive taxonomy of the enthymeme (including its relation to example and maxim), enumerating twenty-eight lines of probative enthymemes, ten lines of apparent (spurious) enthymemes, and the methods for refuting them.

```
Book II (continued)
├─ II.12  Character of Youth (ἤθη νέων)
│   ├─ Transition: character types in relation to emotions, states, ages, fortunes
│   │   ├─ Emotions: anger, desire, etc. (covered in II.2--11)
│   │   ├─ States of character: excellences and vices
│   │   ├─ Ages: youth, prime, old age
│   │   └─ Fortunes: birth, wealth, power
│   ├─ Young men have strong passions, indiscriminate desires
│   │   ├─ Sexual desire predominant among bodily desires
│   │   ├─ Changeable, fickle, quickly satisfied
│   │   └─ Impulses keen but not deep-rooted
│   ├─ Hot-tempered, quick-tempered, apt to give way to anger
│   │   └─ Love of honour makes them unable to bear slighting
│   ├─ Love victory and superiority over others
│   │   └─ Love honour more than money (not yet learned value of money)
│   ├─ Trust others readily (not yet been cheated)
│   │   └─ Sanguine: nature warms blood as with excess of wine
│   ├─ Live mainly in expectation, not memory
│   │   ├─ Youth has long future, short past
│   │   └─ Hope concerns future; memory concerns past
│   ├─ Easily cheated owing to sanguine and hopeful disposition
│   ├─ More courageous than older men (hot temper + hopeful disposition)
│   ├─ Shy, accepting conventional standards of honour
│   ├─ Exalted notions: hopeful disposition + not yet humbled by life
│   ├─ Prefer noble deeds to useful ones
│   │   └─ Lives regulated by character rather than reasoning
│   └─ Fond of friends and companions (enjoy company)
│       └─ All mistakes in direction of excess and vehemence
│           └─ Disobey Chilon's precept: they overdo everything
│
├─ II.13  Character of Old Age (ἤθη γερόντων)
│   ├─ Elderly = contrary of youth in most respects
│   ├─ Sure about nothing, under-do everything
│   │   ├─ Think but never know; add "possibly" or "perhaps"
│   │   └─ Cynical: put worse construction on everything
│   ├─ Distrustful: experience makes them suspicious
│   ├─ Small-minded: humbled by life
│   │   └─ Desires set on what helps them keep alive
│   ├─ Not generous: money is necessities, experience taught how hard to get
│   ├─ Cowardly: unlike hot-blooded young, old age paves way for cowardice
│   │   └─ Fear is a form of chill
│   ├─ Love life: desire strongest for what we lack
│   ├─ Too fond of themselves: small-mindedness
│   ├─ Guide lives by what is useful, not what is noble
│   │   └─ Useful = what is good for oneself; noble = what is good absolutely
│   ├─ Not shy but shameless: care less for noble than useful
│   ├─ Lack confidence in future (experience of failure)
│   ├─ Live by memory rather than hope
│   │   └─ Continually talking of the past (enjoy remembering)
│   ├─ Fits of anger sudden but feeble
│   ├─ Sensual passions gone or lost vigour
│   │   └─ Actions inspired by gain, not passion
│   ├─ Self-controlled by default (passions slackened)
│   ├─ Guide lives by reasoning → utility and excellence
│   └─ Querulous, not disposed to laughter
│       └─ Love of laughter = opposite of querulousness
│
├─ II.14  Character of Men in Their Prime (ἀκμή)
│   ├─ Character between youth and old age, free from extremes
│   │   ├─ Neither excess of confidence (rashness) nor too much timidity
│   │   ├─ Neither trust nor distrust everybody -- judge correctly
│   │   └─ Guided not by sole consideration of noble or useful, but both
│   ├─ Neither parsimonious nor prodigal: guided by what is fit and proper
│   ├─ Brave as well as temperate; temperate as well as brave
│   │   └─ In youth/age these virtues divided; in prime united
│   ├─ Excesses/defects replaced by moderation and fitness
│   └─ Body in prime: 30--35; mind about 49
│
├─ II.15  Character Produced by Good Birth (εὐγένεια)
│   ├─ Good birth makes men more ambitious
│   │   └─ Good birth = ancestral distinction; adds to pile of inherited merit
│   ├─ Well-born look down on equals who are as good as ancestors
│   │   └─ Far-off distinction greater than near distinction
│   ├─ Distinguished from nobility (coming of fine stock)
│   │   └─ Nobility = being true to family nature (quality not usually in well-born)
│   ├─ Stocks vary: good yield → exceptional men → decadence
│   │   └─ Clever stock → insane (Alcibiades); steady stock → fatuous (Cimon, Pericles, Socrates)
│   └─ Fortune of birth = character effects mostly obvious
│
├─ II.16  Character Produced by Wealth (πλοῦτος)
│   ├─ Wealthy men: insolent and arrogant
│   │   └─ Wealth becomes standard of value for everything
│   ├─ Luxurious, ostentatious, vulgar
│   │   └─ Minds regularly occupied with object of admiration
│   ├─ Saying of Simonides to Hiero's wife
│   │   └─ "I see the wise spending their days at rich men's doors"
│   ├─ Consider themselves worthy to hold office
│   ├─ Character of prosperous fool
│   │   ├─ Newly-enriched worse than old rich (no education in riches)
│   │   └─ Wrongs spring from insolence or self-indulgence
│   └─ 1391a1 (PDF 68)
│
├─ II.17  Character Produced by Power (δύναμις)
│   ├─ Shares elements with wealthy type but more ambitious and manly
│   ├─ Those in power aspire to great deeds (power permits)
│   ├─ Responsibility makes them more serious
│   ├─ Dignified rather than arrogant
│   │   └─ Dignity = mild and becoming form of arrogance
│   └─ Wrong others on small scale, not great
│
│   Good fortune: produces character types = more or less the kinds just described
│   └─ Good fortune makes men supercilious and reckless
│       └─ One excellent quality: piety and respect for divine power
│
├─ II.18  Transition to Common Topics (κοινοὶ τόποι)
│   ├─ [[Bracketed passage: persuasive speech leads to decisions]]
│   ├─ Character-investment of speeches now complete
│   ├─ Programme: arguments common to ALL oratory
│   │   ├─ Possible and impossible
│   │   ├─ Past fact (has happened / has not happened)
│   │   ├─ Future fact (will happen / will not happen)
│   │   └─ Greatness and smallness (amplification / depreciation)
│   ├─ Assignment of commonplaces to speech types
│   │   ├─ Amplification → most appropriate to epideictic
│   │   ├─ Past fact → forensic (always about the past)
│   │   └─ Possibility and future → deliberative
│   └─ General principles of enthymeme and example to follow
│
├─ II.19  Possible and Impossible; Past Fact; Future Fact; Greatness
│   ├─ Possible and impossible
│   │   ├─ If one of a pair of contraries can be/happen, so can the other
│   │   ├─ If harder of two things is possible, so is the easier
│   │   ├─ If a thing can come into existence in good/beautiful form, it can exist generally
│   │   ├─ If beginning is possible, so is the end (and vice versa)
│   │   ├─ If posterior in generation can come into being, so can the prior
│   │   ├─ Things whose love or desire is natural are possible
│   │   ├─ Where parts are possible, whole is possible (and vice versa)
│   │   ├─ If genus can occur, so can species (and vice versa)
│   │   └─ If a thing can be produced without art, it can be produced more certainly with art
│   │       └─ Agathon: "Some things we by art must needs attain, / Others by destiny or luck we gain"
│   ├─ Impossibility: by contraries of the above
│   ├─ Past fact
│   │   ├─ If less likely of two things occurred, more likely must have too
│   │   ├─ If one thing usually follows another and it happened, the other happened too
│   │   ├─ If a man had power and wish, he has done it
│   │   └─ If intention + nothing external prevented + anger/desire, he has done it
│   ├─ Future fact
│   │   ├─ Clear from same considerations as past fact
│   │   ├─ Power + wish + craving/anger/calculation → will be done
│   │   ├─ If another thing that naturally precedes has happened, the thing itself likely to follow
│   │   └─ If means to end have occurred, end likely to occur
│   ├─ Greatness and smallness
│   │   └─ Already covered in deliberative oratory (I.7) -- same principles apply
│   └─ These topics common to all three branches of oratory
│
├─ II.20  Example (παράδειγμα) and Enthymeme (ἐνθύμημα)
│   ├─ Special forms common to all oratory: example and enthymeme
│   │   └─ Maxim is part of enthymeme
│   ├─ Example: nature of induction; two varieties
│   │   ├─ (a) Mention of actual past facts (historical parallels)
│   │   │   └─ E.g., "Darius/Xerxes crossed after seizing Egypt; therefore present king will too"
│   │   └─ (b) Invention of facts by the speaker
│   │       ├─ (i) Illustrative parallels (Socrates: choosing officials by lot)
│   │       └─ (ii) Fables (Aesop, Stesichorus)
│   │           ├─ Stesichorus: fable of horse and stag (against Phalaris)
│   │           └─ Aesop: fox and hedgehog (at Samos)
│   ├─ Fables: suitable for popular assemblies
│   │   ├─ Easy to invent; hard to find historical parallels
│   │   └─ Political speaker: parallels from history more valuable
│   ├─ Relationship of example to enthymeme
│   │   ├─ If we cannot argue by enthymeme → demonstrate by example
│   │   ├─ If we can argue by enthymeme → use examples as supplementary evidence
│   │   ├─ Examples after enthymemes = witnesses giving evidence
│   │   └─ Examples before enthymemes = inductive air (rarely suits speech-making)
│   └─ Many examples needed if first; one sufficient if following enthymeme
│
├─ II.21  Maxims (γνῶμαι)
│   ├─ Definition: statement not about particular facts but about practical conduct
│   │   ├─ Not about any subject (e.g., "straight is contrary of curved" = not maxim)
│   │   └─ About courses of conduct to be chosen or avoided
│   ├─ Maxim = premiss or conclusion of enthymeme with syllogistic element removed
│   │   └─ E.g., "Never should any man whose wits are sound / Have his sons taught more wisdom than their fellows"
│   │       └─ Add reason → enthymeme: "It makes them idle; and therewith they earn / Ill-will and jealousy"
│   ├─ Four kinds of maxims
│   │   ├─ (1) Paradoxical or disputable → need supplement (reason)
│   │   ├─ (2) Nothing paradoxical → no supplement needed
│   │   ├─ (3) Have supplement attached → are parts of enthymemes
│   │   └─ (4) Have essential character of enthymemes but reason implied
│   │       └─ E.g., "O mortal man, nurse not immortal wrath"
│   ├─ Use of maxims
│   │   ├─ Appropriate to elderly and experienced (not young men)
│   │   │   └─ Young man using maxims = "unbecoming" (like telling stories in inexperienced matters)
│   │   ├─ Most appropriate when working up feelings of horror/indignation
│   │   └─ Even hackneyed/commonplace maxims useful if they suit purpose
│   ├─ Advantages of maxims
│   │   ├─ (1) Want of intelligence in hearers: they love hearing general truths they already hold
│   │   ├─ (2) Invest speech with moral character
│   │   │   └─ Speech with maxims displays general declaration of moral preferences
│   │   └─ Proverbs count as maxims (e.g., "Know thyself", "Nothing in excess")
│   └─ Contradicting maxims appropriate when: raising hearers' opinion, conveying character
│
├─ II.22  Enthymemes: General Principles (ἐνθυμήματα)
│   ├─ Enthymeme = deduction, and in that sense a deduction of dialectic
│   │   └─ Differences between enthymeme and dialectical deduction noted
│   ├─ Do not carry reasoning too far back or too long
│   │   └─ Simplicity more effective: uneducated "charm the crowd's ears more finely"
│   ├─ Start from commonly held opinions (ἔνδοξα), not from any opinion
│   │   └─ Must be held by judges or by persons whose authority judges accept
│   ├─ First principle of selection: commonplaces (what the subject is about)
│   │   ├─ Common = true of many subjects alike (Achilles = human being)
│   │   └─ Special = true of that subject alone (Achilles slew Hector)
│   ├─ Elements of enthymemes = commonplaces (τόποι)
│   ├─ Two kinds of enthymemes
│   │   ├─ (1) Probative (demonstrative): inference from accepted to unaccepted
│   │   └─ (2) Refutative: inference to what is unaccepted (by opponent)
│   │       └─ Difference parallels refutation vs. deduction in dialectic
│   └─ For special subjects: must select propositions about good/evil, noble/base, just/unjust + character/emotions/fortunes
│
├─ II.23  Twenty-Eight Lines of Enthymeme (τόποι ἐνθυμημάτων)
│   ├─ (1) From opposites: if opposite has opposite quality, so does the thing
│   │   └─ E.g., "Temperance is beneficial; for licentiousness is hurtful"
│   ├─ (2) From modification of the key-word
│   │   └─ E.g., "just" does not always mean "beneficially"
│   ├─ (3) From correlative ideas
│   │   └─ If one gave noble/just treatment, other must have received it
│   ├─ (4) A fortiori
│   │   └─ "If even the gods are not omniscient, certainly human beings are not"
│   ├─ (5) From considerations of time
│   │   └─ E.g., Iphicrates: "if before doing the deed I had bargained..."
│   ├─ (6) Turning opponent's words against him
│   │   └─ E.g., Iphicrates' reply to Aristophon about bribery
│   ├─ (7) From definition
│   │   └─ E.g., "What is the supernatural? Surely either a god or the work of a god"
│   ├─ (8) From various senses of a word
│   │   └─ As explained in Topics: "sharp" in various senses
│   ├─ (9) From logical division
│   │   └─ "All men do wrong from one of three motives, A, B, or C"
│   ├─ (10) From induction
│   │   └─ E.g., women everywhere can settle facts about children
│   ├─ (11) From a previous decision (authority)
│   │   └─ If everyone/most/wise/judges have decided so
│   ├─ (12) From parts to whole (or whole to parts)
│   │   └─ E.g., in Topics: "What sort of motion is the soul?"
│   ├─ (13) From consequences (both good and bad)
│   │   └─ E.g., education leads to unpopularity (bad) and wisdom (good)
│   ├─ (14) When two opposite courses each have both good and bad results
│   │   └─ Priestess: "if you say what is right, gods will love you; if what is wrong, men will love you"
│   ├─ (15) People approve secretly what they do not approve openly
│   │   └─ Try to establish the contradicting common opinion
│   ├─ (16) From rational correspondence (analogy)
│   │   └─ E.g., Iphicrates: "If you count tall boys men, you will next be voting short men boys"
│   ├─ (17) If two results are the same, antecedents are the same
│   │   └─ E.g., Xenophanes: "as impious to say gods are born as to say they die"
│   ├─ (18) From the fact that men do not always make same choice later
│   │   └─ E.g., "when exiles, we fought to return; now returned, strange to choose exile"
│   ├─ (19) Asserting a possible motive as the real one
│   │   └─ E.g., a gift given to cause pain by its withdrawal
│   ├─ (20) From inducements and deterrents
│   │   └─ Things that make us act or refrain: possibility, ease, utility
│   ├─ (21) Things supposed to happen but incredible (improbable + incredible = true)
│   │   └─ "Fish need salt" -- improbable and incredible, therefore likely true
│   ├─ (22) Noting disagreements in opponent's case
│   │   └─ In dates, conduct, and statements
│   ├─ (23) Meeting slander: why facts are not as supposed
│   │   └─ Show false impression has a reason
│   ├─ (24) From cause to effect and effect to cause
│   │   └─ If cause present, effect exists; if effect present, cause exists
│   ├─ (25) Whether a better course could have been taken
│   │   └─ If someone knowingly chose worse course → not guilty of what worse course implies
│   ├─ (26) Examining inconsistency with contemplated and past actions
│   │   └─ E.g., Medea of Carcinus: accusers say "not to be seen" but she sent children away
│   ├─ (27) From meanings of names (etymological argument)
│   │   └─ E.g., Sophocles: "O steel in heart as thou art steel in name"
│   │   └─ Conon: Thrasybulus "rash in counsel"; Draco's laws = "of a dragon"
│   └─ (28) Refutative enthymeme has greater reputation than demonstrative
│       └─ Within small space works out opposing arguments side by side
│       └─ Deductions most applauded: conclusions not obvious at first sight but foreseeable
│
├─ II.24  Apparent (Spurious) Enthymemes (φαινόμενα ἐνθυμήματα)
│   ├─ Besides genuine deductions, there are those that look genuine but are not
│   ├─ (1) From particular words: making final statement as if conclusion without syllogism
│   │   └─ "Therefore so-and-so is not true" / "Therefore so-and-so must be true"
│   ├─ (2) From homonymy (ambiguous words)
│   │   └─ E.g., "the mouse is noble" (gives name to august rites)
│   ├─ (3) Combining what is separate or dividing what is combined
│   │   └─ E.g., double portion of certain thing: each half is harmful, whole must be bad
│   ├─ (4) Exaggeration (indignant language without proving facts)
│   │   └─ Hearer infers guilt from speaker's emotion, but no proof
│   ├─ (5) From a sign (non-deductive)
│   │   └─ E.g., lovers useful to countries because Harmodius/Aristogeiton killed Hipparchus (non sequitur)
│   ├─ (6) From the accidental
│   │   └─ E.g., mice gnawed bowstrings → therefore came to rescue (accidental coincidence)
│   ├─ (7) From consequence (affirming the consequent)
│   │   └─ Paris "had a lofty soul" because he despised society (but many who despise are not lofty)
│   ├─ (8) Post hoc ergo propter hoc
│   │   └─ E.g., Demades: policy of Demosthenes caused all mischief because war followed
│   ├─ (9) Leaving out when and how (omitting circumstances)
│   │   └─ E.g., Paris justified in taking Helen (father's choice) -- but father's freedom was not perpetual
│   └─ (10) Confusing absolute with particular probability
│       └─ Corax's Art of Rhetoric: strong man charged → "not likely"; but if known to be strong → also "not likely"
│       └─ Making worse argument appear better -- Protagoras' claim
│
├─ II.25  Refutation of Enthymemes
│   ├─ Refutation by counter-deduction or by objection
│   ├─ Counter-deductions: built from same commonplaces (reputable opinions often contradict)
│   ├─ Objections raised in four ways
│   │   ├─ (a) Attacking opponent's own statement directly
│   │   ├─ (b) Putting forward another statement like it
│   │   ├─ (c) Putting forward a contrary statement
│   │   └─ (d) Quoting previous decisions (authorities)
│   ├─ Enthymemes based on four things
│   │   ├─ (1) Probabilities: usually true, can be refuted by showing not inevitable
│   │   ├─ (2) Examples: refuted by single negative instance
│   │   ├─ (3) Evidences (τεκμήρια): irrefutable if true; refutable only if fact untrue
│   │   └─ (4) Signs: always refutable (non-deductive); every sign is non-deductive
│   ├─ Probability-based: refutation not genuine but spurious (shows not inevitable, not improbable)
│   │   └─ Hence defence has advantage: accuser uses probabilities, defender shows only "not inevitable"
│   ├─ Objection must state what is more usually true than opponent's claim
│   │   └─ Most convincing: in both time and fact
│   └─ Signs and enthymemes based on them refutable even if facts correct (every sign non-deductive)
│       └─ Shown in Analytics: Prior Analytics II.27
│
└─ II.26  Amplification and Depreciation; Final Summary
    ├─ Amplification ≠ element of enthymeme; ≠ line of enthymeme
    │   ├─ Element = commonplace (general category)
    │   ├─ Amplification/depreciation = used to show something great or small
    │   └─ Subject-matter of deductions and enthymemes, not a commonplace
    ├─ Refutative enthymemes ≠ a separate species
    │   └─ Refutation = proving opposite of opponent using same means
    ├─ Objection ≠ enthymeme: it is a statement of opinion showing false assumption
    │   └─ As said in Topics (Topics I.10)
    └─ Three points of speech-making now complete:
        ├─ (1) Examples
        ├─ (2) Maxims
        └─ (3) Enthymemes (= the thought-element)
            └─ Next: language and arrangement (Book III)
```

## 6. Tensions

| Tension | Node A | Node B | Type | Description |
|---------|--------|--------|------|-------------|
| Prime as ideal mean vs. specific age range | Prime character as rational ideal: "free from the extremes of either" (1390a28, PDF 67) | Physical prime 30--35, mental prime about 49 (1390b10, PDF 67) | Internal | Aristotle presents the prime both as an idealised ethical mean between youth and old age and as a specific biological fact, creating tension between normative and descriptive accounts of character |
| Maxim as general truth vs. maxim as enthymematic component | Maxim defined as general statement about practical conduct, independent of particular facts (1394a21, PDF 73) | Maxim is premiss/conclusion of enthymeme with deductive element removed (1394a27, PDF 73) | Analytical | If maxims are self-standing general truths, their persuasive force should be independent; but if they are truncated enthymemes, their force depends on the suppressed deductive structure the audience must supply |
| Example as induction vs. example as supplementary witness | Example has "the nature of induction" and is primary when enthymemes unavailable (1393a27, PDF 71) | When enthymemes available, examples function as "witnesses giving evidence" after the argument (1394a13, PDF 73) | Methodological | Aristotle assigns example a dual and potentially conflicting role: as an independent mode of proof (inductive) and as merely confirmatory evidence subordinate to deductive enthymeme |
| Enthymeme from common opinions vs. genuine knowledge | Enthymemes must start from commonly held opinions (ἔνδοξα), not from any and every opinion (1395b30, PDF 76) | Aristotle also demands that we know the facts (1396a5, PDF 77): "Otherwise we can have no materials out of which to construct arguments" | Epistemological | Rhetorical argument is anchored simultaneously in popular belief and in factual knowledge, yet these may diverge -- the orator must negotiate between what is accepted and what is true |
| Spurious enthymeme as deception vs. as necessary technique | Ten lines of apparent enthymeme catalogued as fallacious (1400b34, PDF 85) | Corax's technique: probability arguments exploitable by both prosecution and defence, making "worse argument appear better" -- Protagoras (1402a25, PDF 87) | Ethical | Aristotle catalogues spurious enthymemes to teach recognition and refutation, yet the very catalogue provides a manual for deception, raising the familiar question of rhetoric's moral neutrality |
