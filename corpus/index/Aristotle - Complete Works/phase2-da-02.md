**One-sentence thesis**: Aristotle defines soul (*psuchē*) as "the first actuality of a natural organized body" (*entelecheia hē prōtē sōmatos phusikou organikou*), establishes a hierarchical ordering of psychic faculties -- nutritive, perceptive, appetitive, locomotive, intellectual -- in which each successive capacity presupposes the prior, and then develops the general conditions of perception (*aisthēsis*) as a transition from potentiality to actuality through the agency of an external object, thereby grounding the entire faculty psychology that undergirds the treatise's later accounts of phantasia and nous.

## 4. Main Positions

### DA-02-P1: Soul is the first actuality of a natural organized body (II.1)

Aristotle opens Book II with a "completely fresh start" on the question "What is soul?" (412a3). He distinguishes three senses of substance (*ousia*): matter (potentiality), form (actuality), and the composite of both. Since every natural body with life is a substance as composite, and the body is the subject or matter, the soul must be substance in the sense of form -- "the actuality of a body of the character described" (412a20). Within actuality itself Aristotle draws the critical distinction between first actuality (possession, *hexis* -- as knowledge possessed but not exercised) and second actuality (exercise, *energeia* -- as the actual exercise of knowledge). Soul corresponds to first actuality: "the soul is the first actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it" (*entelecheia hē prōtē sōmatos phusikou dunamei zōēn echontos*, 412a27). The qualifying clause "the body so described is a body which is organized" specifies that soul is not the actuality of just any matter but of an appropriately structured, instrumental body (*sōma organikon*). The axe analogy drives the point home: if an axe were a natural body, its "cutting" would be its soul; if the eye were an animal, "sight would have been its soul" (412b12-13). An eye without the power of sight is an eye "in name only" -- like the eye of a statue (412b20). This entails that soul is inseparable from the body at least for those faculties that are actualities of bodily parts, while leaving open the possibility that intellect, if it is not the actuality of any body, may be separable (413a5-7).

**Dual citation**: Aristotle, *De Anima* II.1, 412a3--413a10 (Smith trans., Barnes ed., pp. 656--657).

### DA-02-P2: The faculty hierarchy -- nutritive, sensitive, appetitive, locomotive, intellectual (II.2--II.3)

In II.2, Aristotle refines the definition by asking what distinguishes ensouled from unensouled bodies: the answer is life (*to zēn*), which manifests as thinking, perception, local movement, or nutrition/growth/decay (413a22-25). He identifies the nutritive faculty (*threptikon*) as the most primitive power of soul, the one "in virtue of which all are said to have life" (415a25), possessed even by plants. The sensitive faculty (*aisthētikon*) is the criterion for calling something an animal: "it is the possession of sensation that leads us for the first time to speak of living things as animals" (413b2). Touch is the primary and indispensable form of sense; all animals have it, while sight, hearing, and smell are found only in some (413b4-7). In II.3, Aristotle introduces the analogy of geometrical figures to explain the serial ordering of faculties: just as there is no figure apart from the triangle and those that follow in order, "so here there is no soul apart from the forms of soul just enumerated" (414b28). Each higher faculty presupposes and potentially contains the lower, as the square contains the triangle; but the converse does not hold. This yields a strict hierarchy: nutritive (plants) < sensitive + nutritive (animals with touch only) < sensitive + appetitive + locomotive (higher animals) < all + intellectual (humans). The consequence for definition is that no single common formula can adequately capture the soul; one must define "the soul of plant, man, beast" separately, seeking "the most adequate definition" by examining "each of its forms for the most appropriate definition" (415a10-12).

**Dual citation**: Aristotle, *De Anima* II.2--II.3, 413a11--415a13 (Smith trans., Barnes ed., pp. 657--660).

### DA-02-P3: Soul is cause in three senses -- efficient, final, and formal (II.4)

In the treatment of the nutritive soul (II.4), Aristotle makes a broader claim about soul as cause (*aitia*). The soul is the cause or source of the living body in three senses: (1) it is the source of movement (*hothen hē kinēsis*), (2) it is the final cause or end (*to hou heneka*, *to telos*), and (3) it is the essence (*to ti ēn einai*) -- the formal cause (415b8-12). As formal cause, "in everything the essence is identical with the cause of its being, and here, in the case of living things, their being is to live, and of their being and their living the soul in them is the cause or source" (415b12-14). As final cause, Aristotle argues that "nature, like thought, always does whatever it does for the sake of something, which something is its end" (415b16). All natural bodies -- of plants as well as animals -- "are organs of the soul" (415b18). The "for the sake of which" (*to hou heneka*) has two senses: the end to achieve, and the being in whose interest the act is done (415b2-3). Since no perishable thing can partake in the eternal directly, it does so through reproduction -- "the production of another like itself" is "the most natural act" of any living thing (415a28-29). This triple causality (efficient + final + formal) marks soul as a radically different kind of principle from the material or elemental causes proposed by the Presocratics. Aristotle explicitly refutes Empedocles on this point: fire is at most a concurrent cause of growth, but "limit and ratio" -- marks of soul -- are the principal cause (416a15-18).

**Dual citation**: Aristotle, *De Anima* II.4, 415a14--416b31 (Smith trans., Barnes ed., pp. 660--663).

### DA-02-P4: Perception is a transition from potentiality to actuality requiring an external agent (II.5)

In the most technical chapter of the unit, Aristotle develops the general theory of perception as a species of "being affected" (*paschein*) from without (416b33). The problem is posed sharply: "why do we not perceive the senses themselves?" -- because "what is sensitive is so only potentially, not actually," like combustible material that requires an external agent to ignite it (417a2-6). Aristotle then distinguishes three grades of the potentiality/actuality schema as applied to knowledge: (1) first potentiality -- a man qua member of the class of knowers, who has not yet learned (417a22-24); (2) second potentiality / first actuality (*hexis*) -- the man who has learned grammar and can exercise it at will (417a24-28); (3) second actuality -- the man who is "already reflecting" and "is a knower in actuality and in the most proper sense" (417a29-30). The transition from (1) to (2) is a genuine alteration -- learning is "a change by repeated transitions from one state to its opposite under instruction" (417b1). But the transition from (2) to (3) is not an alteration at all; it is "the transition from the inactive possession of sense or grammar to their active exercise" -- it would be "absurd to speak of a builder as being altered when he is using his skill" (417b6-9). Crucially, "to be acted upon" itself has two senses: (a) the extinction of a contrary by its opposite, and (b) the preservation/development of a potential by an actual -- "being in reality a development into its true self or actuality" (417b2-16). Perception exemplifies (b): sense at birth is first potentiality; the first transition to having sense is accomplished by the "action of the male parent" at birth; the second transition -- from possessing to exercising sense -- is triggered by external objects (the seen, the heard, etc.), which are individual and external (417b16-19).

**Dual citation**: Aristotle, *De Anima* II.5, 416b32--418a6 (Smith trans., Barnes ed., pp. 663--665).

### DA-02-P5: The threefold classification of sensible objects -- proper, common, incidental (II.6)

In the opening of II.6 (the final chapter of this unit), Aristotle classifies the objects of sense into three kinds. (1) *Proper sensibles* (*idia aisthēta*): objects that "cannot be perceived by any other sense" and "in respect of which no error is possible" (418a11). Colour is the proper object of sight, sound of hearing, flavour of taste; touch "discriminates more than one set of different qualities" (418a13). Each sense "has one kind of object which it discerns, and never errs in reporting that what is before it is colour or sound," though it may err about what the coloured thing is or where it is (418a14-16). (2) *Common sensibles* (*koina aisthēta*): "movement, rest, number, figure, magnitude," which "are not special to any one sense, but are common to all" -- perceptible by both touch and sight at least (418a17-19). (3) *Incidental sensibles* (*kata sumbebēkos aisthēta*): for example, the white object seen is incidentally the son of Diares -- "being the son of Diares is incidental to the white which is perceived" and "in no way as such affects the senses" (418a20-24). The critical normative claim closes the classification: "the special objects are properly called perceptible and it is to them that in the nature of things the structure of each several sense is adapted" (418a24-25). This threefold classification is foundational for the subsequent analyses of each sense (II.7--II.12) and for the later treatment of common sense and phantasia in Book III.

**Dual citation**: Aristotle, *De Anima* II.6, 418a7--418a25 (Smith trans., Barnes ed., p. 665).

## 4b. Cross-Work Connections

### To Physics:
- The actuality/potentiality framework deployed in II.1 and II.5 depends directly on the Physics' analysis. Physics III defines motion (*kinēsis*) as "the actuality of the potential qua potential" (*energeia tou dunatou hēi dunaton*, Phys. 201a10). De Anima II.5 develops this into a more refined three-grade schema specific to perception. The relationship between "being affected" (*paschein*) and "being altered" (*alloiousthai*) in II.5 connects to Physics V's classification of changes and VII's treatment of alteration.
- The doctrine that soul is cause in three senses (II.4, 415b8-28) maps directly onto the Physics' four-cause framework (Phys. II.3, 194b16ff.).

### To Metaphysics:
- The three senses of *ousia* (matter, form, composite) in II.1 (412a6-9) presuppose Metaphysics Z-H on substance. The identification of soul with form/actuality is an application of the general hylomorphic ontology of Metaphysics VII-IX.
- *Entelecheia* as the key term for the soul's relation to the body connects to the actuality/potentiality analysis of Metaphysics Theta.

### To Rhetoric:
- The general theory of perception (II.5--II.6) grounds the later account of how phantasia mediates between perception and thought (DA III.3), which in turn undergirds the Rhetoric's account of metaphor as "setting before the eyes" (*pro ommatōn poiein*, Rhet. III.11). The threefold classification of sensible objects -- especially the distinction between proper and common sensibles -- is essential background for understanding how phantasia can produce quasi-perceptual images of absent objects.

### To Movement of Animals:
- The faculty hierarchy of II.2--II.3 (nutritive < sensitive < appetitive < locomotive < intellectual) is presupposed by MA chs. 6-7, where desire (appetitive) plus phantasia (sensitive/imaginative) combine in the practical syllogism to produce locomotion.

## 5. Tensions

### T1: Soul as inseparable from body vs. possible separability of nous

In II.1, Aristotle states that "the soul is inseparable from its body, or at any rate certain parts of it are (if it has parts) -- for the actuality of some of them is the actuality of the parts themselves" (413a3-5). Yet he immediately adds: "some may be separable because they are not the actualities of any body at all" (413a6-7). This is the fundamental tension of De Anima's hylomorphism: if soul is defined as the actuality of a natural organized body, then the soul in principle cannot exist apart from a body. But Aristotle leaves open the case of intellect (*nous*), which may not be the actuality of any bodily organ. The tension is not resolved in this unit; it drives the inquiry of Book III (especially III.4-5 on nous).

### T2: Perception as "being affected" vs. perception as "not alteration"

In II.5, Aristotle characterizes perception as a process of "being moved or affected" (*paschein*, 416b33) from without -- it is "held to be some sort of change of quality" (*alloiōsis tis*, 417a1). Yet in the same chapter he argues that the transition from possessing to exercising a sense is NOT a genuine alteration -- "it would be absurd to speak of a builder as being altered when he is using his skill" (417b8). He distinguishes two senses of "being acted upon": (a) destruction of one contrary by another, and (b) preservation/development of potential by actual. Perception falls under (b), not (a). This creates a tension: perception is a *paschein* (being affected) but not an *alloiōsis* (alteration) in the proper sense. The resolution depends on the non-standard sense of "being acted upon" as "development into one's true self" (417b6-7), but this creates conceptual strain with the initial characterization of perception as a process of affection from without.

### T3: The axe/eye analogy and the limits of hylomorphism

The axe analogy (412b11-15) is introduced to make the soul-body relation intuitive: if an axe were a natural body, its cutting power would be its soul. But the analogy immediately reveals a tension. An axe is an artifact, not a natural body; its function is imposed externally, not self-generated. Aristotle is aware of this ("if, that is, the axe were a natural body," 412b12) and immediately shifts to the eye analogy, where sight is the "soul" of the eye. But the eye is only a part of the body, not the whole organism. The question of how the relation between part-faculty and part-body scales up to the whole organism and its total soul is acknowledged ("we must now extend our consideration from the parts to the whole living body," 412b17-18) but not fully resolved. The serial ordering of faculties (II.3) is partly a response to this difficulty, but the metaphysical question of how one *entelecheia* can encompass multiple subordinate faculties remains.

### T4: The figure analogy and the unity of soul

In II.3 (414b20-32), Aristotle compares the serial relation among soul-types to the serial relation among geometrical figures: "as the triangle is the first figure, so the nutritive faculty is the first form of soul." Each successive figure contains its predecessor; similarly, each higher soul-type includes the lower. But the analogy is imperfect: geometrical figures are abstract objects with clear containment relations, while soul-faculties are powers of living organisms with causal interactions. The question of whether the nutritive soul in an animal is *numerically identical* with the soul that perceives, or merely *logically contained* in it, is raised ("Is each of these a soul or a part of a soul?" 413b13) but deferred. The figure analogy suggests logical containment but does not settle the ontological question.

### T5: Infallibility of proper sensibles vs. error in perception

Aristotle claims that each sense "never errs in reporting that what is before it is colour or sound" (418a15) -- the proper sensibles are the domain of infallible perception. Yet he concedes that the sense "may err as to what is coloured or where that is, or what is sounding or where that is" (418a15-16). The incidental sensibles are the primary locus of perceptual error. This creates a tension between the claim of infallibility and ordinary perceptual experience, where errors about colour and sound seem possible (e.g., colour constancy failures, auditory illusions). Aristotle's resolution depends on a strict distinction between perceiving *that* something is a colour and perceiving *what* the coloured thing is -- but this distinction is philosophically fraught and becomes more pressing in the treatment of phantasia in III.3, where phantasia is defined precisely as the faculty that can be false where perception of proper objects is always true.

## 8. Dissertation Relevance

### 8.1 Why DA-02 is Critical

This unit provides the foundational definitions that the entire psychology and, by extension, the rhetorical theory depend upon. Three elements are indispensable for the dissertation:

1. **The hylomorphic definition of soul** (*entelecheia hē prōtē*): This is the ontological ground for treating perception and phantasia as actualities of the living body, not as disembodied mental operations. The dissertation's argument that rhetorical experience is an embodied, world-directed phenomenon depends on this definition.

2. **The faculty hierarchy**: The serial ordering of faculties establishes that phantasia (treated in III.3) is not a standalone faculty but one that presupposes the perceptual faculty, which in turn presupposes the nutritive. This hierarchy constrains how phantasia can function in the Rhetoric's account of "setting before the eyes" -- it is always already embedded in a living, perceiving, desiring organism.

3. **The general theory of perception (II.5)**: The three-grade potentiality/actuality schema and the distinction between two senses of "being acted upon" are the theoretical framework within which phantasia will be analyzed. Phantasia inherits the perceptual structure but extends it beyond the presence of the external object -- a move that is only intelligible against the background of II.5's account of how perception actualizes.

### 8.2 Key Passages for Dissertation Use

- **412a27--b1**: The definition of soul ("the first actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it... a body which is organized").
- **413a3-7**: Soul's inseparability from body, with the exception for nous.
- **414b20-32**: The figure analogy and serial ordering of faculties.
- **415b8-28**: Soul as cause in three senses.
- **417a21-b19**: The three grades of potentiality/actuality applied to perception.
- **418a7-25**: The threefold classification of sensible objects.

## 9. Flags & Open Questions

1. **Entelecheia vs. energeia**: Aristotle uses *entelecheia* for the soul-definition (412a27) and *energeia* for the exercise of knowledge (412a11, 417a29). Are these terms synonymous or is there a systematic distinction? The literature is divided. Track usage across subsequent units (especially DA-04 on nous) to determine whether Aristotle maintains a consistent distinction.

2. **The figure analogy's limits**: Does the serial ordering of faculties entail that higher faculties are *logically* or *ontologically* prior to the lower? Aristotle's method in II.4 ("activities/actions prior in definition to potentialities; objects of activities prior to the activities") suggests definitional priority, but the ontological question remains. This matters for whether phantasia is merely logically distinguished from perception or is a distinct ontological capacity.

3. **Touch as indispensable sense**: Aristotle insists that "all animals have one sense at least, viz. touch" (414b3). What is the relationship between touch as the most basic sense and the common sensibles (movement, rest, magnitude, figure, number) that are perceived through multiple senses? This question bridges to DA-03 (the five senses) and to DA-04 (common sense / *koinē aisthēsis*).

4. **"Not alteration" claim in II.5**: The claim that the transition from first to second actuality is not a genuine alteration (417b6-9) creates a tension with the opening characterization of perception as "being affected" from without. This anticipates the later problem of how phantasia can produce quasi-perceptual states without an external stimulus -- if perception is already "not alteration" in the standard sense, the line between perception and phantasia becomes more porous than the standard reading suggests.
