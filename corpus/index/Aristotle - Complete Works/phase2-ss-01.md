## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Prolegomenon: Attributes of soul-body conjunction (Ch. 1, 436a1–437a3)
- Transition from *De Anima*: having treated the soul in itself, we must now survey the attributes common to all or peculiar to certain animals (436a1–5 | SS I.1, p. 693).
- Enumeration of paired attributes: *sensation*, *memory*, *passion*, *appetite*, *desire*, *pleasure*, *pain*; and paired contraries: *waking/sleeping*, *youth/old age*, *inhalation/exhalation*, *life/death* (436a8–15 | SS I.1, p. 693).
- Methodological principle: the natural scientist must investigate *health* and *disease* because these pertain to living bodies (436a17–436b1 | SS I.1, p. 693).
- All these attributes involve soul and body in conjunction: sensation is generated in the soul through the medium of the body (436b1–8 | SS I.1, pp. 693–694).

### 2.2 Hierarchy of the senses (Ch. 1, 437a3–437b10)
- Touch and taste belong necessarily to all animals, by reason of nutrition (437a3–8 | SS I.1, p. 694).
- The three "distance" senses — *smelling*, *hearing*, *seeing* — operate through external media and are found in all animals possessing locomotion (437a8–14 | SS I.1, p. 694).
- Seeing ranked highest for the primary needs of life; hearing ranked highest for intellectual development, because rational discourse is audible, and each word is a symbol (437a14–b10 | SS I.1, p. 694).
- Common sensibles: *figure*, *magnitude*, *motion*, *number* — perceived especially through sight (437a8–10 | SS I.1, p. 694).

### 2.3 The eye and the medium of sight (Ch. 2, 437b10–438b30)
- Critique of the four-element correlation theory: some match five senses with four elements, leaving the fifth sense (touch) unaccounted for (437b10–15 | SS II.2, p. 694).
- Refutation of the fire-theory of the eye: the flash when the eye is pressed or moved does not prove the eye is fire; it occurs because smooth surfaces shine in darkness (437b15–438a5 | SS II.2, pp. 694–695).
- Critique of Empedocles: if vision were fire issuing from the eye (as from a lantern), the eye should see in darkness — but it does not (438a5–438a25 | SS II.2, p. 695). Includes Empedocles' lantern-poem (438a25–438b2 | SS II.2, p. 695).
- Democritus rightly says the eye is of water, but wrongly explains vision as mirroring (438b3–10 | SS II.2, pp. 695–696).
- The eye is composed of water, not because it is water, but because it is *transparent* — a property common to water and air (438b5–15 | SS II.2, p. 696).
- Water is the material of the pupil; the moisture of the eye proves this empirically (438b15–25 | SS II.2, p. 696).
- Without light, vision is impossible; whether the medium is air or light, vision is caused by a process through this transparent medium (438b25–438b30 | SS II.2, p. 696).

### 2.4 Sense organs correlated to elements (Ch. 2, 438b30–439a5)
- If one explains sense organs by correlating them with elements: sight = water, sound = air, smell = fire (438b30–439a5 | SS II.2, pp. 696–697).
- The organ of smell is *potentially* what the object of smell is *actually*; odour is smoke-like evaporation from fire (439a1–5 | SS II.2, p. 697).
- Touch = earth; taste is a particular form of touch, and both are closely related to the heart (439a1–5 | SS II.2, p. 697).

### 2.5 Colour as the limit of the transparent (Ch. 3, 439a5–439b18)
- Reference to *De Anima* for general treatment of sensibles (colour, sound, odour, savour, touch); now we determine what each sensible *is*, beginning with colour (439a5–15 | SS III.3, p. 697).
- Actuality vs. potentiality: sensibles may be spoken of as actual or potential; actual seeing differs from the capacity to see (439a15–20 | SS III.3, p. 697).
- The transparent (*to diaphanes*): light is the colour of the transparent incidentally; when a fiery element is present in a medium, there is light; its privation is darkness (439a18–25 | SS III.3, p. 697).
- The transparent is not peculiar to air or water but is a common nature and power (*koine physis kai dynamis*) subsisting in all bodies in greater or lesser degree (439a25–b5 | SS III.3, p. 697).
- Light is a nature inhering in the transparent when the latter is without determinate boundary; colour appears when the transparent is in determinate bodies, at the bounding extreme (439b5–14 | SS III.3, p. 697).
- **Key definition**: Colour is the limit of the transparent in a determinately bounded body (439b10–14 | SS III.3, p. 697). The Pythagoreans named the surface of a body its "hue" (439b14 | SS III.3, p. 697).

### 2.6 Generation of colours from white and black (Ch. 3, 439b14–440a25)
- Air and water are coloured (brightness = nature of colour); colour in unbounded media appears different at different distances, but in determinate bodies it is fixed (439b14–440a5 | SS III.3, pp. 697–698).
- White and black are generated in determinate bodies by light and darkness respectively (440a5–8 | SS III.3, p. 698).
- **Three hypotheses for intermediate colour generation**:
  - (a) Juxtaposition (*parathesis*): white and black placed side by side in imperceptibly small quantities produce a visible mixed colour, as numerical ratios (3:2, 3:4, etc.) — colours analogous to musical concords (439b18–440a5 | SS III.3, p. 698).
  - (b) Superposition: one colour appears through another, as painters overlay colours, or as the sun appears crimson through fog (440a7–15 | SS III.3, p. 698).
  - (c) Mixture (*mixis*): genuine blending at the level of minimal parts, where ingredients are wholly blent together — not mere juxtaposition (440a15–440b25 | SS III.3, pp. 698–699).
- Critique of emanation theories: saying colours are emanations and explaining sense-perception through touch is absurd; better to say perception is by contact between object and organ through the medium (440a15–20 | SS III.3, p. 699).
- Aristotle favors mixture: when bodies are mixed, their colours are necessarily mixed simultaneously; this explains why resultant colour is uniform at all distances (440b20–25 | SS III.3, p. 699).

### 2.7 Species of colour, and analogy to savour (Ch. 3–4 transition, 440b25–441a5)
- Colours, savours, and sounds are determinate in species and not infinite (440b25–441a1 | SS III.3–IV.4, p. 700).
- Seven species of colour (matching seven species of savour): white, black, and five intermediates — grey (= variety of black), crimson, violet, leek-green, deep blue (442a18–25 | SS IV.4, p. 702).
- Black is a privation of white in the transparent; saline or bitter is a privation of sweet in the nutrient moist (442a25–29 | SS IV.4, p. 702).

### 2.8 Savour (*chumos*): the sapid dry in the moist (Ch. 4, 441a1–442a30)
- Colour explained; now odour and savour, which share nearly the same physical affection but differ in their media (441a1–5 | SS IV.4, p. 700).
- Man's olfactory sense is the least perfect of all animals; his sense of touch is the most refined, and taste is a modification of touch (441a3–5 | SS IV.4, p. 700).
- Water is naturally tasteless: three hypotheses — (a) water contains all savours in imperceptible amounts (Empedocles); (b) water is matter (*hyle*) qualified to produce germs of all savours; (c) water is undifferentiated and an external agent (heat, sun) causes savour. Empedocles refuted: pericarp fruits change savour on the tree without drawing from water (441a5–441b10 | SS IV.4, pp. 700–701).
- **Key definition**: Savour is the affection wrought by the sapid dry (*to xyron*) in the moist (*to hygron*), transforming the sense of taste from potentiality to actuality (441b15–22 | SS IV.4, p. 701).
- The nutritive function of savour: heat causes growth and fits food for alimentation; sweetness nourishes, salt and bitter are rejected; other savours blended in for seasoning (442a1–15 | SS IV.4, pp. 701–702).
- Intermediate savours arise from sweet and bitter as intermediate colours arise from white and black, involving definite or indefinite ratios, mixture and motion (442a10–18 | SS IV.4, p. 702).

### 2.9 Democritus' errors on sense and the common sensibles (Ch. 4, 442a29–443a5)
- Democritus and most natural philosophers err by reducing all sensibles to objects of touch (442a29–442b2 | SS IV.4, p. 702).
- If all senses were modes of touch, each sense should be deceived about its special sensibles — but sight is not deceived about colour, nor hearing about sound (442b2–10 | SS IV.4, p. 702).
- Democritus reduces common sensibles to special ones: roughness/smoothness to touch, but these are common sensibles (magnitude, figure, roughness, sharpness) perceived by all senses, especially sight and touch (442b5–15 | SS IV.4, p. 702).
- Taste, not sight, should best perceive the common sensibles if the criterion is discerning the smallest objects (442b15–20 | SS IV.4, p. 702).
- Contrariety: white/black in colour, bitter/sweet in savour; but figures lack a clear contrary (no figure is contrary to any other), and figures are infinite in number while savours are not (442b20–443a5 | SS IV.4, p. 702).

### 2.10 Transition to odour (Ch. 4 end, 443a5–443b)
- Discussion of taste/savour completed; other affections of savour treated elsewhere in connection with the natural history of plants (443a3–8 | SS IV.4, p. 703).
- Chapter 5 opens: odours are analogous to savours, operating in a different medium (air and water vs. the nutrient moist) (443a8–15 | SS V.5, p. 703).

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **The transparent as universal medium vs. element-specific media (Chs. 2–3)**: Aristotle insists the transparent is a "common nature and power" not peculiar to any element (439a25–b5 | SS III.3, p. 697), yet he also correlates specific sense organs with specific elements (sight = water, sound = air, smell = fire, touch = earth) at 438b30–439a5. The tension is whether the transparent genuinely transcends the elements or is simply the relevant property of water/air that happens to be shared. This bears on whether the media doctrine is a genuine theory of mediation or a classificatory convenience. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: SS-01-P3, SS-01-P4.

2. **Mixture vs. juxtaposition: the status of numerical ratios (Ch. 3)**: Aristotle states that colours involving numerical ratios (3:2, 3:4) are most agreeable, analogous to musical concords (440a1–5 | SS III.3, p. 698). But he also acknowledges that some ratios are "incommensurable" and not numerically expressible (440a28–30). Whether the colour-concord analogy is structural or merely illustrative, and whether all colours reduce to ratios, is left unresolved. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: SS-01-P5.

3. **Savour and the potentiality/actuality schema (Ch. 4)**: Savour is defined as the sapid dry actualizing the moist (441b15–22 | SS IV.4, p. 701), and the analogy to acquiring knowledge is invoked. But the role of *heat* as external efficient cause complicates the schema: is it the dry, or is it heat acting on the dry-in-the-moist, that constitutes the proper object of taste? The efficient cause seems to stand outside the potentiality-actuality pair. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: SS-01-P6.

4. **Seven species of colour — why seven? (Ch. 3–4)**: Aristotle asserts seven species of colour and seven of savour (442a18–25 | SS IV.4, p. 702), paralleling the seven notes of the musical scale. Whether this is an empirical observation, a Pythagorean inheritance, or a structural requirement of the ratio-theory is not made explicit. The claim sits in tension with the acknowledgement that some colour-ratios are incommensurable. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: SS-01-P5, SS-01-P7.

5. **Privation-language for black and bitter (Ch. 4)**: Black is called a "privation of white in the transparent" and bitter/saline a "privation of sweet in the nutrient moist" (442a25–29 | SS IV.4, p. 702). This risks making the negative term merely an absence rather than a genuine quality. Whether black and bitter are positive qualities or genuine privations has consequences for the ratio-theory: a ratio between a positive quality and its privation is structurally different from a ratio between two positive quantities. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: SS-01-P4, SS-01-P6.
