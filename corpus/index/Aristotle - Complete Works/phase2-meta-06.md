- **unit_thesis**: This unit constitutes the core of Aristotle's positive account of substance (ousia) in the central books of the Metaphysics. Beginning from the question of what the essence (to ti en einai) of a thing is and whether a thing is identical with its essence (chs. iv-vi), Aristotle investigates generation (genesis) and its implications for matter, form, and the composite (chs. vii-ix), then turns to the relation between definition, parts, and wholes (chs. x-xii), argues that no universal can be substance (ch. xiii), draws destructive consequences for Platonic Forms (chs. xiv-xv), establishes that the parts of substances are potentialities rather than independent actualities (ch. xvi), and concludes by identifying substance with cause and form (eidos) -- the "why" that makes matter into a determinate this (tode ti) (ch. xvii).

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Essence and definition (Z iv-vi, 1029b-1032a)

#### Z iv (1029b-1030b): Essence belongs primarily to substance
- The essence (to ti en einai) of a thing is "what it is said to be in virtue of itself" (1029b14, PDF 78).
- Essence belongs primarily and simply to substance; it also belongs to the other categories but in a secondary and qualified sense.
- Definition (horismos) in the strictest sense belongs only to substance; for other categories, definition requires addition (e.g., "snub nose" = concavity + nose), generating infinite regress (1030b, PDF 79).
- Coupled terms like "snub nose" are not definable per se because the attribute is inseparable from the substrate.

#### Z v (1030b-1031a): Definability of coupled terms
- Whether things that are not simple but coupled (e.g., "snub nose") can have definitions is a difficult question.
- Since we must explain them by addition, and the attribute is not accidental but essential to the substrate, such items generate a peculiar problem for definition.
- Clearly then, only substance is definable in the primary and unqualified sense.

#### Z vi (1031a-1032a): Each primary thing is identical with its essence
- For self-subsistent things, a thing and its essence are the same -- each primary and self-subsistent thing is one and the same as its essence (1031b20, PDF 80).
- For accidental compounds (e.g., "white man"), a thing and its essence are not the same in the same way.
- If they were different, an infinite regress of essences would follow (the essence of the essence, etc.).
- The sophistical objection about whether Socrates and "the essence of Socrates" are the same is answered from this standpoint.
- This identity holds for Forms too (if they exist): the Good and the essence of Good must be one, or knowledge collapses.

### 2.2 Generation, form, and matter (Z vii-ix, 1032a-1034b)

#### Z vii (1032a-1033b): The three kinds of generation
- Things come to be in three ways: by nature (physis), by art (techne), or spontaneously (1032a12, PDF 81).
- All generation proceeds from something (the agent/mover), by means of something (the form), and into something (the matter).
- All things produced by nature or art have matter (hyle); each is capable both of being and of not being, and this capacity is the matter in each (1032a20, PDF 81).
- In art, the form (eidos) is in the soul of the maker: "the medical art is the formula of health" (1032b1, PDF 81). The physician produces health by thinking: "since this is health, if the subject is to be healthy this must first be present" (1032b6, PDF 81).
- Substance without matter (the form, the essence) is not itself produced; what is produced is the composite (1033b, PDF 82): "it is obvious that the thing, in the sense of form or substance, is not produced."
- The form is not generated, nor is the matter; only the composite (synolon) comes into being.

#### Z viii (1033b-1034a): Form is not produced; rejection of Platonic Forms as patterns
- Since anything produced is produced from something (matter) and by something (efficient cause) and becomes something (form), we do not make the form itself but bring it into matter (1033b, PDF 82).
- "To make the bronze round is not to make the round or the sphere, but something else, i.e. to produce this form in something else" (1033b1, PDF 82).
- If we had to make the form, the process would regress to infinity.
- It is "quite unnecessary to set up a Form as a pattern" (1034a2, PDF 83): the begetter is adequate to the making of the product; there is no need for Platonic Forms.
- The producer is of the same kind as the produced (not in number, but in form): man produces man.

#### Z ix (1034a-1034b): Spontaneous generation and the priority of actuality
- Some things are produced spontaneously when the matter can move itself in the way the art would move it (1034a10, PDF 84).
- Substance is the starting-point of all production: "it is from the 'what' that deductions start; and from it also we now find processes of production to start" (1034b20, PDF 84).
- Not only substance but all the primary classes (quantity, quality) require that their form pre-exist in actuality, while matter need pre-exist only potentially.

### 2.3 Parts, wholes, and definition (Z x-xii, 1034b-1038a)

#### Z x (1034b-1036a): Parts of the formula vs. parts of the concrete thing
- A definition (horismos) is a formula (logos), and every formula has parts; the part of the formula is to the thing as the part of the formula is to the part of the thing (1034b20, PDF 84).
- The formula of the circle does not include that of the segments, but the formula of the syllable includes that of the letters (1034b25, PDF 85).
- Matter is in a sense part of the thing, but the formula of the form does not include the formula of the material parts; only the elements of which the formula of the form consists are parts of the formula.
- "For each thing must be referred to by naming its form, and as having form, but never by naming its material aspect as such" (1035a5, PDF 85).
- Things whose formula and whose name are of the form only (not the composite) do not pass away into their material parts.

#### Z xi (1036a-1037a): Which parts belong to the form and which to the composite?
- The question of what sort of parts belong to the form and what sort to the concrete thing is naturally raised (1036a26, PDF 87).
- For things found in specifically different materials (bronze circle, stone circle), the matter is no part of the essence of the circle, since it is found apart from material (1036a31, PDF 87).
- The form of man is always found in flesh and bones -- but whether these are parts of the form and the formula, or matter, is a hard question (1036b3, PDF 87).
- Since some maintain the Ideas consist of the Forms, and the Forms exist apart from individuals, the Forms would need to be self-subsistent -- but they are useless with regard to both comings-to-be and substances (1036b15, PDF 87).
- Definition is of the universal and of the form; the soul is the primary substance; Socrates is composed of this particular formula and this particular matter (1037a, PDF 88).

#### Z xii (1037b-1038a): Unity of definition
- The problem of the unity of definition: why is "two-footed animal" one thing and not many? (1037b10, PDF 89).
- The genus does not exist apart from the species; the differentiae divide the genus successively.
- If a differentia is taken at each step, the last differentia will be the form and the substance (1038a25, PDF 89).
- The definition is the formula which comprises the differentiae; it is necessary to take the differentia of the differentia (e.g., "endowed with feet" -> "cloven-footed") (1038a10, PDF 89).
- The genus is not something alongside the differentiae; it is matter qualified by successive differentiation.

### 2.4 The universal is not substance (Z xiii, 1038b-1039b)

#### Z xiii (1038b-1039a): The universal cannot be substance
- Let us return to substance: the substrate, the essence, and the compound are called substance, and so also is the universal (1038b1, PDF 90).
- The universal cannot be substance in the way that the essence is substance, because: (1) the substance of each thing is that which is peculiar to it and does not belong to anything else, but the universal is common (1038b10, PDF 90); (2) substance means "not predicable of a subject," but the universal is always predicable of some subject (1038b15, PDF 90).
- "No universal attribute is a substance, and this is plain also from the fact that no common predicate indicates a 'this', but rather a 'such'" (1039a1, PDF 91).
- A substance cannot consist of substances present in it actually (for things that are actually two are never actually one -- though they can be potentially two): "for the actualization of the halves divides them from one another" (1039a5, PDF 91).
- Democritus is cited approvingly: one thing cannot come from two nor two from one, since he identifies his indivisible magnitudes with substances (1039a10, PDF 91).
- If no substance can consist of universals, and no composite substance can be composed of actual substances, every substance would be incomposible -- there would not even be a formula of any substance (1039a15, PDF 91).

### 2.5 Consequences for Platonic Forms (Z xiv-xv, 1039a-1040b)

#### Z xiv (1039a-1039b): Absurdities following from the Theory of Forms
- If the Forms are substances and exist apart, and the Form consists of genus and differentiae, then either animal-in-itself is in each species (absurd, for the animal in horse and in man would be one and the same in number or different) (1039a25, PDF 91).
- If the genus is one, how will the one in things that exist apart be divided even from itself? (1039b5, PDF 92).
- If the Ideas consist of elements, those elements must be predicable of many subjects -- but an Idea cannot be predicated of many (1039b15, PDF 92).
- There is one Form for many things whose Form is evidently different (a conclusion that also confronted the Pythagoreans) (1039b25, PDF 92).

#### Z xv (1040a-1040b): No definition of sensible individuals or Ideas
- Substance is of two kinds: the concrete thing (formula + matter) and the formula in its generality (1040a, PDF 93).
- Substances of the former kind are capable of destruction (there is no destruction of "the formula"), but there is no generation or destruction of the form itself -- "no one makes or generates the form" (1040a15, PDF 93).
- Of sensible individuals there is neither definition nor demonstration, since they have matter whose nature involves both being and not being (1040a30, PDF 93).
- It is not possible to define any Idea either: the Idea is an individual (its supporters say) and exists apart, yet the formula must consist of words common to many; it cannot be peculiar to one thing alone (1040a10, PDF 93).
- "People do not realize that it is impossible to define in the case of eternal things, especially those which are unique, like the sun or the moon" (1040a25, PDF 93).

### 2.6 Parts of substances as potentialities (Z xvi, 1040b-1041a)

#### Z xvi (1040b-1041a): Unity and actuality; parts are potentialities
- Most things thought to be substances are only potentialities: the parts of animals, earth, fire, air -- "for none of them is one, but they are like a heap before it is fused" (1040b10, PDF 94).
- Since the term "unity" is used like "being," and the substance of that which is one is one, nothing that is common is substance: substance belongs only to itself and to that which has it (1040b20, PDF 94).
- "No universal is the name of a substance, and no substance is composed of substances" (1041a5, PDF 94).
- Even the imperishable substances (Forms, if they exist) are not composed of universals; the universal is not a substance but a "such" (1041a1, PDF 94).

### 2.7 Substance as cause and form (Z xvii, 1041a-1041b)

#### Z xvii (1041a-1041b): Substance is the cause; the "why" question
- Substance is a principle and a cause (1041a10, PDF 95); the "why" is always sought in the form: "why does one thing attach to another?"
- "Why is a thing itself" is a meaningless inquiry unless one asks why the matter is some individual thing (1041b5, PDF 95): "why are these materials a house? Because that which was the essence of a house is present."
- What we seek is the cause (i.e., the form) by reason of which the matter is some definite thing (1041b8, PDF 95).
- The cause is the substance of the thing -- the form (eidos).
- An element is that into which a thing is divided and which is present as matter; but the substance is not an element but a principle: "the syllable is not the same as b and a... but also something else" (1041b15, PDF 95).
- The syllable is something beyond its letters; it is not another element but the cause that makes this flesh and this syllable what they are -- and this is the substance of each thing, "for this is the primary cause of its being" (1041b25, PDF 95).

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **Matter as "unknowable in itself" vs. matter as explanatorily necessary (Z x-xi)**: Aristotle states that "matter is unknowable in itself" (1036a9, PDF 87) and that the formula of the form does not include material parts. Yet the analysis of generation (Z vii-ix) assigns matter an indispensable role: it is that from which things come to be and that which persists. If matter is unknowable in itself, how can we identify the right matter for a given form? The tension between matter's epistemological opacity and its ontological indispensability is never fully resolved in Z. [INTERP-high] -- Linked to: META-06-P5, META-06-P8.

2. **Form as universal vs. form as individual (Z xiii vs. Z viii)**: Z xiii argues that no universal can be substance, and Z viii shows that form is not generated (man generates man -- same in form, different in number). But if the form of man is numerically the same across all humans, it looks universal; if numerically distinct in each, what individuates it? Aristotle explicitly raises the question: "Are these [flesh and bones] parts of the form and the formula? No, they are matter" (1036b5, PDF 87) -- but the individuation of form remains a standing problem. [INTERP-high] -- Linked to: META-06-P3, META-06-P6.

3. **The status of the composite (synolon)**: Z iv-vi establish that essence belongs primarily to substance; Z xvii identifies substance with form as cause. But ordinary substances (Callias, this horse) are composites of form and matter. Is the composite a substance in the fullest sense, or is form alone primary substance? Z seems to privilege form (the composite is destructible, the form is not -- Z xv), but the composite is the tode ti par excellence. The relationship between form-substance and composite-substance is left in productive tension. [INTERP-high] -- Linked to: META-06-P1, META-06-P8.

4. **Genus as matter (Z xii) vs. universals not being substance (Z xiii)**: In Z xii Aristotle says the genus functions as matter relative to the differentia (1038a6, PDF 89). In Z xiii he argues no universal can be substance. If genus is matter, is it then a potentiality rather than a substance? But matter itself is one of the candidates for substance (Z iii). The tension between genus-as-matter and the rejection of universals as substance requires the potentiality/actuality framework of Books H-Theta to resolve. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: META-06-P6, META-06-P7.

5. **Definability of sensible individuals (Z xv)**: Aristotle argues that "there can neither be definition nor demonstration of sensible individuals" (1040a1, PDF 93) because they have matter capable of being and not-being. Yet the inquiry of Z is driven by the question of what the substance of sensible things is. If the primary substances are individual composites but individuals cannot be defined, the epistemological access to primary substance is in question. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: META-06-P1.

6. **The syllable analogy and emergent form (Z xvii)**: Aristotle insists the syllable is "not the same as b and a... but also something else" (1041b15, PDF 95), and this "something else" is the substance/form. But he also says it is "not an element" (1041b20) -- it is the cause. Whether this "something else" is a further component (generating a regress) or a structuring principle of a categorically different kind is the crux. Aristotle opts for the latter, but the analogy's force depends on accepting formal causation as genuinely explanatory. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: META-06-P8.

## Section 9: Dissertation Relevance

### 9.1 Central importance of eidos/hyle for dissertation argument
Metaphysics Z provides the canonical articulation of the form-matter (eidos/hyle) distinction that the dissertation must engage. Three claims from Z are structurally indispensable:

1. **Form as primary substance** (Z xvii): Aristotle's conclusion that substance is the formal cause -- the "why" that makes matter into a determinate "this" -- establishes the priority of form (eidos) over matter (hyle) that Heidegger will interpret as the Greek privileging of presence (Anwesenheit) and production (Herstellung). The dissertation must show how this priority shapes the ontological tradition.

2. **Matter as "unknowable in itself"** (Z x, 1036a9): This claim is central to the dissertation's argument about the marginalization of materiality in the Western metaphysical tradition. Ambient rhetoric (Rickert) and the rehabilitation of hyle depend on contesting this Aristotelian dictum.

3. **No universal is substance** (Z xiii): The tode ti / toionde distinction (this vs. such) and the argument that substance is always individual, never common, has implications for how rhetoric addresses particular situations vs. universal categories. The dissertation's concern with rhetorical emplacement and situatedness connects to this Aristotelian insistence on individuality.

### 9.2 Key passages for direct citation
- 1032b1-6 (PDF 81): Form in the soul of the maker; production as the paradigm for understanding substance.
- 1033b5-20 (PDF 82): "The thing, in the sense of form or substance, is not produced."
- 1036a8-9 (PDF 87): "Matter is unknowable in itself."
- 1038b8-15 (PDF 90): The substance of each thing is peculiar to it; universals are common.
- 1041a25-b25 (PDF 95): Substance as cause; the syllable analogy; "the primary cause of its being."
