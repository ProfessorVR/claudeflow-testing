# Phase 2 Analysis: COL-01 — On Colours

## Section 1: Unit Metadata

- **unit_id**: COL-01
- **work**: *On Colours* (*De Coloribus*)
- **translator**: T. Loveday and E. S. Forster (Revised Oxford Translation, ed. Barnes)
- **chapter_range**: Chapters 1–6
- **bekker_range**: 791a1–799b30
- **pdf_pages**: 3–12 (Aristotle - On Colours_(2014)_[Clean Copy].pdf)
- **tier**: Light
- **cluster**: psychology
- **authorship_note**: Disputed — traditionally attributed to the Peripatetic school, not securely to Aristotle. Text edited by C. Prantl (Teubner, Leipzig, 1881).
- **Unit thesis**: The treatise develops a systematic account of colour as arising from the interaction of light and darkness with the four elements (fire, air, water, earth), explains the generation of simple and intermediate colours through mixture and blending, and extends the analysis to the chromatic changes observable in plants (through maturation and nutriment) and in animal hair, feathers, and hides (through nutriment, moisture, and heat). The entire framework is empirical-observational, grounding colour theory in the transmutation and mixture of elemental constituents.

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Simple colours and the elements (Ch. 1, 791a1–792a5)
- Simple colours belong to the four elements: fire and sun are white, air and water are white by nature, earth is naturally white (791a1–5 | PDF p. 3).
- Fire (and sun) is yellow; earth's variety of hues is due to dying/transmutation (791a5–10 | PDF p. 3).
- Black is the proper colour of elements in process of transmutation; remaining colours arise from blending by mixture (791a10–12 | PDF p. 3).
- Darkness is due to privation of light; three conditions under which things appear black (791a12–791b1 | PDF pp. 3–4).
- Darkness is not a colour but merely privation of light; it has no definite magnitude or shape (791b1–5 | PDF p. 4).
- Light is the colour of fire; some things produce light though not fire themselves (791b5–15 | PDF p. 4).
- Black occurs when air, water, and fire are thoroughly burnt; wood, charcoal, smoke as examples (791b18–792a1 | PDF pp. 4–5).
- Things turn black through water percolation and lichen; this completes the list of simple colours (792a1–5 | PDF p. 5).

### 2.2 Intermediate colours: blending and mixture (Ch. 2, 792a5–792b25)
- All remaining chromatic effects derived from blending of simple colours in varying strengths (792a5–10 | PDF p. 5).
- Crimson and violet depend on differences in constituent strength; grey from mixture of white with black (792a10–12 | PDF p. 5).
- Empirical colour generation: black + light = crimson; feeble sunlight + thin dusky white = vivid bright violet; purple hues at sunrise/sunset (792a8–20 | PDF p. 5).
- Brown from diminished light on extended purple; flame-colour from crimson + vividness and lustre (792a25–30 | PDF p. 5).
- Methodological principle: treat all colour differences by comparing coloured objects, assimilating different cases of mixture, verifying by experience and observation (792b1–20 | PDF pp. 5–6).
- Wine colour results from blending airy rays with pure lustrous black; grapes ripen from wine-coloured to violet (792b1–5 | PDF p. 6).

### 2.3 Conditions affecting colour variation (Ch. 3, 793a1–794a15)
- Several conditions give rise to manifold tints: varying strengths of light and shade, purity of constituent colours, proportions of blending (793a1–5 | PDF p. 6).
- Brightness, lustre, dimness, and dullness depend on continuity and density of light; pigeons' necks and falling water as examples of lustre (793a10–15 | PDF p. 6).
- Objects change colour through polishing and rubbing; metals (silver, gold, copper, iron) and stones assume variety of hues (793a15–25 | PDF pp. 6–7).
- Substances of small dense and black particles: tincture passes through pores, taking the material's colour; rubbing breaks up the pores, revealing the original black (793a25–b5 | PDF p. 7).
- Burning, dissolving, and melting produce dark hazy smoke; dark smooth objects (water, clouds, birds' plumage) show variety of hue (793b5–10 | PDF p. 7).
- Colour never seen in absolute purity: always blent with light, shadow, or other colours; objects assume different tints in shade vs. sunshine (793b15–25 | PDF p. 7).
- Threefold mixture of light: a translucent medium (water or air), underlying colours from which light is reflected (794a1–5 | PDF p. 7).
- Air: translucent white medium; dense = hazy; very dense = dark blue; when densified, air is the whitest of things (794a5–15 | PDF p. 8).

### 2.4 Dyeing and colour transference (Ch. 4, 794a15–794b10)
- Dyed things take colour from the dye; sources include flowers, plants, roots, bark, animal juices (purple-fish), wine, smoke, lye, sea-water (794a15–25 | PDF p. 8).
- Principle: anything having colour can transfer that colour to other things through moisture and heat entering the pores (794a25–b1 | PDF p. 8).
- Steeping during dyeing produces great variety of hues and mixtures; black fleeces used for dyeing produce dark, dull results because black pores absorb dye differently (794b1–10 | PDF pp. 8–9).

### 2.5 Colour changes in plants through maturation (Ch. 5, 794b10–797a5)
- All plants undergo changes of colour coinciding with maturation (794b10–15 | PDF p. 9).
- Original colour in all plants is herb-green; herb-green is the first colour to form in plants because water turns yellow-green on blending with sunlight, then gradually turns black, producing herb-green (794b20–30 | PDF p. 9).
- Fruit colour changes through maturation: herb-green to yellow-green to white to black, following nutriment and moisture cycles (795a1–20 | PDF pp. 9–10).
- When fruit ceases to grow, moisture is consumed by heat; each species takes colour from its juice as dyed material takes colour from dye (795a20–30 | PDF p. 10).
- Fruits ripen through chromatic sequence: white, black, grey, yellow, crimson, wine-coloured, saffron — practically every variety of colour (795a30–b5 | PDF p. 10).
- Analogy to dyeing: as moisture is heated and boiled, hues separate and recombine, paralleling the boiling of purple dye from the purple-fish (795b5–20 | PDF p. 10).
- Colour changes in black fruits: crimson at first, then wine colour, then dark-bluish when crimson is mixed with pure black (795b25–796a5 | PDF pp. 10–11).
- Leaves of most trees turn yellow in the end due to failure of nutriment; colour change due to moisture drying and black blending with yellow-green (796a15–30 | PDF p. 11).

### 2.6 Colour in animal hair, feathers, and hides (Ch. 6, 797a1–799b30)
- Hair, feathers, and hides grow white, grey, reddish, or black depending on moisture and maturation (797a1–5 | PDF p. 11).
- White when moisture containing proper colouring is dried up in maturation; black when moisture settles and becomes stale (797a1–10 | PDF p. 11).
- Correspondence between skin and hair colour; hair and feathering follows the colour of the skin over the whole body (797a10–20 | PDF p. 11).
- Nutriment theory: young children's heads reddish due to scanty nutriment; hair turns black as nutriment increases; pubic and beard hair follows same pattern (797b25–798a5 | PDF pp. 11–12).
- White as the colour of deviation from natural colour: weakness and ageing produce white; hares, deer, bears sometimes turn white (798a25–b5 | PDF p. 12).
- White animals weaker than black in most classes; white specimens mature before turning white, as unhealthy fruit turns white (798b1–10 | PDF p. 12).
- In horses and dogs, generous nutriment causes change from natural colour to white; moisture not settling long but absorbed in growth (798b5–15 | PDF p. 12).
- Black hair is not always caused by heat burning nutriment; some animals are black from birth (dogs, goats, oxen); they are less black as they age (798b15–25 | PDF p. 12).
- Birds change colour when nutriment fails; feathers start black and gain varied colours through maturation outside the body (peacock, dove, swallow) (799a5–15 | PDF p. 13).
- Concluding sentence: "From what has been set forth in this treatise one may best understand the theory of colours" (799b20 | PDF p. 13).

## Section 4: Interlocutors & Cross-Work References

No named interlocutors appear in *On Colours*. The treatise is entirely expository and does not engage in polemic against named predecessors (unlike *Sense and Sensibilia*'s critiques of Empedocles and Democritus).

**Cross-work references (implicit)**:
- **Aristotle, *Sense and Sensibilia* (SS-01)**: The *De Coloribus* colour theory — simple colours from elements, intermediate colours from mixture — parallels and simplifies the more technical account in *Sens.* ch. 3 (439a5–440b25), where colour is defined as the limit of the transparent. The *De Coloribus* lacks the concept of the transparent (*to diaphanes*) entirely.
- **Aristotle, *De Anima* II.7 (DA-02)**: The doctrine of light and the transparent that underlies the *Sens.* colour theory is absent here, suggesting either an earlier or more popular-level treatment.
- **Aristotle, *Meteorology* IV**: The maturation framework (heat acting on moisture) and the dyeing analogies presuppose the theory of pepsis (concoction) developed in *Meteor.* IV, though the term is not used.
