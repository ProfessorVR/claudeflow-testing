## 2. Hierarchical Outline (Chapter-Level)

- **III.8** (1408b–1409a; PDF 100–101). **Prose Rhythm**. Prose must be rhythmical but not metrical. The heroic rhythm is too dignified; the iambic is too ordinary; the trochee is too akin to wild dancing. The paean — with its ratio of three-to-two — is the best prose rhythm, used at both beginnings and endings of sentences.

- **III.9** (1409a–1409b; PDF 101–102). **Periodic and Free-Running Style**. Prose may be either free-running (eiromene lexis), which has no natural stopping-places, or compact/periodic (katestrammene), composed of periods with members. The periodic style is satisfying because it is finite, numbered, and easily remembered. Periods may be simple or divided into antithetical members.

- **III.10** (1410a–1411a; PDF 103–105). **Smart Sayings and Metaphor as "Setting Before the Eyes"**. Lively and well-received sayings derive from metaphor, especially the proportional kind. The best metaphors convey a new idea quickly, combine antithetical form with metaphorical wording, and "set the thing before the eyes" (pro ommaton poiein) by representing objects in a state of activity (energeia).

- **III.11** (1411b–1413a; PDF 105–109). **Energeia and Visualization Through Metaphor**. Expands the doctrine of "setting before the eyes." Homer's practice of giving metaphorical life to lifeless things is paradigmatic. Liveliness comes from proportional metaphor and the power of surprise — the hearer says "Yes, to be sure; I never thought of that." Similes, proverbs, hyperboles, and successful jests all depend on the same metaphorical mechanism. Jokes work by exploiting the double meaning of words (e.g., arche as both "beginning" and "empire").

- **III.12** (1413b–1414a; PDF 109–110). **Spoken vs. Written Style**. Each kind of rhetoric has its own appropriate style. The spoken style admits of dramatic delivery; the written style is more finished. Forensic style is the most highly finished; epideictic is the most literary; deliberative is the broadest and least refined. Strings of unconnected words suit spoken delivery but look silly in writing.

- **III.13** (1414a–1414b; PDF 110–111). **Essential Parts of a Speech**. A speech has at minimum two necessary parts: the statement (prothesis) of the case and the proof (pistis). Introduction, narration, statement, argument, and epilogue are the standard divisions, but only statement and proof are strictly essential.

- **III.14** (1414b–1415b; PDF 111–113). **The Introduction (Prooimion)**. The introduction corresponds to the prologue in poetry and the prelude in flute-music. In epideictic speeches it may begin from praise, censure, advice, or appeals to the audience. In forensic speeches, it functions like a dramatic prologue, orienting the hearer. Its essential function is to show the aim of the speech. Introductions also serve to remove or excite prejudice, and to secure attention and goodwill.

- **III.15** (1416a–1416b; PDF 114–116). **Dealing with Prejudice (Diabole)**. Commonplaces for dispelling prejudice include: denying the alleged fact, denying harm, denying disgrace, minimizing, attributing one's act to mistake or necessity, and returning calumny upon the accuser. Methods for both calumniator and apologist are detailed.

- **III.16** (1416b–1417b; PDF 116–118). **Narration (Diegesis)**. Narration in epideictic oratory is not continuous but intermittent. In forensic narration, the orator provides a survey of actions: some actions are the orator's art, others are the subject matter. Narration should depict character (ethos) through the indication of choice. The defendant must treat events as past and gone; events should be narrated rapidly — "as much as will make the facts plain" suffices.

- **III.17** (1417b–1418b; PDF 118–120). **Demonstrative Proofs (Pisteis)**. Proofs must bear directly on the question in dispute. In forensic oratory, the main task is to prove facts. In epideictic, the task is to show that the deed is noble and useful. In political oratory, the task is to show that a proposal is practicable and just. Arguments by example suit deliberative oratory; arguments by enthymeme suit forensic. Maxims should be employed in narration and arguments. Refutative enthymemes are more popular than demonstrative ones.

- **III.18** (1419a–1419b; PDF 120–121). **Interrogation and Jests**. Interrogation is best employed when the opponent has already answered in a way that reduces his position to absurdity. Ambiguous questions must be met with reasonable distinctions. Jests have been classified in the *Poetics*; irony better befits a gentleman than buffoonery.

- **III.19** (1419b–1420b; PDF 121). **The Epilogue (Epilogos)**. The epilogue has four parts: (1) making the audience well-disposed toward yourself and ill-disposed toward your opponent; (2) magnifying or minimizing the facts; (3) exciting the emotions of the hearers (pity, indignation, anger, hatred, envy); (4) refreshing their memories by recapitulation. The conclusion should employ disconnected language (asyndeton): "I have done. You have heard me. The facts are before you. I ask for your judgement."

### Positions

**P1. Prose rhythm must be paean-based, neither metrical nor unrhythmical.** Aristotle rejects the heroic hexameter as too dignified and the iambic as too ordinary for prose. The paean alone avoids the feel of definite metre while providing rhythmic structure: "the other two kinds of rhythm must be rejected in writing prose, partly for the reasons given, and partly because they are too metrical; and the paean must be adopted" 1408b30 (100). This establishes a middle course between the formlessness of unrhythmical speech and the artificiality of verse.

**P2. Metaphor achieves its highest rhetorical effect by "setting things before the eyes" through the representation of activity.** The doctrine of pro ommaton poiein is the culmination of the metaphor theory begun in RHET-05. Aristotle holds that "we ought to give our language a 'foreign air'; for people admire what is distant, and what produces admiration is pleasant" 1410b11 (104), but the decisive quality is energeia — representing things "in a state of activity" 1411b25 (106). Homer's practice of giving "metaphorical life to lifeless things" 1412a1 (107) is paradigmatic: all such passages are distinguished "by the effect of activity they convey" 1411b32 (107).

**P3. Only statement and proof are strictly necessary parts of a speech.** Against the elaborate divisions proposed by Theodorus, Licymnius, and others (who distinguish "post-narration," "pre-narration," "secundation," "divagation," "ramification"), Aristotle insists that "the only necessary parts of a speech are the statement and the argument" 1414a31 (110). Introduction, narration, and epilogue are standard but not essential: "even forensic speeches do not always need epilogues" 1414a5 (110).

## 4. Interlocutors and Cross-Work References

### Named Figures
- **Homer**: Paradigmatic practitioner of energeia — gives metaphorical life to lifeless things; cited extensively in III.11 for passages where inanimate objects are represented as active (arrows that "fly," spears "eager to feed on flesh") 1412a1 (107).
- **Isocrates**: *Panegyricus* and *Helen* cited as examples of antithetical and periodic style 1410a1 (102); uses episodic praise in epideictic oratory 1418b30 (120).
- **Gorgias**: *Olympic Speech* cited as example of epideictic introduction 1414b29 (111); also exemplifies jesting in controversy 1419b3 (121).
- **Theodorus and Licymnius**: Criticized for multiplying the divisions of a speech with "pointless and silly" neologisms 1414b15 (111).
- **Socrates**: Cross-reference to his interrogation of Meletus in Plato's *Apology* 1419a8 (120).
- **Sophocles**: *Antigone* cited for narration that depicts character through choice 1417a28 (117); reply to the Four Hundred cited for interrogation technique 1419a25 (121).
- **Euripides**: *Iphigenia in Aulis*, *Troades*, *Hippolytus* cited for rhetorical figures and emotional speech 1416b33 (116), 1418b33 (120), 1416a28 (115).
- **Thrasymachus**: Introduced the paean rhythm; cited for simile about Niceratus and Pratys 1409a2 (100), 1413a2 (108).
- **Stesichorus**: Saying about cicadas cited as example of liveliness through unexpected metaphor 1412a22 (107).

### Cross-Work References
- ***Poetics***: Jests classified in the *Poetics* 1419b5 (121); metaphor theory referred back to *Art of Poetry* throughout.
- ***Topics***: Methods of refutation "known to us from the *Topics*" 1419a20 (120).
- **Rhetoric I–II**: Proofs and emotional appeals from Books I–II presupposed throughout the treatment of arrangement.
