- **unit_thesis**: Book III establishes the foundational definitions of the two concepts that govern the remainder of the Physics: motion (kinesis), defined as "the fulfilment of what is potentially, as such" (201a10–11), and the infinite (apeiron), which Aristotle argues exists only potentially and by division, never as an actual completed magnitude. The book opens by situating motion and the infinite as prerequisites for understanding nature itself, proceeds through the formal definition of motion and its difficulties (chs. 1–3), then turns to the infinite — surveying predecessors, arguing against actual infinity, and establishing that the infinite exists as a process of always becoming, never as a completed whole (chs. 4–8).

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Motion as the subject of natural science (III.1, 200b12–201a9)

- Nature is a principle of motion and change; understanding motion is prerequisite to understanding nature itself. "If it [motion] were unknown, nature too would be unknown" (200b14–15 [PDF 30]).
- Motion belongs to the class of continuous things; the infinite presents itself first in the continuous. The account of the infinite is "often used in definitions of the continuous" (200b18–19 [PDF 30]).
- Place, void, and time are thought to be necessary conditions of motion (200b20–21 [PDF 30]).
- Motion is common to all things and universal; common attributes must be investigated before special ones (200b23–25 [PDF 30]).
- Preliminary distinctions: things exist in fulfilment only, in potentiality only, or in potentiality and fulfilment together. The categories in which motion occurs are substance, quantity, quality, and place (200b26–201a3 [PDF 30]).

### 2.2 The definition of motion (III.1, 201a9–202a3)

- The formal definition: "the fulfilment [entelecheia] of what is potentially, as such, is motion" (201a10–11 [PDF 31]). Examples: the fulfilment of what is alterable, as alterable, is alteration; of what is increasable/decreasable, increase/decrease; of what can come to be and pass away, coming-to-be/passing-away; of what can be carried along, locomotion (201a11–15 [PDF 31]).
- Clarification by example: when what is buildable, as buildable, is in fulfilment, it is being built, and that is building. Same for learning, doctoring, rolling, jumping, ripening, aging (201a16–19 [PDF 31]).
- The same thing can be both potential and fulfilled in different respects — e.g., potentially hot and actually cold. Things that are both potential and actual "will act and be acted on by one another in many ways" (201a20–23 [PDF 31]).
- What effects motion is itself also moved: "when a thing of this kind causes motion, it is itself also moved" (201a24–25 [PDF 31]). This has led some to suppose that every mover is moved, but this is addressed later (Book VIII) — "it is possible for a thing to cause motion, though it is itself incapable of being moved" (201a26–27 [PDF 31]).
- The definition restated in terms of potentiality: "it is the fulfilment of what is potential when it is already fulfilled and operates not as itself but as movable, that is motion" (201a27–29 [PDF 31]). Motion is the actuality of the potential precisely *as* potential — not the actuality of what has already achieved its final state.
- Temporal precision: motion occurs "just when the fulfilment itself occurs, and neither before nor after" (201b4–5 [PDF 31]). The buildable, as buildable, in its actuality is the process of building; when the house exists, the buildable is no longer there.

### 2.3 Soundness of the definition and difficulties (III.2, 201b16–202a3)

- The definition's soundness is confirmed by the difficulty of defining motion otherwise. Others have tried to identify motion with difference, inequality, or non-being, but "such things are not necessarily moved" (201b20–21 [PDF 32]).
- The reason motion is hard to classify: it seems indefinite, and the principles in the Pythagorean column (privative column) are indefinite because they are privative (201b24–27 [PDF 32]). Motion cannot be classed simply as potentiality or as actuality (202a1–2 [PDF 32]).
- The suggested mode of definition: motion is "a sort of actuality, or actuality of the kind described, hard to grasp, but not incapable of existing" (202a2–3 [PDF 32]).
- Motion is the fulfilment of the movable as movable; the cause is "contact with what can move, so that the mover is also acted on" (202a6–7 [PDF 32]). The mover always transmits a form — "either a 'this' or such or so much" — and is "the principle and cause of the motion, e.g. the actual man begets man from what is potentially man" (202a9–11 [PDF 32]).

### 2.4 Resolution: motion is in the movable (III.3, 202a13–202b29)

- The solution to the puzzle about where motion resides: "motion is in the movable" (202a13 [PDF 32]). It is "the fulfilment of this potentiality by the action of that which has the power of causing motion" (202a14–15 [PDF 32]).
- The actuality of the mover and the moved are not two separate motions but one: "there is a single actuality of both alike" (202a18 [PDF 32]) — as the road from Thebes to Athens and from Athens to Thebes is the same road (202b13–14 [PDF 33]).
- The dialectical difficulty: if teaching and learning are the same motion (one from the agent's side, one from the patient's), are agency and patiency the same? Aristotle resolves this: they are "the same in the sense in which the road from Thebes to Athens and the road from Athens to Thebes are the same" — same in substrate, different in definition (202b12–16 [PDF 33]).
- Teaching is not the same as learning, though they are one actualization: "it by no means follows from the fact that teaching is the same as learning, that to learn is the same as to teach" (202b19–20 [PDF 33]).
- What motion is, generally and particularly stated: "alteration is the fulfilment of the alterable as alterable (or, more scientifically, the fulfilment of what can act and what can be acted on, as such)" (202b23–26 [PDF 33]).

### 2.5 The science of the infinite: introduction and predecessors (III.4, 202b30–203b15)

- The science of nature must treat the infinite because nature is concerned with magnitudes, motion, and time, "and each of these is necessarily infinite or finite" (202b30–31 [PDF 33]).
- All major predecessors made the infinite a principle: the Pythagoreans and Plato "make the infinite a principle in its own right, and not as an accident of some other thing" (203a4–5 [PDF 34]). The Pythagoreans place the infinite among sensible things and assert "what is outside the heaven is infinite" (203a7–8 [PDF 34]). Plato holds that the infinite is present "not only in the objects of sense but in the Forms also" (203a9–10 [PDF 34]).
- The Pythagoreans identify the infinite with the even; the Platonists posit two infinites, the Great and the Small (203a10–15 [PDF 34]).
- The physicists regard the infinite as an attribute of a substance — water, air, or what is intermediate. Those who make the elements infinite in number (Anaxagoras, Democritus) say the infinite is continuous by contact (203a19–23 [PDF 34]).
- Anaxagoras: "once upon a time all things were together" — therefore there is a principle of separation, which he calls Mind (Nous) (203a25–30 [PDF 34]).
- Democritus: no element arises from another; the common body is a principle of all things, differing from part to part in size and shape (203a33–b2 [PDF 34]).
- Five considerations for the existence of the infinite: (1) from time — it is infinite; (2) from the division of magnitudes; (3) from the inexhaustibility of coming-to-be; (4) from the fact that the limited always borders on something; (5) from the difficulty that number, mathematical magnitudes, and what is outside the heaven seem to have no limit (203b15–25 [PDF 35]).

### 2.6 Arguments against actual infinity (III.5, 204a1–206a8)

- The infinite cannot be "a thing which is in itself infinite, separable from sensible objects" (204a8–9 [PDF 35–36]). If it is neither a magnitude nor an aggregate, but a substance and not an accident, it will be indivisible (since what is divisible is either magnitude or aggregate). But if indivisible, then not infinite — except in the way the voice is invisible (204a10–14 [PDF 36]).
- The infinite cannot be an actual thing and a substance and principle: "it is plain, too, that the infinite cannot be an actual thing and a substance and principle" (204a20 [PDF 36]).
- The infinite is not actually a body: "If 'bounded by a surface' is the definition of body there cannot be an infinite body either intelligible or sensible" (204b5–6 [PDF 36]). The infinite cannot be compound (the contraries must balance) or simple (it would annihilate the finite contrary elements) (204b10–22 [PDF 36]).
- Arguments from natural place: every sensible body has a natural place; an infinite body would have no determinate place, weight, or lightness. It cannot be a whole nor have an extremity or centre (206a1–7 [PDF 38]).
- Summary: "It is plain from these arguments that there is no body which is actually infinite" (206a7–8 [PDF 38–39]).

### 2.7 The infinite exists potentially, by division (III.6, 206a9–206b33)

- The infinite cannot simply not exist either: "there will be a beginning and an end of time, a magnitude will not be divisible into magnitudes, number will not be infinite" (206a10–12 [PDF 39]).
- Resolution: "clearly there is a sense in which the infinite exists and another in which it does not" (206a13–14 [PDF 39]). Things exist both potentially and in fulfilment; a thing is infinite either by addition or by division (206a14–16 [PDF 39]).
- The infinite must not be construed as a "this" (a statue, a man, a horse) that exists in actuality. Rather, it exists "in the sense in which we say it is day or it is the games, because one thing after another is always coming into existence" (206a22–24 [PDF 39]).
- The infinite exhibits itself in different ways: "in time, in the generations of man, and in the division of magnitudes" (206a26–27 [PDF 39]). In each case, "one thing is always being taken after another, and each thing that is taken is always finite, but always different" (206a28–29 [PDF 39]).
- Crucially, being is spoken of in several ways: "we must not regard the infinite as a 'this', such as a man or a horse, but must suppose it to exist in the sense in which we speak of the day or the games as existing — things whose being has not come to them like that of a substance, but consists in a process of coming to be or passing away, finite, yet always different" (206a29–33 [PDF 39]).
- In spatial magnitudes, what is taken persists; but in time and in human generations, the successive members pass away "in such a way that the source of supply never gives out" (206b1–3 [PDF 39]).
- The infinite by addition is in a way the same as the infinite by division: "in a finite magnitude, the infinite by addition comes about in a way inverse to that of the other" (206b3–5 [PDF 39]).

### 2.8 The infinite as always becoming: definition and characteristics (III.6–7, 207a1–207b34)

- The infinite is "not what has nothing outside it, but what always has something outside it" (207a1–2 [PDF 40]). Rings with no bezel are described as "infinite" (apeira) because it is always possible to take a part outside a given part (207a2–4 [PDF 40]).
- "Something is infinite if, taking it quantity by quantity, we can always take something outside" (207a7–8 [PDF 40]).
- The whole (to holon) is "that from which nothing is wanting" — it is complete (teleion). "Nothing is complete which has no end and the end is a limit" (207a13–14 [PDF 40]).
- Parmenides must be thought to have spoken better than Melissus: "the latter says that the whole is infinite, but the former describes it as limited, 'equally balanced from the middle'" (207a15–17 [PDF 40]).
- The infinite, in the fourfold scheme of causes, "is a cause in the sense of matter" and "its essence is privation" (207b35–208a1 [PDF 41]). The subject as such that is infinite is "what is continuous and sensible" (207b36 [PDF 41]).
- The infinite is unknowable qua infinite, "for the matter has no form" (207a25 [PDF 41]). It stands in the relation of part rather than of whole: "for the matter is part of the whole, as the bronze is of the bronze statue" (207a26 [PDF 41]).
- In number, the infinite proceeds in the direction of largeness (bisection of any magnitude can be repeated indefinitely), but number must stop at the indivisible — "what is one is indivisible whatever it may be" (207b6–7 [PDF 41]). In magnitude, what is continuous is divided *ad infinitum*, but there is no infinite in the direction of increase: "the size which it can potentially be, it can actually be" — hence no sensible magnitude is infinite (207b17–20 [PDF 41]).
- The infinite is not the same in magnitude, movement, and time: "the posterior depends on the prior, e.g. movement is called infinite in virtue of the magnitude covered by the movement (or alteration or growth), and time because of the movement" (207b23–26 [PDF 41]).

### 2.9 Arguments against sophistical defenses of actual infinity (III.8, 208a1–208a23)

- The arguments supposed to support the view that the infinite exists "not only potentially but as a separate thing" lack cogency (208a5–6 [PDF 42]).
- Against the argument from inexhaustible coming-to-be: "the passing away of one thing may be the coming to be of another, the universe being limited" (208a9–10 [PDF 42]).
- Against the argument from touching (that the limited always touches something): "there is a difference between touching and being limited" — touching is relative and an attribute of limited things, while "what is limited is not limited in relation to anything" (208a11–14 [PDF 42]).
- Against the argument from thought (that we can always think something bigger): "the excess or defect is not in the thing but in the thought" — one might think a man infinitely large, "but it does not follow that he is bigger than the size we are" (208a15–18 [PDF 42]).
- Time and movement are infinite "and also thinking; but the parts that are taken do not persist" (208a20–21 [PDF 42]).
- "Magnitude is not infinite either in the way of reduction or of magnification in thought" (208a22–23 [PDF 42]).
- Final summary: "This concludes my account of the way in which the infinite exists, and of the way in which it does not exist, and of what it is" (208a23–24 [PDF 42]).

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **Motion as "incomplete actuality" — how can actuality be incomplete? (III.1–2)**: The definition of motion as "the fulfilment of what is potentially, as such" makes motion a peculiar kind of actuality: it is actual (unlike mere potentiality) yet not fully actual (unlike the completed state). Aristotle acknowledges this difficulty: motion "seems to be a sort of actuality, but incomplete" (201b31–32 [PDF 32]), and it is "hard to grasp" because it cannot be classified as pure potentiality or pure actuality (202a2–3 [PDF 32]). The ontological status of this "incomplete actuality" — whether it is a genuine third category or a limit case — remains an interpretive question central to the dissertation's treatment of entelecheia/energeia. [INTERP-high] — Linked to: PHYS-03-P1, PHYS-03-P2; cf. Metaphysics Theta on energeia.

2. **One actuality of mover and moved — how can one thing have two definitions? (III.3)**: Aristotle argues that the actuality of the mover and the actuality of the moved are numerically one but definitionally two — like the road from Thebes to Athens and from Athens to Thebes (202b12–14 [PDF 33]). But the analogy is strained: a road is a static object traversed in two directions, while motion is a dynamic process. Whether the "one actuality" thesis can be maintained without collapsing the distinction between agent and patient is debated by commentators. The claim that teaching and learning are one actualization "in what is acted on" (202b20 [PDF 33]) raises the further question of whether the agent's causal contribution is adequately captured. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: PHYS-03-P3.

3. **The infinite as purely potential — is potential existence without possible actualization coherent? (III.6)**: Aristotle insists that the infinite exists only potentially and processually, "in the sense in which we say it is day or it is the games" (206a22 [PDF 39]). But in Metaphysics Theta, Aristotle argues that "the size which it can potentially be, it can actually be" (207b17 [PDF 41]) — and uses this principle *against* the infinite by addition. The tension is: if potentiality normally implies the possibility of actualization, how can the infinite be purely potential without ever being actualized? Aristotle's answer — that the infinite is potential in a special, non-substantive sense — requires a distinct mode of being for the potential that has no parallel elsewhere in his metaphysics. [INTERP-high] — Linked to: PHYS-03-P5; Phase 0 Tension #6 (energeia vs. entelecheia).

4. **The infinite and time: does the infinity of time undermine the argument against actual infinity? (III.6–8)**: Aristotle concedes that "time indeed and movement are infinite, and also thinking; but the parts that are taken do not persist" (208a20–21 [PDF 42]). This concession suggests that the infinite in time (which is successive, not simultaneous) differs structurally from the infinite in magnitude (which would be simultaneous). Whether this distinction is principled or *ad hoc* — and whether the infinity of time threatens the general conclusion that the infinite does not exist in actuality — is a standing question. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: PHYS-03-P5; connects to PHYS-04 (treatment of time).

5. **The infinite as material cause and privation (III.7)**: Aristotle concludes that the infinite "is a cause in the sense of matter" and "its essence is privation" (207b35–208a1 [PDF 41]). But the infinite is also said to "contain" (the many claim the infinite contains all things, 207a18 [PDF 41]), and matter is that which is contained by form. The assignment of the infinite to the material-privative pole sits in tension with the intuition (shared by predecessors) that the infinite is a containing, encompassing principle. Whether Aristotle's reversal — the infinite is part, not whole; contained, not container — is fully consistent with his own hylomorphism is an open question. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: PHYS-03-P6.
