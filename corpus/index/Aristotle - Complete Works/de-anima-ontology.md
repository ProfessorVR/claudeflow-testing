## Section 3B: Internal Trajectory

*De Anima* follows a systematic arc from aporia through definition to the progressive disclosure of psychic capacities. The internal trajectory can be mapped as five movements:

### Movement 1: Aporetic Ground-Clearing (Book I = DA-01)

Book I surveys and systematically refutes predecessor accounts of soul: soul as self-mover (Democritus, Plato/*Timaeus*), as harmony (Pythagoreans), as self-moving number (Xenocrates), as composed of elements (Empedocles). The conclusion is negative but methodologically essential: the *logoi enuloi* principle establishes that adequate definitions of psychic states must include both form and matter (403a25--403b19). This clears the ground for the hylomorphic definition of Book II.

### Movement 2: Hylomorphic Definition and Faculty Hierarchy (II.1--II.6 = DA-02)

The "completely fresh start" of II.1 defines soul as *entelecheia hē prōtē sōmatos phusikou organikou* (412a27). The axe and eye analogies drive the point: the soul stands to the body as cutting stands to the axe, as sight stands to the eye. Soul is substance as form, not matter or composite. The faculty hierarchy (II.2--3) orders psychic powers serially: nutritive < sensitive < appetitive < locomotive < intellectual, where each higher faculty presupposes the lower (the figure analogy: as the square contains the triangle). Soul is cause in three senses (II.4). The general theory of perception (II.5) establishes the three-grade potentiality/actuality framework that governs the rest of the treatise. The threefold classification of sensible objects -- proper, common, incidental (II.6) -- sets up the detailed sense analyses.

### Movement 3: The Individual Senses and the Wax-Signet Principle (II.7--II.12 = DA-03)

Each sense is analyzed through its proper object, requisite medium, and conditions of actualization: sight/colour/the transparent; hearing/sound/air; smell/odour/air-water; taste/flavour/contact; touch/tangible qualities/flesh. The culminating thesis of II.12 gives the general formula: sense is the capacity to receive sensible forms without matter, as wax receives the impress of a signet-ring without the iron or gold (424a17--28). The sense-organ is a *mesotēs* (mean) between contraries, which grounds its discriminative power. Voice (*phōnē*) requires soul, imagination (*phantasia*), and meaning -- the earliest linkage of perception and imagination in the treatise (420b29--33).

### Movement 4: Common Sense, Phantasia, and Nous (III.1--III.8 = DA-04a)

The unity of perception is secured through the *koinē aisthēsis*, which discriminates across sense-modalities in a single indivisible act (426b8--427a1). Phantasia is then isolated through systematic elimination: it is not sense (occurs in dreams), not opinion (brutes have it without discourse of reason), not knowledge (can be false). The positive definition: phantasia is *kinēsis hupo tēs aisthēseōs tēs kat' energeian gignomenē* -- a movement resulting from actual sensation (429a1--2). Phantasia inherits the three-tier reliability structure of perception but operates in the absence of the sensed object. Nous is then analyzed as impassible, unmixed, and without nature of its own (429a13--29), with the active/passive distinction of III.5 (430a10--25) producing the most compressed and contested passage in the Aristotelian corpus. The pivotal bridge claim: "the soul never thinks without an image" (431a17) -- nous depends on phantasmata as quasi-perceptual content.

### Movement 5: Desire, Movement, and the Conditions of Animal Life (III.9--III.13 = DA-04b)

The practical architecture: appetite (*orexis*) together with practical thought or imagination is the source of animal locomotion. The object of appetite (the real or apparent good) moves as unmoved mover; the faculty of appetite moves as moved mover; the animal is the thing moved (433a9--b30). Two kinds of imagination underlie this: sensitive (all animals) and deliberative (calculative animals only). Touch is the indispensable sense for animal existence -- excess in tangible qualities destroys not merely the organ but the animal itself.

## Section 3D: Work-Level Tensions

### T1: Hylomorphic Inseparability vs. Separability of Nous

The definition of soul as *entelecheia* of a natural body entails that soul cannot exist apart from body (413a3--5). Yet Aristotle explicitly allows that intellect, if it is not the actuality of any body, "may be separable" (413a6--7), and active nous is declared separable, immortal, and eternal (430a17--22). This is the fundamental tension of the treatise: the hylomorphic framework that governs all other psychic faculties is breached at its highest point.

### T2: Phantasia as Derivative yet Functionally Autonomous

Phantasia is causally derived from sensation (429a1--2), yet it occurs in dreams without sensation (428a6--8), guides animal action independently of present perception (429a4--8), and mediates between sensation and thought. The question of how a dependent capacity achieves functional independence -- and in what sense imagination "is in our power" (427b17--18) -- is raised but not fully resolved.

### T3: Nous Requires Phantasmata yet Is Impassible

If nous requires images for all its operations (431a17; 432a8), in what sense is it genuinely impassible and unmixed (429a15)? The form-reception model applied to sense (wax-signet) and to thought (429a13--18) creates a parallel that threatens to collapse the distinction between embodied perception and separable intellection.

### T4: Perception as Being-Affected vs. Not-Alteration

Perception is characterized as *paschein* (being affected, 416b33) yet is explicitly said not to be genuine *alloiōsis* (alteration) but rather a preservation/development of a potentiality (417b2--16). The tension between the language of passivity and the doctrine of formal reception is constitutive of Aristotle's account.

### T5: The Unity of the Moving Faculty

DA III.10 argues that both appetite and practical thought originate movement, yet concludes that appetite is "the single moving faculty" (433b10). Thought never produces movement without appetite, but appetite can move contrary to calculation (*akrasia*, 433b5). The apparent subordination of nous to orexis in the practical domain creates tension with the general superiority of nous asserted in III.4--5.

### T6: Sensitive vs. Deliberative Imagination -- Principled or Permeable?

The distinction between *phantasia aisthētikē* (all animals) and *phantasia bouleutikē* (calculative animals only) at 434a5--10 is introduced very briefly and depends on the capacity to "make a unity out of several images." If sensitive imagination already involves residual perceptual content, the boundary with deliberative imagination is permeable rather than sharp.

## Section 3E: Contested / Unstable Nodes

### D1: ἐντελέχεια vs. ἐνέργεια

Aristotle uses *entelecheia* for the soul-definition (412a27) and *energeia* for the exercise of knowledge (417a29) and the joint actualization of sense and sensible (425b26). Whether these are synonymous or systematically distinct is debated. The DA usage suggests a functional distinction -- first actuality (possession) vs. second actuality (exercise) -- but the terms overlap in ways that resist clean separation.

### D2: φαντασία

The term shifts across the treatise: in II.8 it appears in the claim that voice requires "an act of imagination" (420b32); in III.3 it receives its formal definition as a kinēsis derived from actual sense (429a1--2); in III.11 it is subdivided into sensitive and deliberative. The translation is itself contested: "imagination" imports creative/productive connotations absent from the Greek; Schofield, Nussbaum, and Caston prefer "appearance" or "representation."

### D3: νοῦς (active/passive)

The terms *nous poiētikos* and *nous pathētikos* are later labels (Alexander of Aphrodisias); Aristotle's text at III.5 simply speaks of a factor that "makes all things" and a factor that "becomes all things" (430a10--15). The brevity and opacity of this passage has made it the most contested in the entire corpus.
