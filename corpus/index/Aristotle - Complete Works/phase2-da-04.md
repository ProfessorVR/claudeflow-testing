## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Common sense and the unity of perception (III.1–2, 424b–427a1)

#### III.1 (424b–426b)
- There is no sixth sense beyond the standard five; the common sensibles (motion, rest, shape, magnitude, number, unity) are perceived through all senses and are not proper to any one sense (424b22–425a13, PDF 39).
- The awareness that we are perceiving (awareness of seeing, hearing) must belong either to the sense itself or to some further sense; an infinite regress is blocked by assigning this reflexive awareness to the same sense, which perceives "in more than one meaning" (425a14–425b12, PDF 39).
- The activity of the sensible object and the activity of the sense-organ are "one and the same activity," though they differ in being; actual sound and actual hearing are simultaneous (425b26–426a27, PDF 39–40).
- The sense is a ratio (*logos*): excess destroys the organ, and the mean is what discriminates (426a27–426b8, PDF 40).

#### III.2 (426b8–427a1)
- Each sense discriminates the differences within its own genus (sight: white/black; taste: sweet/bitter), but we also discriminate across genera (white from sweet) (426b8–426b29, PDF 40).
- That which discriminates between white and sweet must be "one and undivided" in its activity, numerically one but divisible in being — it occupies an indivisible moment of time and acts as a boundary-point between contraries (426b29–427a1, PDF 40–41).

### 2.2 Phantasia (III.3, 427a1–429a9) — THE FOCAL CHAPTER

#### III.3, opening: distinguishing phantasia from other faculties (427a1–427b27)
- Two distinctive marks characterize the soul: (1) local movement and (2) thinking, understanding, and perceiving (427a17–20, PDF 41).
- The ancients (Empedocles, Homer) identified thinking with perceiving; Aristotle separates them: perceiving is universal in the animal world, thinking is found only in a small division (427b6–9, PDF 42).
- Thinking (*noein*) is also distinct from perceiving: perception of proper objects is always free from error and is found in all animals; thinking can be right or wrong and is found only where there is discourse of reason (427b9–14, PDF 42).
- **Imagination is different from either perceiving or discursive thinking**, though it is not found without sensation, or judgement without it (427b14–16, PDF 42).
- Imagining lies within our power whenever we wish (e.g., calling up a picture, practising mnemonics), but forming opinions is not in our power — we cannot escape the alternative of falsehood or truth (427b17–21, PDF 42).
- When we merely imagine something fearful or encouraging we remain unaffected, but when we *think* it fearful emotion is immediately produced — imagination and belief have different affective consequences (427b21–24, PDF 42).

#### III.3, middle: phantasia is not sense, opinion, knowledge, or their combinations (427b27–428b9)
- Thinking is "in part imagination, in part judgement"; we must first mark off the sphere of imagination and then speak of judgement (427b27–428a1, PDF 42).
- **Imagination is not sense**: sense is either a faculty or an activity; imagination takes place in the absence of both (e.g., in dreams). Sense is always present; imagination is not. If imagination and sensation were the same, all brutes would have it, but ants, bees, and grubs do not. Sensations are always true; imaginations are for the most part false (428a1–a16, PDF 42).
- **Imagination is not knowledge or intelligence**: imagination may be false, but knowledge and intelligence are never in error (428a16–18, PDF 43).
- **Imagination is not opinion**: opinion involves belief, belief involves conviction, conviction involves discourse of reason; but some brutes have imagination without discourse of reason (428a19–24, PDF 43).
- Imagination cannot be opinion *plus* sensation, or opinion mediated by sensation, or a blend of opinion and sensation; the content of supposed imagination must be the blending of the perception of white with the opinion that it is white, which collapses the distinction (428a24–b9, PDF 43).
- **Key distinction**: what we imagine is sometimes false though our contemporaneous judgement about it is true — e.g., we imagine the sun to be a foot in diameter though we are convinced it is larger than the inhabited earth. A true opinion becomes false only when the fact alters without being noticed; imagination is therefore "neither any one of the states enumerated, nor compounded out of them" (428b2–10, PDF 43).

#### III.3, conclusion: the positive definition of phantasia (428b10–429a9)
- Since when one thing is set in motion another may be moved by it, and imagination is held to be a movement, it must occur in beings that are percipient and have for its content what can be perceived; since movement may be produced by actual sensation and is necessarily similar in character to the sensation itself, this movement cannot exist apart from sensation (428b10–16, PDF 43).
- This movement — phantasia — does and undergoes many things by virtue of its being true and false (428b17, PDF 43).
- **Three degrees of truth/falsehood in perception** that ground phantasia's reliability: (1) perception of the special objects of sense is never in error or admits the least possible amount of falsehood; (2) perception of incidental attributes (e.g., that the white thing is this or that) may be false; (3) perception of the common sensibles (movement, magnitude) is most subject to sense-illusion (428b18–25, PDF 43).
- **The motion arising from the activity of sense in these three modes differs**: the first kind is free from error while the sensation is present; the others may be erroneous whether the sensation is present or absent, **especially when the object of perception is far off** (428b25–429a2, PDF 44).
- **Definition**: "If then imagination presents no other features than those enumerated and is what we have described, then **imagination must be a movement resulting from an actual exercise of a power of sense**" (*phantasia* = *kinēsis hupo tēs aisthēseōs tēs kat' energeian gignomenē*) (429a1–2, PDF 44).
- **Etymology**: "As sight is the most highly developed sense, the name *phantasia* (imagination) has been formed from *phaos* (light) because it is not possible to see without light" (429a2–4, PDF 44).
- **Because imaginations remain in the organs of sense and resemble sensations, animals in their actions are largely guided by them** — some (brutes) because of the non-existence of thought (*nous*), others (men) because of the temporary eclipse of thought by feeling, disease, or sleep (429a4–8, PDF 44).

### 2.3 Nous: the thinking part of the soul (III.4–6, 429a10–431a14)

#### III.4 (429a10–430a9)
- Turning to the part of the soul with which it knows and thinks: is this separable from the others in definition only, or spatially as well? (429a10–13, PDF 44).
- If thinking is like perceiving, the thinking part must be impassible (*apathes*), capable of receiving the form of an object without being the object — **as sense is to sensibles, so mind is to thinkables** (429a13–18, PDF 44).
- Mind (*nous*), in order to know, must be pure from all admixture (Anaxagoras); it has no nature of its own other than a certain capacity. It was a good idea to call the soul "the place of forms" (*topos eidōn*), though this holds only of the thinking soul, and the forms are only potentially, not actually, present (429a18–29, PDF 44).
- The impassibility of mind is not like that of sense: strong stimulation of a sense makes it *less* able afterward (bright light → cannot see dim), but thinking about what is highly thinkable makes it *more* able to think of lesser objects. **Sensation is dependent on the body; thought is separable from it** (429a29–b5, PDF 44).
- When thought has become each thing it knows (first actuality → second actuality), it can think of itself; thought is then able to exercise its power on its own initiative (429b5–9, PDF 45).

#### III.5 (430a10–25)
- **In every class of things, as in nature as a whole**, there are two factors: (1) a matter that is potentially all the particulars, and (2) a cause that is productive, making them all — "as an art to its material" (430a10–14, PDF 46).
- **Active nous**: "thought, as we have described it, is what it is by virtue of becoming all things"; but there is another thought which "is what it is by virtue of making all things: this is a sort of positive state like light" — light makes potential colours into actual colours (430a14–17, PDF 46).
- **This thought is separable, impassible, unmixed**, since it is in its essential nature activity (430a17–18, PDF 46).
- "The active is always superior to the passive factor, the originating force to the matter" (430a18–19, PDF 46).
- **Actual knowledge is identical with its object.** Potential knowledge is in time prior to actual knowledge in the individual, but absolutely it is not prior even in time. Active thought does not sometimes think and sometimes not think. **When separated, it is alone just what it is, and this alone is immortal and eternal** (we do not remember because this is impassible, while passive thought is perishable); and without this, nothing thinks (430a19–25, PDF 46).

#### III.6 (430a26–431a14)
- Thinking of indivisibles: where falsehood is impossible (no combination), thought apprehends in a quasi-unity; where truth and falsehood apply, there is always a combining of objects of thought (430a26–b6, PDF 46).
- "Indivisible" is ambiguous: either "not capable of being divided" or "not actually divided"; thinking of what is undivided (e.g., a length) occupies an undivided time (430b6–20, PDF 46).
- That which cognizes must be its objects potentially, and they must be in it; that which has no contrary knows itself and is actually and possesses independent existence (430b24–26, PDF 47).
- Assertion (*kataphasis*) is the saying of something concerning something, and is true or false; but thinking of the definition (*to ti ēn einai*) is never in error, just as seeing the special object of sight is never in error (430b26–31, PDF 47).

### 2.4 The relationship between thought, perception, and imagination (III.7–8, 431a14–432a14)

#### III.7 (431a14–431b10)
- Actual knowledge is identical with its object (431a1–3, PDF 47).
- Movement is an activity of what is imperfect; perception is different — it is the activity of what has been perfected (431a4–7, PDF 47).
- To perceive is like bare asserting or thinking; when the object is pleasant or painful, the soul makes "a sort of affirmation or negation" and pursues or avoids (431a8–12, PDF 47).
- **"To the thinking soul, images serve as if they were contents of perception (and when it asserts or denies them to be good or bad it avoids or pursues them). That is why the soul never thinks without an image"** (*dio oudepote noei aneu phantasmatos hē psuchē*) (431a14–17, PDF 47).
- The process is like that in which the air modifies the pupil and the pupil transmits the modification to some third thing — **"the ultimate point of arrival is one, a single mean, with different manners of being"** (431a17–20, PDF 47).

#### III.8 (431b20–432a14)
- The so-called abstract objects: the mind thinks of the snub *qua* snub in the flesh, but of the hollow *qua* hollow without flesh — mathematical objects are thought as if they were separate though they are not (431b12–432a1, PDF 48).
- **"The soul is in a way all existing things"**: existing things are either sensible or thinkable; knowledge is in a way what is knowable, sensation is in a way what is sensible (432a1–3, PDF 48).
- Knowledge and sensation are divided to correspond with the realities: potential to potentialities, actual to actualities. The faculties of the soul are *potentially* these objects — the knowable and the sensible. They must be either the things themselves or their forms; not the things themselves (the stone is not in the soul), but their forms (432a3–10, PDF 48).
- **"The soul is analogous to the hand; for as the hand is a tool of tools, so thought is the form of forms and sense the form of sensible things"** (432a1–3, PDF 48).
- Since nothing exists outside and separate from sensible spatial magnitudes, the objects of thought are in the sensible forms. Hence no one can learn or understand anything in the absence of sense, and **when the mind is actively aware of anything it is necessarily aware of it along with an image** (*phantasma*); for images are like sensuous contents except that they contain no matter (432a3–10, PDF 48–49).

### 2.5 The parts of the soul and the problem of movement (III.9, 432a15–433a1)

- Two faculties characterize the soul of animals: (1) discrimination (thought and sense) and (2) originating local movement (432a15–18, PDF 49).
- The question of what part of the soul originates movement: is it the soul as a whole, or a separate part? (432a18–22, PDF 49).
- The division into rational and irrational is insufficient; the imaginative faculty (*to phantastikon*) is different from all parts — "in its being, different from all, while it is very hard to say with which of the others it is the same or not the same" (432b24–433a1, PDF 49).
- The appetitive faculty (*to orektikon*): wish is found in the calculative part, desire and passion in the irrational; if the soul is tripartite, appetite will be found in all three parts (432b3–7, PDF 49).

### 2.6 Desire, imagination, and the source of animal movement (III.10, 433a1–434a21)

#### III.10 (433a1–433b30)
- **Two things appear to be sources of movement: appetite and thought** (if one may venture to regard imagination as a kind of thinking) (433a9–12, PDF 50).
- In animals other than man, there is no thinking or calculation but only imagination (433a11–12, PDF 50).
- Both thought and appetite are capable of originating local movement: practical thought (which calculates means to an end) differs from speculative thought (433a13–15, PDF 50).
- **Appetite is in every form relative to an end**; the object of appetite is the stimulant of practical thought, and what is last in the process of thinking is the beginning of the action (433a15–18, PDF 50).
- The two sources of movement are **appetite and practical thought** (*orexis kai dianoia praktikē*) (433a18–20, PDF 50).
- "The object of appetite which originates movement" — **the apparent or real good** (*to phainomenon agathon*) — is the unmoved mover of animal action (433a27–29, PDF 51).
- Three factors in all movement: (1) the mover (unmoved: the good, the object of appetite); (2) that by which it originates movement (the faculty of appetite, which is itself moved); (3) that which is moved (the animal) (433b10–18, PDF 51).
- **"Inasmuch as an animal is capable of appetite it is capable of self-movement; it is not capable of appetite without possessing imagination; and all imagination is either calculative or sensitive. In the latter all animals partake"** (433b27–30, PDF 51).

### 2.7 Sensitive vs. deliberative imagination (III.11, 434a1–434a21)

- Imperfect animals (those with only touch): do they have imagination or desire? They have feelings of pleasure and pain, therefore they must have desire; but how can they have imagination? (434a1–4, PDF 51–52).
- Their movements are indefinite; therefore they have imagination and desire, but indefinitely (434a5–6, PDF 52).
- **"Sensitive imagination, as we have said, is found in all animals; deliberative imagination only in those that are calculative: for whether this or that shall be enacted is already a task requiring calculation; and there must be a single standard to measure by, for that is pursued which is greater. It follows that what acts in this way must be able to make a unity out of several images"** (434a5–10, PDF 52).
- This is why imagination does not involve opinion based on inference, though opinion involves imagination (434a10–11, PDF 52).
- Three modes of movement are possible: (1) appetite alone overriding thought; (2) appetite overcoming appetite (moral weakness, *akrasia*); (3) higher faculty overriding lower (434a12–15, PDF 52).

### 2.8 The hierarchy of soul-faculties and the necessity of touch (III.12–13, 434a22–435b25)

#### III.12 (434a22–435a10)
- The nutritive soul is possessed by everything that is alive, from birth to death (434a22–25, PDF 52).
- Sensation need not be found in all living things; bodies without matter cannot have touch, and bodies incapable of taking in forms without matter cannot have sensation (434a25–30, PDF 52).
- Animals must be endowed with sensation because Nature does nothing in vain; all the other senses (smell, sight, hearing) apprehend through media; where there is immediate contact, sensation is needed to avoid/approach (434b9–24, PDF 53).
- Touch and taste are indispensable to the animal; the other senses serve well-being rather than bare existence (435a11–b3, PDF 53–54).

#### III.13 (435a11–435b25)
- The body of an animal must be either simple or compound; simple is impossible because it could not have touch, which is indispensable (435a11–14, PDF 54).
- Touch is a mean between all tangible qualities; its organ can receive not only specific tangible qualities (hot/cold) but all tangible qualities whatsoever — that is why we perceive by means of bones, hair, etc. (composed of earth) (435a20–b1, PDF 54).
- Touch alone is necessary for being an animal; excess in tangible qualities destroys the animal itself, not merely the organ (435b4–17, PDF 54).
- The other senses are necessary not for being but for well-being: sight (for creatures in air or water), taste (for nutriment), hearing (for communication) (435b19–25, PDF 54).

## 7. Cross-Pipeline Hooks (optional)

- **BCAP (Heidegger's GA 18)**: Heidegger's 1924 lecture course *Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy* devotes extensive treatment to *aisthēsis*, *phantasia*, and *orexis* in the context of Aristotle's practical philosophy. DA III.3's definition of phantasia as *kinēsis hupo tēs aisthēseōs* is precisely the text Heidegger interprets phenomenologically in BCAP §§13–15 as the "being-disclosed" of the *Umwelt*. The distinction between sensitive and deliberative imagination (DA III.11, 434a5–10) maps onto Heidegger's analysis of *Umsicht* and *Besonnenheit*. [INTERP-high]

- **Being and Time**: Heidegger's claim that Dasein is always already "outside" alongside entities (B&T §13, H.59–62) can be read as a radicalization of DA III.8's thesis that "the soul is in a way all existing things" (432a1–3) — both deny the "cabinet" model of mind, but Heidegger replaces the form-reception schema with Being-in-the-world. The DA III.7 claim that "the soul never thinks without an image" (431a17) is a locus Heidegger repeatedly engages: *Stimmung* and *Befindlichkeit* as pre-theoretical disclosive structures parallel the role phantasia plays between aisthēsis and nous. [INTERP-high]

- **Rickert, *Ambient Rhetoric***: Rickert's concept of "ambient rhetoric" — rhetoric as environmentally embedded and affectively attuned — draws on the phantasia-orexis-kinēsis circuit of DA III.9–11 (appetite + imagination → movement). The DA III.3 insight that imagination produces affective responses only under belief conditions (427b21–24) is directly relevant to Rickert's account of how rhetorical situations "move" bodies prior to deliberative judgement. [INTERP-high]

## 8. Tensions

| tension | node_a | node_b | type | description |
|---------|--------|--------|------|-------------|
| T1: Phantasia as derivative yet autonomous | phantasia | aisthēsis | internal | Phantasia is defined as a "movement resulting from actual sense" (429a1–2, PDF 44), making it ontologically derivative from perception. Yet it can occur in dreams, in the absence of sensation (428a6–8, PDF 42), and guides animal action independently of present perception (429a4–8, PDF 44). The tension is how a causally dependent capacity achieves functional independence. Linked to DA-04-P1, DA-04-P2. |
| T2: Active nous — separable yet requiring phantasmata | active nous | phantasia | internal | Active nous is separable, impassible, immortal (430a17–22, PDF 46), yet "the soul never thinks without a phantasma" (431a17, PDF 47) and "the objects of thought are in the sensible forms" (432a5, PDF 48). If nous requires images for all its operations, in what sense is it genuinely separable from the body? The brevity and opacity of III.5 has made this the most contested passage in the entire Aristotelian corpus. Linked to DA-04-P3, DA-04-P5. |
| T3: Imagination as true-and-false vs. perception as always true | phantasia | aisthēsis | internal | Perception of proper sensibles is "never in error or admits the least possible amount of falsehood" (428b18–19, PDF 43), yet phantasia — which is *caused by* perception — is "for the most part false" (428a12, PDF 42). How does a reliable cause produce an unreliable effect? The three-tier reliability structure (428b18–429a2) partially addresses this but does not fully resolve it, since even the first (most reliable) tier only applies "while the sensation is present" — phantasia by definition operates when the object is absent or far off. Linked to DA-04-P1, DA-04-P9. |
| T4: The unity of the moving faculty | orexis | nous | internal | DA III.10 argues that appetite and practical thought are both sources of movement (433a9–20, PDF 50), yet concludes that "that which moves is therefore a single faculty — the faculty of appetite as such" (433b10, PDF 51). Thought is never found producing movement without appetite (433a22–23, PDF 50), but appetite can originate movement contrary to calculation (433b5, PDF 51). The apparent subordination of nous to orexis in the domain of action creates a tension with the general superiority of nous asserted in III.4–5. Linked to DA-04-P5, DA-04-P6. |
| T5: Deliberative vs. sensitive imagination — principled distinction or degrees? | phantasia bouleutikē | phantasia aisthētikē | internal | The distinction between sensitive imagination (all animals) and deliberative imagination (calculative animals only) at 434a5–10 (PDF 52) is introduced very briefly. It depends on the capacity to "make a unity out of several images" and to "measure by a single standard." But if sensitive imagination already involves residual perceptual content, and deliberative imagination adds only comparison and calculation, the line between them is permeable — especially in "imperfect animals" that have imagination "but indefinitely" (434a5). Linked to DA-04-P7. |
| T6: Common sense as one-but-divisible | koinē aisthēsis | individual senses | internal | The discriminating faculty is said to be "numerically one and undivided" in the time of its exercise, yet "divisible in its being" and "spatially and numerically undivided" only in a qualified sense (427a2–9, PDF 41). Aristotle uses the analogy of a point (which is both one and two, dividing and connecting), but the analogy strains under the demand that a single faculty exercise genuinely different capacities simultaneously. Linked to DA-04-P8. |
