## 1. Corpus Overview

### 1.1 Description

This corpus comprises nine treatises by Aristotle (or attributed to him) drawn from the Barnes edition of the Revised Oxford Translation. The works span the full range of Aristotle's theoretical and practical philosophy, from first philosophy (metaphysics) through natural science (physics), psychology and perception theory (De Anima and the Parva Naturalia), to the art of rhetoric. Together they constitute the primary textual foundation for a dissertation on rhetorical ontology, grounding the conceptual vocabulary of phantasia, perception, motion, substance, and persuasion in Aristotle's own systematic framework.

The corpus totals approximately 553 PDF pages across 9 works. Four major treatises (Metaphysics, Physics, Rhetoric, De Anima) account for ~487 pages; five minor works account for ~66 pages. All texts use the same translation tradition (Revised Oxford), ensuring terminological consistency across works. Two works — *On Colours* and *On Things Heard* — are of disputed authorship (traditionally attributed to the Peripatetic school rather than to Aristotle himself) and are included as peripheral, low-priority texts.

The Barnes edition preserves the Bekker pagination system in the margins, which is the canonical reference system for all Aristotle scholarship. Bekker numbers take the form `980a` (page 980, column a) with line numbers, and appear in the margins of the PDF text. The PDFs also carry the Barnes edition's running page numbers (e.g., 1552, 1553...) in the headers. Both systems are recorded here; Bekker is canonical and Barnes page numbers are secondary.

### 1.3 Thematic Clusters

The 9 works fall into 4 thematic clusters that map to different aspects of the dissertation's framework:

**Cluster 1: First Philosophy** — *Metaphysics*
- Being qua being, substance, causation, the four causes, actuality/potentiality, the unmoved mover.
- Provides the ontological ground for all other inquiries: what it means for something to be, the structure of substance, and the priority of actuality over potentiality.

**Cluster 2: Natural Philosophy** — *Physics*
- Nature, motion/change, causation, time, place, the infinite, continuity, the prime mover.
- Provides the theory of motion (kinesis) and time (chronos) that undergirds the psychology of perception and the analysis of rhetorical action.

**Cluster 3: Psychology & Perception** — *De Anima*, *Sense and Sensibilia*, *On Memory*, *Movement of Animals*, *On Colours*, *On Things Heard*
- Soul, perception, phantasia, common sense, proper and common sensibles, memory, recollection, animal motion, colour theory, acoustics.
- The core cluster for the dissertation's treatment of phantasia and the perceptual conditions of rhetorical experience.

**Cluster 4: Rhetoric** — *Rhetoric*
- Persuasion, proofs (pisteis), enthymeme, pathos, ethos, style (lexis), metaphor, "setting before the eyes."
- Provides the theory of rhetorical art that the dissertation integrates with the ontological and psychological frameworks.

### 1.4 Inter-Work Relationships

The works form a systematic network of cross-references:

- **Metaphysics <-> Physics**: The Physics presupposes metaphysical principles (substance, causation, actuality/potentiality) while the Metaphysics frequently refers to natural philosophy for examples. The unmoved mover argument appears in both (Metaphysics Lambda and Physics VIII).
- **Physics <-> De Anima**: The De Anima's definition of soul as "first actuality" (entelecheia) depends on the Physics' analysis of motion as "incomplete actuality" (energeia ateles). Time-perception in De Anima/Parva Naturalia relies on the Physics' analysis of time.
- **De Anima <-> Sense and Sensibilia / On Memory**: Sense and Sensibilia opens by explicitly continuing from De Anima ("Having now considered the soul, by itself, and its several faculties, we must next make a survey of animals..."). On Memory similarly presupposes De Anima's faculty psychology.
- **De Anima <-> Movement of Animals**: The Movement of Animals presupposes De Anima's account of phantasia and desire as movers, applying it to the practical syllogism and animal locomotion.
- **De Anima <-> Rhetoric**: The Rhetoric's account of pathos (emotions) in Book II connects to De Anima I.1's treatment of affections as "enmattered accounts" (logoi enuloi). Phantasia bridges both: it is the psychological faculty in De Anima III.3 and the rhetorical mechanism of "setting before the eyes" in Rhetoric III.
- **On Colours / On Things Heard**: These disputed works extend the sensory theory of De Anima and Sense and Sensibilia into detailed accounts of colour-mixing and acoustics. They are methodologically distinct (more empirical, less theoretical) and may not be by Aristotle.

## 2. Per-Work Summaries

### 2.1 Metaphysics (*Metaphysica*)

- **Bekker range**: 980a-1093b (confirmed: 980a visible on PDF p.3 margin; 1093b visible on PDF p.179 margin)
- **PDF pages**: 179
- **Books**: 14 (Alpha through Nu: A, alpha, B, Gamma, Delta, E, Z, H, Theta, I, K, Lambda, M, N)
- **Translator**: W. D. Ross
- **Volume**: Volume Two of the Barnes edition

**Opening thesis/question** (Book I / Alpha, 980a21): "All men by nature desire to know." Aristotle begins with an ascending scale from sensation through memory, experience, art, and science toward wisdom (sophia), which he identifies as knowledge of first causes and principles. The opening question is: what is wisdom, and what are the first causes and principles that it investigates?

**Core problematic**: What is being qua being (to on hei on)? What is substance (ousia)? What is the relationship between form and matter, actuality and potentiality? The Metaphysics pursues these questions through historical review (Books I-II), aporetic method (Book III), the science of being qua being (Book IV), a philosophical lexicon (Book V), the central books on substance (Books VII-IX), and culminates in the theology of the unmoved mover (Book XII) and critique of Platonic Forms and mathematical ontology (Books XIII-XIV).

**Key interlocutors**: Plato (throughout, especially on Forms), the Pre-Socratics (Thales, Empedocles, Anaxagoras, Democritus, Parmenides), Pythagoreans (on number as substance), Speusippus and Xenocrates (Books XIII-XIV).

**Closing passage** (Book XIV, 1093b): The work ends with a critique of Pythagorean and Platonist number-metaphysics, concluding that the objects of mathematics are not separable from sensible things and are not the first principles.

**Relationship to other works**: The Metaphysics provides the ontological framework (substance, causation, actuality/potentiality) presupposed by the Physics, De Anima, and indirectly by the Rhetoric. References to the Ethics appear in Book I (982a25). The unmoved mover of Book XII connects directly to Physics VIII.

### 2.2 Physics (*Physica*)

- **Bekker range**: 184a-267b (confirmed: 184a10 visible on PDF p.3; 265b-267b visible on PDF pp.131-134)
- **PDF pages**: 134
- **Books**: 8 (I-VIII)
- **Translator**: R. P. Hardie and R. K. Gaye
- **Volume**: Volume One of the Barnes edition

**Opening thesis/question** (Book I, 184a10): "When the objects of an inquiry, in any department, have principles, causes, or elements, it is through acquaintance with these that knowledge and understanding is attained." The Physics opens with the question of method in natural science: we must begin from what is more knowable to us and proceed toward what is more knowable by nature.

**Core problematic**: What are the principles of nature (phusis)? What is motion (kinesis), and how is it to be defined? What are place (topos), void, time (chronos), and the infinite (apeiron)? How is continuous motion possible, and what is the ultimate source of motion? The work progresses from principles (Book I) through the four causes and nature vs. art (Book II), the definition of motion and the infinite (Book III), place/void/time (Book IV), classification and continuity of motion (Books V-VI), to the argument for an eternal unmoved mover (Books VII-VIII).

**Key interlocutors**: Parmenides and Melissus (Book I, on the impossibility of motion), Empedocles (on the elements), Democritus (on void and atoms), Zeno (Book VI, on continuity and the paradoxes of motion), Anaxagoras (on Mind as first mover).

**Closing passage** (Book VIII, 267b): The work concludes with the proof that the first mover must be without parts and without magnitude — indivisible, eternal, and the cause of continuous, eternal motion.

**Relationship to other works**: Directly presupposes Metaphysics on substance and causation. The definition of motion (Physics III) as "the actuality of the potential qua potential" is foundational for De Anima's account of soul as actuality. Physics VIII's unmoved mover argument parallels Metaphysics Lambda. The treatment of time (Physics IV) is essential background for On Memory's account of temporal perception.

### 2.3 Rhetoric (*Ars Rhetorica*)

- **Bekker range**: 1354a-1420b (confirmed: 1354a1 visible on PDF p.3; 1420b1 visible on PDF p.120)
- **PDF pages**: 120
- **Books**: 3 (I-III)
- **Translator**: W. Rhys Roberts
- **Volume**: Volume Two of the Barnes edition

**Opening thesis/question** (Book I, 1354a1): "Rhetoric is the counterpart of dialectic." Aristotle opens by positioning rhetoric relative to dialectic — both deal with matters that are common knowledge and belong to no specialized science. He immediately critiques existing rhetorical handbooks for focusing on non-essentials (emotional appeals to judges) rather than the proper modes of persuasion (the enthymeme).

**Core problematic**: What is the art (techne) of rhetoric? What are the proper means of persuasion (pisteis)? Aristotle defines rhetoric as "the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion" (1355b25). Book I treats the three species of rhetoric (deliberative, forensic, epideictic) and their respective topics. Book II treats ethos (character of the speaker), pathos (emotions of the audience), and the common topics and enthymemes. Book III treats lexis (style) — including metaphor and the technique of "setting before the eyes" (pro ommaton poiein) — and taxis (arrangement).

**Key interlocutors**: Plato (implicitly, on rhetoric as mere flattery vs. genuine art), Isocrates, Gorgias, Socrates (mentioned in examples), Corax and Tisias (as founders of the art), Homer and the tragedians (as examples of style). The closing passage cites Plato's *Apology*.

**Closing passage** (Book III, 1420b1-2): "For the conclusion, the disconnected style of language is appropriate, and will mark the difference between the oration and the peroration. 'I have done. You have heard me. The facts are before you. I ask for your judgement.'"

**Relationship to other works**: The Rhetoric presupposes De Anima's faculty psychology (especially phantasia) for its account of how metaphor and vivid language work. The treatment of pathos in Rhetoric II connects to De Anima I.1 on emotions as "enmattered accounts." References to the *Topics* appear in Book I. The concept of enthymeme connects to the logical works (not in this corpus). The "setting before the eyes" technique (Rhetoric III) is a direct rhetorical application of phantasia from De Anima III.3.

### 2.4 On the Soul (*De Anima*)

- **Bekker range**: 402a-435b (confirmed: 402a1 visible on PDF p.3; 434a1-435b visible on PDF pp.51-54)
- **PDF pages**: 54
- **Books**: 3 (I-III)
- **Translator**: J. A. Smith
- **Volume**: Volume One of the Barnes edition

**Opening thesis/question** (Book I, 402a1): "Holding as we do that, while knowledge of any kind is a thing to be honoured and prized, one kind of it may, either by reason of its greater exactness or of a higher dignity and greater wonderfulness in its objects, be more honourable and precious than another, on both accounts we should naturally be led to place in the front rank the study of the soul." Aristotle raises the methodological question: what method suits the study of soul? Is soul a substance, a quality, a quantum? Does it belong to the class of potential existents or is it rather an actuality?

**Core problematic**: What is the soul (psuche)? Aristotle's answer — soul is "the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially" (412a27) — defines the treatise. Book I surveys and critiques predecessors' views. Book II defines soul and its faculties (nutritive, perceptive, intellectual), then analyzes the five senses and their objects (proper sensibles, common sensibles, incidental sensibles). Book III treats the common sense, phantasia (III.3 — the crucial chapter for the dissertation), nous (intellect), and the relationship between desire, phantasia, and animal motion.

**Key interlocutors**: Plato (on soul as self-moving), Empedocles (like perceives like), Democritus (soul as atoms), Anaxagoras (nous as unmixed), Heraclitus (soul as fire). The opening explicitly notes the difficulty of the subject: "To attain any knowledge about the soul is one of the most difficult things in the world" (402a10).

**Closing passage** (Book III, ch. 13, 435b): The work concludes with the argument that touch is the indispensable sense for animal survival (all animals must have touch), while the other senses serve well-being rather than mere being. Hearing enables communication and thus contributes to intelligence.

**Relationship to other works**: De Anima is the central psychological treatise. It is presupposed by Sense and Sensibilia, On Memory, and Movement of Animals (all explicitly continue its investigations). Its definition of soul as "first actuality" depends on the Metaphysics/Physics framework of actuality/potentiality. Phantasia (III.3) is the key bridge concept to Rhetoric III's "setting before the eyes." The treatment of emotions as "enmattered accounts" (I.1, 403a25) bridges to Rhetoric II's analysis of pathos.

### 2.5 Sense and Sensibilia (*De Sensu et Sensibilibus*)

- **Bekker range**: 436a-449b (confirmed: 436a1 visible on PDF p.3; 449b1 visible on PDF p.23)
- **PDF pages**: 23
- **Chapters**: 7
- **Translator**: J. I. Beare
- **Volume**: Volume One of the Barnes edition

**Opening thesis/question** (436a1): "Having now considered the soul, by itself, and its several faculties, we must next make a survey of animals and all living things, in order to ascertain what functions are peculiar, and what functions are common, to them." The work explicitly continues from De Anima, turning to the sensory attributes of animals: sensation, memory, passion, appetite, desire, pleasure, pain, waking/sleeping, youth/old age, inhalation/exhalation, life/death.

**Core problematic**: What are the objects of each sense? How do the sensory media (the transparent, air, water) function? What is colour, and how does vision work? The work provides detailed analysis of the five senses and their objects, the role of media in perception, and the problem of simultaneous perception. Chapters 1-4 treat sight (colour, the transparent, light), sound, smell. Chapters 5-7 treat taste, touch, common sensibles, and the unity of perception.

**Key interlocutors**: Empedocles (on vision as fire from the eye, with his Lantern simile at 437b-438a1), Democritus (on vision as water/reflection), Plato's *Timaeus* (on the visual ray).

**Closing passage** (Ch. 7, 449b): Concludes the survey of sense-organs and their objects, noting that "of the remaining subjects, we must first consider that of memory and remembering" — transitioning directly to *On Memory*.

**Relationship to other works**: Directly continues De Anima, presupposing its faculty psychology. Transitions explicitly to On Memory at its conclusion. The treatment of colour and the transparent (to diaphanes) connects to On Colours (though the latter is a separate, disputed work). The analysis of common sensibles connects to De Anima III.1.

### 2.6 On Memory (*De Memoria et Reminiscentia*)

- **Bekker range**: 449b-453b (confirmed: 449b visible near start; 453b1 visible on PDF p.9)
- **PDF pages**: 9
- **Chapters**: 2
- **Translator**: J. I. Beare
- **Volume**: Volume One of the Barnes edition

**Opening thesis/question** (449b1): "We have to treat of memory and remembering, considering its nature, its cause, and the part of the soul to which this experience, as well as that of recollecting, belongs." The opening distinguishes memory from recollection: those with retentive memory are not identical with those who excel in recollection; slow people often have better memory while quick-witted people are better at recollecting.

**Core problematic**: What is memory, and how does it differ from recollection? Memory is defined as "a state or affection, conditioned by lapse of time" (449b25) — it is always of the past, never of the present (which is perception) or the future (which is expectation). The critical passage for the dissertation is the claim that "the subject of imagination has been already considered in our work *On the Soul*. Without an image thinking is impossible" (450a1). Memory belongs to the faculty of phantasia; its vehicle is the phantasma (image). Chapter 2 treats recollection (anamnesis) as a distinct process: active, deliberate searching through associated movements/images, starting from a starting-point and tracing connections.

**Key interlocutors**: Few named — the work is largely constructive rather than dialectical. References to geometric demonstrations (memory as analogous to mathematical reasoning about proportions).

**Closing passage** (Ch. 2, 453b): "Such then is our theory as regards memory and remembering — their nature, and the particular organ of the soul by which animals remember; also as regards recollection, its definition, and the manner and causes of its performance."

**Relationship to other works**: Directly presupposes De Anima (especially the treatment of phantasia in III.3) and continues from Sense and Sensibilia. The claim that "without an image thinking is impossible" echoes De Anima III.7, 431a16-17 ("the soul never thinks without a phantasma"). The temporal dimension of memory connects to Physics IV's analysis of time. The wax-impression analogy for memory (450a30-b1) is a concrete application of De Anima's account of perception as "receiving the form without the matter."

### 2.7 Movement of Animals (*De Motu Animalium*)

- **Bekker range**: 698a-704b (confirmed: 698a1 visible on PDF p.3; 704b1 visible on PDF p.12)
- **PDF pages**: 12
- **Chapters**: 11
- **Translator**: A. S. L. Farquharson (text: M. C. Nussbaum, Princeton UP, 1978)
- **Volume**: Volume One of the Barnes edition

**Opening thesis/question** (698a1): "Elsewhere we have investigated the movement of animals after their various kinds, the differences between them, and the causes of their particular characters... there remains an investigation of the common cause of any sort of animal movement whatsoever." The work opens by noting it presupposes earlier discussions of eternal motion and the unmoved mover (a cross-reference to Physics VIII).

**Core problematic**: What is the common cause of animal movement? The work argues that all animal motion requires (1) an immovable point of support, (2) desire or phantasia as the psychological mover, and (3) the practical syllogism as the logical structure of action. Chapters 6-7 present the practical syllogism: the animal has a desire (major premise), perceives or imagines a particular situation (minor premise), and acts. Phantasia functions as the bridge between desire and action — "on this account thinking that one ought to go and going are virtually simultaneous, unless there be something else to hinder action" (701a15-20). Chapter 10 introduces the concept of "connate spirit" (sumphutos pneuma) as the bodily instrument of soul-caused motion.

**Key interlocutors**: Homer (*Iliad* VIII.20-22, on Zeus pulling the chain — used as an illustration of the unmoved mover's causal power). Cross-references to Physics VIII and *On the Heavens* I.

**Closing passage** (Ch. 11, 704b): "And so we have finished our account of the reasons for the parts of each kind of animal, of the soul, and further of sense-perception, of sleep, of memory, and of movement in general; it remains to speak of generation."

**Relationship to other works**: Directly presupposes De Anima's account of phantasia and desire. The practical syllogism (chs. 6-7) is the key application of De Anima III.10-11's account of how desire and phantasia cause motion. The unmoved mover argument (ch. 1-4) connects to Physics VIII and Metaphysics Lambda. The distinction between sensitive and deliberative imagination (ch. 8, from De Anima III.11) is applied here to explain how higher animals can act on calculated future goods.

### 2.8 On Colours (*De Coloribus*) [DISPUTED]

- **Bekker range**: 791a-799b (confirmed: 791a1 visible on PDF p.3; 799b visible on final pages)
- **PDF pages**: 12
- **Chapters**: 6
- **Translator**: T. Loveday and E. S. Forster (text: C. Prantl, Teubner, Leipzig, 1881)
- **Volume**: Volume One of the Barnes edition
- **Authorship**: Disputed. Title page marked with ** asterisks. Traditionally attributed to the Peripatetic school, possibly Strato of Lampsacus or Theophrastus.

**Opening thesis/question** (791a1): "Simple colours are those which belong to the elements, i.e. to fire, air, water, and earth." The work begins by assigning basic colours to the elements: fire/sun = white, earth = white naturally, water = white by nature. It then proceeds to explain colour-mixing, darkness, light, and the generation of derivative colours.

**Core problematic**: How are colours produced from the simple/elemental colours? The treatise is thoroughly empirical in method — it proceeds by observation of natural phenomena (ash, smoke, plants ripening, animal colouring, dyes) rather than by the theoretical analysis characteristic of Aristotle's own style. It treats: simple colours (ch. 1), colour-mixing and blending (ch. 2), conditions affecting colour (light, shade, strength — ch. 3), colour changes in materials (ch. 4), colour in plants and their maturation (ch. 5), colour in animal hair, feathers, and hides (ch. 6).

**Key interlocutors**: None named. The work is entirely constructive and empirical.

**Closing passage** (Ch. 6, ~799b): "From what has been set forth in this treatise one may best understand the theory of colours."

**Relationship to other works**: Extends the colour theory of Sense and Sensibilia (chs. 1-3) and De Anima II.7 (on colour as the object of sight and the role of the transparent medium). However, its empirical method and lack of theoretical depth mark it as distinct from Aristotle's own approach. Low priority for the dissertation.

### 2.9 On Things Heard (*De Audibilibus*) [DISPUTED]

- **Bekker range**: 800a-804b (confirmed: 800a1 visible on PDF p.3; 804b1 visible on PDF pp.9-10)
- **PDF pages**: 10
- **Chapters**: 1 (continuous treatise, no chapter divisions)
- **Translator**: T. Loveday and E. S. Forster (text: C. Prantl, Teubner, Leipzig, 1881)
- **Volume**: Volume One of the Barnes edition
- **Authorship**: Disputed. Title page marked with ** asterisks. Traditionally attributed to the Peripatetic school, possibly Strato.

**Opening thesis/question** (800a1): "All sounds, whether articulate or inarticulate, are produced by the meeting of bodies or of the air with bodies, not because the air assumes certain shapes, as some people think, but because it is set in motion." The work begins with a materialist/mechanical account of sound production.

**Core problematic**: How are sounds produced, transmitted, and differentiated? The treatise gives a detailed account of the physics of sound: the role of breath, the windpipe, the mouth in voice production; the conditions of vocal clarity, hardness, roughness, thinness; the differences between voices and instrumental sounds. It also treats the perception of sound — clearness, continuity, distance effects, concords. The analogy between clearness in sound and clearness in colour (801a1) bridges auditory and visual perception.

**Key interlocutors**: None named. Purely constructive and empirical.

**Closing passage** (~804b): The work ends with a discussion of stammering — it is due not to an affection of the veins or windpipe but to the difficulty of changing the position of the tongue to produce a second sound.

**Relationship to other works**: Extends De Anima II.8 (on sound as the object of hearing) and Sense and Sensibilia (on the auditory sense). The cross-modal analogy between sound and colour connects to De Anima III.1's treatment of common sensibles. Low priority for the dissertation; of interest only insofar as it illustrates the Peripatetic approach to sensory analysis.

## 3. Confirmed PDF Offsets

### 3.1 Offset Methodology

Each PDF is an extract from the Barnes Complete Works. Pages 1-2 of each PDF are the Barnes title page and copyright page. The text begins on PDF page 3. The Barnes edition uses a continuous page numbering across the complete works (e.g., Metaphysics pages are numbered 1552-1728, Physics pages 315-446). Bekker numbers appear in the margins as the canonical reference system.

The "offset" for each work is the relationship between the Bekker page numbers and the PDF page numbers. Since Bekker numbers are not simple page numbers but page-column references (e.g., 980a = page 980 in the Bekker edition, column a), the offset is best understood as: **what Bekker number appears on which PDF page**.

## 4. Provisional Concept List Refinement

Starting from the 50 provisional concepts in the plan, the following additions and annotations are based on observations from the PDF survey.

### Original 50 Concepts: CONFIRMED

All 50 concepts from the plan are confirmed as present and relevant. No removals.

### Additions (6 new concepts)

51. **πνεῦμα σύμφυτον** (*pneuma sumphutos*) — connate/innate spirit. Appears in Movement of Animals ch. 10 (703a1) as the bodily instrument through which soul causes animal motion. Bridge concept between soul-as-form and body-as-matter in the context of locomotion.

52. **τὸ πρακτόν** (*to prakton*) / **πρᾶξις** (*praxis*) — the practicable / action. Central to Movement of Animals' practical syllogism. The practical syllogism concludes in action, not in a proposition. Connects desire + phantasia to bodily motion.

53. **μορφή** (*morphē*) — shape/form. Appears alongside eidos but with subtly different usage (morphe tends to denote the visible, external shape; eidos the intelligible form). Important for the hylomorphism of Metaphysics VII and the perception theory of De Anima (receiving the form without the matter).

54. **τὸ διαφανές** (*to diaphanes*) — the transparent/diaphanous medium. Already listed as #36 in the original plan but worth annotation: confirmed as central in Sense and Sensibilia chs. 1-3 and De Anima II.7. The transparent medium is what makes colour visible; it actualizes under the influence of light.

55. **εἰκών** (*eikōn*) — image/likeness. Distinct from phantasma. Appears in On Memory (450b-451a) in the discussion of the wax-impression analogy and the painting analogy (a picture is both an object of contemplation in itself and a likeness/reminder of something else). Important for the dissertation's account of how phantasmata function as both present images and representations of absent things.

56. **συλλογισμὸς πρακτικός** (*sullogismos praktikos*) — practical syllogism. The logical structure of action in Movement of Animals chs. 6-7. Major premise = desire/universal rule; minor premise = particular perception/imagination; conclusion = action. Bridges phantasia and motion.

### Annotations on Existing Concepts

- **#42 πάθος** (*pathos*): Confirmed as operating across multiple registers — Rhetoric II (emotions as judgments with cognitive conditions), De Anima I.1 (affections as enmattered accounts, logoi enuloi — 403a25), Movement of Animals (corporeal affections accompanying desires). The cross-work polysemy of this term is significant.
- **#23 φαντασία** (*phantasia*): Confirmed as appearing in De Anima III.3 (the foundational treatment), On Memory (phantasma as vehicle of memory), Movement of Animals chs. 6-8 (phantasia as practical mover), and implicitly in Rhetoric III (pro ommaton poiein). The distinction between sensitive (aisthētikē) and deliberative (bouleutikē) imagination from De Anima III.11 is applied in Movement of Animals ch. 8.
- **#6 ἐνέργεια** and **#8 ἐντελέχεια**: These two terms need careful tracking across works. In Metaphysics Theta, energeia is actuality as opposed to dunamis. In De Anima II.1, entelecheia is used for the soul's relation to the body ("first actuality"). In Physics III, energeia ateles (incomplete actuality) defines motion. The terminological variation matters for the dissertation.

**Revised total: 55 provisional concepts** (50 original + 5 genuinely new; #54 was already listed as #36, so net addition is 5).

## 5. Provisional Tensions Refinement

### Original 12 Tensions: CONFIRMED

All 12 tensions from the plan are confirmed as present and well-motivated. No removals.

### Additions (3 new tensions)

13. **Phantasma as image vs. phantasma as likeness** — In On Memory (450b20-451a15), Aristotle uses the painting analogy: the same mental image can be contemplated as an object in itself (a mere thought/image) or as a likeness of something else (a reminder). This duality of the phantasma — present object vs. representation of an absent thing — is structurally important for the dissertation's account of how phantasia mediates between perception and thought, and between present and past.

14. **Practical syllogism: universal desire vs. particular perception** — In Movement of Animals chs. 6-7, the practical syllogism shows how a universal desire ("I should drink") combines with a particular phantasia/perception ("this is something to drink") to produce action. The tension is between the universality of the major premise (which belongs to desire/thought) and the particularity of the minor premise (which belongs to perception/phantasia). This mirrors the broader tension in Aristotle between the universal and the particular.

15. **Connate spirit (pneuma) as bodily vs. soul-caused** — Movement of Animals ch. 10 introduces pneuma sumphutos as the instrument by which the soul moves the body. But pneuma is bodily (it has the characteristics of the fiery element), while the soul is defined as form/actuality. The tension is: how does a formal/actual principle (soul) cause change through a material instrument (pneuma)? This is a local instance of the general hylomorphic problem.

### Annotations on Existing Tensions

- **Tension #7 (pathos as cognitive vs. bodily)**: Confirmed with specific textual evidence. De Anima I.1, 403a25-b19 presents the paradigmatic statement: anger is both "a boiling of the blood around the heart" (the physicist's definition) and "a desire for retaliation" (the dialectician's definition). These are logoi enuloi — enmattered accounts. Rhetoric II defines emotions in cognitive terms (anger = a desire for perceived revenge upon a perceived slight). The tension is real and productive.
- **Tension #4 (phantasia vs. aisthesis)**: Confirmed and sharpened. De Anima III.3 argues that phantasia is distinct from perception: perception of proper objects is always true, but phantasia can be false. Yet phantasia cannot exist without perception. On Memory adds a temporal dimension: memory-phantasmata represent past perceptions, so phantasia inherits content from perception but deploys it in the absence of the perceived object.

**Revised total: 15 provisional tensions** (12 original + 3 new).
