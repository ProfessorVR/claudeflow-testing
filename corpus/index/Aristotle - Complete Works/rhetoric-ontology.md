## Section 3B: Internal Trajectory

The *Rhetoric* exhibits a tripartite macrostructure corresponding to three dimensions of rhetorical competence:

### Book I: Invention -- The Matter of Persuasion
Book I establishes rhetoric's identity (counterpart of dialectic), defines its function (observing available means of persuasion), classifies the three technical proofs (ethos, pathos, logos), identifies the enthymeme as the body of proof, and then furnishes the material content (topoi) for each of the three species: deliberative (I.4--8: happiness, the good, forms of government), epideictic (I.9: excellence, the noble), and forensic (I.10--14: wrong-doing, pleasure, justice, equity). The non-technical proofs close Book I (I.15).

### Book II: Character, Emotion, Argument
Book II completes the analysis of the three means of persuasion. II.1 recapitulates and identifies the three sources of character-trust (φρόνησις, ἀρετή, εὔνοια). II.2--11 provide the systematic catalogue of emotions (πάθη), each defined under three heads. II.12--17 catalogue character types by age and fortune. II.18--26 supply the common topics (possibility, fact, greatness), the doctrine of example and maxim, the twenty-eight lines of enthymeme, the ten spurious enthymemes, and the methods of refutation.

### Book III: Style and Arrangement
Book III turns from what to say to how to say it. III.1--7 treat style (λέξις): clarity as foundational virtue, metaphor as supreme instrument, naturalness as governing principle, correctness, impressiveness, and appropriateness. III.8--12 treat prose rhythm, periodic style, and the distinction between spoken and written style. III.10--11 develop the doctrine of πρὸ ὀμμάτων ποιεῖν (setting before the eyes) through ἐνέργεια. III.13--19 treat arrangement (τάξις): introduction, narration, proof, epilogue.

### Trajectory of Key Concepts

**ῥητορική**: Defined relationally in I.1 (counterpart of dialectic), functionally in I.2 (faculty of observing means of persuasion), then progressively filled with content through the material topoi (I.4--15), the emotional and character analyses (II.1--17), the argumentative resources (II.18--26), and the formal properties of style and arrangement (III.1--19).

**πίστις**: Introduced in I.1 as the technical focus, taxonomized in I.2 (ethos/pathos/logos), then expanded across three Books: ethos receives its content in II.1 + II.12--17, pathos in II.2--11, logos in II.18--26 (enthymeme, example, maxim).

**ἐνθύμημα**: Named in I.1 as the "body of persuasion," given its material basis (probabilities and signs) in I.2, then fully developed in II.22--25 with twenty-eight lines, ten spurious lines, and refutation methods.

**λέξις / μεταφορά**: Absent from Books I--II, introduced in III.1 as the second point of study. Metaphor emerges as the supreme stylistic instrument in III.2, then culminates in the πρὸ ὀμμάτων ποιεῖν doctrine of III.10--11. The trajectory moves from clarity (III.2) through correctness (III.5) and impressiveness (III.6) to the synthesis of appropriateness (III.7).

**πάθος**: Named as the second proof in I.2, given a brief functional role in I.10--11 (pleasure and wrong-doing), then systematically developed in II.2--11 through the twelve-emotion catalogue. Returns in III.7 (style must express emotion) and III.19 (epilogue must excite emotion).

## Section 3D: Work-Level Tensions

### T1. Rhetoric as morally neutral techne vs. rhetoric as truth-directed
Aristotle concedes rhetoric can be misused (1355b2--4) yet insists "truth and justice are naturally stronger" (1355a21). The work oscillates between treating rhetoric as a value-neutral capacity and as inherently oriented toward truth. The naturalness principle (III.2: art concealing art) deepens this tension: the most truthful-seeming speech is the most artfully constructed.

### T2. Ethos in the speech vs. ethos from reputation
Aristotle insists character must be "achieved by what the speaker says" (1356a8--10), but the character-type catalogues (II.12--17) and the appropriateness doctrine (III.7) presuppose stable character-classes (age, fortune, nationality) that the audience brings to the encounter.

### T3. Enthymeme as logical demonstration vs. rhetoric as domain of the contingent
The enthymeme is a "sort of deduction" (1355a8), yet rhetoric deals exclusively with contingent matters (1357a22--30). This creates a structural tension: rhetorical proof aspires to logical form but operates on material that resists it. Probabilities are "for the most part" true; signs are refutable.

### T4. Metaphor as unteachable genius vs. systematic classification
Aristotle claims metaphor "is not a thing whose use can be taught by one man to another" (1405a9), yet the *Rhetoric* and *Poetics* provide detailed taxonomies (genus-to-species, species-to-genus, proportional). The teachability of the unteachable is a constitutive paradox.

### T5. Clarity vs. distinction in prose style
Clarity requires current, ordinary words (κύρια ὀνόματα); distinction requires unfamiliar, metaphorical words. Aristotle resolves this by claiming metaphor achieves both simultaneously (1405a8), but the four frigidities (III.3) demonstrate how easily the balance collapses.

### T6. The exhaustiveness of the three species
The deliberative/forensic/epideictic division rests on three audience roles in 4th-century Athenian institutions (1358a36--b8). Whether these exhaust the possible functions of rhetoric is an open question that later rhetorical tradition (letter-writing, homiletics, digital rhetoric) has repeatedly pressed.

### T7. Emotion as irrational affect vs. emotion as structured judgement
The emotions are "attended by pain or pleasure" (1378a20), suggesting affective, non-rational states, yet each is analysed through cognitive components (beliefs about desert, persons, grounds), implying propositional structure. This tension anticipates the modern debate between cognitivist and non-cognitivist theories of emotion.
