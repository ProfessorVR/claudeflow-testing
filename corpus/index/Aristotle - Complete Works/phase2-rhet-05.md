# Phase 2 Analysis: RHET-05 -- Style (lexis), Metaphor, Prose Rhythm

**Unit**: RHET-05 (Rhetoric III.1--III.7, Bekker 1403b--1408b, PDF pp. 88--98)
**Source**: Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, trans. W. Rhys Roberts, in *The Complete Works of Aristotle: The Revised Oxford Translation*, ed. Jonathan Barnes, Bollingen Series LXXI-2, 2 vols. (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1984), 2:2237--2246.
**Date**: 2026-03-09
**Tier**: Critical
**Dissertation-critical**: Yes -- lexis, metaphor, pro ommaton poiein

**One-sentence thesis**: Aristotle establishes that the excellence of prose style (lexis) consists in clarity achieved through current words combined with distinction achieved through metaphor, that metaphor operates by "setting before the eyes" (pro ommaton poiein) through the transfer of alien names that reveal likeness in unlike things, and that the formal properties of prose -- rhythm (especially the paean), correctness, impressiveness, and appropriateness to subject and character -- constitute the conditions under which language becomes persuasive by appearing natural rather than artificial.

## 1. Chapter-by-Chapter Summary

### III.1. The Three Points of Study: Persuasion, Language, and Arrangement (1403b--1404a)

Aristotle opens Book III by identifying three points that must be studied in making a speech: first, the means of producing persuasion; second, the language (lexis); third, the proper arrangement (taxis) of the parts of the speech (1403b6--8; Barnes 2237). The first has already been treated in Books I--II. The second -- how to set facts out in language -- is a matter that "affects the success of a speech greatly; but hitherto the subject has been neglected" (1403b20--22; Barnes 2238). Aristotle traces the origin of style-consciousness to poetry: "It was naturally the poets who first set the movement going; for words represent things, and they had also the human voice at their disposal" (1404a20--21; Barnes 2239). Delivery (hypokrisis) -- the management of the voice to express emotions -- is acknowledged as powerful but dismissed as unworthy of systematic study; it is "not regarded as an elevated subject of inquiry" (1404a1; Barnes 2238). Still, since "the whole business of rhetoric is concerned with appearances, we must pay attention to the subject of delivery, unworthy though it is" (1404a3--5; Barnes 2238). The arts of language cannot help having real importance: "the way in which a thing is said does affect its intelligibility" (1404a8--10; Barnes 2238). The language of prose is distinct from that of poetry: tragedy adopted iambics because they are "the most prose-like of all metres" (1404a30--32; Barnes 2239), and prose must confine itself to the part of language that concerns rhetoric, not the whole question of poetic diction treated in the *Art of Poetry*.

### III.2. The Virtues of Prose Style: Clarity, Appropriateness, Metaphor (1404b--1405b)

This chapter is the programmatic statement on prose excellence. Aristotle declares that "the stipulation that language to be good must be clear" is foundational, "as is proved by the fact that speech which fails to convey a plain meaning will fail to do just what speech has to do" (1404b1--3; Barnes 2239). Style must also be "appropriate, avoiding both meanness and undue evaluation" (1404b3--4; Barnes 2239). Clearness is secured by using "the words (nouns and verbs alike) that are current and ordinary" (1404b5--6; Barnes 2239). Freedom from meanness and positive adornment are secured by using unfamiliar words -- strange words, compound words, and above all metaphorical words -- as described in the *Art of Poetry* (1404b8--10; Barnes 2239). Naturalness (to phainesthai) is persuasive; artificiality is the contrary. The writer "must disguise his art and give the impression of speaking naturally and not artificially" (1404b18--19; Barnes 2239). We can hide our purpose by "taking the single words of our composition from the speech of ordinary life," as Euripides was the first to do in poetry (1404b24--25; Barnes 2239--2240).

There are two classes of terms in prose: "the proper or regular and the metaphorical -- these and no others -- are used by everybody in conversation" (1404b35--36; Barnes 2240). Synonyms are useful to the poet; metaphor is of "great value both in poetry and in prose" because prose-writers' other resources are scantier (1405a5--7; Barnes 2240). "Metaphor, moreover, gives style clearness, charm, and distinction as nothing else can: and it is not a thing whose use can be taught by one man to another" (1405a8--10; Barnes 2240). Metaphors must be fitting -- "they must fairly correspond to the thing signified: failing this, their inappropriateness will be conspicuous" (1405a10--12; Barnes 2240). The metaphor must not be far-fetched but drawn "from kindred and similar things, so that the kinship is clearly perceived as soon as the words are said" (1405a35--1405b1; Barnes 2241). Good riddles furnish good metaphors: "for metaphors imply riddles, and therefore a good riddle can furnish a good metaphor" (1405b5--6; Barnes 2241). The materials of metaphor "must be beautiful; and the beauty, like the ugliness, of all words may, as Licymnius says, lie in their sound or in their meaning" (1405b7--9; Barnes 2241). "The materials of metaphor must be beautiful to the ear, to the understanding, to the eye or some other physical sense" (1405b18--20; Barnes 2241).

### III.3. Frigidities of Style (1405b--1406b)

Aristotle identifies four forms of "frigidity" (psychrotēs) in language -- faults that destroy the warmth and vividness of good style. The first is the misuse of compound words: Lycophron talks of "the many-visaged heaven" and "the giant-crested earth"; Gorgias speaks of "pauper-poet flatterer" and "oath-breaking and ever-oath-keeping" (1405b35--1406a1; Barnes 2241--2242). The second is the employment of strange words (glōttai): Lycophron calls Xerxes "the towering," Alcidamas calls philosophy "a bulwark of the laws" (1406a7--10; Barnes 2242). The third is the use of "long, unseasonable, or frequent epithets": while appropriate in poetry ("white milk"), in prose such epithets "are sometimes lacking in appropriateness or, when spread too thickly, plainly reveal the author turning his prose into poetry" (1406a12--16; Barnes 2242). Alcidamas's epithets are singled out as "so numerous and swollen and obtrusive" that they function "as the seasoning of the meat, but as the meat itself" (1406a19--21; Barnes 2242). The fourth form of frigidity is metaphor: "Metaphors like other things may be inappropriate," either because they are "ridiculous" (as those used by comic and tragic poets alike), or because they are "too grand and theatrical," or because they are "far-fetched" and "may also be obscure" (1406b5--8; Barnes 2243). Gorgias is cited as the paradigmatic offender: calling events "green and full of sap" and saying "foul was the deed you sowed and evil the harvest you reaped" is "too much like poetry" (1406b9--11; Barnes 2243).

### III.4. Simile (1406b--1407a)

Aristotle defines the simile (eikōn) as a species of metaphor: "The simile also is a metaphor; the difference is but slight" (1406b20; Barnes 2243). When the poet says "He leapt on the foe as a lion," this is a simile; when he says of him "the lion leapt," it is a metaphor -- "here, since both are courageous, he has transferred to Achilles the name of 'lion'" (1406b22--25; Barnes 2243). Similes are useful in prose as well as verse, "but not often, since they are of the nature of poetry" (1406b25--26; Barnes 2243). The following examples illustrate the device: Androtion said of Idrieus that he was "like a terrier let off the chain" (1406b30; Barnes 2243); Theodamas compared Archidamus to "a Euxenus who could not do geometry -- a proportional simile" (1406b31--32; Barnes 2243). The proportional metaphor "must always apply reciprocally to either of its co-ordinate terms" -- if a drinking bowl is the shield of Dionysus, a shield may fittingly be called the drinking-bowl of Ares (1407a15--17; Barnes 2244). "All these ideas may be expressed either as similes or as metaphors; those which succeed as metaphors will obviously do well also as similes" (1407a11--13; Barnes 2243).

### III.5. The Foundation of Good Style: Correctness (1407a--1407b)

Aristotle identifies five heads under which "the foundation of good style" -- correctness (to hellēnizein) -- falls (1407a19--20; Barnes 2244). First, the proper use of connecting words (syndesmoi): the connective men requires the correlative de, and "the answering word must be brought in before the first has been forgotten, and not be widely separated from it" (1407a23--25; Barnes 2244). Second, calling things by their own special names and not by vague general ones. Third, avoiding ambiguities, "unless, indeed, you definitely desire to be ambiguous, as those do who have nothing to say but are pretending to mean something" (1407a32--35; Barnes 2244). Empedocles is cited: "by his long circumlocutions he imposes on his hearers; these are affected in the same way as most people are when they listen to diviners" (1407a35--b1; Barnes 2244). Fourth, observing Protagoras's classification of nouns as masculine, feminine, and neuter. Fifth, expressing the singular and the plural by the correct wording (1407b6--8; Barnes 2244). A general rule: "a written composition should be easy to read and therefore easy to deliver" -- this is impossible "where there are many connecting words or clauses, or where punctuation is hard, as in the writings of Heracleitus" (1407b11--14; Barnes 2244).

### III.6. Impressiveness of Language (1407b--1408a)

Six suggestions for giving language "impressiveness" (onkos). First, describe a thing instead of naming it: "do not say 'circle,' but 'that surface which extends equally from the middle every way'" (1407b26--28; Barnes 2245). To achieve conciseness, do the opposite -- use the name instead of the description. Second, when mentioning anything ugly or unseemly, use its name if the description is ugly, and use the description if the name is ugly (1407b29--31; Barnes 2245). Third, represent things with the help of metaphors and epithets, "being careful to avoid poetical effects" (1407b33; Barnes 2245). Fourth, use plural for singular (as in poetry). Fifth, do not bracket two words under one article but put one article with each: "tēs hēmeteras gynaikōs" not "tēs gynaikōs tēs hēmeteras" (1407b35--1408a1; Barnes 2245). Conversely, to secure conciseness, reverse this. Sixth, use plenty of connecting words; conversely, to secure conciseness, dispense with connectives while still preserving connexion (1408a1--5; Barnes 2245). The practice of Antimachus is also noted: "to describe a thing by mentioning attributes it does not possess" (1408a5--7; Barnes 2245). Expressions formed through negation produce proportional metaphors, "as when the trumpet's note is called 'a lyreless melody'" (1408a9--10; Barnes 2245).

### III.7. Appropriateness of Style: Emotion, Character, and Subject (1408a--1408b)

Aristotle states the governing principle: "Your language will be appropriate (prepon) if it expresses emotion and character, and if it corresponds to its subject" (1408a10--11; Barnes 2245). Correspondence to subject means "that we must neither speak casually about weighty matters, nor solemnly about trivial ones; nor must we add ornamental epithets to commonplace nouns, or the effect will be comic" (1408a12--14; Barnes 2245). To express emotion, one employs "the language of anger in speaking of outrage; the language of disgust and discreet reluctance to utter a word when speaking of impiety or foulness; the language of exultation for a tale of glory, and that of humiliation for a tale of pity" (1408a16--20; Barnes 2246).

The "aptness of language" makes people "believe in the truth of your story": they draw the false conclusion that the speaker is trustworthy from the fact that others behave as he describes them (1408a20--23; Barnes 2246). An emotional speaker "always makes his audience feel with him, even when there is nothing in his arguments" (1408a24--25; Barnes 2246). Character is expressed by matching language to class: "Under 'class' I include differences of age, as boy, man, or old man; of sex, as man or woman; of nationality, as Spartan or Thessalian" (1408a28--30; Barnes 2246). By "dispositions" Aristotle means "those only which determine the character of a man's life" (1408a30--31; Barnes 2246). A rustic and an educated man will not say the same things nor speak in the same way (1408a33; Barnes 2246).

All variations of oratorical style can be "used in season and out of season" (1408b1; Barnes 2246). The best corrective for any exaggeration is the "well-worn device" of self-criticism: "for then people feel it must be all right for him to talk thus, since he certainly knows what he is doing" (1408b2--4; Barnes 2246). Not everything should correspond at once; art must be hidden: "if you adopt one device and not another, you are using art all the same and yet nobody notices it" (1408b7--9; Barnes 2246). Compound words, plentiful epithets, and strange words "best suit an emotional speech" (1408b11; Barnes 2246). Such heightened language is excused "when the speaker has his hearers already in his hands and has stirred them deeply either by praise or blame or anger or affection" (1408b14--15; Barnes 2246), as Isocrates does at the end of his *Panegyric*.

## 3. Positions (Hermeneutic Claims)

### P1. The Foundational Virtue of Style Is Clarity

Aristotle holds that clarity (saphēneia) is not merely one virtue among several but the constitutive condition of linguistic excellence: "the stipulation that language to be good must be clear" is grounded in the functional nature of speech itself -- "speech which fails to convey a plain meaning will fail to do just what speech has to do" (1404b1--3; Barnes 2239). This position grounds the entire treatment of lexis in the communicative function of logos rather than in ornamental aesthetics.

### P2. Metaphor Is the Supreme Instrument of Prose Style

Metaphor alone gives "clearness, charm, and distinction as nothing else can" (1405a8--9; Barnes 2240), and "it is not a thing whose use can be taught by one man to another" (1405a9--10; Barnes 2240). Metaphor occupies a privileged position because it is the only device that is simultaneously ordinary (everybody uses metaphors in conversation) and extraordinary (it introduces the unfamiliar through the familiar). The "good metaphor" implies "an intuitive perception of the similarity in dissimilars" (cf. *Poetics* 1459a7--8), a capacity Aristotle treats as a mark of natural genius.

### P3. Naturalness Is the Principle of Persuasive Style

Aristotle argues that "naturalness is persuasive, artificiality is the contrary; for our hearers are prejudiced and think we have some design against them, as if we were mixing their wines for them" (1404b18--21; Barnes 2239). The art of rhetoric must therefore conceal itself: the writer must "disguise his art and give the impression of speaking naturally" (1404b18--19; Barnes 2239). This principle -- art concealing art -- governs the entire treatment of lexis and is restated at III.7 (1408b7--9; Barnes 2246): "if you adopt one device and not another, you are using art all the same and yet nobody notices it."

### P4. Prose Style Is Distinct from Poetic Style but Derived from It

Aristotle argues that the poets "first set the movement going" in stylistic consciousness (1404a20; Barnes 2239), that prose adopted the iambic metre because it is "the most prose-like of all metres" (1404a30--32; Barnes 2239), and that the language of prose "is distinct from that of poetry" (1404a28--29; Barnes 2239). Prose style occupies a middle ground: it must not be mean (tapeinē) like bare ordinary speech nor elevated like poetry, but must achieve distinction through metaphor while remaining within the bounds of conversational naturalness (1404b--1405a; Barnes 2239--2240).

### P5. Metaphor Must Be Drawn from Kindred and Similar Things

The materials of metaphor must be drawn "from kindred and similar things, so that the kinship is clearly perceived as soon as the words are said" (1405a35--1405b1; Barnes 2241). Metaphors imply riddles, and "a good riddle can furnish a good metaphor" (1405b5--6; Barnes 2241), because both operate through the perception of likeness in apparently unlike things. The materials of metaphor "must be beautiful to the ear, to the understanding, to the eye or some other physical sense" (1405b18--20; Barnes 2241) -- an important claim linking metaphor to perceptual experience.

### P6. Appropriateness Requires Correspondence to Emotion, Character, and Subject

At III.7, Aristotle articulates the principle of to prepon as a threefold correspondence: language must express the speaker's emotional state (pathē), reflect the speaker's character-class (ēthos as determined by age, sex, nationality, and disposition), and match the gravity of the subject matter (1408a10--35; Barnes 2245--2246). This principle is not merely decorative but epistemic: appropriate language "makes people believe in the truth of your story" because they infer trustworthiness from the fit between language and situation (1408a20--23; Barnes 2246).

### P7. Frigidity Arises from Four Specific Forms of Excess

The four frigidities -- misuse of compounds, strange words, excessive epithets, inappropriate metaphor -- are all species of the same error: "lifting style above the usual level" (1406a16; Barnes 2242) in ways that make the art conspicuous rather than concealing it. Each form represents a failure to maintain the balance between clarity and distinction that constitutes good prose. Alcidamas and Gorgias serve as cautionary examples (1406a--1406b; Barnes 2242--2243).

## 5. Tensions and Open Questions

### T1. Art Concealing Art vs. Systematic Teachability

Aristotle says naturalness is the governing principle and art must be hidden (1404b18--19; Barnes 2239), yet he is writing a systematic treatise that teaches these techniques. If the audience is to be unaware of the art, does making the art explicit in a handbook undermine its effectiveness? The treatise presupposes that one can teach what must appear unteachable.

### T2. Metaphor as Unteachable Genius vs. Systematic Classification

Aristotle claims metaphor "is not a thing whose use can be taught by one man to another" because it depends on "an intuitive perception of the similarity in dissimilars" (1405a9--10; Barnes 2240). Yet the Rhetoric and the *Poetics* provide detailed classifications of metaphor types (genus-to-species, species-to-genus, species-to-species, proportional). Can systematic classification coexist with the claim of unteachability?

### T3. Prose vs. Poetry as Stylistic Registers

Aristotle insists that prose language is distinct from poetic language (1404a28--29; Barnes 2239), yet the chief instrument of prose distinction -- metaphor -- is shared with poetry, and the theory of metaphor is explicitly referred to the *Art of Poetry* (1404b8--10; Barnes 2239; 1405a4--5; Barnes 2240). The boundary between the two registers seems permeable: the four frigidities are all defined as importing poetic excess into prose.

### T4. Clarity vs. Distinction as Competing Demands

Clarity requires current, ordinary words (kyria onomata); distinction requires unfamiliar, metaphorical words. Aristotle resolves this by arguing that metaphor simultaneously achieves both ("gives style clearness, charm, and distinction," 1405a8; Barnes 2240), but the four frigidities demonstrate how easily metaphorical language can destroy clarity. The resolution depends on the "fitting" quality of the metaphor, which Aristotle defines intuitively rather than formally.

### T5. Appropriateness (to prepon) and the Epistemic Status of Style

At III.7, Aristotle claims that appropriate language "makes people believe in the truth of your story" (1408a20; Barnes 2246) -- they draw a "false conclusion" about the speaker's trustworthiness from the fit of his language. Is this inference genuinely false? If appropriate language reflects real character and emotion, the audience's inference may be warranted. Aristotle oscillates between treating style as a tool of genuine self-disclosure and as a technique of managed appearance.

## 7. Dissertation Relevance

This unit is critical to the dissertation for three reasons:

1. **Lexis as ontological disclosure**: The claim that appropriate language "makes people believe in the truth" (1408a20; Barnes 2246) reveals that style is not mere ornament but a mode of disclosure -- language that fits its object and its speaker discloses the world in a way that produces conviction. This connects directly to Heidegger's reading of rhetoric as "the hermeneutic of concrete being-there" (BCAP, U-FP3a).

2. **Metaphor and perceptual experience**: Aristotle's insistence that metaphorical materials "must be beautiful to the ear, to the understanding, to the eye or some other physical sense" (1405b18--20; Barnes 2241) grounds metaphor in aisthēsis. This prepares the ground for the pro ommaton poiein doctrine of III.10--11, where metaphor functions as a mode of phantasia -- making the audience "see" what is described.

3. **Naturalness as concealed technē**: The principle that art must appear natural (1404b18--19; Barnes 2239) establishes a fundamental paradox of rhetorical ontology: the most effective disclosure of truth occurs through artifice that conceals itself. This connects to the dissertation's broader argument about the relationship between technē and phusis in rhetorical experience.
