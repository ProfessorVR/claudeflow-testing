# Phase 2 Analysis: MEM-01 — On Memory (Chapters 1–2)

## Section 1: Unit Metadata

- **unit_id**: MEM-01

- **chapters**: 1–2 (complete work)
- **Bekker range**: 449b4–453b11
- **PDF pages**: 3–9 (Aristotle - On Memory_(2014)_[Clean Copy].pdf)
- **Edition**: Barnes, *Complete Works of Aristotle* (ROT), Vol. I, trans. J. I. Beare; text: W. D. Ross, *Parva Naturalia*, Clarendon Press, 1955
- **tier**: Critical
- **cluster**: psychology
- **dissertation_critical**: true — μνήμη, φάντασμα as memory-image, time-perception
- **Unit thesis**: Memory (μνήμη) is neither perception nor conception but a state (ἕξις) or affection (πάθος) of one of these, conditioned by the lapse of time; it belongs to the primary faculty of sense-perception (τὸ πρῶτον αἰσθητικόν), i.e. the common sense, and requires imagination (φαντασία) as its medium — the memory-image (φάντασμα) functions simultaneously as a likeness (εἰκών) of the thing remembered and as an affection in itself. Recollection (ἀνάμνησις) is a distinct, quasi-inferential process of recovering memory through the sequential reactivation of habitual movements (κινήσεις), governed by association (succession, similarity, contrariety, contiguity) and essentially involving the perception of time as magnitude.

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 The nature and object of memory (Ch. 1, 449b4–450a25)
- Memory is to be treated with respect to its nature, its cause, and the part of the soul to which it belongs; recollection (ἀνάμνησις) is a separate topic (449b4–6).
- Retentive memory and the power of recollection are not identical: slow people have better memory; quick-witted people are better at recollecting (449b6–8).
- The object of memory is the past: one cannot remember the future (that is expectation) or the present (that is perception); memory relates to what is past (449b10–15).
- Memory is not perception or conception but a state (ἕξις) or affection (πάθος) of one of these, conditioned by the lapse of time (449b24–25).
- All memory implies time; therefore only animals that perceive time can remember, and the organ by which they perceive time is that by which they remember (449b28–30).

### 2.2 Memory, imagination, and the common sense (Ch. 1, 450a1–451a2)
- Imagination (φαντασία) has already been treated in *De Anima*; without an image (φάντασμα), thinking is impossible (450a1–2).
- One must cognize magnitude and motion by the same faculty by which one cognizes time; the cognition of these objects is effected by the primary faculty of perception (τὸ πρῶτον αἰσθητικόν), i.e. the common sense (450a9–12).
- Memory, even of intellectual objects, involves an image (φάντασμα) and is thus an affection of the common sense; memory belongs incidentally to the faculty of thought but essentially to the primary faculty of sense-perception (450a12–14).
- Not only humans but also certain other animals possess memory, because memory is not a function of the thinking parts alone but of the time-perceiving faculty (450a15–19).
- All objects of which there is imagination are in themselves objects of memory; those which do not exist without imagination are objects of memory incidentally (450a22–25).

### 2.3 The memory-image as impression and likeness (Ch. 1, 450a25–451a18)
- The affection (πάθος) generated through sense-perception is imprinted in the soul and in the bodily part that is its seat; the process of movement stamps in an impression (τύπος) as a seal stamps wax (450a27–32 / 450b1).
- Those strongly moved by passion or very young/old are defective in memory: the receiving surface is either too fluid or too hard for the impression to take (450b1–11).
- The central puzzle: how can one remember something absent by contemplating an affection that is present? The image within us is both something in itself (an object of contemplation, θεώρημα) and relative to something else (a likeness, εἰκών) (450b11–20).
- Analogy of the painted panel: the picture is at once a picture (ζῷον) and a likeness (εἰκών); when the soul perceives it *in itself*, it is a mere thought or image; when it perceives it *qua* related to something else, it is a reminder (ὑπόμνημα) (450b20–451a2).
- Memory is thus the having of an image (φάντασμα) related as a likeness (εἰκών) to that of which it is an image; it is a function of the primary faculty of sense-perception, i.e. that faculty whereby we perceive time (451a14–18).

### 2.4 Recollection distinguished from memory (Ch. 2, 451a18–451b22)
- Recollection (ἀνάμνησις) is not the recovery or acquisition of memory: at the instant of first learning there is no preceding memory to recover, nor is memory acquired *ab initio* (451a20–22).
- Memory exists only after the state or affection has been implanted concurrently with the sensory experience and has been established in the organ (451a22–27).
- Remembering in the incidental sense may not occur until time has elapsed; one remembers what one formerly experienced, not what one is currently experiencing (451a28–31).
- It is possible to remember without recollection: as a continued consequence of original perception or experience; the recovery of such a state may then amount to recollection, and memory follows on recollection (451b2–5).
- Recollection is not the "reinstatement of something which was there before" without qualification — one may twice learn or twice discover the same fact; recollecting must imply the presence of some source beyond what was originally learned (451b6–10).

### 2.5 The mechanics of recollection: movements, succession, and habit (Ch. 2, 451b10–452a12)
- Acts of recollection arise because one movement (κίνησις) has by nature another that succeeds it: if the connection is necessary, the sequence is invariable; if customary, it usually follows (451b10–16).
- Custom and frequent repetition create a quasi-natural order; things arranged in a fixed order (like geometrical demonstrations) are easy to remember; badly arranged subjects are difficult (451b28–452a1).
- Recollecting differs from relearning in that the recollector moves *by his own effort* to the next term after the starting-point; when this cannot be done by oneself but only by external assistance, it is no longer memory (452a4–7).
- Remembering is the existence of a movement capable of stimulating the mind to the desired movement, and this must be moved from within the person, i.e. in consequence of movements wholly contained within himself (452a7–12).

### 2.6 Starting-points, middle terms, and the topography of recollection (Ch. 2, 452a12–452b7)
- One must get hold of a starting-point (ἀρχή): the method of "places" (τόποι) involves passing swiftly from one point to another (e.g. milk → white → mist → moist → Autumn) (452a12–16).
- The middle point among a series is a good general starting-point: from any middle term, movement in either direction is possible (452a17–24).
- The cause of sometimes recollecting and sometimes not, from the same starting-point, is that a movement can branch in several directions (e.g. from C to B or to D); custom steers the mind along the more travelled path (452a26–30).
- Custom and nature produce regularity; in the realm of nature the same obtains *a fortiori* — frequency creates nature (452a30–452b1).
- Thus recollection takes place by hunting up the series from the present or from something similar, contrary, or contiguous to what is sought (452b1–7).

### 2.7 Recollection and the perception of time as magnitude (Ch. 2, 452b7–453a4)
- The point of capital importance is the time-relation: recollection requires perceiving time determinately or indeterminately (452b7–8).
- There is in the mind something proportional to magnitudes and movements: if one has the movement AB and constructs CD (proportional), one thinks of the greater and smaller analogously to how the eye judges distant magnitudes by a proportionate movement (452b8–20).
- When the movement corresponding to the object and that corresponding to its time concur, one actually remembers; if one supposes so without this concurrence, one only supposes oneself to remember (452b23–29).
- The movement answering to time is of two kinds: sometimes remembering a fact has no determinate time-notion; in other cases one has a determinate notion of the time — yet even without exact determination one genuinely remembers (452b29–453a4).

### 2.8 Recollection as inference; the corporeal substrate (Ch. 2, 453a4–453b11)
- Good memory and quick recollection are not identical capacities; the act of recollecting differs from that of remembering not only in respect of time but in that recollection is a mode of inference (453a5–10 / 720.10–12).
- Recollection is, as it were, a mode of inference: one who recollects infers that he formerly saw, heard, or experienced something — the process is a sort of investigation (ζήτησις), belonging naturally to those endowed with the faculty of deliberation (βουλευτικόν), for deliberation is a form of inference (453a10–14).
- The affection is corporeal: recollection is a searching for an image in a corporeal substrate; the inability to recollect produces discomfort, especially in melancholic persons, who are most powerfully moved by images (453a14–23).
- The effort of recollection is not under full voluntary control — like a thrown stone, once the movement is set in motion it is not easily brought to rest; the moisture around the centre of sense-perception resists settling (453a23–28).
- Physiological extremes explain defective memory: infants and the very old are in states of flux; those with abnormally large upper parts (like dwarfs) have disproportionate weight on the organ of perception, impairing the directness of recollective movements (453a31–453b7).
- Closing summary: memory and remembering concern the nature and organ of the soul by which animals remember; recollection concerns its definition, its manner, and its causes (453b7–11).

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **The dual-aspect puzzle of the φάντασμα (450b11–451a2)**: Aristotle poses the question of how one can remember what is absent by contemplating a present affection, then answers via the picture/likeness distinction. But the mechanism by which the soul "shifts" from perceiving the image *in itself* to perceiving it *qua* likeness of something absent is left unexplained. What triggers the relational perception? The text says "whenever, from contemplating a mental object in itself, one changes his point of view, and regards it as relative to something else" (451a5–7), but this seems to presuppose precisely the awareness of the absent original that it is meant to explain. [INTERP-high] — Linked to: MEM-01-P3.

2. **Recollection as inference vs. recollection as quasi-mechanical movement (451b10–453a14)**: Aristotle describes recollection both as a "mode of inference" and "investigation" (ζήτησις) belonging to beings with deliberation, and as a chain of quasi-mechanical movements governed by habit and natural succession. The tension between the deliberative-rational characterization and the associative-mechanical one is never fully resolved. Is recollection a rational act or an automatic physiological process? The text seems to allow both without integrating them. [INTERP-high] — Linked to: MEM-01-P5, MEM-01-P6.

3. **The scope of animal memory (450a15–19)**: Aristotle states that not only humans but "certain other animals" possess memory, since memory requires only the faculty of perceiving time, not the thinking parts. But he also states that recollection belongs only to beings with the deliberative faculty, implying only humans recollect. The boundary is clear in principle but raises the question of what exactly "perceiving time" means for non-rational animals — is their time-perception identical to ours or merely analogous? [INTERP-low] — Linked to: MEM-01-P2.

4. **The physiological mechanism and the seat of memory (450a27–450b11, 453a14–453b7)**: Aristotle locates memory in the "primary faculty of sense-perception" and its bodily organ, and describes impressions stamped in a corporeal substrate with properties of moisture and hardness. But the identity of this organ is never specified in *De Memoria* itself (the cardiac centre is implied from *De Anima* and *De Sensu*). The relation between soul-state and body-state in the hylomorphic account remains schematic. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: MEM-01-P4.

5. **Time as magnitude and the proportionality analogy (452b7–24)**: The argument that the mind constructs proportional movements corresponding to external magnitudes (the AB/CD analogy) is one of the most obscure passages in the treatise. The claim is that recollection requires an internal representation of time that is structurally isomorphic to spatial magnitude, but the analogy is developed in quasi-geometrical terms that resist straightforward interpretation. Whether this is meant as a literal psychophysiological claim or a structural analogy is unclear. [INTERP-high] — Linked to: MEM-01-P7.

### 9b. Dissertation Relevance

This unit is **dissertation-critical** on multiple axes:

1. **φάντασμα as memory-image**: The dual-aspect structure of the φάντασμα (object-in-itself / likeness-of-absent-original) is central to any dissertation argument about Aristotle's image-theory. The *De Memoria* account provides the most explicit analysis of how φαντάσματα function representationally — not as simple copies but as entities requiring a shift in cognitive attitude (from θεώρημα to εἰκών). This complicates naive representationalist readings and connects directly to the rhetorical function of images in persuasion.

2. **μνήμη and time-perception**: The essential dependence of memory on χρόνος, mediated by the common sense (τὸ πρῶτον αἰσθητικόν), establishes that memory is a temporal-perceptual phenomenon, not an intellectual one. This has consequences for how φαντασία functions across the Aristotelian corpus — if memory of intellectual objects still requires φαντάσματα and time-perception, then the perceptual apparatus is never fully left behind even in theoretical cognition.

3. **ἀνάμνησις as quasi-rhetorical investigation**: Aristotle's characterization of recollection as ζήτησις (investigation) and a mode of inference belonging to the βουλευτικόν (deliberative faculty) places it structurally parallel to rhetorical deliberation. The "places" (τόποι) from which recollection starts — moving from similar, contrary, or contiguous items — are associative topoi in a psychological register. This connection between recollective topoi and rhetorical topoi is potentially significant for the dissertation's argument about the ambient, embodied character of rhetorical invention.

4. **Corporeal substrate of memory**: The wax-seal model, the emphasis on moisture/hardness of the receiving surface, and the role of the heart-region (implied) as the centre of sense-perception all reinforce the embodied, material dimension of Aristotelian psychology. Memory is not disembodied information storage but a physiological process with material conditions — directly relevant to any ambient-rhetorical reading of Aristotle.
