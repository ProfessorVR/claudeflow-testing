## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Ch. 1 — Method: from universals to particulars (184a10–184b14)

- The science of nature concerns principles, causes, and elements; knowledge requires acquaintance with these — 184a10–14 (PDF 3).
- The natural method is to proceed "from the things which are more knowable and clear to us" toward "what is clearer and more knowable by nature" — 184a16–21 (PDF 3).
- What is plain to us at first are "rather confused masses"; the elements and principles become known by analysis — 184a21–23 (PDF 3).
- We must advance from universals to particulars: "a universal is a kind of whole, comprehending many things within it, like parts" — 184a23–26 (PDF 3).
- The analogy of the child: "a child begins by calling all men father, and all women mother, but later on distinguishes each of them" — 184b12–14 (PDF 3).

### 2.2 Ch. 2 — The principles: one or many? Refutation of the Eleatics (184b15–187a11)

- The principles must be one or more than one; if one, either motionless (Parmenides, Melissus) or in motion (the physicists who posit air, water, etc.) — 184b15–22 (PDF 3).
- To investigate whether what exists is one and motionless is not properly a contribution to natural science, since denying principles denies the science altogether — 185a1–5 (PDF 4).
- Refutation of Melissus: his argument is "gross and offers no difficulty at all: accept one ridiculous proposition and the rest follows" — 185a9–12 (PDF 4).
- We must take for granted that natural things are in motion — "which is indeed made plain by induction" — 185a12–14 (PDF 4).
- The question "In what sense is it asserted that all things are one?" admits multiple readings: substance, quantity, or quality — 185a20–25 (PDF 4).
- If both substance and quantity exist independently, what exists is not one but two — 185a27–29 (PDF 4).
- The infinite is a quantity, not a substance; Melissus says what exists is infinite, hence a quantity, hence not truly one — 185a32–185b1 (PDF 4–5).
- "'One' itself, no less than 'is', is used in many ways" — 185b5 (PDF 5); the continuous is one, the indivisible is one, things with the same essence are one.
- If the One is continuous, it is many (the continuous is divisible ad infinitum) — 185b10 (PDF 5).
- Difficulty of part and whole: whether the part and the whole are one or many, and in what way — 185b11–16 (PDF 5).
- If the indivisible One has no quantity or quality, "nothing will have quantity or quality" — absurd — 185b18–20 (PDF 5).
- Refutation of the Heraclitean thesis (all things are one in being) via the multiple senses of "is" — 185b26–186a3 (PDF 5).

### 2.3 Ch. 3 — Continued refutation: "being" and "one" said in many ways (186a4–187a11)

- The fallacy of Melissus: "what has come into being always has a beginning" does not entail "what has not come into being has no beginning" — 186a7–10 (PDF 6).
- Against Parmenides: if "is" is used in a single way only, "his conclusion does not follow" — 186a25 (PDF 6).
- Whiteness is not one: "for what is white will not be one either in the sense that it is continuous or in the sense that it must be defined in only one way" — 186a29–32 (PDF 6).
- Parmenides had not come in sight of the distinction between substance and attribute — 186b33–35 (PDF 6).
- "What just is" cannot be an attribute of something, for then that of which it is predicated would not be; "the subject cannot be a being" unless "is" means several things — 186b1–4 (PDF 7).
- Some atomists responded to the Eleatic argument by positing atomic magnitudes (bisection yields atoms) — 187a1–3 (PDF 7).
- It is clearly impossible for all things to be one in the sense Parmenides and Melissus require — 187a7–10 (PDF 7).

### 2.4 Ch. 4 — Survey of predecessors on the number of principles (187a12–188a18)

- The physicists have two modes of explanation: (a) making the underlying body one (air, fire, water) and generating multiplicity by condensation and rarefaction; (b) extracting contraries from a mixture (Anaximander, Empedocles, Anaxagoras) — 187a12–23 (PDF 7).
- Compare Plato's "Great and Small" — except Plato makes these his matter, the one his form, while others treat the one as matter and the contraries as differentiae — 187a17–20 (PDF 7).
- The second group assert that contrarieties are contained in the one and emerge by segregation — Empedocles and Anaxagoras — 187a20–26 (PDF 7).
- Anaxagoras: the principles are infinite; "everything has been mixed in everything" and things receive their names from what is numerically predominant — 187a23–187b7 (PDF 8).
- The infinite qua infinite is unknowable; if the principles are infinite in multitude and kind, it is impossible to know things composed of them — 187b7–13 (PDF 8).
- Parts of a whole may be indefinitely big or small; "it is impossible for an animal or plant to be indefinitely big or small" — 187b15–20 (PDF 8).
- On Anaxagoras's theory, complete separation never takes place, since "there is no minimum magnitude, and of quality, because affections are inseparable" — 188a2–5 (PDF 9).
- "It is better to assume a smaller and finite number of principles, as Empedocles does" — 188a17 (PDF 9).

### 2.5 Ch. 5 — All thinkers make the principles contraries (188a19–189a10)

- All thinkers identify the principles with contraries: "hot and cold, or again moist and dry, the causes of becoming; while others make odd and even, or again Love and Strife" — 188a19–22 (PDF 9–10).
- This is reasonable: (a) first principles are not derived from one another; (b) first principles are not derived from other things; (c) everything is derived from them. Contraries satisfy all three conditions — 188a27–30 (PDF 10).
- Coming-to-be is from contraries: "white comes from not-white — and not from any not-white, but from black or some intermediate" — 188a35–188b1 (PDF 10).
- Change proceeds not into "the first chance thing" but into the corresponding opposite: "white does not pass into musical (except accidentally), but into not-white" — 188b1–5 (PDF 10).
- The principle of contrary change holds universally: "whether we take attunement, order, or composition," a house comes from shapelessness, a statue from conjunction — 188b15–20 (PDF 10).
- The earlier thinkers' principles are "all are taken from the same table of columns" — some pairs being wider, others narrower, but all analogous — 189a1–5 (PDF 10).
- "It is clear then that our principles must be contraries" — 189a10 (PDF 10).

### 2.6 Ch. 6 — The principles are two or three, not one, not innumerable (189a11–190a21)

- The principles are neither one (there cannot be one contrary) nor innumerable (then nature would be unknowable); also, substance is one genus, so the principles differ only as prior and posterior — 189a11–20 (PDF 11).
- "It will suffice to show that the principles are neither one nor innumerable" — 189a20 (PDF 11).
- Contraries do not constitute the substance of anything; "a first principle ought not to be predicated of any subject" — 189a30–32 (PDF 11).
- Substance is not contrary to another substance; therefore we must posit some third thing — the underlying nature (ὑποκείμενον) — alongside the pair of contraries — 189a33–189b1 (PDF 11).
- Argument from the number of contrarieties: substance is a single genus, so there is always a single primary contrariety, and all others reduce to it — 189b22–27 (PDF 12).
- "The number of elements is neither one nor more than two or three; but whether two or three is, as I said, a question of considerable difficulty" — 189b27–29 (PDF 12).

### 2.7 Ch. 7 — The three principles: form, privation, and underlying subject (189b30–191a22)

- Aristotle's own account approaches the question through an analysis of becoming in its widest sense — 189b30–32 (PDF 12).
- Becoming involves (a) something which comes to be, (b) something which it becomes, and (c) something from which it becomes — "the man becomes musical, or what is not-musical becomes musical" — 189b34–190a1 (PDF 12).
- The simple elements of change: "man" and "not-musical" are simple; "not-musical man" is complex — 190a1–5 (PDF 12).
- In every case of becoming, one part survives (the subject) and the other does not (the opposite/privation): "the man remains a man," but "what is not musical or is unmusical does not survive" — 190a10–13 (PDF 12).
- From surveying cases of becoming: "there must always be an underlying something, namely that which becomes," always one in number though two in form (subject + privation) — 190a13–17 (PDF 12).
- Substances come to be without qualification; other things come to be "so-and-so" — 190a31–33 (PDF 13).
- In all cases other than substance, there must be a subject which becomes — 190a33–35 (PDF 13).
- Substances too come from an underlying thing: "animals and plants come from seed" — 190b1–4 (PDF 13).
- Things come to be in different ways: by change of shape, by addition, by taking away, by putting together, by alteration — 190b5–8 (PDF 13).
- Everything comes to be from both subject and form: "the musical man is composed in a way of man and musical" — 190b20 (PDF 13).
- The subject is one numerically but two in form: "man, gold, and in general matter" are countable, while the privation (the absence of form) is accidental — 190b24–27 (PDF 13).
- "There is a sense in which we must declare the principles to be two, and a sense in which they are three": the contraries are two (form and privation), but the principles are three (form, privation, underlying subject) — 190b29–33 (PDF 13).
- The underlying nature can be known by analogy: "as the bronze is to the statue, the wood to the bed, or the matter and the formless before receiving form to any thing which has form, so is the underlying nature to substance, i.e. the 'this' or existent" — 191a8–12 (PDF 14).
- "This then is one principle (though not one or existent in the same sense as the 'this'); one is the form or definition; then further there is its contrary, the privation" — 191a12–15 (PDF 14).

### 2.8 Chs. 8–9 — Resolving the Eleatic difficulty: coming-to-be from what is not (191a23–192b5)

- The early thinkers went astray because they supposed "that nothing else comes to be or exists apart from what is itself, thus doing away with all becoming" — 191a25–28 (PDF 14).
- We agree that nothing can come to be from what is not without qualification; "but nevertheless we maintain that a thing may come to be from what is not in a qualified sense, i.e. accidentally" — 191b13–15 (PDF 15).
- "A thing comes to be from the privation, which in its own nature is something which is not — this not surviving as a constituent of the result" — 191b15–17 (PDF 15).
- The same things can be spoken of "in terms of potentiality and actuality" — what is potentially X can come to be actually X, resolving the puzzle — 191b27–29 (PDF 15).
- Ch. 9: Distinction between matter and privation — "the matter, accidentally, is not; and that the privation in its own nature is not; and that the matter is nearly, in a sense, substance, while the privation in no sense is" — 192a3–6 (PDF 16).
- The Platonists (Great and Small) also distinguish matter and privation but "identify their Great and Small alike with what is not" — making their triad different from Aristotle's — 192a6–8 (PDF 16).
- "Something divine, good, and desirable" — form is what matter desires, as "the female desires the male and the ugly the beautiful" — though the contrary does not desire its own extinction; rather the matter desires the form — 192a16–23 (PDF 16).
- Matter as primary substratum: "the primary substratum of each thing, from which it comes to be, and which persists in the result, not accidentally" — 192a31–32 (PDF 16).
- The accurate determination of form in respect of "whether it is one or many and what it is or what they are" is the province of first philosophy — 192b2–4 (PDF 16).
- "The above, then, may be taken as sufficient to establish that there are principles and what they are and how many there are" — 192b4–5 (PDF 16).

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **The status of the underlying subject (Ch. 7)**: Aristotle says the underlying nature is known "by analogy" — as bronze to statue, wood to bed. But whether the underlying subject is itself a determinate substance or pure indeterminate potentiality is left ambiguous. In Ch. 9 he says "the matter is nearly, in a sense, substance" (192a5), hedging on its ontological standing. This ambiguity becomes central to the Metaphysics (Zeta 3, whether matter can be substance). [INTERP-medium] — Linked to: PHYS-01-P4.

2. **Privation as a principle (Chs. 7–9)**: Aristotle insists there are three principles, yet concedes that "in a sense the principles are two, in a sense three" (190b29). Privation is a principle only per accidens — it is the absence of form, not a positive nature. Whether a per accidens principle is genuinely a principle, or merely a logical requirement of describing change, remains contentious. The Platonists' identification of matter with privation (Great and Small = non-being) is rejected, but Aristotle's own distinction is subtle enough to invite conflation. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: PHYS-01-P4, PHYS-01-P5.

3. **Matter's "desire" for form (Ch. 9)**: Aristotle says the matter desires the form "as the female desires the male and the ugly the beautiful" (192a22–23). This teleological language applied to matter is striking given that matter is supposedly the indeterminate substrate. Whether this desire is metaphorical, or whether it signals a genuine teleological tendency within matter itself (anticipating later hylomorphic doctrines), is contested. [INTERP-medium] — Linked to: PHYS-01-P4.

4. **Completeness of the refutation of Eleatic monism (Chs. 2–3)**: Aristotle spends considerable effort on Parmenides and Melissus but ultimately declares that "to investigate whether what exists is one and motionless is not a contribution to the science of nature" (185a1). He then proceeds to refute them anyway. The tension between dismissing the question as non-physical and devoting two chapters to it suggests that the Eleatic challenge, though officially external, is dialectically indispensable for establishing the possibility of natural science. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: PHYS-01-P2.

5. **Potentiality/actuality invoked without full explanation (Ch. 8)**: The resolution of the Eleatic puzzle at 191b27–29 appeals to the distinction between potentiality and actuality, but Aristotle explicitly notes this "has been done with greater precision elsewhere" (an apparent cross-reference to Metaphysics IX). Whether Physics I can stand as an independent argument without the metaphysical apparatus of Theta is an open question about the self-sufficiency of the physical treatise. [INTERP-low] — Linked to: PHYS-01-P5.
