| Field | Value |

| Scope | II.7--II.12 (418a--424b) |
| Density | High (systematic treatment of each sense modality; culminating doctrine of sense as reception of form without matter) |

**One-sentence thesis**: Aristotle undertakes a systematic analysis of each of the five senses -- sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch -- establishing for each the proper object (ἴδιον αἰσθητόν), the requisite medium (μεταξύ), and the conditions of actualization, then identifies the common sensibles (κοινὰ αἰσθητά) perceived non-incidentally through multiple senses, and concludes with the general principle that sense-perception is the capacity to receive the sensible forms of things without their matter, as wax receives the impress of a signet-ring without the iron or gold.

|---------|------------|---------------------|---------------|
| Proper sensible | ἴδιον αἰσθητόν | "proper/peculiar object of sense" | Each sense has a unique proper object: colour for sight, sound for hearing, odour for smell, flavour for taste, tangible qualities for touch. The proper sensible is that about which no error is possible (or error is minimal). |
| Common sensible | κοινὸν αἰσθητόν (pl. κοινὰ αἰσθητά) | "common object of sense" | Movement, rest, figure, magnitude, number, unity -- perceived non-incidentally through more than one sense. No special organ for these; perceived by a common sensibility (κοινὴ αἰσθητική). |
| The transparent | τὸ διαφανές | "the transparent, the see-through" | The medium of sight; not a body but a quality found in air, water, and certain solids. Its actuality is light; its potentiality is darkness. |
| Light | φῶς | "light" | The actuality of the transparent qua transparent; not fire, not a body, not an efflux; the presence of fire or something resembling fire in the transparent medium. |
| Medium | μεταξύ | "what is between, intermediate" | The intervening body between sense-organ and object. Required for all distance senses (sight, hearing, smell). Touch appears to lack a medium but actually uses flesh as medium. |
| Sound | ψόφος | "sound, noise" | The object of hearing; produced by impact of two solids against air. Actual vs. potential sound distinguished. Requires smooth surface for air to rebound. |
| Voice | φωνή | "voice" | A kind of sound proper to ensouled beings; must be accompanied by imagination (φαντασία); is a sound with meaning (σημαντικός). |
| Sense as mean | μεσότης / μέσον | "mean, middle state" | The sense-organ is a ratio (λόγος) or mean between the contraries it perceives (hot/cold, white/black). This is why it can discriminate: what is in the middle can judge either extreme. |
| Form without matter | εἶδος ἄνευ τῆς ὕλης | "form without the matter" | The general principle of II.12: sense receives the sensible forms without the material substratum, as wax receives the impress of a signet-ring without the iron or gold. |
| Affection / being-affected | πάθος / πάσχειν | "affection, undergoing, being acted upon" | Sense-perception is a kind of being-affected (πάσχειν); the sense is potentially what the sensible object is actually. This is the core mechanism of perceptual actualization. |
| Actuality of sense and sensible | ἐνέργεια αἰσθήσεως / αἰσθητοῦ | "actuality of perception / of the perceived" | The actuality of the sensible object and the actuality of the sense are one and the same (though their being differs). Actual hearing and actual sounding occur simultaneously. |
| Flavour | χυμός | "flavour, juice, sap" | Object of taste; species include sweet, bitter, pungent, harsh, astringent, acid, succulent, saline. Requires liquid medium; perception through direct contact. |

## 4. Main Positions

### DA-03-P1: Every sense requires a proper object, and about its proper object each sense is infallible or nearly so

Aristotle organises the entire treatment of the five senses around the principle that each sense has a unique proper object (ἴδιον αἰσθητόν) that it alone can perceive and about which it cannot err, or errs least. Colour is proper to sight, sound to hearing, odour to smell, flavour to taste, and tangible qualities (hot/cold, moist/dry, hard/soft) to touch. This structural principle governs II.7--II.11: each chapter identifies the proper sensible, then the conditions under which it is perceived. The proper sensibles are contrasted with the common sensibles and the incidental sensibles (αἰσθητὰ κατὰ συμβεβηκός), which are perceived through or alongside the proper objects.

**Dual citation**: *De Anima* II.6, 418a7--20 (programmatic statement applied throughout II.7--II.12) | PDF p. 27--28.

### DA-03-P2: All distance senses require a medium (metaxy), and even touch, which seems to involve direct contact, operates through flesh as its medium

Aristotle demonstrates that sight requires the transparent (air, water) as medium, hearing requires air, and smell requires air or water. The pivotal argument comes in II.11, where touch -- apparently a contact sense -- is shown to operate through flesh as its medium, just as the transparent is the medium for sight. "The distinction is unsound; we perceive what is hard or soft, as well as the objects of hearing, sight, and smell, through a medium, only that the latter are perceived over a greater distance than the former; that is why the facts escape our notice. For we do perceive everything through a medium" (423b1--8). The membrane experiment confirms this: if a membrane is stretched over the flesh, sensation still occurs, proving flesh is medium not organ.

**Dual citation**: *De Anima* II.11, 423b1--8 | PDF p. 35.

### DA-03-P3: Light is neither a body nor an efflux but the actuality of the transparent qua transparent

Against Empedocles and the efflux theorists, Aristotle argues that light is not fire, not any body, and not an emanation. Rather, "light is as it were the proper colour of what is transparent" (418b10--11), and its presence constitutes the actuality of the medium. Darkness is the potentiality of this same medium. The argument against light-as-travelling is that the distance from East to West is too great for any such movement to be unobservable, refuting the view that light "travels" or is "being at a given moment between the earth and its envelope" (418b20--26).

**Dual citation**: *De Anima* II.7, 418b3--13, 418b20--26 | PDF pp. 28--29.

### DA-03-P4: The sense is a mean (mesotes) between the contraries it perceives, which grounds its power of discrimination

In II.11--II.12, Aristotle articulates the principle that each sense-organ is a ratio or mean between the opposed qualities it discriminates. "Sense-perception is a process of being so affected; so that that which makes something such as it itself actually is makes the other such because it is already potentially such. That is why we do not perceive what is equally hot and cold or hard and soft, but only excesses, the sense itself being a sort of mean between the opposites that characterize the objects of perception" (424a2--7). The organ of touch must be neither hot nor cold; to perceive white and black, the organ of sight must be actually neither but potentially both. This principle of the mean explains both the discriminative power and the vulnerability of sense (excesses destroy organs as "concord and tone are destroyed by too violently twanging the strings of a lyre," 424a30--32).

**Dual citation**: *De Anima* II.12, 424a2--7, 424a28--32 | PDF pp. 36--37.

### DA-03-P5: Sense-perception is the reception of sensible forms without matter (the wax-signet analogy)

The culminating thesis of II.12 gives the general account of all sense-perception: "Generally, about all perception, we can say that a sense is what has the power of receiving into itself the sensible forms of things without the matter, in the way in which a piece of wax takes on the impress of a signet-ring without the iron or gold; what produces the impression is a signet of bronze or gold, but not qua bronze or gold: in a similar way the sense is affected by what is coloured or flavoured or sounding not insofar as each is what it is, but insofar as it is of such and such a sort and according to its form" (424a17--28). This is the definitive Aristotelian formula: the sense receives the εἶδος (form, ratio, λόγος) of the object while leaving behind the ὕλη (matter). The sense and its organ are materially the same thing but differ in essence -- the being-a-sense (τὸ αἰσθητικῷ εἶναι) is a certain form or power in a magnitude.

**Dual citation**: *De Anima* II.12, 424a17--28 | PDF p. 37.

### DA-03-P6: The common sensibles are perceived non-incidentally through a common sensibility, not through a special sense

In II.6 (programmatic) and applied in the transition from II.11 to III.1, Aristotle identifies movement, rest, figure, magnitude, number, and unity as common sensibles (κοινὰ αἰσθητά). These are perceived non-incidentally through more than one sense (e.g., movement perceived by both sight and touch). There cannot be a special sense-organ for them; rather, "in the case of the common sensibles there is already in us a common sensibility which enables us to perceive them non-incidentally; there is therefore no special sense required for their perception" (425a27--30). If there were a special sense for common sensibles, our perception of them "would have been exactly like what has been above described" -- i.e., merely incidental, as perceiving Cleon's son not as Cleon's son but as white. The common sensibles are perceived through the co-operation of the special senses, each of which apprehends them alongside its proper object.

**Dual citation**: *De Anima* III.1, 425a27--30 | PDF p. 38.

### DA-03-P7: Voice (phone) requires soul, imagination, and meaning -- it is not mere sound

Aristotle distinguishes voice (φωνή) from mere sound (ψόφος) in II.8. Voice is characteristic of ensouled beings; "nothing that is without soul utters voice" (420b5--6). Mere air movement (coughing) is not voice; voice "must be accompanied by an act of imagination, for voice is a sound with a meaning" (420b29--33). Voice is produced by the impact of inbreathed air against the windpipe by the soul resident in the parts of the body, requiring both respiration and a special organ. Nature employs the tongue for two purposes: tasting (necessary for existence) and articulating speech (serving well-being). This account makes voice ontologically dependent on psychic capacities beyond mere sensation.

**Dual citation**: *De Anima* II.8, 420b5--6, 420b29--33 | PDF pp. 31--32.

## 4b. Cross-Text Anticipations

**DA-03 to DA-04 (III.1--III.2)**: The common sensibles and the unity of sense-perception receive fuller treatment in III.1--2. The question of how the senses perceive each other's special objects incidentally (e.g., perceiving that what is sweet is also yellow) leads to the doctrine of a unified perceptual capacity.

**DA-03 to *De Sensu***: The detailed treatment of colour-species, sound-species, and flavour-species is expanded in *De Sensu et Sensibilibus*, where Aristotle gives more precise physical explanations of the ratios underlying sensible qualities.

**DA-03 to *De Motu Animalium***: The doctrine that sense-perception involves being-affected (πάσχειν) without matter-transfer connects to *De Motu*'s account of how perceptual alteration initiates animal movement.

**DA-03 to *Metaphysics* Theta**: The actuality/potentiality framework (sense as potentially what the sensible is actually; their joint actualization) is a specific application of the general metaphysical doctrine of ἐνέργεια and δύναμις developed in *Metaphysics* IX.

## 5. Tensions

### T1: Touch as contact-sense vs. touch as mediated sense

Aristotle insists in II.11 that touch, like the other senses, operates through a medium (flesh), and that "the distinction is unsound" between contact and distance perception (423b1--5). Yet the phenomenology of touch continually pulls toward direct contact: we feel the object *upon* us, not through an interposed medium in the way we see through air. Aristotle acknowledges this: "in the perception of objects of touch we are affected not *by* but *along with* the medium" (423b12--15). The shield analogy (the shock passes through the shield to the man, not *to* the shield and then separately *to* the man) attempts to resolve this but leaves the disanalogy with the transparent only partially bridged.

### T2: Is touch one sense or many?

Aristotle raises but does not conclusively resolve whether touch is a single sense or a group of senses (422b23--27). Sight perceives one pair of contraries (white/black and their intermediates), hearing another (sharp/flat). But touch perceives multiple irreducible pairs: hot/cold, dry/moist, hard/soft, rough/smooth. Aristotle notes that the other senses also have more than one pair, but "we are unable clearly to detect in the case of touch what the single subject is which corresponds to sound in the case of hearing" (422b30--32). The problem is acknowledged and deferred rather than solved.

### T3: Sense as alteration vs. sense as non-standard change

The general account in II.12 characterises sense-perception as a πάσχειν, a being-affected. But Aristotle has earlier (II.5, 417a14--417b28) distinguished two kinds of alteration: one that is a destruction by a contrary, and one that is a preservation or fulfilment of a potentiality by what is actual. Sense-perception is the latter kind. The tension arises because the language of πάσχειν and being-affected naturally suggests the first (ordinary physical alteration), while the wax-signet analogy demands the second (reception of form without matter). Aristotle signals awareness of this but the precise mechanism by which form is received without matter remains underdetermined in this text.

### T4: The status of plants -- affected by tangibles but without sensation

Aristotle ends II.12 by observing that plants are "affected by tangible objects" (their temperature can be raised or lowered) yet lack sensation. The explanation is that plants have "no mean" and cannot take on forms without matter (424b1--3). But the criterion is difficult: how exactly does "having a mean" differ from merely being in a state intermediate between contraries? The distinction between genuine perceptual reception (form without matter) and mere physical alteration (form with matter) is asserted rather than mechanistically explained.

### T5: Smell as poorly understood and taxonomically dependent on taste

Aristotle admits in II.9 that smell "is much less easy to determine than what we have hitherto discussed" (421a7) and that human olfaction is poor. The species of smells are classified by analogy with the species of tastes (sweet, pungent, etc.), with Aristotle noting that "the names of these varieties are applied to smells in virtue of similarity" (421b1--3). This raises the question whether smell has genuine species of its own or is taxonomically parasitic on taste -- a tension between the structural requirement that each sense have its own proper object with its own contrariety and the admitted phenomenological poverty of olfactory discrimination in humans.

## 8. Dissertation Relevance

### 8a. Connection to Phantasia and Perception Theory

DA-03 provides the indispensable foundation for the dissertation's treatment of αἴσθησις and its relation to φαντασία. The doctrine that sense receives form without matter (II.12) establishes the ontological mechanism that φαντασία will subsequently be shown to preserve and re-present in the absence of the sensible object (III.3). The common sensibles -- especially movement and magnitude -- are critical because φαντασία inherits these perceptual contents and makes them available for deliberation and desire. The claim that voice requires φαντασία (II.8, 420b32) is the earliest explicit linkage of perception and imagination in the *De Anima*, anticipating the full treatment in III.3.

### 8b. The Medium Doctrine and Rhetoric

The insistence that all perception is mediated (μεταξύ) has consequences for any account of rhetoric that takes seriously the materiality of perception. If even touch requires a medium, then the perceiving animal is never in unmediated contact with the world. This structures the ambient-rhetorical problematic: rhetoric operates not in a disembodied space of propositions but through the material media (air, light, vibration) that transmit sensible forms to the perceiving organism.

### 8c. Touch, Intelligence, and the Body

The claim that "man is the most intelligent of all animals" because touch "reaches in man the maximum of discriminative accuracy" (421a22--23) directly connects perceptual sensitivity to cognitive capacity. The dissertation can use this to argue that for Aristotle, intelligence is not opposed to embodiment but is a function of the refinement of embodied perception -- a thesis that runs counter to intellectualist readings of Aristotle and supports the ambient-rhetorical framework.
