# Phase 2 Analysis: SS-02 — Sense and Sensibilia 5–7

## Section 1: Unit Metadata

- **unit_id**: SS-02
- **work**: *Sense and Sensibilia* (*De Sensu et Sensibilibus*)
- **translator**: J.I. Beare (Revised Oxford Translation, ed. Barnes)
- **chapter_range**: Chapters 5–7

- **pdf_pages**: 14–23 (Aristotle - Sense And Sensibilia_(2014)_[Clean Copy].pdf)
- **tier**: Light
- **cluster**: psychology
- **Unit thesis**: These chapters complete the survey of the individual senses by treating odour as analogous to savour but operating through air and water, classify odours into two species (nutritive and pleasant-in-themselves), argue that the divisibility of sensible qualities is finite and determined by the potential/actual distinction, demonstrate that simultaneous perception of objects from different sense-provinces requires a numerically one but specifically different faculty, and conclude that every sensible object is a magnitude with a determinate threshold of perceptibility.

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline (Chapter-Level)

### Chapter 5 (443b–445b): Odour — Two Species, Medium, and Organ
- Odour is analogous to savour: the moist (whether in air or water) produces the effect of the sapid dry upon the sense of smell, just as the sapid dry acts upon taste through the nutrient moist 443b–444a1 (PDF pp. 14–15).
- Two species of odour: (1) odours correlated with savours, whose pleasantness belongs incidentally — these are shared by all animals and connected with nutrition and appetite 444a1–5 (PDF p. 14); (2) odours agreeable in their essential nature (e.g. flowers), which do not stimulate appetite and are pleasant or unpleasant irrespective of bodily health 443b27–444a1 (PDF p. 14).
- Only humans perceive the second class of odours, because the human brain is large, moist, and cold relative to the body; the coldness of the cerebral region admits the heat and stimulation of these odours 444a1–444b1 (PDF pp. 14–15).
- The organ of smell: respiration serves both thorax relief and odour inhalation; non-breathing creatures (fish, insects) perceive odour directly, without respiration, via a sense analogous to but not identical with the five standard senses 444b15–445a5 (PDF pp. 15–16).
- The sense of smell occupies a middle position between the tactual senses (touch, taste) and those that perceive through a medium (sight, hearing); its object is an affection of both the tangible and the audible/visible 445a5–15 (PDF p. 16).
- Refutation of the Pythagorean theory that some animals are nourished by odours alone: food must be composite because bodies nourished by it are not simple; odour *qua* odour does not contribute to nutrition, though it is serviceable to health 445a15–445b1 (PDF p. 16).
- Conclusion of the survey of the several organs of sense-perception 445b1 (PDF p. 16).

### Chapter 6 (445b1–446b28): Divisibility of Sensible Qualities and Common Sensibles
- Opening problem: if every body is infinitely divisible, are its sensible qualities (colour, savour, odour, sound, weight, hardness) also infinitely divisible? 445b3–10 (PDF p. 16).
- Every sensible quality is productive of sense-perception; hence if the quality were infinitely divisible, every part of a body would be a perceptible magnitude — but we cannot perceive a thing that is white but not of a certain magnitude 445b7–446a1 (PDF pp. 16–17).
- Refutation of atomism: on the supposition that non-perceptible parts exist, every perceptible object would be composed of non-perceptible parts, which cannot be right since it is not by thought alone that we discern sensible qualities, and the atomistic hypothesis is rejected 446a1–20 (PDF p. 17).
- Resolution via potential/actual distinction: sensible qualities are limited in species because in all classes of contraries the intermediates are limited; the continuous is divisible into a finite number of unequal parts but into species that are finite in number 446a20–b5 (PDF p. 17).
- The difference between potential and actual perceptibility: a grain of millet contains colour potentially but not actually visible at that scale; a quarter-tone is in the musical continuum but escapes notice; extremely small constituents are only potentially, not actually, visible until separated from the whole 446b1–20 (PDF pp. 17–18).
- Magnitudes and their sensible qualities escape notice below certain thresholds, and sensible qualities — whether colours, tastes, or sounds — are limited in number 446b20–28 (PDF p. 18).

### Chapter 7 (447a1–449b) : Simultaneous Perception, Unity of the Sense Faculty, and Perceptual Thresholds
- Perception and temporal mediation: odour and sound arrive at a middle point before reaching the perceiver (the nearer perceiver smells sooner, sound arrives after a stroke); Empedocles says light from the sun travels through intervening space before reaching the eye or the earth 447a1–446b1 (PDF pp. 18–19).
- Any given time is divisible; perception and its objects come into being simultaneously, not by travelling, just as colour and light do not occupy particular places but subsist by equalization between perceiver and perceived 446b1–10 (PDF p. 19).
- Sound and odour are continuous media: the object first in order of proximity is the same as that perceived by each subsequent person, yet no two persons perceive numerically the same object — they perceive the same object specifically but numerically different affections 447a15–b5 (PDF p. 19).
- Qualitative change vs. local movement: light is due to the presence of something, not a movement; qualitative change may affect a whole simultaneously, unlike local movement which proceeds point by point 447b1–10 (PDF pp. 19–20).
- Tasting would have been like smelling if we lived in a liquid medium; the parts of media between organ and object are not all affected at once except in the case of light 447b5–10 (PDF p. 20).
- The problem of simultaneous perception of two objects: the stronger movement tends to extrude the weaker; it is easier to perceive simple objects than ingredients in a mixture 447b12–25 (PDF p. 20).
- Equal but heterogeneous stimuli efface each other; the resultant is a compound perception, not perception of either stimulus in its simple form 447b25–448a1 (PDF p. 20).
- Mixtures that are contraries (e.g. white and high, which are not concordant) cannot be discerned simultaneously; when stimuli are equal, they tend to efface one another, while if unequal, the stronger alone is distinctly perceptible 448a1–5 (PDF p. 21).
- The soul is more likely to perceive simultaneously two things in the same sensory province (e.g. low and high in sound) than two in different provinces (e.g. sight and hearing) 448a5–15 (PDF p. 21).
- The actualized perception is numerically one: when mixed, things are perceived by a perception actually one; when not mixed, they are perceived by two distinct actualized perceptions, and one faculty at one moment can have only one actualized perception 448a10–20 (PDF p. 21).
- The soul predicates numerical unity by virtue of simultaneous perception together with the discriminating faculty's specific mode of operation: the same sense discerns white and black (specifically different from one another), and another discerns sweet and bitter, but both faculties proceed analogously in their respective provinces 448a20–b5 (PDF pp. 21–22).
- Contraries under one sense-faculty cannot be discerned simultaneously; homogeneous sensibles in the same province that are not contrary (e.g. white with white co-ordinates, sweet with sweet co-ordinates in different provinces) likewise cannot be simultaneously discerned because they stand yet more aloof from each other 448b5–15 (PDF p. 22).
- Some writers on concords assert that sounds combined do not really reach us simultaneously; whether any portion of time is truly imperceptible is denied — every instant of time is perceivable, and it is impossible to be unaware of one's own existence during any time however small 448b15–448b1 (PDF p. 22).
- All magnitudes are perceptible, but their actual dimensions do not present themselves immediately (e.g. the sun appears indivisible); nothing one sees is really indivisible; no portion of time is imperceptible 448b1–449a1 (PDF pp. 22–23).
- The faculty of perception is *qua* one indivisible in its actualization but different when it has become divisible in actualization: it is numerically one and the same but different in its being — different, that is to say, in genus as regards some objects and in species as regards others 449a1–20 (PDF p. 23).
- Every sensible object is a magnitude; nothing which it is possible to perceive is indivisible; the distance from which an object can be perceived is determinate, and there is an extreme place beyond which the object is imperceptible and within which it is perceptible — this boundary is itself indivisible 449a20–b5 (PDF p. 23).
- Closing: this concludes the survey of the organs of sense-perception and their objects; the next topic is memory and remembering 449b5 (PDF p. 23).

### Main Positions

| id | claim | premises | status |
|----|-------|----------|--------|
| SS-02-P1 | Odour, *qua* odour, does not contribute to nutrition; odours of the second class (pleasant-in-themselves) are generated for human beings as a safeguard to health. | (1) Food must be composite, not simple vapour (445a20–30). (2) The Pythagorean view that animals can be nourished by odours alone is refuted. (3) The second class of odours is peculiar to man because of the distinctive coldness and moisture of the human brain (444a8–b1). | core_claim |
| SS-02-P2 | Sensible qualities are not infinitely divisible: they are limited in species because contraries are the extremes of each sensible genus and intermediates are finite. | (1) Every sensible quality is productive of sense-perception (445b7). (2) If infinitely divisible, non-perceptible parts would compose perceptible objects — but we do not cognize sensibles by thought alone (446a1–20). (3) The continuous is divisible into finite unequal parts; species of sensible qualities are finite (446a20–b5). | core_claim |
| SS-02-P3 | It is impossible to perceive two distinct objects simultaneously with one and the same sense in one actualized act of perception; the actualized perception at any given moment is necessarily one. | (1) The stronger movement extrudes the weaker (447b12–15). (2) Equal heterogeneous stimuli efface each other, producing compound perception (447b25–448a1). (3) One stimulation of a single faculty at a single instant is necessarily one; hence two distinct objects cannot be perceived simultaneously with one sense (448a15–20). | core_claim |
| SS-02-P4 | The faculty of sense-perception is numerically one in the soul but different in being, perceiving each genus of sensibles through a different organ; it is analogous to a point that is one but functions as two by being a boundary of two lines. | (1) There must be one general faculty since sense-perception is one (449a5–10). (2) It perceives each different genus through a different organ (449a10–12). (3) The same numerically one thing can be different in species, as a point can be a boundary of two lines (449a15–20). | core_claim |
