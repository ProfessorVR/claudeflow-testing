## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### Book Γ (IV)

#### Chapter 1 (1003a20–1003a32, PDF 35)
- **The science of being qua being**: There is a science that investigates being as being and the attributes belonging to it in virtue of its own nature. This is distinct from the special sciences, each of which cuts off a part of being. First philosophy seeks the first principles and highest causes of being as such.

#### Chapter 2 (1003a33–1005a18, PDF 35–37)
- **The focal meaning of "being"**: "Being" is said in many senses but always in relation to one central point — substance (*ousia*). Everything called "being" is so called in reference to substance: qualities, quantities, affections, etc. are beings because they are related to substance.
- **Being and unity are coextensive**: Being and unity are the same and one nature; "one man" and "existent man" add nothing to "man." The species of unity (same, similar, equal) correspond to the species of being.
- **Contrariety, privation, and plurality** fall under the same science, since plurality is opposite to unity and the contraries are forms of otherness/privation.
- **Philosophy vs. dialectic vs. sophistic**: All three deal with being, but dialectic merely tests, sophistic merely appears to know, and philosophy genuinely knows (1004b17–26, PDF 37).

#### Chapter 3 (1005a19–1005b34, PDF 37–38)
- **Axioms belong to first philosophy**: The philosopher who studies being qua being must also investigate the axioms that hold for all beings. These are not the province of any special science.
- **The firmest principle**: The principle of non-contradiction (PNC) is the most certain of all principles — it is impossible for the same attribute to belong and not belong to the same subject at the same time and in the same respect. This is non-hypothetical; anyone who knows anything about being must already possess it.
- **PNC is the starting-point for all demonstration**: All who carry out demonstrations refer back to PNC as an ultimate belief.

#### Chapter 4 (1005b35–1009a5, PDF 38–43)
- **Elenctic defence of PNC**: PNC cannot be demonstrated directly (that would require a prior principle), but it can be demonstrated "negatively" (elenchically) — by showing that anyone who says anything meaningful already presupposes it.
- **The semantic argument** (1006a11–1007a20, PDF 39–40): If "man" has one meaning (e.g., "two-footed animal"), then it is impossible for the same thing to be and not be a man at the same time. Words must signify something definite; otherwise reasoning is impossible.
- **Substance and accident** (1007a20–1007b18, PDF 40–41): Denying PNC collapses the distinction between substance and accident. If all attributes are accidental, there will be no primary subject; predication goes on *ad infinitum*, which is impossible.
- **Against Protagorean relativism** (1007b18–1008b1, PDF 41): If all contradictories are true of the same subject, all things will be one (a trireme, a wall, a man). This is the consequence of Protagoras's doctrine.
- **Practical self-refutation** (1008b1–1009a5, PDF 42–43): No one actually acts as if PNC were false — people avoid cliffs, judge some things better than others, and make qualified judgments. Even those who deny PNC make unqualified judgments about better and worse.

#### Chapter 5 (1009a6–1010b1, PDF 43–45)
- **Origins of PNC-denial in sense perception**: Both Protagoras's relativism and the denial of PNC originate from observing the sensible world — seeing contraries come from the same thing, or supposing that whatever appears to the senses must be true.
- **Empedocles, Democritus, Parmenides, Homer, Anaxagoras**: Various thinkers identified knowledge with sensation and therefore held that all appearances are true. Empedocles: "wisdom increases in men according to their present state." Parmenides: "'Tis one thing thinks — the substance of their limbs."
- **Cratylus's radical flux**: The professed Heracliteans, especially Cratylus, carried the doctrine of flux to its extreme, refusing to say anything and only moving a finger (1010a10–15, PDF 45).
- **Reply: change presupposes something stable**: That which is changing must have something from which it changes and something to which. Not everything is in motion; there is something that moves other things and is itself unmoved (1010a25–35, PDF 45).

#### Chapter 6 (1010b1–1011b22, PDF 45–48)
- **Appearance vs. truth**: Not everything that appears is true. Sensation is at least of its proper object; appearance is not the same as sensation. The healthy and the sick do not perceive alike, nor do the waking and the sleeping — and we do not treat these as equally authoritative.
- **Relative vs. absolute**: If all things are relative, still what appears does so *to someone*, *at some time*, *in some way*. The demand for an irresistible argument must itself guard against making everything relative.
- **Excluded middle and PNC restated**: Contradictory statements cannot both be true of the same thing; contraries cannot belong to a subject at the same time. Privation is the denial of a predicate to a determinate genus (1011b15–22, PDF 48).

#### Chapter 7 (1011b23–1012a28, PDF 48–49)
- **No intermediate between contradictories**: Of one subject, we must either affirm or deny any one predicate. The true and the false are defined: to say of what is that it is, is true; to say of what is that it is not, is false.
- **No grey between black and white for contradictories** (vs. contraries): The intermediate between contradictories would require either a change intermediate between generation and destruction, or a being that is in neither state — both are impossible.
- **Against Anaxagoras's "mixture"**: Heraclitus makes everything true; Anaxagoras, by positing an intermediate between contradictories, makes everything false (since the mixture is neither good nor not-good).

#### Chapter 8 (1012a29–1012b31, PDF 49–50)
- **Not everything is true; not everything is false**: The theory that everything is true is refuted because it also makes its own contrary true, hence self-defeating. The theory that everything is false likewise makes itself false.
- **The unmoved mover** (brief): Not all things are at rest or in motion; there is something that always moves things and is itself unmoved. This passage closes Book IV and pivots toward Book V's lexicon.

## 7. Cross-Pipeline Hooks

**Heidegger B&T**: The concept of "being qua being" (to on hēi on) is the Aristotelian antecedent to Heidegger's *Seinsfrage*. In B&T Introduction (§1, H.1–2), Heidegger explicitly names the forgetting of this question as the impetus for his project. The focal-meaning structure (*pros hen*) is the basis for Heidegger's account of the "analogical" unity of being, which he critiques as insufficient (SZ §1–2). [INTERP-high]

**Heidegger BCAP (GA 18)**: Heidegger's 1924 lecture course on "Basic Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy" devotes extensive discussion to *to on hēi on*, *ousia* as focal meaning, and the structure of Metaphysics Γ itself. The *pros hen* relation and the primacy of *ousia* are central to Heidegger's reading of Aristotle's ontology. [INTERP-high]

**Rickert**: The defence of PNC has a rhetorical dimension — the elenctic argument is a form of "ambient" constraint on discourse (anyone who speaks meaningfully is already within the space PNC governs). This resonates with Rickert's notion that rhetoric is not merely persuasion but a condition of intelligibility. [INTERP-high]
