# Phase 2 Analysis: RHET-02 — Rhetoric I.4–I.15

## Section 1: Unit Metadata

- **unit_id**: RHET-02
- **tier**: Standard
- **work**: Rhetoric
- **books/chapters**: I.4–I.15
- **Bekker range**: 1359a30–1377b12
- **PDF page range**: 12–44 (Revised Oxford Translation, ed. Barnes 2014)
- **label**: Topics of Deliberative, Forensic, Epideictic
- **cluster**: rhetoric
- **unit_thesis**: This unit furnishes the material content (*topoi*) for each of the three rhetorical species introduced in I.3. Chapters I.4–I.8 treat the five subjects of deliberation (ways and means, war and peace, national defence, imports/exports, legislation) and the political knowledge required for each, culminating in an account of the four forms of government and their ends. Chapters I.5–I.7 provide an extended analysis of happiness, the good, and the greater good as the substantive premises of deliberative rhetoric. Chapter I.9 treats excellence (*arete*) and the noble (*kalon*) as the matter of epideictic oratory. Chapters I.10–I.14 treat wrong-doing, pleasure, the states of mind and victims of wrong-doers, justice, equity, and degrees of wrong-doing as the matter of forensic oratory. Chapter I.15 treats the five non-technical proofs (laws, witnesses, contracts, tortures, oaths) specific to forensic contexts.

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 The five subjects of deliberation (I.4, 1359a30–1360a37)
- The deliberative orator counsels about things that are possible and within human power; he does not deal with all things but only those about which we may deliberate. 1359a30–1359b1 (p. 12).
- The five main subjects of deliberation: (1) ways and means (revenue), (2) war and peace, (3) national defence, (4) imports and exports, (5) legislation. 1359b19–23 (p. 12).
- Ways and means: the speaker must know the country's sources of revenue, whether any can be added or increased, and all expenditures that might be abolished or reduced. 1359b23–33 (p. 12).
- War and peace: one must know the military strength of the country (actual and potential), the nature of past wars, and the strength of neighbouring states. 1359b33–1360a5 (pp. 12–13).
- National defence: methods of defence, strength and position of forts, garrison sizes. 1360a5–14 (p. 13).
- Food supply: what is produced at home, what must be imported, and what commercial treaties are needed. 1360a14–23 (p. 13).
- Legislation: the speaker must know the various forms of government and what conditions preserve or destroy each. 1360a23–37 (p. 13).

### 2.2 Happiness as the end of deliberation (I.5, 1360b4–1362a14)
- Every individual and all men in common aim at happiness; deliberative rhetoric advises on what produces or destroys happiness. 1360b4–14 (p. 14).
- Happiness defined as "prosperity combined with excellence; or as independence of life; or as the secure enjoyment of the maximum of pleasure; or as a good condition of property and body, together with the power of guarding and making use of them." 1360b14–18 (p. 14).
- Constituent parts of happiness: good birth, plenty of friends, good friends, wealth, good children, plenty of children, a happy old age, bodily excellences (health, beauty, strength, stature, athletic powers), fame, honour, good luck, excellence. 1360b19–25 (p. 14).
- Extended inventory of each constituent: good birth, wealth (its constituents: coined money, territory, estates, implements, livestock, slaves), honour, health, beauty, strength, athletic excellence, old age, friends, good luck. 1360b31–1362a12 (pp. 14–16).

### 2.3 Goodness and utility as deliberative premises (I.6, 1362a15–1363b4)
- The deliberative orator's aim is utility (*to sumpheron*); deliberation seeks not ends but means to ends; utility is a good thing. 1362a15–21 (p. 16).
- The good defined as "that which ought to be chosen for its own sake; or that for the sake of which we choose something else; or that which is sought after by all things, or by all things that have sensation or reason." 1362a21–29 (p. 17).
- Extended list of things admitted to be good: happiness, justice, courage, temperance, magnanimity, magnificence, the excellences of the soul, health, beauty, the faculty of speech and action, the sciences, life itself. 1362b10–28 (p. 17).
- Things whose goodness is disputed may be argued for using contraries, consequences, and what is praised or chosen by the wise, the good, or the many. 1363a5–20 (p. 18).
- What is practicable, what is easy, what benefits friends or harms enemies — all are good. 1363a20–b4 (p. 18).

### 2.4 The greater good and the more useful: comparative topoi (I.7, 1363b5–1365b20)
- When two things are both useful, we must determine which is more so: the greater good, the more useful. 1363b5–12 (p. 19).
- A thing that surpasses another may be regarded as that other plus something more. 1363b12–15 (p. 19).
- Extended catalogue of comparative topoi: what is more lasting vs. more fleeting; what is an end vs. what is a means; what is self-sufficient vs. what needs supplement; what is an origin or cause; what is rarer; what is harder; what the wise or the many would choose; what better men possess; what is more honourable; what a science studies (as the science is more honourable, so its objects); what the practically wise judge to be better. 1363b15–1365b20 (pp. 19–22).
- The greater is what surpasses the normal; the great is what surpasses others. 1363b10–12 (p. 19).
- Things that are desirable for their own sake are greater goods than things desirable for something else. 1364a1–5 (p. 19).
- What is chosen by all, or most, or the wise is a greater good. 1364b1–5 (p. 21).
- The better thing is judged by better men, by judges, by experts, or by the authoritative in each science. 1364b5–15 (p. 21).

### 2.5 Forms of government and their ends (I.8, 1365b21–1366a22)
- The most important qualification for persuading audiences on public affairs is understanding the forms of government. 1365b21–28 (p. 24).
- Four forms of government: democracy, oligarchy, aristocracy, monarchy (kingship and tyranny). 1365b29–31 (p. 24).
- The end of democracy is freedom; of oligarchy, wealth; of aristocracy, the maintenance of education and national institutions; of tyranny, the protection of the tyrant. 1366a1–5 (p. 24).
- Rhetorical persuasion is effected not only by demonstrative argument but by ethical argument: the speaker must show himself to have certain qualities and know the character of each form of government. 1366a6–16 (p. 24).

### 2.6 Excellence and the noble: matter of epideictic (I.9, 1366a23–1368a37)
- Excellence (*arete*) and the noble (*kalon*) are the objects of praise and blame, the province of epideictic rhetoric. 1366a23–28 (p. 25).
- Excellence defined as "a faculty of providing and preserving good things; or a faculty of conferring many great benefits." 1366a36–b1 (p. 25).
- Parts of excellence: justice, courage, temperance, magnificence, magnanimity, liberality, gentleness, prudence, wisdom. 1366b1–4 (p. 25).
- Each virtue defined with its opposite: justice is the excellence through which everybody enjoys his own possessions in accordance with the law; courage disposes men to noble deeds in danger; temperance concerns obedience to the law regarding physical pleasures; liberality is spending money for others' good; magnanimity disposes to good on a large scale; magnificence is greatness in matters of spending; prudence is the understanding enabling wise decisions about goods and evils. 1366b4–1367a5 (pp. 25–26).
- The noble: what is desirable for its own sake and worthy of praise; what is good and pleasant because good. 1366a33–36 (p. 25).
- Noble actions include those done for one's country, not for oneself; those whose advantage is enjoyed after death; those done for others' sake; and all successes that benefit others rather than oneself. 1366b36–1367a5 (pp. 26–27).
- Praise and encomium: praise (*epainos*) sets forth a man's greatness of excellence; encomium (*enkomion*) recounts his deeds. Praise and counsel are essentially convertible — restate counsel as praise, and vice versa. 1367b26–1368a10 (pp. 28–29).
- Heightening: compare with famous men, represent coincidences as choices, use amplification. 1368a10–37 (pp. 28–29).

### 2.7 Wrong-doing, its causes, and the pleasant: matter of forensic (I.10–I.11, 1368b1–1372a33)
- Forensic rhetoric concerns accusation and defence; three things to ascertain: the nature and number of incentives to wrong-doing, the state of mind of wrong-doers, the kind of persons wronged and their condition. 1368b1–7 (p. 29).
- Wrong-doing defined as "injury voluntarily inflicted contrary to law." Law is either special (particular community) or general/universal. 1368b7–12 (p. 29).
- Voluntary acts are done with knowledge and without constraint. 1368b9–12 (p. 29).
- The causes of harmful and wicked acts contrary to law are vice and incontinence. 1368b15–17 (p. 29).
- Every action is due to one or more of seven causes: chance, nature, compulsion, habit, reasoning, anger, appetite. 1369a5–7 (p. 30).
- Rational desire (*boulesis*) is a desire for good; irrational desire is twofold: anger and appetite. 1369a1–4 (p. 30).
- Pleasure (I.11): "a movement by which the soul as a whole is consciously brought into its normal state of being; pain is the opposite." 1369b33–35 (p. 31).
- What is habitual is pleasant; what is natural is pleasant; habits are virtually natural. 1370a3–9 (p. 31).
- Imagination (*phantasia*) is "a feeble sort of sensation," and memory and expectation involve imagination; therefore things remembered and expected can be pleasant. 1370a28–33 (p. 32).
- Victory, honour, and good repute are among the most pleasant things. 1371a3–10 (pp. 33–34).
- Learning and wondering are pleasant; imitation is pleasant because "the spectator draws inferences." 1371b4–12 (p. 34).
- Things akin to oneself are pleasant: "mate delights mate," "jackdaw to jackdaw." 1371b12–18 (p. 34).

### 2.8 The state of mind and victims of wrong-doers; justice, equity, degrees of wrong (I.12–I.14, 1372a1–1377a5)
- I.12: Wrong-doers suppose the act can be done and done by them, that they can escape detection or punishment, or that the gain outweighs the penalty. 1372a5–13 (p. 35).
- Those who most easily wrong others: the eloquent, the experienced, those with many friends, the wealthy. 1372a13–19 (p. 35).
- Victims of wrong: those who have what the wrong-doer wants; those far off; the trusting; the careless; those who have been wronged many times before; those without friends; those unable to prosecute. 1372b17–1373a1 (pp. 37–38).
- I.13: Just and unjust actions classified relative to two kinds of law — particular law (the written law of each community) and universal law (the law of nature). 1373a1–18 (p. 38).
- Sophocles' *Antigone* cited: the burial of Polyneices was just by nature though forbidden by particular law. 1373b6–13 (p. 38).
- Empedocles cited: "not just for some people while unjust for others" — universal natural justice. 1373b14–17 (p. 38).
- Equity (*epieikeia*): a correction of legal justice where the law is defective owing to its generality. The law must speak in general terms; equity fills the gap. 1374a26–b2 (pp. 39–40).
- Equity bids us consider the intention rather than the act, the whole rather than the part, what a man is now rather than what he was. 1374b2–16 (p. 40).
- I.14: The worse wrong is that prompted by the worse disposition; the most trifling acts may be the worst (Callistratus and the half-obols). 1374b19–25 (p. 40).
- The worse deed is that which breaks unwritten law, which is done despite penalties, and which involves more victims or greater shame. 1375a5–15 (pp. 41–42).

### 2.9 Non-technical proofs: laws, witnesses, contracts, tortures, oaths (I.15, 1375a22–1377b12)
- Five non-technical proofs (*atechnoi pisteis*), specially characteristic of forensic oratory: laws, witnesses, contracts, tortures (*basanoi*), oaths. 1375a22–24 (p. 41).
- Laws: if the written law is against your case, appeal to universal law and equity; if it supports your case, argue that the juror's oath "to give my honest opinion" means not simply following the letter. 1375a25–b5 (p. 41).
- Witnesses: ancient (poets and notable persons) and recent. Ancient witnesses include Homer and Solon. Proverbs are a form of evidence. 1375b26–1376a7 (p. 42).
- Recent witnesses deal with questions of fact; "ancient witnesses are concerned with past events." 1376a7–8 (p. 42).
- Contracts: their credibility depends on the character of the signers; argue that contracts are a form of law, and that disregarding a contract repudiates law itself. 1376a30–b10 (p. 43).
- Tortures: argue compulsory evidence is truthful; or argue people under torture say what their torturers want. Both arguments are available. 1376b31–1377a7 (pp. 43–44).
- Oaths: a fourfold division — offer and accept, offer but not accept, accept but not offer, neither offer nor accept. The arguments for and against each combination are catalogued. 1377a8–b12 (p. 44).

## Section 6: Tensions & Open Questions

1. **Happiness: competing definitions (I.5, 1360b14–18)**: Aristotle offers four alternative definitions of happiness without settling on one: prosperity with excellence, independence of life, secure enjoyment of maximum pleasure, or good condition of body and property with the power to guard them. The hedonistic definition sits uneasily beside the virtue-based one. This plurality contrasts with the more unified account in *Nicomachean Ethics* I.7. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: RHET-02-P2.

2. **Rhetoric's dependence on political science (I.4, 1359b2–18; I.8, 1365b21–1366a22)**: Aristotle insists the orator must know political science — the forms of government, their ends, their conditions of preservation and destruction — yet also insists that rhetoric "does not belong to a more instructive art and a more real branch of knowledge" but has been "given a far wider subject-matter than strictly belongs to it" (1359b2–8). The boundary between rhetoric's legitimate claims and political science's proper domain remains unresolved. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: RHET-02-P1, RHET-02-P4.

3. **Universal law and its status (I.13, 1373a5–18)**: The appeal to a universal "law of nature" recognized by all sits in tension with Aristotle's general empiricist and contextualist tendencies. The citations of Sophocles, Empedocles, and Alcidamas as "evidence" for natural law are more rhetorical than philosophical — they establish common opinion (*endoxa*) rather than demonstrative proof. Whether Aristotle endorses natural law as a philosophical thesis or merely catalogues it as a useful rhetorical topos is disputed. [INTERP-medium] -- Linked to: RHET-02-P5.

4. **Praise and counsel as convertible (I.9, 1367b26–1368a10)**: The claim that praise and deliberative counsel are formally convertible — "restate the praise as counsel, and vice versa" — collapses the distinction between epideictic and deliberative species established in I.3. This creates a tension with the tripartite classification that is not fully resolved. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: RHET-02-P4.

5. **Equity as both legal and ethical (I.13, 1374a26–b16)**: Equity is introduced as a correction of written law's deficiency, yet the description shifts from a legal corrective to a broadly ethical disposition (considering intention over act, the whole character of a man, preferring arbitration to litigation). Whether equity is a principle within the legal system or a challenge to its adequacy from outside remains ambiguous. [INTERP-low] -- Linked to: RHET-02-P5.
