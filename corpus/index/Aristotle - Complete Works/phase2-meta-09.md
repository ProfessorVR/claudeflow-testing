## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 Three kinds of substance (ch. 1, 1069a18–1069b2)
- Inquiry concerns substance (οὐσία), since the principles and causes sought are principles of substances.
- Three kinds of substance: (1) sensible-perishable (plants, animals), (2) sensible-eternal (heavenly bodies), (3) unmovable (dealt with in this book).
- Sensible substances are the subject of physics; unmovable substance, if it exists, is the subject of a prior science (first philosophy).

### 2.2 Change and the principles of sensible substance (chs. 2–3, 1069b3–1070a30)
- All change is from something to something: from a subject (ὑποκείμενον) by way of privation (στέρησις) to form (εἶδος/μορφή).
- The substratum persists through change; form and privation are the two contraries.
- Matter (ὕλη) is the proximate substratum that is potentially each thing.
- The principles of sensible substance are form, privation, and matter — analogically the same for all things though different in each case.

### 2.3 Causes and principles are analogically the same for all things (ch. 4, 1070a31–1070b35)
- The causes of different things are different in species but analogically the same: form, privation, and matter.
- The external moving cause is also a principle: actuality (ἐνέργεια) and potentiality (δύναμις) function as principles.
- Universals do not exist; the individual is the cause of the individual (Peleus of Achilles, "your father of you").

### 2.4 Actuality and potentiality as principles; the primacy of actuality (ch. 5, 1071a1–1071b2)
- Some things can exist apart and others cannot; the former are substances.
- All things have the same causes because without substances there are no affections and movements.
- The causes will probably be soul and body, or reason and desire and body.
- Actuality and potency are principles in yet another way, applying differently in different cases.

### 2.5 The argument for an eternal unmoved mover (ch. 6, 1071b3–1072a18)
- Movement must be eternal, since time cannot come into being or cease to be (there is no "before" or "after" without time).
- Time is continuous; therefore movement is continuous; circular motion alone is continuous.
- There must be a principle whose very substance is actuality — not merely potential, for then it might not act and there would be no eternal movement.
- This substance must be without matter, for it must be eternal and actual.
- **The difficulty**: everything that acts seems able not to act; potentiality seems prior to actuality. But if so, nothing at all need exist. Against Leucippus, Plato, and the mythologists who derive the world from night or chaos: actuality is prior to potentiality.
- Actuality's priority is confirmed by Anaxagoras (νοῦς as actuality), Empedocles (love and strife), and Leucippus (eternal movement).

### 2.6 The unmoved mover as object of desire and thought (ch. 7, 1072a19–1073a13)
- Something always moved with unceasing circular motion exists both in theory and in fact: the first heaven is eternal.
- There is also something that moves it without being moved: the mover is eternal, substance, and actuality.
- **The object of desire and the object of thought move without being moved.** The primary objects of desire and thought are the same: the apparent good is the object of appetite, the real good is the primary object of wish.
- Desire is consequent on opinion rather than opinion on desire; thinking is the starting-point.
- Thought is moved by the object of thought; in the column of "in itself," substance is first, and in substance, that which is simple and exists actually.
- The good and the desirable are on the same side of the column of opposites; the first in any class is always best.
- **The unmoved mover produces motion by being loved** (ἐρώμενον), not by mechanical pushing; all other things move by being moved.
- The first mover, of necessity, exists; and in so far as it is necessary, it is good, and is a first principle.
- **On such a principle depend the heavens and the world of nature.**

### 2.7 The divine life: thought thinking itself (ch. 7 cont., 1072b14–1073a13)
- God's life is like our best state — but God is always in that state, while we enjoy it only briefly.
- God's actuality is also pleasure: waking, perception, and thinking are most pleasant because of their reference to actuality.
- **Thought in itself deals with that which is best in itself; thought in the fullest sense with that which is best in the fullest sense.**
- Thought thinks itself by sharing the nature of its object; it becomes its object in coming into contact with and thinking it, so that **thought and object of thought are the same**.
- That which is *capable* of receiving the object of thought is thought; it is *active* when it *possesses* it. Therefore the latter — the actuality of possession — is the divine element.
- **The actuality of thought is life; God is that actuality; God's essential actuality is life most good and eternal.**
- God is a living being, eternal, most good; life and duration continuous and eternal belong to God.

### 2.8 Refutation of the Pythagoreans and Speusippus on beauty (ch. 7 end, 1073a14–1073a13)
- Against those who say supreme beauty and goodness are not present in the beginning (because seeds are *causes* but beauty is in the *effects*): the seed comes from a prior complete individual, so the complete is first.
- The substance that is the unmoved mover is eternal, separate from sensibles, without parts and indivisible, without magnitude.
- It produces movement through infinite time; nothing finite has infinite power; therefore it has no magnitude.
- It is impassive and unalterable; all other changes are posterior to change of place.

### 2.9 The number of unmoved movers / celestial spheres (ch. 8, 1073a14–1074b14)
- Whether one or many unmovable substances must be considered; other opinions (Ideas, mathematical objects) provide no account of how the number should be determined.
- The number of substances must equal the number of eternal celestial movements.
- Eudoxus: sun and moon each have 3 spheres; planets each have 4 spheres.
- Callippus added more spheres for sun, moon, and planets.
- Counter-acting ("unwinding") spheres are required so that the motion of outer planets does not disrupt inner ones: total spheres = 55 (or 49 if some adjustments are omitted).
- The unmovable substances and principles are this many in number — "the assertion of necessity must be left to more powerful thinkers."
- There is one heaven: the unmovable first mover is one in formula and in number; therefore that which is moved always and continuously is one; therefore there is one heaven alone.

### 2.10 The divine thought and its object (ch. 9, 1074b15–1075a10)
- **The nature of divine thought involves certain problems.** If it thinks nothing, it is like one who sleeps. If it thinks something other than itself, its substance is not the act of thinking but a capacity, and it is not the best substance.
- What does it think? Either itself or something else; either always the same or something different.
- It thinks that which is most divine and precious, and it does not change; change would be for the worse.
- If it is not thinking but a capacity, continuous thinking would be wearisome. Something else would be more precious than thought — the object.
- **Therefore it must think itself, and its thinking is a thinking on thinking** (νόησις νοήσεως νόησις).
- But knowledge, perception, opinion, and understanding always have something else as object. If thinking and being thought are different, in respect of which does goodness belong to thought? Answer: in some cases the knowledge *is* the object (in productive sciences, the substance minus matter; in theoretical sciences, the formula or the act of thinking *is* the object).
- For things without matter, thinking and the object of thought are the same; the divine thinking and its object will be one.

### 2.11 The good as principle of cosmic order (ch. 10, 1075a11–1076a4)
- In which of two ways does the universe contain the good: as something separate (like a general) or as the order of the parts (like an army)? Probably in both ways, but more in the former — the good does not depend on the order but the order depends on it.
- All things are ordered together somehow; the world is not a series of disconnected episodes.
- Against rival cosmologies: contraries cannot produce everything; no one explains *why* there should always be becoming; those who posit two principles need a third; the Forms and numbers are causally idle.
- Empedocles makes the good (love) a motive principle, but paradoxically also matter.
- Anaxagoras makes the good (νοῦς) a motive principle — thought moves things for the sake of something.
- Against those who make the good and the bad equal co-principles: the good is a principle in the highest degree, but *how* — as end, as mover, or as form — they do not say.
- The universe must not be governed badly: **"The rule of many is not good; let there be one ruler"** (Homer, *Iliad* II.204).

## 7. Cross-Pipeline Hooks (optional)

- **ἐνέργεια / Wirklichkeit**: Lambda's identification of the unmoved mover's substance with pure ἐνέργεια (actuality) is the exact terminological target of Heidegger's BCAP §§25–28, where Heidegger deconstructs the translation of ἐνέργεια as *Wirklichkeit* (mere "effectiveness") and argues for a recovery of its original sense as "being-at-work." Lambda 1071b12–22 is the decisive passage. [INTERP-high]

- **κίνησις / Bewegtheit**: Lambda's argument that eternal motion requires an actual (not merely potential) mover connects directly to BCAP's treatment of κίνησις as the fundamental character of being for Aristotle. Heidegger reads Lambda 6–7 as the culmination of Aristotle's ontology of movement. [INTERP-high]

- **νόησις νοήσεως νόησις / Erschlossenheit**: The self-referential structure of divine thought (thought thinking itself, ch. 9) is a key point of contact for Heidegger's analysis of Dasein's self-disclosedness in B&T §§29–34, though Heidegger explicitly breaks with the theistic-contemplative framing. [INTERP-high]

| T1: Actuality prior vs. potentiality prior | ἐνέργεια | δύναμις | internal | Aristotle argues forcefully that actuality is prior to potentiality (1071b22–1072a18), but acknowledges the contrary view that "everything that acts is able to act but not everything that is able to act acts, so that potentiality is prior" (1071b23). | T5: Teleological order vs. mechanical astronomy | τέλος (the good as ordering principle) | astronomical sphere mechanics | internal | Ch. 10 presents the universe as ordered toward the good; ch. 8 presents a complex mechanical model of nested counter-rotating spheres.
