- **unit_thesis**: Book V classifies types of change and motion by distinguishing change in general (metabole) from motion in the strict sense (kinesis), identifies the three species of motion (alteration, growth/diminution, locomotion), defines the topological relations of together, apart, contact, between, succession, contiguity, and continuity, establishes the criteria for unity of motion (generically, specifically, and numerically one), determines what it means for motions to be contrary, and analyzes the contrariety of motion with rest.

## 4. Main Positions

### PHYS-05-P1: Only change from subject to subject counts as motion proper

Aristotle distinguishes four logical combinations of change: subject-to-subject, subject-to-non-subject, non-subject-to-subject, and non-subject-to-non-subject 225a1--12 (PDF 68). The last is ruled out as not involving opposition. Non-subject-to-subject is coming to be (genesis), and subject-to-non-subject is perishing (phthora); neither qualifies as motion because the terminus of one and the origin of the other is a non-subject, and "that which is not cannot be in motion" 225b33 (PDF 68). Only change from subject to subject -- that is, between contraries or their intermediates -- is motion in the strict sense (kinesis). This classification provides the logical framework for the subsequent identification of the three species of motion.

### PHYS-05-P2: There are exactly three species of motion -- alteration, growth/diminution, and locomotion

Since motion requires both a subject and a pair of contraries, Aristotle eliminates the categories in which no contrariety obtains. There is no motion in substance, because substance has no contrary 226a10 (PDF 69). There is no motion in relation, because when one correlative changes, the other may change without itself being in motion 226a10--15 (PDF 69). No motion of motion or becoming of becoming is possible, on pain of infinite regress 226a25--b1 (PDF 70). What remains are the categories that admit contraries: quality (alteration), quantity (growth and diminution), and place (locomotion) 226b1--16 (PDF 70--71). These three exhaust the species of motion.

### PHYS-05-P3: Continuity, contiguity, and succession form a nested hierarchy of topological relations

Chapter 3 defines a series of terms in ascending order of structural intimacy. Things are "in succession" when nothing of the same kind lies between them 227a1 (PDF 72). Things are "contiguous" when they are in succession and their extremities are in contact 227a6 (PDF 72). Things are "continuous" when the touching limits become one and the same, forming a unity 227a10 (PDF 72). Continuity entails contiguity, which entails succession, but not conversely: "if there is continuity there is necessarily contact, but if there is contact, that alone does not imply continuity" 227a15--20 (PDF 72). This hierarchy provides the apparatus for Book VI's demonstration that magnitudes, motions, and times are continuous and infinitely divisible.

### PHYS-05-P4: Motions are contrary only when one is from a contrary to the opposite contrary and the other reverses this direction

Chapter 5 systematically eliminates alternative accounts of contrariety among motions. Motion from A to contrary B is not contrary to motion from B to A, because these are the same motion (health-to-disease is sickening in both directions of description) 229b10--20 (PDF 75). Nor are motions from contraries to their respective opposites contrary, for a motion from a contrary to an intermediate is also possible 229b20--230a5 (PDF 75). The only genuine contrariety holds between a motion from one contrary to the other and the reverse motion: "thus falling ill is regarded as contrary to recovering one's health, and being taught as contrary to being led into error by another; for their goals are contrary" 230a5--10 (PDF 76).

## 6. Tensions

### T1: Change (metabole) includes coming-to-be/perishing, but these are not motions

Aristotle classifies coming-to-be and perishing as genuine changes (metabolai) while excluding them from motion (kinesis) on the grounds that their termini involve non-subjects 225a1--b9 (PDF 68). Yet perishing and coming-to-be are among the most conspicuous phenomena studied by the physicist. The tension lies in the fact that the strict definition of motion (subject-to-subject change) excludes what are arguably the most dramatic natural transformations. Aristotle handles this by making "change" (metabole) the wider genus and "motion" (kinesis) a species within it, but this leaves open the question of what principles govern the non-motional changes.

### T2: No motion in substance vs. substantial change as fundamental natural process

At 226a10 (PDF 69), Aristotle denies motion in the category of substance because substance has no contrary. Yet substantial generation and corruption are central to his natural philosophy (De Generatione et Corruptione). The exclusion of substance from the domain of kinesis creates a systematic gap: the most fundamental kind of natural change -- the coming-to-be and passing-away of substances -- falls outside the classificatory scheme that is supposed to govern natural motion. The resolution depends on the distinction between change (metabole) and motion (kinesis), but the tension between the primacy of substance in Aristotle's ontology and its exclusion from the kinetic framework persists.

### T3: Locomotion as one of three species vs. locomotion as primary motion

Book V treats alteration, growth/diminution, and locomotion as coordinate species of motion 226b1--16 (PDF 70--71). Yet Aristotle elsewhere (Physics VIII.7, 260a26--261a26) argues that locomotion is the primary and most fundamental kind of motion, upon which the others depend. Within Book V itself, the treatment is purely classificatory and taxonomic, granting no priority to locomotion. This creates a tension between the egalitarian taxonomy of V and the hierarchical ordering established in VIII.
