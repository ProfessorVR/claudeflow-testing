## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### Book A (I): First Causes and Wisdom (980a21–993a27, PDF 3–20)

#### Ch. 1 — The cognitive hierarchy: sensation to experience (980a21–981b13, PDF 3–4)

- 980a21–25 (PDF 3): All men by nature desire to know; the senses, especially sight, are loved for their own sake.
- 980a27–981a1 (PDF 3): Animals possess sensation; from sensation arises memory in some; from many memories of the same thing arises experience.
- 981a1–15 (PDF 3–4): Experience is knowledge of individuals; art (*techne*) is knowledge of universals and knows the "why."
- 981a15–b5 (PDF 4): In practice, experience is not inferior to art; the experienced physician who knows the individual case may succeed where the theoretician who knows only the universal fails.

#### Ch. 1–2 — Art, wisdom, and the marks of the wise (981b13–982b10, PDF 4–5)

- 981b1–982a1 (PDF 4): Master-workers are wiser than manual workers because they know causes; ability to teach is a sign of knowledge.
- 982a4–b10 (PDF 5): Characteristics of the wise man enumerated: knows all things (as far as possible), knows what is difficult, is more exact, is more capable of teaching causes, pursues knowledge for its own sake, is architectonic rather than subservient.

#### Ch. 2 — Wisdom as knowledge of first causes; philosophy begins in wonder (982b11–983a23, PDF 5–6)

- 982b7–10 (PDF 5): Wisdom is knowledge of the first principles and causes.
- 982b11–25 (PDF 5–6): Philosophy begins in wonder — men wonder first at the obvious, then at greater matters (the moon, the sun, the genesis of the universe). They philosophize to escape ignorance, not for utility.
- 982b25–983a5 (PDF 6): This is the only free science, pursued for its own sake; it is most divine.
- 983a5–10 (PDF 6): This science is divine in two senses: it is knowledge God would have, and its object (first causes) is divine.

#### Ch. 3 — The four causes introduced (983a24–983b6, PDF 6)

- 983a24–b6 (PDF 6): We know a thing when we know its cause; causes are spoken of in four senses: (1) substance/essence (*to ti en einai*), (2) matter/substratum (*hule*), (3) source of movement (*arche tes kineseos*), (4) the final cause / the good (*to hou heneka*).
- 983a33–b6 (PDF 6): These have been treated in the *Physics*; we now survey predecessors to see whether they discovered any further kind of cause.

#### Chs. 3–4 — Material cause: the early natural philosophers (983b6–985b4, PDF 7–9)

- 983b6–984a5 (PDF 7): Most early thinkers posited only material causes — that of which all things consist, from which they come to be, and into which they are finally resolved.
- 983b20–27 (PDF 7): Thales posited water as the first principle.
- 984a5–7 (PDF 7): Anaximenes and Diogenes posited air; Hippasus and Heraclitus posited fire.
- 984a8–16 (PDF 7): Empedocles posited four elements (earth, air, fire, water); Anaxagoras posited infinite homogeneous principles.
- 984b8–25 (PDF 8): The material cause alone was found inadequate: the substratum does not make itself change; something must explain the source of change.

#### Ch. 4 — Efficient cause: love, reason, strife (984b23–985b4, PDF 8–9)

- 984b15–22 (PDF 8): Anaxagoras uses *nous* (reason) as the cause of the world's order, but as a *deus ex machina* — when at a loss for a cause, he drags in reason; in all other cases he ascribes events to anything but reason.
- 984b23–985a10 (PDF 8): Hesiod and Parmenides first looked for love or desire as a cosmogonic principle.
- 985a21–b4 (PDF 9): Empedocles first to introduce the efficient cause in a divided form: friendship as cause of good, strife as cause of evil; first to speak of four material elements.

#### Ch. 5 — The Pythagoreans and number (985b23–986b8, PDF 10–11)

- 985b23–986a13 (PDF 10): The Pythagoreans held that numbers are the principles of all things; they saw numerical ratios in musical scales, fitted the heavens into a numerical scheme, and supposed number to be the element of all things.
- 986a15–22 (PDF 10): They consider number as both matter and as modifications/states of things.
- 986a22–b2 (PDF 10–11): Ten pairs of contraries arranged in two columns: limit/unlimited, odd/even, one/plurality, right/left, male/female, resting/moving, straight/curved, light/darkness, good/bad, square/oblong.
- 986a27–b2 (PDF 10): Alcmaeon of Croton similarly held contrarieties as principles.

#### Ch. 5 (cont.) — The Eleatics: Parmenides, Xenophanes, Melissus (986b8–987a28, PDF 11–12)

- 986b18–27 (PDF 11): Xenophanes, first of the Eleatic monists, said the One is God; gave "no clear statement."
- 986b27–987a2 (PDF 11): Parmenides posits the One as being; forced by phenomena, he admits two principles — hot (being) and cold (non-being). Parmenides "seems to speak with somewhat more insight."
- 987a9–28 (PDF 11–12): Summary: up to the Italian school, two kinds of cause recognised — matter and the source of movement.

#### Ch. 6 — Plato's philosophy (987a29–988b16, PDF 12–13)

- 987a29–b14 (PDF 12): Plato influenced by Cratylus/Heraclitean flux and by Socrates' search for universal definitions; posited Forms as a separate class of entities. "Participation" was new only in name (Pythagoreans said "imitation").
- 987b18–988a7 (PDF 12): The Forms are causes of essence; the One is the cause of the Forms; the great and the small is the material cause.
- 988a8–16 (PDF 13): Plato used only two causes: the formal (Forms) and the material (great and small); he assigns good to the Forms and evil to the elements.

#### Ch. 7 — Summary: only four causes found (988a17–988b16, PDF 13)

- 988a18–22 (PDF 13): No predecessor has mentioned any cause beyond the four. All thinkers have grasped the causes vaguely; none has stated them clearly.
- 988a22–b16 (PDF 13): Some speak of the first principle as matter, some as the source of movement, some of both. No one has clearly stated the formal cause except Plato; the final cause has been touched on only incidentally.

#### Ch. 8 — Critique of monistic material principles (988b17–990a32, PDF 14–16)

- 988b22–989a18 (PDF 14): Those who posit one material element err: they cannot account for incorporeal things, for generation and destruction, or for the cause of movement.
- 989a18–b21 (PDF 14–15): Priority dispute among elements (fire vs. earth vs. water vs. air): those who favour the finest-grained element (fire) have the best argument from combination, but the argument from generation favours earth/water.
- 989b21–990a32 (PDF 15–16): Anaxagoras' theory that all things were mixed: if followed out, his position becomes something like the later distinction between the indefinite and the One. These thinkers deal only with generation/destruction/movement; they neglect substance and being.

#### Ch. 9 — Critique of Platonic Forms (990a33–993a10, PDF 16–20)

- 990a33–b8 (PDF 16): Those who posit Forms, seeking to grasp causes, introduced others equal in number — as if trying to count things by adding more.
- 990b8–17 (PDF 16): Arguments for Forms are inconclusive; some prove Forms of things we do not wish to admit (negations, perishable things). The "third man" argument produces infinite regress.
- 990b17–991a8 (PDF 16–17): The more accurate arguments lead to Ideas of relations and the "third man" difficulty.
- 991a8–b9 (PDF 17): Forms contribute nothing to sensible things — no movement, no change, no knowledge of other things. They are not even in the things that share them.
- 991b9–992b1 (PDF 18–19): Forms as numbers: difficulties with generation of numbers from the One and the indefinite dyad. How can one Form come from many numbers? The intermediates between Forms and sensibles are unexplained.
- 992a32–b18 (PDF 19): Philosophy has become mathematics for modern thinkers; they cannot explain how elements of existing things combine, nor can they account for movement.
- 992b18–993a10 (PDF 19–20): General critique: if we search for elements without distinguishing the many senses of "element" and "being," we cannot succeed. Lines, planes, and solids cannot be explained on the theory.

#### Ch. 10 — Conclusion and transition (993a11–993a27, PDF 20)

- 993a11–15 (PDF 20): All predecessors seem to have sought the causes named in the *Physics*, though vaguely; the earliest philosophy "lisps" on all subjects, since in its beginnings it is but a child.
- 993a24–27 (PDF 20): The difficulties raised here may get some help toward later inquiries.

## 6. Tensions

| Tension | Node A | Node B | Type | Description |
|---------|--------|--------|------|-------------|
| Experience vs. art in practice | Experience sometimes succeeds better than art (981a15, PDF 4) | Art is higher knowledge because it knows universals and causes (981b2, PDF 4) | epistemological | Aristotle grants that the experienced physician may cure better than the theoretician who lacks knowledge of individuals, yet insists art is superior knowledge. The hierarchy of knowing does not perfectly track practical success. |
| Completeness claim for four causes | No predecessor found any cause beyond these four (988a18, PDF 13) | The historical survey shows thinkers grasping causes only "vaguely" and partially (993a15, PDF 20) | methodological | If predecessors only groped toward the four causes, the inductive confirmation of their completeness rests on a survey of thinkers who may have been looking for something different. The framework is presented as exhaustive but proven only by the failure of alternatives. |
| Critique of Forms vs. reliance on formal cause | Extended refutation of Platonic Forms (990a33–993a10, PDF 16–20) | Aristotle himself requires a formal cause (essence/definition) for his four-cause framework (983a27, PDF 6) | ontological | Aristotle demolishes the Platonic version of formal causation without yet providing his own positive account. The reader is left with the four-cause scheme minus an adequate treatment of how form functions non-Platonically. |
| Wisdom as divine yet humanly attainable | "God alone can have" this science; it is "most divine" (983a5–10, PDF 6) | Yet it is not impossible for man, and we pursue it freely (982b25–28, PDF 6) | theological | Aristotle insists the science is divine in two senses (its object is divine, and God would possess it), yet denies the poets' claim that God would be jealous. The tension between the superhuman character of first philosophy and its human pursuit is noted but not resolved here. |
| Anaxagoras: praised and criticised | Anaxagoras "seemed like a sober man" for introducing *nous* (984b17, PDF 8) | Yet he uses reason only as a *deus ex machina* and otherwise ascribes events to anything but reason (985a18–21, PDF 8) | historical | Aristotle's ambivalence toward Anaxagoras reveals a recurring pattern: each predecessor glimpses a truth but fails to develop it. The praise/blame structure is methodologically productive but historiographically tendentious. |
