# Phase 2 Analysis: AUD-01 — On Things Heard

- **authorship_note**: Disputed — traditionally attributed to the Peripatetic school (possibly Strato of Lampsacus), not securely to Aristotle. Text edited by C. Prantl (Teubner, Leipzig, 1881).
- **Unit thesis**: This treatise provides a systematic material account of sound production and vocal quality, arguing that all sounds arise from the impact of air against bodies or of bodies against air, and that the qualitative differences among sounds (hard, soft, clear, rough, thick, thin, piping, cracked) are determined by the physical properties of the lungs, windpipe, mouth, and instrumental materials (horn, reed, string), together with the conditions of the transmitting air.

## Section 2: Hierarchical Outline

### 2.1 General principles of sound production (800a1–800b5)
- All sounds, articulate or inarticulate, are produced by the meeting of bodies or of air with bodies — not because the air assumes shapes, but because it is set in motion by contraction, expansion, compression, or impact from breath or strings (800a1–5 | PDF p. 3).
- Sound travels unaltered in quality as far as the disturbance of air reaches; though the disturbance originates at a particular point, its force disperses over an extending area like breezes (800a8–15 | PDF p. 3).
- Breath differs among creatures owing to differences in vessels (windpipe, lungs, mouth); the mouth makes most difference to the voice, since animals imitate diverse sounds merely by expelling air from the mouth differently (800a18–30 | PDF p. 3).
- The lungs: when small, thick, and hard, they cannot admit air in large quantities nor expel it with force; force of breath depends on the lungs contracting after wide distension (800a31–800b5 | PDF pp. 3–4).

### 2.2 The windpipe and vocal differentiation (800b5–801a25)
- The lung must be large, soft, and flexible to admit and expel air at will (800b5–18 | PDF p. 4).
- A long and narrow windpipe emits the voice with difficulty and requires considerable force; illustrated by long-necked birds (geese, cranes, fowls) (800b20–30 | PDF p. 4).
- A narrow windpipe compresses breath, which disperses like streams through narrow straits; a wide windpipe allows breath to pass easily but lacks solidity, so creatures with wide windpipes cannot differentiate clearly (800b30–801a1 | PDF pp. 4–5).
- An irregular windpipe causes difficulties of every kind; a short windpipe necessitates quick expulsion and higher pitch (801a1–10 | PDF p. 5).
- Condition of vessels matters: moisture in the lungs and windpipe disperses and thickens the breath (catarrh, drunkenness); absolute dryness makes the voice hard and dispersed; slight moisture holds the air together and produces unity in the voice (801a10–20 | PDF p. 5).

### 2.3 Sound localisation and the conditions of clearness (801a25–802a1)
- Though sounds originate at various locations, we hear them only when they strike the ear; air struck by impact is borne along in a mass then gradually disperses, so we distinguish near from distant sounds (801a25–30 | PDF p. 5).
- Pipe and trumpet analogy: sound passing through a tube is not dispersed, so it remains uniform and localised (801a30–801b1 | PDF pp. 5–6).
- Analogy to painting: just as near and far objects in a picture appear to occupy the same plane, articulate and inarticulate sounds striking the ear simultaneously seem equidistant (801b1–801b10 | PDF p. 6).
- Voices are distinct in proportion to accuracy: sounds not perfectly articulated are indistinct, like imperfect sealings of signet-rings (801b10–15 | PDF p. 6).
- Children, the elderly, the drunk, and those with speech impediments cannot speak distinctly; in music, brass instruments and horns produce less distinct sound when breath escapes irregularly (801b15–25 | PDF p. 6).
- A single voice is understood better than many simultaneous ones; stringed instruments and oboes played together confuse the ear (801b25–802a1 | PDF p. 6).

### 2.4 Clearness and colour-analogy; hard and soft sounds (802a1–803a5)
- Clearness in sound resembles clearness in colour: the most penetrating, solid, and pure stimuli produce the most distinct sense-impressions (802a1–10 | PDF p. 6).
- "Grey" voices are harsh, confused, and uncontrolled; "white" voices are clear with concentrated breath; vocal clearness requires concentrated and intense impacts on the external air (802a10–802b1 | PDF pp. 6–7).
- Oboes and other instruments: clear sounds come from concentrated breath; weak or scattered breath produces indistinct notes (802b1–15 | PDF p. 7).
- Horns: notes produced by horns striking air in a continuous mass are indistinct; the horn must be regular, smooth, dry, of uniform thickness, with straight pores (802b15–802b40 | PDF p. 7).
- The direction of sound follows a straight line, shown by carpenters testing beams; flaws in wood cause sound to cease and disperse, as knots in timber interrupt transmission (802b25–35 | PDF p. 7).
- Baking of horns contributes to tonal excellence; dry, callous, porous horns of old animals produce better notes than soft, moist horns of young animals (802b40–803a5 | PDF pp. 7–8).

### 2.5 Qualities of voice and sound: hard, soft, shrill, rough, thin, thick, piping, cracked (803a5–804b10)
- Hard voices are those that strike forcibly upon the hearing, difficult to start but travelling with added force; illustrated by missiles, streams through straits, forcible sounds of bronze, iron, and thunder (803a5–803a40 | PDF p. 8).
- Quickness of breathing makes the voice shrill; force makes it hard; the same individual may have sometimes a shriller, sometimes a deeper voice (803a40–803b10 | PDF p. 8).
- The real cause of vocal hardness or softness is the force of the impact of breath from the lungs, not the hardness of the windpipe alone; soft and hard lung tissue produces corresponding breath (803b10–25 | PDF pp. 8–9).
- Sounds produced by strings, horns, oboes, and reeds follow the same principles: tightly stretched strings and well-baked horns are hard; loosely stretched strings and unbaked horns are softer (803b25–803b40 | PDF p. 9).
- Rough voices arise when the impact of air on the ear is not single but divided and dispersed, each portion striking separately; compared to the sense of touch with rough objects and to the sound of a file (803b40–804a15 | PDF p. 9).
- Thin voices are emitted when the breath is small in quantity: children's voices, women's, eunuchs', and the enfeebled; thin strings produce thin sounds with narrow impacts on the air (804a18–804a30 | PDF p. 9).
- The quality of the air striking the ear preserves the quality of the sound at its origin; string impacts, though many and distinct, appear united and continuous to the ear due to rapid intermittence, like separate coloured objects appearing to join when moved rapidly (804a30–804b1 | PDF p. 9).
- Concords: notes overlapping and ceasing at the same moment cause intermediate sounds to escape notice; in all concords the more frequent (shriller) note strikes simultaneously with the earlier slower sound, producing apparent continuity (804b1–10 | PDF pp. 9–10).

### 2.6 Remaining vocal types: thick, piping, cracked, aspirated, broken, stammering (804b10–end)
- Thick sounds are produced when breath is emitted in great quantity; men's voices are thicker than women's, and "perfect" oboes well filled with air are thick (804b10–20 | PDF p. 10).
- Piping voices are thin, concentrated, and not followed by a second sound; piping quality depends on shrillness and accuracy, not volume or tension; lightly constructed instruments and running water produce piping notes (804b20–804b30 | PDF p. 10).
- Cracked voices travel in a solid mass then disperse; illustrated by earthenware vessels, broken horns, badly strung strings (804b30–804b40 | PDF p. 10).
- Aspirated sounds are formed when breath is emitted immediately with the sound; smooth sounds are formed without emission of breath (804b40–804b45 | PDF p. 10).
- Voices become broken when the lungs collapse after distension, as legs and shoulders collapse from a strained position (804b45–804b50 | PDF p. 10).
- Stammering is due to difficulty in changing the position of the tongue; stammerers keep repeating the same word because the movements of articulation continue in the same direction by impetus, like a running body difficult to divert; stammerers often stammer when angry because they force out their breath (804b50–end | PDF p. 10).
