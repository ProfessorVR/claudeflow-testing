## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### Book Δ (V)

Book Δ is a philosophical lexicon of thirty key terms. Each chapter distinguishes multiple senses of a single term, typically moving from ordinary or derivative senses to the philosophically primary sense. The entries do not form a continuous argument but collectively map the conceptual vocabulary presupposed by the investigations of Books Ζ–Θ.

#### Chapter 1: Beginning (archē) (1012b34–1013a23, PDF 54)
- Six senses of "beginning" (*archē*): (1) starting-point of a line or journey; (2) the best starting-point for learning; (3) the intrinsic first component from which a thing arises (e.g., keel of a ship); (4) the external cause from which movement or change first proceeds; (5) that at whose will what is moved moves (e.g., civic authorities); (6) the starting-point of knowledge of a thing.
- Common feature: all beginnings are the first point from which a thing is, comes to be, or is known.

#### Chapter 2: Cause (aition) (1013a24–1014a25, PDF 54–55)
- Four senses of "cause" corresponding to the four causes: (1) material cause — that from which a thing comes to be and persists (bronze of a statue); (2) formal cause — the formula or pattern (the ratio 2:1 for the octave); (3) efficient cause — the primary source of change or rest (the father as cause of the child, the advisor as cause of the action); (4) final cause — the "that for the sake of which" (health as cause of walking).
- Causes may be causes of each other (exercise causes fitness, fitness causes exercise), though in different senses.

#### Chapter 3: Element (stoicheion) (1014a26–1014b15, PDF 55–56)
- Element is the primary immanent component of a thing, indivisible into other kinds. Letters are elements of speech; the elements of bodies are those into which bodies are ultimately divided (earth, water, etc.). Extended to elements of geometrical proofs and of arguments generally.
- Common feature: the element is first, simple, and indivisible.

#### Chapter 4: Nature (phusis) (1014b16–1015a19, PDF 56–57)
- Five senses: (1) the genesis of growing things; (2) the primary immanent element from which growth proceeds; (3) the source of primary movement in each natural object, present in it in virtue of its own essence; (4) the primary matter of which a non-natural object consists (bronze of a statue); (5) the substance of natural things — the form or essence.
- In the primary and strict sense, nature is the substance of things that have in themselves a source of movement.

#### Chapter 5: Necessary (anankaion) (1015a20–1015b15, PDF 57)
- Five senses: (1) a contributing condition without which life is impossible (breathing, food); (2) that without which the good cannot be obtained or evil avoided; (3) compulsion — what impedes and hinders contrary to impulse and choice; (4) that which cannot be otherwise; (5) demonstration, as the conclusion of a demonstration cannot be otherwise.
- The necessary in the primary sense is the simple, for the simple cannot be in more than one state.

#### Chapter 6: One (hen) (1015b16–1017a6, PDF 57–59)
- **Accidentally one**: Coriscus and musical are one because one is an accident of the other.
- **One by nature** (*kath' hauto*): (a) the continuous; (b) that whose substratum is indivisible in kind; (c) things whose genus is one though distinguished by opposite differentiae; (d) things whose formula of essence is one.
- **(e)** That which is a quantity and continuous, and has one form, is most truly one — whole and complete.
- **What it is to be one**: to be a beginning of number; the first measure of each class. The one is everywhere indivisible either in quantity or in kind.
- Unity by analogy, by genus, by species, by number: things one in number share the same matter; in species the same formula; in genus the same figure of predication; by analogy the same proportion.

#### Chapter 7: Being (to on) (1017a7–1017b9, PDF 59–60)
- Four senses: (1) accidental being (the musical is a man); (2) being as indicated by the figures of predication — quality, quantity, relation, activity, passivity, place, time; (3) being as truth and non-being as falsity; (4) being as potentiality and actuality.
- Being in the sense of truth: "Socrates is musical" means this is true.

#### Chapter 8: Substance (ousia) (1017b10–1017b26, PDF 60)
- Four senses: (1) the simple bodies (earth, fire, water, air) and composite bodies, animals, divine beings, and their parts; (2) the cause of being present in things not predicated of a subject (e.g., soul); (3) the parts limiting and marking individuality, whose destruction destroys the whole (e.g., body is destroyed by the destruction of the plane, the plane by the line); (4) the essence — the formula of which is a definition.
- Substance in two senses: (a) the ultimate substratum not predicated of anything else; (b) that which is a "this" and separable — the shape or form.

#### Chapter 9: Same, Other, Different (tauton, heteron, diaphoron) (1017b27–1018b8, PDF 60–61)
- **Same**: (1) accidentally the same (white and musical of the same man); (2) same by nature — same in kind, number, or substance.
- **Other**: general correlate of "same."
- **Different**: things which are the same in some respect but not in number, or differ in genus, or whose contraries are in their substance.

#### Chapter 10: Opposites (antikeimena) (1018a9–1018b8, PDF 61)
- Four types of opposition: contradictories, contraries, relative terms, and privation/possession. These are the ultimate forms of opposition, from which others derive.

#### Chapter 11: Prior and Posterior (proteron, husteron) (1018b9–1019a14, PDF 61–62)
- Prior (1) in place — nearer to some determinate first point; (2) in time — further from the present (past events) or nearer to the present (future events); (3) in movement — nearer to the first mover; (4) in power — the more powerful is prior; (5) in arrangement.
- Prior in formula and in knowledge: universals are prior in formula, accidents prior in perception. In formula, the accident is prior to the whole (musical is prior to musical man); yet musicalness cannot exist without someone who is musical.

#### Chapter 12: Capacity / Potency (dunamis) (1019a15–1020a6, PDF 62–63)
- **Capacity**: (1) source of movement or change in another thing, or in the thing itself *qua* other (the art of building is a capacity not in the thing built); (2) capacity of being acted on — the principle of passive change; (3) the state of impassivity or unchangeability for the worse.
- **Incapacity**: privation of capacity — either lacking the capacity entirely, or lacking it when one would naturally have it.
- **Possible / impossible**: that whose contrary is not of necessity false is possible; that whose contrary is of necessity true is impossible.

#### Chapter 13: Quantity (poson) (1020a7–1020a33, PDF 64)
- Quantity is that which is divisible into two or more constituent parts. A *plurality* is numerable; a *magnitude* is measurable. Plurality is potentially divisible into non-continuous parts; magnitude into continuous parts.
- Kinds of magnitude: line (one dimension), surface (two), solid (three).
- Some things are quantities in virtue of their own nature, others accidentally (the line is a quantity; the musical is a quantity accidentally).

#### Chapter 14: Quality (poion) (1020a33–1020b25, PDF 64)
- Four senses: (1) the differentia of substance (man is a two-footed animal; the horse four-footed); (2) quality of immovable mathematical objects (numbers have qualities such as being composite vs. prime); (3) modifications of substances in motion (heat, cold, whiteness, blackness); (4) quality in respect of excellence and badness.
- The primary quality is the differentia of substance.

#### Chapter 15: Relative (pros ti) (1020b26–1021b11, PDF 64–66)
- Three senses: (1) numerical relations (double, half, triple); (2) active and passive capacities (that which can heat to that which can be heated); (3) the measurable to the measure, the knowable to knowledge, the perceptible to perception.
- Sense (3) is distinctive: knowledge is relative to the knowable, not vice versa — the thought is relative to its object.

#### Chapter 16: Complete / Perfect (teleion) (1021b12–1022a3, PDF 66)
- Three senses: (1) that outside which it is impossible to find even one part (the complete time of something); (2) that which cannot be excelled in its kind (a complete doctor, a complete thief); (3) things that have attained their end (*telos*), when the end is good.
- By extension, destruction and death are called "complete" (*teleion*) because they are endpoints.

#### Chapter 17: Limit (peras) (1022a4–1022a13, PDF 66)
- Limit: the last point of a thing, the first point within which every part is, the form of a spatial magnitude, the end (*telos*) of each thing. "Limit" has as many senses as "beginning," and yet more.

#### Chapter 18: "That in virtue of which" / Per se (kath' ho) (1022a14–1022a36, PDF 67)
- Multiple senses: (1) the form or essence (Callias is good in virtue of himself); (2) the proximate subject in which an attribute is found (colour is in a surface); (3) the initial cause in a chain (he came because he was exiled — i.e., exile was the first cause). Also: "in virtue of itself" (*kath' hauto*) applies to the essence and to whatever a thing receives in itself directly.

#### Chapter 19: Disposition (diathesis) (1022a37–1022b3, PDF 67)
- Disposition: the arrangement of that which has parts, in respect of place, capacity, or kind. There must be a certain position, as the word "disposition" implies.

#### Chapter 20: Having / State (hexis) (1022b4–1022b22, PDF 67)
- Three senses: (1) a kind of activity of the haver and the had — like an action or movement; (2) a disposition according to which a thing is well or ill disposed; (3) a part of such a disposition (the excellence of the parts is a having).

#### Chapter 21: Affection (pathos) (1022b15–1022b22, PDF 68)
- Four senses: (1) a quality in respect of which alteration is possible (white, black, sweet, bitter); (2) the actualized alterations themselves; (3) especially injurious alterations and movements; (4) great calamities and painful experiences.

#### Chapter 22: Privation (sterēsis) (1022b22–1023a7, PDF 68)
- Four senses: (1) not having an attribute that something might naturally have, even if not naturally proper to it (a plant is deprived of eyes); (2) not having what it or its genus would naturally have; (3) not having it at the age when it would naturally have it; (4) the violent taking away of anything.
- As many kinds of privation as there are negative prefixes ("unequal," "invisible," "footless").

#### Chapter 23: "To have" (echein) (1023a8–1023a25, PDF 68)
- Multiple senses: (1) to treat according to one's nature or impulse (fever "has" a man); (2) to be present as receptive of something (bronze "has" the form of a statue); (3) to contain (the vessel "has" liquid); (4) to hinder a thing from moving according to its own impulse (pillars "have" incumbent weights).

#### Chapter 24: "To come from" (ek tinos) (1023a26–1023b11, PDF 69)
- Multiple senses: (1) from something as matter (the statue comes from bronze); (2) from the first moving principle (the fight comes from abusive language); (3) from the compound of matter and form (the parts come from the whole); (4) from part (man from two-footed); (5) in time (night comes from day); (6) as the differentiae from the genus.

#### Chapter 25: Part (meros) (1023b12–1024a10, PDF 69)
- Five senses: (1) that into which a quantity may be divided; (2) the parts in the first sense that are also measures; (3) the elements into which the kind may be divided apart from quantity (species are parts of genus); (4) the elements of the formula — the whole is divided into the form or the matter; (5) the elements in the formula that explain a thing (genus is part of species in this sense).

#### Chapter 26: Whole (holon) (1023b26–1024a10, PDF 69–70)
- Whole: (a) that of which no part is absent that makes it naturally a whole; (b) that which contains things so that they form a unity.
- The continuous and limited is a whole when there is a unity of several parts. Totals vs. wholes: where position makes no difference, we say "total" (water); where it does, "whole" (a coat). Things whose nature remains after transposition are totals; those that do not are wholes. Wholeness is a kind of oneness.

#### Chapter 27: Mutilated (kolobon) (1024a11–1024a28, PDF 70)
- Not every quantitative thing can be called mutilated. The thing must have unlike parts, not be deprived of any chance part but of a determining part, and must be continuous. A cup is mutilated if bored through but not if chipped. Baldness is not mutilation.

#### Chapter 28: Genus (genos) (1024a29–1024b9, PDF 70–71)
- Four senses: (1) continuous generation of the same form (the human race); (2) the first mover of a race (Hellenes from Hellen, Ionians from Ion); (3) the kind of plane figures (plane is the genus of plane figures, solid of solid figures); (4) the first constituent element in formulae — the genus whose differentiae are the qualities. Kind is the material substratum of the differentia.

#### Chapter 29: False (pseudos) (1024b17–1025a13, PDF 71–72)
- False as a *thing*: (a) because not put together or impossible to put together (the diagonal is commensurate with the side); (b) things that exist but whose nature is to appear other than they are (a sketch, a dream).
- False as a *formula*: the formula of non-existent objects is false; a formula applied to something other than what it defines is false.
- False as a *person*: one who is ready at and fond of false formulae, not for any reason but for their own sake, and one who impresses such formulae on others. The proof in the *Hippias* that the same man is false and true is misleading — it confuses willing and ability.

#### Chapter 30: Accident (symbebēkos) (1025a14–1025a34, PDF 72–73)
- Accident: that which attaches to something truly but neither of necessity nor usually. Finding treasure while digging a hole is an accident; being white is an accident of a musical man.
- The accidental has no definite cause and therefore no science. The accidental is close to non-being.

## 6. Tensions

| Tension | Node A | Node B | Type | Description |
|---------|--------|--------|------|-------------|
| Substance as substratum vs. substance as form | Ultimate substratum — that which is not predicated of anything else (1017b23) | Separable "this" — the form or shape (1017b25) | ontological | The two senses of substance pull in opposite directions: the substratum criterion points toward matter, but the separability and "this"-ness criterion points toward form. | Lexical enumeration vs. philosophical argument | The method of Δ: listing and distinguishing senses of terms | The method of Ζ–Θ: sustained argument toward the primary sense | methodological | Δ catalogues polysemy without resolving which sense is primary in every case. | Nature as matter vs. nature as form | Nature as primary matter from which a thing is made (1014b26–32) | Nature as substance and form of things having a source of movement (1015a11) | ontological | Aristotle gives both as legitimate senses but declares the latter primary. The tension recurs in Physics II.1 where the identification of nature with form must be argued against the Antiphon-style identification of nature with matter. |
| One as indivisible vs. one as whole and complete | The one is everywhere indivisible, either in quantity or in kind (1016b23) | That which is most truly one is whole and complete, having one form (1016b11) | ontological | Indivisibility and wholeness pull in different directions: indivisibility suggests the atomic or simple, while wholeness suggests organized complexity.
