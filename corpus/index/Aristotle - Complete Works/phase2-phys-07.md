## 2. Hierarchical Outline

### Ch. 1 (241b24--243a30): Everything in motion is moved by something; there must be a first mover
- Everything in motion must be moved by something: if a thing has the source of motion in itself, removing a part shows the remainder either moves or does not, establishing external dependence 241b24--242a15 (PDF 96).
- Everything in motion must be divisible: if AB is in motion, it can be divided at C; if CB is not in motion, AB would not be in motion primarily; therefore what is in motion is always divisible 242a30--49 (PDF 96).
- Proof of a first mover: if A is moved by B, B by C, C by D and so on, the series cannot go to infinity; since *ex hypothesi* the mover while causing motion is itself in motion, the motions of mover and moved are simultaneous; an infinite series would yield an infinite motion traversed in finite time, which is impossible 242a49--243a30 (PDF 96--97).

### Ch. 2 (243a30--245a10): The mover and moved are together; classification of locomotion
- The first mover (in the sense of immediate source of motion) is always together with that which is moved; there is nothing between moved and mover 243a30--35 (PDF 97).
- Three kinds of motion (local, qualitative, quantitative) yield three kinds of mover: that which causes locomotion, that which causes alteration, and that which causes increase or decrease 243a35--b5 (PDF 97).
- Four kinds of locomotion caused by something other than the moved thing: pulling, pushing, carrying, and twirling; all other locomotion reduces to these 243b10--20 (PDF 98).
- Pushing on, pushing off, pushing apart, pushing together defined; carrying is derivative on pulling, pushing, or twirling; twirling is compound of pulling and pushing 243b20--244a5 (PDF 98).
- In all locomotion there is nothing between moved and mover: demonstrated by reviewing pulling and pushing definitions 244a5--b2 (PDF 98--99).
- Nothing intermediate between that which undergoes and that which causes alteration: shown by induction from sense-perception (air continuous with colour, colour with sight; flavour with taste) 244b2--245a5 (PDF 99).
- No intermediary between that which undergoes and that which causes increase or decrease: what causes increase does so by becoming attached, what causes decrease by becoming detached; both are continuous 245a5--10 (PDF 99).

### Ch. 3 (245a10--248a9): Alteration is restricted to sensible qualities
- Alteration occurs only in things affected by sensible qualities; changes in figures, shapes, and states are not alterations 245a10--b5 (PDF 100).
- Figures and shapes: when something is completely shaped we name it by the material, not the shape (brazen, waxen); but when affected and altered, we still call it by the original name---thus becomings of shapes are not alterations 245b7--246a1 (PDF 100).
- Excellences and defects are not alterations but perfections and departures; they exist in virtue of particular relations 246a1--b3 (PDF 100--101).
- States of the soul: moral excellences concern the sensitive part, altered by sensible objects; their becoming is accompanied by alteration but is not itself an alteration 246b3--247a19 (PDF 101).
- Intellectual states of the soul are not alterations or becomings: knowledge is acquired when the intellect reaches a state of rest, not by being moved; understanding and knowledge come from the soul's settling down out of restlessness 247b1--248a9 (PDF 102).

### Ch. 4 (248a10--249b25): The commensurability of motions
- Not every motion is commensurable with every other: an alteration cannot equal a locomotion, since an affection cannot equal a length 248a10--b5 (PDF 102).
- Circular and rectilinear motion: it would be absurd to suppose the motion of something in a circle and something in a straight line cannot be compared, yet they are not commensurable since a circumference and a straight line are not commensurable 248b5--249a1 (PDF 102--103).
- Homonymy problem: "quick" does not have the same meaning applied to alteration and locomotion; things are commensurable only if the terms applied are non-homonymous 249a1--b5 (PDF 103--104).
- Specific difference in motions: locomotion is differentiated according to the instrument (feet = walking, wings = flying) and the path; things of equal velocity traverse the same magnitude with no specific difference in the motion 249a15--25 (PDF 104).
- Alteration presents further difficulties: two alterations are of equal velocity if in equal time they perform the same change, but the sameness of affections and the commensurability of their recipients must be established 249a25--b25 (PDF 104--105).

### Ch. 5 (249b25--250b7): Proportional rules of force, weight, distance, and time
- A mover always moves something in some time over some distance; force A moves B over distance C in time D; proportional rules: A moves 1/2B over 2C in D, or 1/2B over C in 1/2D 249b25--250a10 (PDF 105).
- Non-reciprocal proportion: if E moves F a distance C in time D, it does not follow that E moves half of A the whole distance in D, or any fraction proportionally; below a threshold of force, no motion results at all---otherwise one man could move a ship 250a10--20 (PDF 105).
- Zeno's millet seed argument refuted: a part of the millet does not move air that the whole bushel moves; the part exists only potentially in the whole 250a20--b3 (PDF 106).
- Two movers each separately moving a weight a given distance in a given time: combined forces move the combined weights an equal distance in equal time; rules of proportion apply 250b3--7 (PDF 106).
- The same proportional relations hold for alteration and increase: if something causes a certain alteration in a certain time, double the alteration takes double the time, and half takes half 250b7--end (PDF 106).

| **first mover** (πρῶτον κινοῦν) | The mover at which the regress of movers-in-motion terminates; required because an infinite series would produce infinite motion in finite time | 242a49--243a30 (PDF 96--97) |

| **proportionality of force and motion** | Force, weight, distance, and time stand in determinate ratios; but below a threshold of force, no motion occurs | 249b25--250b7 (PDF 105--106) |

## 5. Tensions

| ID | Tension | Loci |
|----|---------|------|
| PHYS-07-T1 | **Authenticity of Book VII**: The textual tradition preserves two substantially different versions of Book VII, and ancient commentators debated whether it is a genuine part of the *Physics* or an early draft later superseded by Book VIII. The arguments of VII.1 are reproved more rigorously in VIII.4. | VII.1 (241b24--243a30) vs. VIII.4--6 |
| PHYS-07-T2 | **Threshold problem for force**: VII.5 asserts that below a threshold, a given force produces no motion at all, violating the otherwise strict proportionality between force, weight, distance, and time. Aristotle does not provide a principled account of where the threshold lies. | 250a10--20 (PDF 105) |
| PHYS-07-T3 | **Knowledge as non-alteration**: VII.3 claims that the acquisition of knowledge is not an alteration but a settling to rest (247b1--248a9). This sits uneasily with the general principle that all change involves a transition from potentiality to actuality, which would seem to make learning a genuine qualitative change. | 247b1--248a9 (PDF 102) vs. III.1 201a10 |
