Actualization-of-Desire Analysis

A method for analyzing player behavior in game worlds — how designed worlds turn desire into action, and how that action can reshape who we are.

Dalton Salvo · Doctoral methodology brief · June 2026 · working name: ADA

This brief sets out a method I am developing for the analysis of player behavior. It takes two established frameworks — Gordon Calleja's model of game involvement and Kenneth Burke's dramatism — as its baseline, but it does not simply apply them in turn. Instead it absorbs them into a single account of action grounded in what I call the actualization of desire. The visual below gives the whole apparatus at a glance; the sections that follow explain each element in plain terms and mark, in a Lineage line, exactly where the element comes from and what I have changed.

1 · The method at a glance

2 · What the method is, and what is new in it

The method begins from a simple claim: all action is the actualization of desire. We encounter something, it strikes us as good or bad, we form an image of it, we judge it, and — if it discloses something worth pursuing — we act. Aristotle treats animal movement in just these terms, with the object of desire as the unmoved mover that sets the whole sequence going; I draw the stages into a single chain and read it as the structure of any action, in a game or out of one. Games matter here not because they offer an illusory second world, but because they are designed environments that can intensify, pattern, and repeat this movement until it sediments into a player's lasting disposition.

Before going further, one piece of Aristotelian scaffolding makes the rest precise. Every action — in a game or out of one — is a motion, a change in some substance over time, and Aristotle explains any such change by four causes: its matter, its form, what sets it going (the efficient cause), and what it is for (the final cause). The chain just described is the fine grain of one of those four — the efficient-and-final cause of a desiring agent, the part where a rational animal's own motion gets started and directed. Seating the method this way does real work, because in a designed world the four causes divide between designer and player: the designer supplies the materials, the rules and the avatar's form, the trigger that starts a beat ('why now'), and often a foreclosed outcome; the player supplies the moving principle ('which way') and her own end, which may or may not coincide with the design's. This division gives the otherwise loose phrase 'what the game supplies versus what the player does' an exact, ontological sense.

Lineage: the four causes are Aristotle's (Physics II; Metaphysics I); the designer/player partition of them — and the seating of the chain in the efficient-final cause — are original to this work. Aristotle's distinction between the art that uses a product (which 'knows the form') and the art that produces it (which 'knows the matter'), Physics II.2, supplies the warrant.

What a designed world drives the player toward is a union — the moment she is absorbed into the world she acts in and bound to the avatar and others she acts with, while remaining herself. This union is the heart of the method, and it is where my two sources meet. Calleja calls the world-side of it incorporation; Burke calls the social side of it consubstantiation. Neither author sees that these are two faces of one event, and neither grounds the event in desire. Treating them as one — and showing that the union can itself source an action — is the method's central contribution. I call the fused event rhetorical incorporation.

Three things, then, are new. First, the chain of desire is used as the analytic spine, so that involvement and motive are read through it rather than alongside each other. Second, incorporation and consubstantiation are re-grounded and fused. Third, the method follows the union past the moment of play into disposition, and so can describe how a game reshapes a player's way of being in the world — the claim my capstone case is built to test. Burke and Calleja are the baseline I build from; the method transforms their concepts rather than borrowing them intact. The result is meant to be a single instrument, not two reports stapled together.

3 · The elements, explained

3.1 · The spine: the actualization of desire

The spine is a five-stage chain — encounter, perception, image, judgment, action — through which desire becomes a deed. Each stage is an achievement; each step between them is a movement; and the object of desire, held in view, is what moves the whole. Reading an episode means tracing this passage: what the player meets, how it registers, what image she forms, what she comes to believe, and what she does. The chain is read in two registers (see 3.7): a single pass in the moment, and the accumulation of passes over time.

Lineage: developed from Aristotle (On the Soul, On the Movement of Animals) and Heidegger; the unified chain, and its use as an analytic of game experience, is original to this work.

3.2 · The five stages

Encounter (A₀) — the world meets the senses, within the flow of motion and time; the designed environment, the gameworld as a place that can be inhabited. Perception (A₁) — what is taken in, together with how it feels: every perception carries a basic tone of pleasant or painful, attractive or aversive. Image (A₂) — the charged mental image (Aristotle's phantasia): the picture-with-a-feeling that the rest of the mind works upon. This is the pivot of the chain. Judgment (A₃) — thinking about the image and, distinctly, believing it (opinion, or doxa); this is where conviction, and with it emotion, enters. Action (A₄) — desire issues in movement; the deed is done, and it changes both the world and the agent.

Lineage: Aristotelian; the stage-by-stage construction is original. Plain-English names are used throughout; the Greek terms appear only where they earn their place.

3.3 · The object of desire (the driving force)

Nothing in the chain moves without an object of desire. It is what the player wants — to win, to belong, to know what is behind the door, to be the kind of person who does this rather than that — and it is the "unmoved mover" that sets perception, judgment, and action going. A central diagnostic of the method is simply: what is desired here, and is that object the player's own or the group's? When the object is collective ("what we want"), the union is doing the work (3.4).

Lineage: Aristotle; the framing as the analyst's first question is original.

3.4 · Rhetorical incorporation — the central event

As the chain runs in a designed world, it reaches a union: the player becomes one with the world she acts in and with the avatar and others she acts as and with — while remaining a distinct self. I call this rhetorical incorporation, and I treat it as having two faces that are really one event. It is the method's pivot: the place where the design's pressure, the player's desire, and the social field all meet. Crucially, the union is not only a state the chain arrives at; it can be what sources the next action — a player throws herself into a fight not from her own grievance but because she is one with the gang whose grievance it is.

Lineage: original fusion. Combines Calleja's incorporation (2011) and Burke's consubstantiation (1950), re-grounded as a single event in the chain of desire. Neither author joins the two faces or grounds them in desire; that step is mine.

3.5 · The world-face: incorporation

The world-face of the union is Calleja's incorporation: the gameworld is taken into the player's awareness as her immediate surroundings, and at the same time the player is embodied in the world through the avatar — both directions at once. Calleja makes the two-way structure and its simultaneity the strict test of incorporation: if only one direction is active, the player is merely "involved," not incorporated. I keep his test (and a seven-point version of his criteria for judging it) but change its standing — from a psychological state of intense engagement to an ontological event within the chain, the world-side of the substantial union.

What that ontological event is, I take from Thomas Rickert's reading of Heidegger's dwelling (Ambient Rhetoric, 2013): the player does not merely take the world into her consciousness — Calleja's own phrase, "the absorption of a virtual environment into consciousness," tilts too far toward the mind — but is constituted with it, already dwelling in a designed world that conditions and claims her before she acts ("not a worldview but a principle of being … a way of being conditioned and permeated by things"). This keeps the world an active party — it makes claims, it pushes back — while the chain keeps the player an agent, not a vessel: she is at once gathered by the world and gathering it. (I take Rickert's distinction between a thing that makes claims and an object available on demand, but not his Heideggerian Fourfold.)

Lineage: Calleja (2011, esp. pp. 169–173), In-Game: From Immersion to Incorporation. Transformed: re-grounded from psychological intensification to an event in the chain; one of the two faces of the union.

3.6 · The other-face: consubstantiation (identification)

The other-face is Burke's consubstantiation: to identify with another is to become "substantially one" with them while remaining "a distinct locus of motives — both joined and separate." In play this is the bond with the avatar, the party, the guild, the faction. Burke locates it in shared symbols and shared action; I keep the bond but change what it does — it becomes a desire-sourced event in the chain, and, as noted, it can supply the very motive of an action. This is why identification is not a side-topic in the method but half of its central event.

Lineage: Burke (1950, p. 21), A Rhetoric of Motives. Transformed: from symbolic persuasion to a desire-sourced union; recognized as a source of action, not only a description of one.

3.7 · The designed channels (from Calleja)

Calleja decomposes game involvement into six dimensions: kinesthetic (control and movement), spatial (place and habitation), shared (other agents), narrative (story), affective (feeling), and ludic (rules and goals). In this method they are not a checklist to be filled in turn; they are the channels through which a designed world applies pressure to the chain — the places where one looks to see how a game engineers the movement toward incorporation. Within the narrative channel I keep Calleja's useful distinction between push narrative (story delivered to the player, as in a cut scene that suspends her action) and pull narrative (story the player must seek out and assemble, as with the audio diaries strewn through a world); a forced, unskippable cut scene is push carried to its limit, where the player no longer acts but is acted upon (see 3.9).

Lineage: Calleja (2011); push/pull at ch. 7 (after Levine, 2008). Transformed: from a descriptive taxonomy of involvement into a map of where design exerts force on the chain.

3.8 · The reading of motive (from Burke)

To explain why a player acts, the method uses Burke's pentad — act, scene, agent, agency, purpose, and the later sixth term, attitude — together with the relations ("ratios") among these terms. Its sharpest use here is diagnostic: it shows how a player renders her own action intelligible, and how she tends to relocate its source — to the situation, to the tools, to the other players — and so conceal her own settled disposition. Burke supplies the grammar of self-explanation; the method uses it to catch the gap between why a player did something and how she accounts for it.

Lineage: Burke (1945, 1950), A Grammar of Motives and A Rhetoric of Motives. Transformed: used to diagnose motive-attribution and the concealment of disposition, rather than as a grid applied to every act.

3.9 · The kinds of action — four types, and a second branch

Not all action is alike. The method sorts agent-caused action — action the player freely takes — by the kind of desire that sources it: appetite (the pursuit of the pleasant), spiritedness (answering a slight, defending honor — always social), rational wish (wanting an end as good), and deliberate choice (choosing the means to a wished end). But some of what happens to a player is not her own action at all: it belongs to a second branch — chance (designed randomness), nature (reflex), and force (an unskippable sequence in which control is taken away). The line between these branches is where the most revealing moments occur — above all when a game strips a willing agent of her agency.

Lineage: developed from Aristotle (the kinds of desire; the causes of action in the Rhetoric; voluntary and involuntary action in the Ethics). The four-type taxonomy and its use in game analysis are original.

3.10 · Disposition: skill and character (hexis)

Repeated action leaves something behind — a settled disposition, what Aristotle calls hexis. The method distinguishes two kinds. Skill is the cultivated competence that makes play transparent: the parry that no longer needs thinking, the rotation that runs itself. Character is the evaluative "how" of one's conduct: what one comes to find acceptable, cruel, honorable, or beneath one. Games cultivate both, and they can cultivate them independently — a player may be highly skilled yet ethically untouched, or be changed in character while still fumbling the controls. This distinction (skill versus wisdom, by design) is one of the method's primary diagnostic instruments.

Lineage: Aristotle and Heidegger (the two senses of disposition); the application to game design — and the claim that games engineer each kind — is original.

3.11 · The two registers: the moment and the long run

The method reads at two scales. Synchronically, it follows a single pass through the chain — one bounded episode, from encounter to deed. Diachronically, it follows how passes accumulate: how a player's history tilts what she notices and how readily she believes it, and how each completed action sediments into the disposition she brings to the next. The diachronic register is where the strongest claim lives — that game experience does not stay in the game, but can be carried back into ordinary life. Because incorporation is not a sealed bubble (Calleja shows that real-world intrusions blend into it rather than breaking it), the boundary between the virtual and the actual is porous, and that porosity is the channel through which a game can change how a player meets the real world.

This is also where the word rhetorical earns its fullest sense. Rickert's thesis is that rhetoric "transforms our fundamental disposition concerning how we are in the world, how we dwell" (2013) — which is precisely this carry-over, stated as rhetorical theory. A designed world that reshapes how a player dwells is thus doing rhetorical work in the deepest sense; and a world's recalcitrance — whether it makes claims and pushes back, or yields objects on demand — is part of what lets it come to dwell in the player at all.

Lineage: the porosity of incorporation is Calleja (2011); the two-register reading and the carry-over claim are original (Aristotle's account of habituation and Heidegger's of historicity in the background).

3.12 · What the method contributes that neither source supplies

Five working concepts belong to the method itself, not to Calleja or Burke:

The union as a source. Identification can supply the very motive of an action, not merely describe a bond. Conscripted imagination. When a game withholds a scene and only implies it, the player's own imagination must supply the missing content — and what she supplies depends on her own history, which is why the same scene can mean robbery to one player and rape to another. The turn from agent to patient. When force takes control away, the player is converted from one who acts into one who is acted upon — a structural account of a scene's violence. Two senses of compulsion. External force (an unskippable scene) is one thing; the habituated capture of the grind, where the means quietly becomes the end, is another. The carry-over. The route by which a sedimented disposition follows the player out of the game and into the world.

4 · A worked example — the saloon brawl in Red Dead Redemption 2

Early in the game the player, as Arthur, drinks with his gang in a saloon; a gang-mate, Bill, shoves a stranger, and a brawl erupts that spills into the muddy street, where a crowd gathers and eggs on a local fighter. The method reads it as a single pass of the chain.

What is desired is not Arthur's own honor but the gang's: he wades in because he is one with them ("we can handle these fools"). So the action is sourced by the union — a spirited action (answering a slight, defending the group, irreducibly social), driven by belonging rather than by a private grievance. This is the reading neither source gives alone: Calleja can show how absorbed the player is, and Burke can name the identification, but only together, on the chain, do we see the union generating the act.

How the world engineers it — the four causes, divided. The designer supplies the material (the saloon, the mud, the fists), the form (the melee rules and Arthur's body, which the player wears), the efficient trigger — Bill's shove is scripted to find Arthur, so the 'why now' is the designer's — and a foreclosed final end: the encounter is scripted to be won, so the player's freedom lies in how she fights, not whether she prevails. The player supplies the other half: the moving principle ('which way' — the spirited impulse to wade in) and her own end, which here happens to coincide with the design's. The on-screen prompt — "Hold R to block" — is the designer's hand reaching directly into the efficient cause, installing a combat skill in real time; and the brawl's true product is exactly that, a lasting change in Arthur's (and the player's) fighting disposition — the very ground the game's later, darker scenes will exploit. (The crowd, meanwhile, raises the stakes on the shared and affective channels.)

How motive is rendered: the brawl is framed as provoked loyalty — attributed to Bill and the stranger — which quietly conceals Arthur's own readiness for violence. What it leaves behind: a combat skill carried into every later fight, a deepened bond with the gang, and — at the close, when a bystander begs Arthur to stop ("I beg you") — a fork of character, staged cinematically, that the game's honor system will compound. In a few minutes of play, the method can show the world cultivating skill with one hand and testing character with the other.
