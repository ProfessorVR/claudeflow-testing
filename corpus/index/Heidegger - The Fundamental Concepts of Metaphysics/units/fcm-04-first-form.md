# fcm-04 — The First Form of Boredom: Becoming Bored *by* Something (*Gelangweiltwerden von etwas*)

**Coverage:** Part One, Chapter Two, §§19–23 · book pp.78–105 · GA pp.118–159 · pdf pp.51–64
**Form analyzed:** the *first* form of boredom — situation-bound, caused by a determinate boring thing.
**Why this unit matters:** §§21 and 23d here mint the two structural primitives — *Leergelassenheit* (being left empty) and *Hingehaltenheit* (being held in limbo) — that the second and third forms (fcm-05, fcm-06) will inherit, transform, and finally temporalize. Everything downstream in the boredom analysis is built on the vocabulary forged here.

---

## §1. The questionableness of boredom and the reversal of the everyday task (§19, p.78)

The chapter opens on a methodological worry inherited from the Preliminary Appraisal: in pointing to profound boredom, has Heidegger smuggled in an "ascertained" fundamental attunement? He answers flatly — "By no means" (p.79 / GA pp.118-19 / pdf 51). An attunement cannot be ascertained at all, because anyone may simply deny that it is there. The issue is not assertion versus denial but the prior question of whether "a profound boredom draws back and forth like a silent fog in the abysses of *Dasein*" (p.78 / GA pp.118-19 / pdf 51).

The pivotal move is a reversal of what "awakening" means. Boredom, Heidegger insists, "is already awake" — "With open eyes it looks into our *Da-sein*" (p.79 / GA pp.118-19 / pdf 51). Therefore awakening this fundamental attunement "does not mean making it awake in the first place, but letting it be awake, guarding against it falling asleep" (p.79 / GA pp.118-19 / pdf 51 — **C1**). This inverts the natural task. All "natural and sound human comportment" exists precisely "to pass the time and precisely not to let boredom arise" (p.79 / GA pp.118-19 / pdf 51). To be told to *let it be awake* is therefore "a strange or almost insane demand [*Zumutung*]" (p.79 / GA pp.118-19 / pdf 51 — **C2**). The footnote flagged by the translators is load-bearing for the whole analysis: *Langeweile* literally means "long while," and "the temporal sense of *Weile* and its stretching" governs everything that follows (Tr. note, p.79 / GA pp.118-19 / pdf 51).

Heidegger also lays down the affective neighborhood: boredom, when we set upon it "with all the means at our disposal," becomes "stubborn, obstinate," slowly propelling us "to the threshold of melancholy [*Schwermut*]" (p.79 / GA pp.118-19 / pdf 51). The phenomenon's defining trait is its evasiveness — we "know," in "a strange kind of knowing," that it "can return at any time" (p.79 / GA pp.118-19 / pdf 51).

## §2. Boredom, time, and the three metaphysical questions (§20, pp.80–82)

§20 ties boredom to time and to the course's governing triad. "We pass the time, in order to master it, because time becomes long in boredom" (p.80 / GA pp.119-21 / pdf 51). Heidegger draws on the "wisdom of language": in Alemannic usage "to have long time" means the same as "to be homesick," so that "Profound boredom — a homesickness" (p.80 / GA pp.119-21 / pdf 51), recalling the Novalis motif from the Preliminary Appraisal that "philosophizing … is supposed to be a homesickness" (p.80 / GA pp.119-21 / pdf 51). Boredom thus becomes "a fundamental attunement of philosophizing."

The decisive structural claim: *Langeweile* "shows, particularly in our German word, an almost obvious relation to time … a way in which we stand with respect to time, a feeling of time" (p.80 / GA pp.119-21 / pdf 51 — **C3**). Boredom "lead[s] us to the problem of time." And time, in turn, is bound to the three questions the course is pledged to: "what world, finitude, and individuation are" (p.81 / GA pp.121-22 / pdf 52). Heidegger rehearses the venerable convictions linking each to time — that world and time "originated together," that "whatever is finite is temporal," and the "ancient doctrine of metaphysics" that something individual "becomes this individual thing by virtue of its specific position in time" (p.81 / GA pp.121-22 / pdf 52). If time both underlies the three questions and stands in "an exceptional relation … to time in human *Dasein*" through boredom, then boredom "offers an exceptional possibility of answering the three questions" (p.81 / GA pp.121-22 / pdf 52). This is the seed of the entire Part One → Part Two hinge (developed in fcm-08).

A second methodological caution closes the section. The inessential, "fleeting, cursory" boredom is to be let *become* essential — but not by our producing it: "We do not need to undertake anything in this respect. On the contrary, we are always already undertaking too much" (p.82 / GA pp.122-24 / pdf 52). What is required is "not to resist straightaway but to let resonate" (p.82 / GA pp.122-24 / pdf 52), approaching not "some psychological experience" but "ourselves as a *Da-sein*" (p.82 / GA pp.122-24 / pdf 52).

## §3. Beginning from boringness, not boredom: the three conventional schemata refused (§21, pp.82–88)

§21 makes the strategic decision that organizes the whole interpretation: "We are not really starting from boredom, but from boringness" (p.82 / GA pp.124-25 / pdf 53 — **C4**). *Boringness* is "what makes something boring what it is whenever it is boring us." This deliberately begins "outside," in the boring thing — a book, a play, a ceremony, a person, "an environment or a place" (p.82 / GA pp.124-25 / pdf 53). The counterintuitive thesis: "boredom is outside, seated in what is boring, and creeps into us from the outside" (p.83 / GA pp.124-25 / pdf 53). Heidegger isolates a tripartite structure — "[1.] that which is boring in its boringness; [2.] becoming bored by this boring thing …; [3.] boredom itself" (p.83 / GA pp.124-25 / pdf 53) — and refuses to decide prematurely how the three relate.

He then dismantles three conventional schemata for interpreting boredom:

**(a) The cause-effect relation.** Does the boring thing cause boredom as cold "causes the column of mercury … to sink," or as "one billiard ball strikes another"? (p.84 / GA pp.125-27 / pdf 53). Heidegger rejects the model: "We will not get anywhere at all on this track" (p.84 / GA pp.125-27 / pdf 53). The eventual verdict is explicit: "in elucidating the facts of the matter, we must disregard the cause-effect relation"; the book "is not the outer cause, nor is the resulting boredom the inner effect" (p.88 / GA pp.132-33 / pdf 56 — **C5**).

**(b) The psychological / subject-object property.** "Boring" looks like "an objective characteristic" of, say, a badly written book (p.84 / GA pp.125-27 / pdf 53), yet it is plainly "related to the subject" too. Heidegger pushes the analysis to show that even apparently objective properties ("non-durable," "bad cover") are "related to the subject" through our dealings, so that being a subject-related-and-object-belonging property is "nothing exceptional" (p.85 / GA pp.127-29 / pdf 53). Yet "we somehow sense that the character of the boringness of a book is something quite different from the fact that it is badly written" (p.85 / GA pp.127-29 / pdf 53).

**(c) Transference (Aristotle's metaphor).** The idealist "old truth" says properties "are representations, ideas that we as subjects transfer onto objects" (p.85 / GA pp.127-29 / pdf 53). Heidegger names the pedigree: "Ever since Aristotle's *Poetics* we have had the expression 'metaphor' (*μεταφορά*)" for the transfer (*μεταφέρειν*) of attunements — "sadness, cheerfulness, boringness — out of ourselves and onto things" (p.85 / GA pp.127-29 / pdf 53). We speak of a "laughing meadow," a "melancholy landscape." But the schema explains nothing: even granting transference, "what is it, then, that here causes the attunement or gives rise to transference?" If it "already lies in the things themselves," then "we are no longer transferring something, but in some way apprehending it from the things themselves" (p.85 / GA pp.127-29 / pdf 53 — **C6**).

The constructive yield of §21 is the first statement of the two structural moments. Returning to the everyday description of the boring book, Heidegger reads "wearisome" and "tedious" against each other: "Wearisome means: it does not rivet us; we are … not taken [*hingenommen*] by it, but merely held in limbo [*hingehalten*] by it. Tedious means: it does not engross us, we are left empty [*leer gelassen*]" (p.87 / GA pp.130-32 / pdf 56 — **C7**). Hence the proto-definition: "that which bores, which is boring, is that which holds us in limbo and yet leaves us empty" (p.87 / GA pp.130-32 / pdf 56). The section closes with the hybrid thesis: "boredom — and thus ultimately every attunement — is a hybrid, partly objective, partly subjective" (p.88 / GA pp.132-33 / pdf 56 — **C8**); the suppressed attunement "plays around the thing outside" without our transferring anything (p.88 / GA pp.132-33 / pdf 56).

## §4. The methodological directive: passing the time as the access route (§22, pp.88–92)

§22 converts the negative results into a positive method. The danger is to make attunement "into an object swimming in the stream of consciousness which we as observers gaze after" (p.90 / GA pp.135-36 / pdf 58). Doing so "refuse[s] it precisely the role it is supposed to have." Heidegger detours through a principle of method — that "the substantive content and kind of being of a being prescribe the possible manifestness (truth) belonging to it" (p.90 / GA pp.135-36 / pdf 58) — to argue that the scientific maxim of "best conditions of observability" is grounded in "an originary, essential connection" between being and truth, and so cannot simply be imported to govern an attunement (p.90 / GA pp.135-36 / pdf 58).

The directive proper: do not analyze consciousness; preserve "the immediacy of everyday *Dasein*" (p.91 / GA pp.136-38 / pdf 58). What is required "is not the effort of working ourselves into a particular attitude, but the reverse: … the releasement [*Gelassenheit*] of our free, everyday perspective — free from psychological and other theories of consciousness" (p.91 / GA pp.136-38 / pdf 58 — **C9**). Because we are "permeated by such theories," planting this releasement "in oneself" is harder than memorizing a theory.

From this the access route follows. Since boredom shows itself precisely as "that to which we are opposed," it manifests "wherever we drive it away," i.e. "wherever we … pass the time" (p.90 / GA pp.136-38 / pdf 58). Therefore: "it is precisely in passing the time that we first gain the correct orientation in which we can encounter boredom undisguised … we must consider it in the way that we move within it" (p.91 / GA pp.136-38 / pdf 58 — **C10**). §22 also previews a distinction it then provisionally discards: "becoming bored by …" still holds us "held fast by that which is boring," whereas "being bored with …" exhibits "a certain detachment," the boredom "no longer nailed fast to something" and "beginning to diffuse" until "everything becomes boring" (pp.91–92 / GA pp.138-39 / pdf 58). The present unit pursues only *becoming bored by* something; the diffuse *being-bored-with* is the threshold to the second form (fcm-05).

## §5. Becoming bored and passing the time (§23a, pp.92–95)

The famous train-station scene anchors §23. "We are sitting … in the tasteless station of some lonely minor railway. It is four hours until the next train arrives" (p.93 / GA pp.139-41 / pdf 59). We read timetables, count trees, draw figures in the sand, and "catch ourselves looking at our watch yet again" (p.93 / GA pp.139-41 / pdf 59).

Heidegger then unpacks *Zeitvertreib* etymologically. "To pass here means to make it pass by, to propel it, drive it on so that it passes," so that "Passing the time is a driving away of boredom that drives time on" (p.94 / GA pp.141-42 / pdf 59 — **C11**); the German footnote gives the formula directly: *"Zeitvertreib ist ein Zeit antreibendes Wegtreiben der Langeweile"* (Tr. note, p.94 / GA pp.141-42 / pdf 59). Looking at the clock, "we merely want to see time passed" — the time "until the train arrives" (p.94 / GA pp.141-42 / pdf 59).

A crucial set of negations follows. Boredom is *not* a waiting: "Being bored with something … is not a waiting for something," and "waiting can be full of suspense," leaving "no room for boredom at all" (p.94 / GA pp.141-42 / pdf 59). Nor is it impatience or ill humour: "There is neither a patient nor an impatient boredom" (p.95 / GA pp.142-44 / pdf 60). Impatience belongs to "the manner … in which we want to get boredom under control" (p.95 / GA pp.142-44 / pdf 60). The verdict: "This having to wait and our impatience may be present and surround boredom, but they never constitute boredom itself" (p.95 / GA pp.142-44 / pdf 60 — **C12**). Heidegger even concedes that the formal definition of passing-the-time "is incorrect": once we say "driving time on," we have "already … said … something about boredom," so the definition cannot be pressed (p.95 / GA pp.142-44 / pdf 60).

## §6. Looking at the watch; becoming bored as paralysing affection by dragging time (§23b, pp.96–99)

The watch returns as a diagnostic. "This looking at our watch is not itself a passing the time" — not on the level of counting trees — "but only a sign that we want to pass the time, or more precisely that our passing the time is not really succeeding" (p.97 / GA pp.146-47 / pdf 61 — **C13**). It is a "helpless gesture" indicating "that we are becoming increasingly bored" (p.97 / GA pp.146-47 / pdf 61).

What we ascertain is "not … what time it is in general" but "how much time is left until the train departs" (p.97 / GA pp.146-47 / pdf 61). This means time "is going slowly." But Heidegger refuses to reduce boredom to grasping slowness: "in boredom we do not ascertain anything … we are bound precisely by — nothing. Not even by time, the slowness of time" (p.97 / GA pp.146-47 / pdf 61). The decisive nuance: the trouble is not that time is *too long* — "the length of time plays no role" (one can be bored in a quarter hour, unbored across a whole night) — and "not because the progress of time is slow, but because it is too slow" (p.97 / GA pp.146-47 / pdf 61 — **C14**). What we fight is "this peculiar vacillating and dragging of time," which "contains whatever it is that is burdensome and paralysing" (p.97 / GA pp.146-47 / pdf 61).

Heidegger opens the enigma of time itself: is time "of a highly temperamental essence," with "hours that are like a moment" and "minutes that are like an eternity"? (p.98 / GA pp.147-49 / pdf 61). Calculable clock-time threatens to reduce lived time "to the level of an illusion … merely subjective" (p.98 / GA pp.147-49 / pdf 61), but he refuses that easy verdict. The result for the guiding problem: "Becoming bored is a peculiar being affected in a paralysing way by time as it drags and by time in general … a being affected which oppresses us in its own way" (p.98 / GA pp.147-49 / pdf 61 — **C15**). This oppression is "a peculiar impressing [*Andrangen*] of the power of … time" (p.98 / GA pp.147-49 / pdf 61), rooting boredom "in this enigmatic essence of time" — in "the way in which it temporalizes itself" (p.98 / GA pp.147-49 / pdf 61). Heidegger nonetheless resists collapsing boredom into time: "We must stick with boredom, so that precisely through its essence we may take a look into the concealed essence of time" (p.99 / GA pp.149-50 / pdf 62 — **C16**).

## §7. Being held in limbo by time as it drags (§23c, pp.99–101)

§23c isolates the first structural moment temporally. *Hingehaltenheit* is paradoxical: "surely something that drags precisely holds back, keeps itself distant, and does not oppress" (p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63). Yet "in becoming bored we are held in limbo, and indeed by time as it drags" (p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63). The "inherent predicament … is precisely that we cannot find anything in particular" (p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63); we look away from the clock "Toward nothing in particular," seeking only "something that will divert our attention … From being oppressed by time as it drags" (p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63).

Heidegger locates *which* time holds us: not "time in general" but "this particular interval of time that drags between our arrival and the departure of the train" (p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63). When we find an occupation, "Time goes more quickly because its dragging is no longer there … because in a certain way we forget time altogether" (p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63). Passing-the-time "betrays to us where we want to get away from, and this is precisely that place to which time in its slowness holds us" (p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63). The interim formula: "becoming bored is a being held in limbo by time as it drags over an interval of time" (p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63 — **C17**), with the deeper "how" of time's holding still unseen.

## §8. Being left empty by the refusal of things, and the interweaving of the two moments (§23d, pp.101–105)

The final subsection develops *Leergelassenheit* and binds it to *Hingehaltenheit*. Being held in limbo "does not alone constitute boredom," for "in passing the time we simultaneously seek to occupy ourselves with something" (p.101 / GA pp.152-53 / pdf 63). What we want is not the object or the result but "being occupied as such" — "Merely so as not to fall into this being left empty that is emerging in boredom" (p.102 / GA pp.153-55 / pdf 63). When occupied, "we are also taken [*hingenommen*] by things, if not altogether lost in them … even captivated [*benommen*]" — the antidote to emptiness (p.102 / GA pp.153-55 / pdf 63).

The phenomenology of refusal is precise. In the station, things *are* at hand — station, timetable, trees — yet "Although the things are at hand, they leave us empty. … We must even say that they leave us empty precisely because they are at hand" (p.102 / GA pp.153-55 / pdf 63). Emptiness is not absence: "To leave empty does not at all mean: to be absent … rather things must be at hand in order to leave us empty" (p.103 / GA pp.155-56 / pdf 63). The things "leave us in peace, do not disturb us. Yet they do not help us either … They abandon us to ourselves" (p.103 / GA pp.155-56 / pdf 63). Hence the definition: "To leave empty means to be something at hand that offers nothing. … Being left empty means to be offered nothing by what is at hand" (p.103 / GA pp.155-56 / pdf 63 — **C18**).

Heidegger sharpens this as *refusal*: the station "refuses itself to us as a station … because the train that belongs to it has not yet arrived" (p.103 / GA pp.155-56 / pdf 63). He distinguishes refusal from disappointment: "Becoming disappointed … does not mean becoming bored. … Where we become disappointed we have nothing more to seek and we withdraw. But here we precisely stay; not only that, but we are held in limbo" (p.104 / GA pp.156-58 / pdf 64 — **C19**). The causal question is bracketed: even if "we were mistaken about the timetable," "We are not asking about what causes or is responsible for boredom" but about the essence of becoming-bored "quite irrespective of how such becoming bored may have been factically caused" (p.104 / GA pp.156-58 / pdf 64).

The culminating insight is the *structural interweaving* of the two moments. They "are not simply pieced together, but are structurally interwoven" (p.105 / GA pp.158-59 / pdf 64). Indeed "even the first moment, being held in limbo, [is] the all-embracing and primarily determinative one" — for "The dragging of time as it were refuses the station the possibility of offering us anything. It forces it to leave us empty" (p.105 / GA pp.158-59 / pdf 64 — **C20**). The unit closes on the thesis that opens onto the rest of the course: "boredom is only possible at all because each thing, as we say, has its time. … If each thing did not have its time, then there would be no boredom" (p.105 / GA pp.158-59 / pdf 64 — **C21**). The "as yet obscure relation of the dragging along of time to the things that refuse themselves" is nothing less than "the question of what time itself is" (p.105 / GA pp.158-59 / pdf 64).

## §9. Structural summary of the first form

| Moment | German | Phenomenal mark | Temporal cash-out (this unit) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Being left empty | *Leergelassenheit* | "tedious"; things at hand offer nothing, refuse themselves, leave us in peace | grounded in the refusal *time* forces upon things |
| Being held in limbo | *Hingehaltenheit* | "wearisome"; held fast yet given leeway toward nothing in particular | held by *time as it drags* over the interval until the train |
| Passing the time | *Zeitvertreib* | counting trees, watching the clock; a "helpless gesture" when it fails | a driving-away of boredom that drives time on |

The first form is the *least* deep of the three precisely because it is anchored to a determinate situation and a determinate boring thing (the station, the missing train). Its emptiness is filled by becoming occupied; its limbo is the bounded interval of a timetable. Yet in extracting *Leergelassenheit* and *Hingehaltenheit* and showing them already governed by time's dragging, §23d hands the second and third forms (fcm-05, fcm-06) the exact apparatus they will deepen — where emptiness becomes self-formed and refusal becomes the "telling refusal" of beings as a whole.

## Boredom-bridge relevance

This unit is **highly bridge-relevant** to the King–Salvo empirical strand (see `_synthesis/phase0-overview.md` §11 and the digests in `bridge-sources/`). Four hooks stand out.

First, the **two structural moments are operationalizable**. *Hingehaltenheit* ("held in limbo … toward nothing in particular," p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63) and *Leergelassenheit* ("offered nothing by what is at hand," p.103 / GA pp.155-56 / pdf 63) decompose boredom into a *holding* component and an *emptying* component — a finer split than a single arousal axis. The bridge's high-arousal "searching gaze" (subjects "look for something that will divert our attention," p.100 / GA pp.150-52 / pdf 63) maps onto active passing-the-time; the low-arousal "zombie stare" maps onto pure *Leergelassenheit* where things "abandon us to ourselves" (p.103 / GA pp.155-56 / pdf 63).

Second, Heidegger **decouples boredom from clock-duration**: "the length of time plays no role … not because the progress of time is slow, but because it is too slow" (p.97 / GA pp.146-47 / pdf 61). This directly predicts the team's puzzle of subjects reporting *dragging time* while gaze remains engaged — duration estimates and physiological arousal can dissociate because what is measured phenomenally is *dragging* (*Hingehaltenheit*), not elapsed seconds. "To be slow and to drag are not the same thing" (p.98 / GA pp.147-49 / pdf 61) is the bridge's sharpest borrowed distinction.

Third, the **watch-checking gesture** is a ready-made behavioral marker. Heidegger reads repeated clock-checks not as passing-time but as "a sign that … our passing the time is not really succeeding" — a "helpless gesture" tracking *increasing* boredom (p.97 / GA pp.146-47 / pdf 61). For an eye-tracking / behavioral protocol this licenses treating off-task glances and check-behaviors as a graded index of failed *Zeitvertreib*, not mere distraction.

Fourth, the unit's **anti-consciousness methodology** (§22: do not make the attunement "an object swimming in the stream of consciousness," p.90 / GA pp.135-36 / pdf 58; preserve "the immediacy of everyday *Dasein*," p.91 / GA pp.136-38 / pdf 58) is a caution for self-report instruments: asking subjects to introspect on "boredom" may distort the very attunement, which is better read off comportment (passing-the-time behaviors) than off verbal report. The *structural interweaving* thesis (**C20**) — that limbo is "all-embracing and primarily determinative" and *forces* the emptiness — gives the bridge a falsifiable ordering hypothesis: manipulate perceived time-dragging and the felt emptiness/refusal of the stimulus should follow, not the reverse. These threads are carried forward in `fcm-king-salvo-bridge.md`.
