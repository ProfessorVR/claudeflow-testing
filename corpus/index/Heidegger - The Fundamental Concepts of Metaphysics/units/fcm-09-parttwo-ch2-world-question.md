# fcm-09 — Part Two, Chapter Two: The Beginning of Metaphysical Questioning with the Question of World (§§42–44)

*Unit of* Martin Heidegger, *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics: World, Finitude, Solitude* (GA 29/30, trans. McNeill & Walker, Indiana UP 1995). Book pp.176–185; GA [262–272]; pdf 100–104. Locus form: `(p.NN / GA pp.NN / pdf NN)`, with GA read from the running heads (the Klostermann pagination drifts non-linearly against the English).

This is the closing unit of the boredom spine's Part-One→Part-Two hinge. Three things happen across these three sections: §42 opens the world-question and chooses the *comparative* path through the three guiding theses; §43 confronts the difficulty — in content and in method — of gaining access to "life," and discovers in that difficulty the circle proper to philosophy; and §44, delivered as a "summary and renewed introduction following the vacation," restates the entire boredom→questions arc in compact form and lays down seven guidelines for the talk of a *fundamental attunement of philosophizing*. For the boredom index, §44 is the prize: it is the tightest single statement in the whole course of why profound boredom must be awakened before the metaphysical questions can be asked at all.

## §1. The three paths to the question "What is world?" (§42)

Heidegger begins with the first of the three questions, *What is world?* — and immediately warns against taking it as "a free-floating question asked along the way" `(p.176 / GA pp.262 / pdf 100)`. We do not yet know where to look for an answer, nor even what we are asking about. He then lays out three approaches.

The **first path** is historical-etymological: pursue the history of the word and concept "world." He has already attempted this in his essay *On the Essence of Ground*, tracing the stages of *kósmos* / *mundus* / "world" `(p.176 / GA pp.262 / pdf 100)`. But the word's history "provides only the exterior"; the inner history can be perceived only in connection with the fundamental problem of metaphysics. Within this external orientation the most familiar form of the problem is the theological distinction "between God and world," where world is "the totality of beings outside of and other than God" `(p.176 / GA pp.262 / pdf 100)`. Crucially, man is both a *part* of the world and stands *over against* it — a "having" of world "as that in which man moves," which he "both masters and serves" `(p.177 / GA pp.262 / pdf 100)`. Man is at once part of the world and "master and servant of the world." This *having-world* is the phenomenon that will let the comparison get underway.

The **second path** is the one taken in *Being and Time*: a preliminary characterization of the phenomenon of world by interpreting how we "at first and for the most part" move about in the everyday world, taking departure from "what lies to hand" `(p.177 / GA pp.262 / pdf 100)`. Heidegger inserts a sharp self-correction here that the index should preserve: it never occurred to him to claim that "the essence of man consists in the fact that he knows how to handle knives and forks" `(p.177 / GA pp.262 / pdf 100)`. The everyday analysis was a way of pressing the phenomenon of world forward "as a problem," not a definition of humanity by tool-use. That path "requires a very broad and wide-ranging perspective" which cannot be reproduced in the lecture.

The **third path** — the one actually chosen — is "a comparative examination" `(p.177 / GA pp.262 / pdf 100)`. Since man "has world," the question arises about the other beings that are also part of the world: animals, plants, the stone. Do they merely belong to the world as parts, while man in addition *has* world? Or does the animal too have world, and if so how? Out of these crude but immediate distinctions Heidegger formulates the three guiding theses that will organize the whole of Part Two: "[1.] the stone (material object) is worldless; [2.] the animal is poor in world; [3.] man is world-forming" `(p.177 / GA pp.263 / pdf 100)`. The German lemmas — *weltlos*, *weltarm*, *weltbildend* — carry the load. The aim is not a "more exact" interpretation of world but to bring "the worldly character of the world into view for the first time" as the theme of a fundamental problem of metaphysics `(p.178 / GA pp.263 / pdf 100)`.

## §2. The "from-without" limitation and the ambiguity of metaphysics (§42 close)

All three paths, Heidegger insists, share a structural defect: each "comes from without," bringing "the principles and perspectives that are characteristic of ordinary understanding" `(p.178 / GA pp.263 / pdf 100)`. Whichever path we take, the task is to "disabuse ourselves of these initially habitual perspectives" or transform them by the force of what we come to see. This is true of every metaphysical problem — and it implies that "we can never assess metaphysics by employing the criteria of ordinary understanding" `(p.178 / GA pp.264 / pdf 101)`. Metaphysics is neither beneficial nor inimical to culture, or, viewed externally, both at once; it is "ambiguous" precisely because it is "basically prior to such distinctions" `(p.178 / GA pp.264 / pdf 101)`. The comparative third path looks the most approachable because it is adaptable, but it harbors "special difficulties of its own" that must be flagged at the outset.

## §3. The difficulty of access to "life" — content and method (§43)

§43 isolates that difficulty. The comparison concerns three realms — material thing, animal, man — so we must already have at our disposal "certain essential distinctions" among them `(p.178 / GA pp.265 / pdf 101)`. But even the man/animal distinction is hard to fix. It is easy to say one is "a rational living being" and the other "a non-rational living being," yet the real question is "what does reason, or the lack of reason, actually mean here?" `(p.179 / GA pp.265 / pdf 101)`. And even if clarified, it is uncertain whether that distinction marks "what is most essential and what is metaphysically important." Hence the man/animal question is *not* the question whether man is "descended from the ape" `(p.179 / GA pp.265 / pdf 101)`; it concerns "the essence of the animality of the animal and the essence of the humanity of man." Determining the animality of the animal in turn requires clarity about "the living character of a living being," as distinct from "the non-living being which does not even have the possibility of dying" — for "a stone cannot be dead because it is never alive" `(p.179 / GA pp.265 / pdf 101)`.

The difficulty is not only of content but "almost more emphatically a methodological one" `(p.179 / GA pp.265 / pdf 101)`: by what path can the living character of the living being be made accessible? Morphology and physiology plus "some form of animal psychology" do not suffice, because they already presuppose that the animal is alive and "disposed in a certain manner." The animal "can perhaps neither observe itself, nor communicate any such observations to us"; even when it expresses itself, "it is we who must first interpret and analyze such forms of expression" `(p.180 / GA pp.265 / pdf 101)`. Heidegger crystallizes this into two fundamental difficulties: how "to determine the essence of life in general," and how "living beings as such ... [are] originarily accessible" `(p.180 / GA pp.266 / pdf 101)`. Both must be left open during the comparison, yet some provisional answer must always be held ready to guide it.

## §4. The circle of philosophy (§43 close)

The reciprocity of these demands means "we constantly find ourselves moving in a circle" `(p.180 / GA pp.266 / pdf 101)` — and this is "an indication that we are moving within the realm of philosophy." The circling is alien to ordinary understanding, which wants to "get the job in hand over and done with." Going round in circles "makes us feel dizzy, and dizziness is something uncanny": we feel "as though we are suspended in the Nothing" `(p.180 / GA pp.266 / pdf 101)`. Logic therefore demands no circle. But anyone "never been seized by dizziness" before a philosophical question "has never asked the question in a philosophical way" `(p.180 / GA pp.266 / pdf 101)`. The decisive matter is "an insight into the centre of the circle as such," possible only in the circling movement, "for the centre only manifests itself as such as we circle around it" `(p.180 / GA pp.267 / pdf 101)`. To argue away the circularity "only leads us away from philosophy itself." (He qualifies: not every circular proof is philosophical — "circle and turbulence" are not the same.) To sum up: we move in a circle when we presuppose a conception of the essence of life and then proceed, on that basis, to "open up a path" toward a conception of life `(p.180 / GA pp.267 / pdf 101)`.

## §5. The vacation recapitulation: metaphysics as comprehensive questioning (§44)

§44 reopens the course after the break and restates its spine. "Metaphysical knowledge is a comprehensive questioning" in a twofold sense `(p.180 / GA pp.268 / pdf 102)`: [1.] beings as a whole are "conceptually included in every metaphysical question," and [2.] whoever questions "is in each case caught up in the question as well," fundamentally affected by both act and object. But one is affected only if "already capable of being affected in his Dasein" — if "this possibility of being gripped lies in his very essence" `(p.181 / GA pp.268 / pdf 102)`. That possibility belongs to man insofar as Da-sein "always — but not exclusively — implies being attuned." This is the pivotal move for the boredom spine: "Only a being that is intrinsically attuned in general can find itself adversely attuned," and "where there is attunement, there is the possibility of a change of attunement, and thus also of awakening attunement" `(p.181 / GA pp.268 / pdf 102)`.

It follows that, to develop a comprehensive questioning, "it was first necessary to awaken a fundamental attunement and create the possibility of our being gripped" `(p.181 / GA pp.268 / pdf 102)`. The first part of the course — "a quite specific interpretation of a profound boredom in the ground of our Dasein" — was dedicated to exactly this `(p.181 / GA pp.268 / pdf 102)`. From the fundamental attunement three questions are developed: "[1.] What is world? [2.] What is finitude? [3.] What is individuation?" — and "the second question is also the most originary and most central one" `(p.181 / GA pp.268 / pdf 102)`. This is the compact spine: profound boredom (Part One) → awakened attunement → the three questions (Part Two), with finitude at the centre.

## §6. The seven guidelines, and melancholy as the attunement of philosophizing

Heidegger then gives seven guidelines to forestall misunderstanding `(pp.181–183 / GA pp.268–272 / pdf 102–103)`:

1. The three questions do not *exhaust* the attunement's significance; "they are simply drawn from it."
2. This attunement is not the *only* path to them — genuine metaphysical questions must be unfoldable "from out of every fundamental attunement of Dasein."
3. Which attunement we choose is "not simply a matter of arbitrary decision"; we choose freely "and yet in the deepest sense we are bound and compelled as well."
4. The choice binds us "to the intrinsic character of metaphysics itself which compels the engagement of a particular finite Dasein" `(p.182 / GA pp.269 / pdf 102)`.
5. "Every genuine fundamental attunement liberates and deepens, binds and releases the others" `(p.182 / GA pp.269 / pdf 102)`; attunements are not items "on the same level" to be exchanged, and are known "through Dasein itself," not "through a psychology of feelings."
6. The occurrence of attunement is "rooted in the history of Dasein," which "cannot be calculated" but "only be inhibited and distorted by calculation and manipulation" `(p.182 / GA pp.270 / pdf 103)`.
7. As a creative and essential activity, "philosophy stands in the fundamental attunement of melancholy" `(p.183 / GA pp.270 / pdf 103)`. This melancholy "concerns the form rather than the content of philosophizing," yet "necessarily prescribes a fundamental attunement which delimits the substantive content" of philosophical questioning.

Guideline 7 carries the long translator's note on the *Mut* word-family (older *Mut* = "cheer/spirits/mood"; *Schwermut* = melancholy; *Gemüt* = "overall mood"; the cognate *Armut* — "poverty" — will anchor the animal's *weltarm* in §46) `(p.183 / GA pp.270–72 / pdf 103)`. Heidegger grounds the claim in Aristotle's *Problemata* XXX.1 (953a10ff., Bekker), the question "why are all those men who have achieved exceptional things ... clearly melancholics?", naming Empedocles, Socrates, and Plato, and distinguishing *melancholía dia phýsin* from *dia nóson* `(p.183 / GA pp.270 / pdf 103)`. He warns that such talk is "particularly dangerous today" because it invites a psychologizing of creative work — "the most complacent and comfortable form of tyranny" `(p.184 / GA pp.271 / pdf 103)` — into which the whole attunement-analysis can "slide of its own accord."

The unit closes by re-listing, for the resumed course, the three paths (historical / everyday / comparative) and re-stating the three theses verbatim — "[1.] The stone is worldless; [2.] The animal is poor in world; [3.] Man is world-forming" `(p.184 / GA pp.272 / pdf 104)` — handing directly into Chapter Three, which begins the comparison "from the middle," with the animal's poverty in world.

## §7. Structural summary for the index

This unit performs three index-relevant functions. (a) It fixes the **three guiding theses** (*weltlos* / *weltarm* / *weltbildend*) that organize all of Part Two, and locates them in man's *having-world*. (b) It states the **double difficulty of access to life** and converts it into the **circle of philosophy**, the methodological signature of the whole work. (c) In §44 it gives the **most compact restatement of the boredom→questions spine** in the course, with the explicit licence for *awakening* an attunement and the explicit rejection of a "psychology of feelings." The finitude-is-central remark and the melancholy-as-form claim are the two most quotable nodes for cross-referencing.
