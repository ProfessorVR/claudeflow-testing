# fcm-01 — Ambiguity in the Essence of Philosophy (Metaphysics)

**Work:** Martin Heidegger, *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics: World, Finitude, Solitude* (*Die Grundbegriffe der Metaphysik. Welt — Endlichkeit — Einsamkeit*), GA 29/30, Freiburg lecture course WS 1929-30; trans. McNeill & Walker, Indiana UP 1995.
**Unit:** Preliminary Appraisal, Chapter Two, §§4-7 (incl. §6a, §6b, and sub-points α/β/γ). Book pp.11-24 / GA pp.[15-37] / pdf 17-24.
**Reading order; GA pagination read from the running heads (non-linear, never computed). Triple locus throughout.**

## §1. Where the chapter stands: ambiguity as a *positive* characteristic of metaphysics

Chapter Two follows the opening reorientation of the course away from "the indifferent expectation of acquiring some knowledge" toward "a fundamental comportment" (*Grundverhalten*) — "essentially and necessarily a matter of a certain readiness" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17). Against the assumption that fundamental concepts merely "presuppose interest" that can be "more or less penetratingly acquired as knowledge," Heidegger insists "it is not like this at all" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17). The "extrinsic arrangement" — "lecture theatre, lectern, lecturer, listeners" — is the same as for mathematics or Greek tragedy, yet because "philosophy is something totally different from science, and yet the exterior form of science still remains," philosophy "hides itself," "does not appear directly," and "presents itself as something which it is not at all" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17).

The pivotal thesis is that this self-concealment "is neither simply a whim of philosophy, nor a fault, but belongs to the positive essence of metaphysics" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17). The name for it is **ambiguity** (*Zweideutigkeit*). "Our preliminary appraisal of philosophy is incomplete until we have provided some indication of this ambiguity … which is a positive characteristic of the essence of metaphysics" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17). Heidegger announces three ambiguities to discuss: "[1.] The ambiguity in philosophizing in general; [2.] The ambiguity in our philosophizing here and now in the comportment of listeners and … the lecturer; [3.] The ambiguity of philosophical truth as such" — and stresses the aim is not "a psychology of philosophizing" but to "clarify the fundamental orientation demanded of us" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17).

## §2. §4 — the ambiguity in philosophizing in general: science *or* worldview-proclamation

The general ambiguity is the "uncertainty as to whether or not philosophy is science or the proclamation of a worldview." Philosophy "presents itself as and looks like a science, and yet is not"; it "presents itself as the proclamation of a worldview and likewise is not this" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17). These "two kinds of semblance" (*Schein*), "of looking like …, are allied," and through this alliance "the ambiguousness first becomes obtrusive": philosophy "looks like the scientific grounding and presentation of a worldview, and yet is something else" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17).

The "dual semblance of being a science and worldview brings about a constant insecurity in philosophy" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17). On one side, "this 'never enough' of scientific knowledge is always too much at the decisive moment"; on the other, the demand that knowledge "be practically applied … and transformed into factical life" remains "superficial to philosophizing" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17). The attempt to "weld together" creative thinking and moral concern yields "those hybrids which, without marrow, bones, or blood, eke out a literary existence [*Dasein*]" — a scientific treatise "with moralising directives" or "a good sermon using scientific expressions" (p.11 / GA pp.15-17 / pdf 17). Conversely, a "strictly scientific treatise, dry, heavy," may be "laden with philosophy through and through," and "a commonplace discussion" may be "of the most rigorous philosophical comprehension" (p.12 / GA pp.17-18 / pdf 18). Hence "philosophy parades in the marketplace in manifold illusory forms or even disguises," recognizable "only by whoever becomes intimately acquainted with it, i.e., takes trouble over it" (p.12 / GA pp.17-18 / pdf 18).

## §3. §5 — the ambiguity here and now: listener and teacher

The ambiguity "always becomes more acute … rather than vanishing, wherever we are explicitly concerned with it, as is the case in our situation here and now": philosophy as "a subject to be taught, an examination subject, a discipline in which people do their doctorates" (p.12 / GA pp.17-18 / pdf 18). For listeners the standard comportment is to "take it in or pass it by"; then "nothing else happens at all, it is simply that something fails to happen" (p.12 / GA pp.17-18 / pdf 18). "The uncanny thing is that we do not notice this at all," that it "makes no difference to us … if we pass it by" (p.12 / GA pp.17-18 / pdf 18). Crucially, attendance does not dissolve the ambiguity: "is not everyone sitting there just as attentively or just as bored?" (p.12 / GA pp.17-18 / pdf 18) — an early, almost offhand registration of boredom as the affective texture of the lecture-situation, well before the thematic analysis of Chapters Three to Five.

The teacher's ambiguity is sharper and "more persistent and dangerous," "because a certain undesired authority always speaks for him, which has the peculiar and obscure effect of persuading others" (p.13 / GA pp.18-20 / pdf 18; cf. p.14 / GA pp.20-21 / pdf 18). This authority rests "only on the fact that we are not understood": "only so long as we are not understood does this dubious authority work for us"; once understood, "it will become apparent whether we are philosophizing or not," and either way "this authority will collapse" or "was never there in the first place" (p.13 / GA pp.18-20 / pdf 18). It then "become[s] clear … that philosophizing fundamentally belongs to each human being as something proper to them," certain people having "the strange fate of being a spur for others, so that philosophizing awakens in them" (p.13 / GA pp.18-20 / pdf 18). Thus "every philosophical lecture — whether it is a philosophizing or not — is an ambiguous beginning in a way unknown to the sciences" (p.13 / GA pp.18-20 / pdf 18).

## §4. §6 — the truth of philosophy and its ambiguity; (a) it concerns everyone

The reply that ambiguity "can be decided on the purely factual grounds of proof" (p.13 / GA pp.18-20 / pdf 18) leads to the innermost ambiguity. Heidegger asks "what can really be proven," suggesting "what is provable is always only that which is essentially irrelevant" (p.14 / GA pp.20-21 / pdf 18). The "last attempt to overcome ambiguity by limiting things to whatever is provable" forces "the question of what the character of philosophical truth … is in general" (p.14 / GA pp.20-21 / pdf 18). This rests on "an underlying presupposition: that even in philosophy what is essential is in general whatever can be proven. Yet perhaps that is a mistake" (p.14 / GA pp.20-21 / pdf 18).

The common understanding misreads philosophy in two ways: "first as something that concerns and is understood by everyone, and second as something ultimate and supreme" (p.14 / GA pp.20-21 / pdf 18). Subsection (a): "Philosophy is something that concerns everyone … not the prerogative of one human being" (p.15 / GA pp.21-22 / pdf 18). But the general awareness "tacitly concludes that what concerns everyone must be understood by everyone," "immediately clear … without further effort on the part of … common sense" (p.15 / GA pp.21-22 / pdf 18). What is so understood "straightaway" is "such statements as 2 times 2 is 4, whatever can be calculated"; grasping such a thing requires "the most negligible amount or even no expenditure of human substance" (p.15 / GA pp.21-22 / pdf 18). The false inference: "Whatever everyone can understand prescribes in general what can be true … what a philosophical truth must look like" (p.16 / GA pp.23-24 / pdf 20).

## §5. §6b — philosophy as ultimate and supreme: (α) the semblance of absolutely certain truth

Subsection (b): philosophy "is something ultimate, extreme," and therefore — so the misreading runs — "must … also be what is most sure," "what is most certain" (p.16 / GA pp.23-24 / pdf 20). What "everyone understands without any strenuous human effort must have supreme certainty," and its developed form is "mathematical knowledge … the highest, most rigorous, and most certain knowledge" (p.16 / GA pp.23-24 / pdf 20). Heidegger marshals the tradition: **Plato** "had inscribed above the entrance to his Academy" that "no one shall have entry who is not well-versed in geometry"; **Descartes** "want[ed] … to furnish philosophical truth with the character of mathematical truth"; from **Leibniz** comes "Without mathematics one cannot penetrate into the ground of metaphysics" (p.16 / GA pp.23-24 / pdf 20).

Yet "if it is as clear as day that philosophical truth is absolutely certain truth, why does precisely this endeavor … never succeed?" Across history we "constantly see one catastrophe after another," with **Aristotle**, Descartes, Leibniz, and **Hegel** "refuted by a doctoral candidate" (p.16-17 / GA pp.23-24, 24-26 / pdf 20-21). The decisive point is not historical failure but essential mismatch: mathematical knowledge "although it objectively comprises a great wealth … is in itself, in terms of its content, the emptiest knowledge imaginable, and as such is … the least binding for man" (p.17 / GA pp.24-26 / pdf 21) — hence "mathematicians can make great discoveries when only seventeen years old," needing not to "be borne by the inner substance of man" (p.17 / GA pp.24-26 / pdf 21). "This emptiest and at the same time least binding knowledge … cannot become the measure for the richest and most binding knowledge imaginable: philosophical knowledge" (p.17 / GA pp.24-26 / pdf 21).

## §6. §6b — (β) the emptiness of formal contradiction; truth rooted in the *fate of Dasein*

The "much more acute objection" is the self-refutation charge: to deny philosophy absolute certainty is itself proclaimed "in a philosophical lecture" with a claim to certainty — "the whole sophistry of this unscientific conduct" (p.18 / GA pp.26-27 / pdf 21). The argument runs: "it is contradictory to assert with absolute certainty that there is no absolute certainty, for this refutes itself" (p.18 / GA pp.26-27 / pdf 21). Heidegger answers on two counts. **First**, "precisely because this argument is so easy to bring up at any time, it has essentially nothing to say … It is completely empty and non-binding" — "a formal argumentation which forces every speaker back into self-contradiction," whose appropriateness "for sustaining and determining philosophy in its essence" has "so far not … been delivered" (p.18 / GA pp.26-27 / pdf 21). **Second**, the argument "is not relevant at all": "We are not in fact claiming, and will never claim, that it is absolutely certain that philosophy is not a science," because "we are not at all absolutely certain … whether we are philosophizing at all in all these discussions" (p.18 / GA pp.26-27 / pdf 21).

This delivers the keystone. The uncertainty "is not some accidental property of philosophy in relation to us, but belongs to philosophy itself," since "Philosophy has a meaning only as human activity. Its truth is essentially that of human Dasein" — and "The truth of philosophizing is in part rooted in the fate (*Geschick*) of Dasein" (p.19 / GA pp.28-29 / pdf 22). Dasein "occurs in freedom," before "possibilities it does not foresee," "subject to a change it does not know," in "a predicament it does not have power over" (p.19 / GA pp.28-29 / pdf 22). We know this neither "with absolute certainty" nor "as something probable" — "probability is merely the conceptual counterpart of a presupposed absolute certainty" — but "in a unique kind of knowing … a hovering between certainty and uncertainty" (p.19 / GA pp.28-29 / pdf 22). And so "everything should start to vacillate," for "God does not philosophize, if indeed (as the name already says) philosophy, this love of … as homesickness for …, must maintain itself in nothingness, in finitude" (p.19 / GA pp.28-29 / pdf 22). The culminating image: "Philosophy is the opposite of all comfort and assurance. It is turbulence (*Wirbel*), the turbulence into which man is spun" — and therefore "constantly remains in the perilous neighbourhood of supreme uncertainty"; "no knower necessarily stands so close to the verge of error … as the one who philosophizes" (p.19 / GA pp.28-29 / pdf 22). For "this reason too the proper fundamental stance rarely awakens" (p.19 / GA pp.28-29 / pdf 22) — the verb *awakens* that will govern the whole boredom analysis.

## §7. §6b — (γ) the ambiguity of the critical stance in Descartes and modern philosophy

The Cartesian sub-point shows the ambiguity "work[ing] itself out in a special way" when philosophy is raised "to the rank of an absolute science" (p.20 / GA pp.29-31 / pdf 22). With Descartes "philosophizing begins with doubt, and it seems as though everything is put into question. Yet it only seems so" — "Dasein, the I (the ego), is not put into question at all" (p.20 / GA pp.29-31 / pdf 22). This "critical stance" is "at most … scientifically critical but not … philosophically critical": it puts in question only "knowledge, consciousness of things … so as to reinforce the assuredness that has already been anticipated," while "Dasein itself is never put into question" (p.20 / GA pp.29-31 / pdf 22). Modern philosophy "since Descartes, puts nothing at all at stake," being "critical in a non-binding and unperilous way," "inherently assured in advance that nothing … can happen to it" (p.20 / GA pp.29-31 / pdf 22).

## §8. §7 — the struggle against insurmountable ambiguity; philosophizing as *Grundgeschehen*

Insight into the ambiguity "acts as a deterrent [*abschreckend*] and ultimately betrays the entire fruitlessness of such activity" (p.21 / GA pp.31-32 / pdf 21). But we must not "weaken this impression of the hopelessness" with consolations; "we must rather uphold and hold out in this terror [*Schrecken*]" (p.21 / GA pp.31-32 / pdf 21). For in the terror "becomes manifest something essential": "in the philosophical concept [*Begriff*], man, and indeed man as a whole … is in the grip of an attack [*Angriff*], driven out of everydayness and driven back into the ground of things" (p.21 / GA pp.31-32 / pdf 21). And the attacker "is not man, the dubious subject of the everyday … Rather, in philosophizing the Da-sein in man launches the attack upon man" (p.21 / GA pp.31-32 / pdf 21). Being "comprehensively included" is "not some blissful awe, but the struggle against the insurmountable ambiguity of all questioning and being" (p.21 / GA pp.31-32 / pdf 21).

Equally, it would be "perverse to see in philosophy a despairing activity … something gloomy, morose, 'pessimistic'" (p.21 / GA pp.31-32 / pdf 21), because this assessment "stems from the social atmosphere … of the normal human being," for whom "what is normal is what is essential," "the eternal average" (p.22 / GA pp.32-34 / pdf 23). "This normal human being takes his or her petty pleasures as the measure of what joy should be," "shallow fears as the measure of … terror and anxiety," "smug comforts as the measure of … security and insecurity" (p.22 / GA pp.32-34 / pdf 23). It must "become questionable … whether philosophizing … may be dragged before such a judge" (p.22 / GA pp.32-34 / pdf 23). Heidegger then concludes positively, twice citing the ancients: **Heraclitus** (DK 108), that "being wise, the *to sophon* [philosophy], is something separated off from everything else" — in Latin *absolutum*, "something that … first forms its own proper place for itself" (p.23-24 / GA pp.34-35, 35-37 / pdf 23-24); and **Plato** (*Republic*), that "the difference between the philosophizing human being and the one who is not philosophizing is the difference between being awake (*hypar*) and sleeping (*onar*)" — "the non-philosophizing human being, including the scientific human being … is asleep. Only philosophizing is wakeful Dasein" (p.23 / GA pp.34-35 / pdf 23). **Hegel** names philosophy "the inverted world" (p.23 / GA pp.34-35 / pdf 23). The thesis that crowns the chapter: "Philosophy does not exist because there are sciences, but vice-versa: there can be sciences only because … there is philosophy" (p.23 / GA pp.34-35 / pdf 23); "Philosophizing itself is rather a fundamental way of Da-sein," which "lets Da-sein first become what it can be" (p.23 / GA pp.34-35 / pdf 23). It "lies prior to every occupation and constitutes the fundamental occurrence (*Grundgeschehen*) of Dasein, something autonomous that stands on its own" (p.24 / GA pp.35-37 / pdf 24), recalling the earlier Novalis figure that "philosophizing is homesickness, the urge to be at home everywhere" (p.23 / GA pp.34-35 / pdf 23). This autonomy is exactly why "an attunement must be awakened" (the agenda of Chs. 3-5): the fundamental occurrence cannot be ascertained from outside but must be enacted from within Dasein's own fate.

## Boredom-bridge relevance

fcm-01 carries no boredom analysis but installs its **enabling conditions and affective register** for the King-Salvo bridge: philosophy's truth is "rooted in the fate of Dasein," is "turbulence," and "cannot be decided on the purely factual grounds of proof" — the same anti-present-at-hand caution (`anticipatory-application`) that warns a fundamental attunement (boredom) is never a single instrument value (EEG / gaze / self-report) but shows only across their divergence, and that the "normal" average (the convenient bipolar metric, the "novelty effect") must not be allowed to dictate what the attunement discloses.
