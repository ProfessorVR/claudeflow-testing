# fcm-12 — Part Two, Chapter Five: The Behaviour and Captivation of the Animal; the Disinhibiting Ring; Driesch & Uexküll; Being-Open as a Not-Having of World (§§58–63)

*Unit of* Martin Heidegger, *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics: World, Finitude, Solitude* (GA 29/30, trans. McNeill & Walker, Indiana UP 1995). Book pp.236–273; GA [343–396]; pdf 130–148. Locus form: `(p.NN / GA pp.NN / pdf NN)`, with GA read from the running heads (the Klostermann pagination drifts non-linearly against the English). This is a *map* (condensed) unit covering the conceptual heart of the "animal poor in world" arc: the full structure of *captivation*, the *disinhibiting ring*, the two decisive biological interlocutors (Driesch and Uexküll), and the resolution of the having/not-having-world aporia.

## §1. Behaviour (*Benehmen*) versus comportment (*Verhalten*) — §58a

Heidegger opens by refusing to read animal activity as a series of "processes" or "unfolding events" `(p.236 / GA pp.343 / pdf 130)`. To watch "the worm flees from the mole, the dog snaps at the fly" as mere event-sequences "misses the decisive thing": the worm's movement is *escape*, the mole's is *pursuit* `(p.236 / GA pp.344 / pdf 130)`. He therefore introduces the governing distinction of the whole chapter. The animal's manner of being is **behaviour** (*Benehmen* / *Sichbenehmen*) — "not a doing and acting, as in human comportment, but a driven performing [*Treiben*]" `(p.237 / GA pp.345 / pdf 130)`. Man's manner of being is **comportment** (*Verhalten*), a "comporting oneself toward. . . ." The two "are fundamentally different from one another" `(p.237 / GA pp.345 / pdf 130)`. Capability is "instinctual, a driving forward"; behaviour is drivenness toward a performance of a particular kind. A brief excursus on language (we also speak of "driving snow") concedes that the terminology is in one sense arbitrary, since "a certain inconsequentiality belongs . . . to the essence of language," which "belongs to the essence of man in his finitude" — "to imagine a god expressing himself in speech is utterly meaningless" `(p.238 / GA pp.346 / pdf 131)`.

## §2. Absorption-in-itself as captivation — §58b

Behaviour rests on the animal's *absorption in itself* (*Eingenommenheit in sich*): in driven performing "the animal does not drive itself away from itself" but "intrinsically retains itself and is intrinsically absorbed in itself," though "no reflection is involved" `(p.239 / GA pp.347 / pdf 131)`. This self-retention, which "has nothing to do with the selfhood of the human being," Heidegger names **captivation** (*Benommenheit*): "the animal can only behave insofar as it is essentially captivated" `(p.238 / GA pp.346 / pdf 131)`. Captivation is "the condition of possibility for the fact that . . . the animal behaves within an environment but never within a world" `(p.238 / GA pp.346 / pdf 131)`. He guards against psychologism: although the everyday word names a human "intermediate state somewhere between consciousness and unconsciousness," and is even a "psychiatric concept," there is "no question of simply transferring this state . . . into the animal." Captivation is "not some state that accompanies the animal" but "the inner possibility of animal being itself" `(p.239 / GA pp.347-49 / pdf 131)`.

## §3. The structure of behaviour and the bee experiments — §59 / §59a

Behaviour is intrinsically a *being-related-to* — a "movement toward . . . , a reaching out for" — yet not a comportment toward present-at-hand things `(p.240 / GA pp.350 / pdf 132)`. To exhibit this, Heidegger turns to "concrete examples . . . drawn from experimental research," noting that insects "have an exemplary function within the problematic of biology" `(p.240 / GA pp.349-50 / pdf 132)`. The worker bee flies consistently to particular blossoms, following scent. The decisive experiment: a bee placed at a bowl of more honey than it can hold begins sucking, breaks off, and flies away — which seems to show it "recognizes that it cannot cope." But "if its abdomen is carefully cut away while it is sucking, a bee will simply carry on regardless … while the honey runs out of the bee from behind" `(p.242 / GA pp.353 / pdf 133)`. This "shows conclusively that the bee by no means recognizes the presence of too much honey." Rather, the bee "is simply taken [*hingenommen*] by its food"; such "driven being taken also excludes the possibility of any recognition of presence" `(p.243 / GA pp.354 / pdf 133)`. Satiation, not recognition, inhibits the drive. The sun-orientation experiments (Bethe, explained by Rádl 1905; box-imprisonment shifting the return angle by the sun's displacement) deepen the point: the bee "knows precisely nothing of all this," is "absorbed by a direction," "driven by the fundamental drive of foraging" — captivated "by what the sun occasions," "not on account of any recognition or reflection" `(p.246 / GA pp.358-59 / pdf 135)`.

## §4. Captivation as the withholding of the "as" — §59b

The general result follows. In all its activity the bee is *related to* the feeding place, sun, hive — yet "this being related to . . . is not an apprehending of these things as feeding place, as sun." "There is no apprehending [*Vernehmen*], but only a behaving [*Benehmen*]," because "the possibility of apprehending something as something is withheld [*genommen*] from the animal" — and withheld in the sense of "not given at all" `(p.247 / GA pp.360 / pdf 135)`. Hence the twofold formula: captivation signifies "essentially having every apprehending of something as something withheld from it," and, "in having this withheld . . . the animal is precisely taken by things" `(p.247 / GA pp.360 / pdf 135)`. The radical consequence: beings are neither disclosed to the animal *nor* closed off from it — "captivation stands outside this possibility"; "the animal as such does not stand within a manifestness of beings" `(p.248 / GA pp.361 / pdf 136)`. The animal is "suspended, as it were, between itself and its environment," yet neither is "experienced as being" `(p.248 / GA pp.361 / pdf 136)`.

## §5. The eliminative character — §60a

This not-attending is not merely negative; behaviour "actually possesses an eliminative character in respect of that to which it relates" — shown "as destruction—as devouring or as avoidance" `(p.250 / GA pp.363 / pdf 137)`. The cannibalism of female insects after copulation is the striking case: the one animal "is never there for the other simply as a living creature, but is only there … either as sexual partner or as prey" — in each case only as a form of "'away' [*weg*]" `(p.250 / GA pp.363-65 / pdf 137)`. Phototropism (the Camberwell Beauty; Rádl's freshwater crabs that orient eyes to the light even upside-down; the moth in the candle; Darwin's moth-and-moon question) shows that "this light-seeking creature plunges into the light precisely because it does not attend to the light or grasp it as such" `(p.251 / GA pp.366 / pdf 138)`. The term *elimination* (*Be-seitigen*) "must be taken in a quite fundamental sense": behaviour is "certainly a relating to … but … in such a way that beings can never … manifest themselves as beings" — "no letting-be of beings as such … not even any not letting-be" `(p.252 / GA pp.367-69 / pdf 138)`.

## §6. The disinhibiting ring (*Enthemmungsring*) — §60b

Here the unit's keystone concept arrives. Capability for . . . "is open for . . . stimuli, for that which initiates, i.e., disinhibits the capability" — and this "other is taken up into this openness . . . in a manner that we shall describe as **disinhibition** [*Enthemmung*]" `(p.253 / GA pp.369 / pdf 139)`. Crucially, "nothing else can ever penetrate the ring around the animal" `(p.253 / GA pp.369 / pdf 139)`. Against the objection that drives are already "uninhibited," Heidegger argues the instinctual drive "possesses an inner tension and charge . . . that essentially must be disinhibited before it can pass over into driven activity" `(p.254 / GA pp.370 / pdf 139)`. That which disinhibits "constantly withdraws [*entzieht sich*]" and is therefore never attended to as something present-at-hand. So the animal's "self-encirclement [*Sich-Einringen*] . . . is not a kind of encapsulation"; rather "the animal surrounds itself with a disinhibiting ring which prescribes what can affect or occasion its behaviour," and "always intrinsically bears its disinhibiting ring along with it . . . as long as it is alive" `(p.255 / GA pp.370 / pdf 139)`. Life is "the struggle [*Ringen*] to maintain this encircling ring." Heidegger then names the comparison: this "leads us toward the distinction … of man's world-forming and the animal's poverty in world, a poverty which … is nonetheless a kind of wealth" — "life is a domain which possesses a wealth of openness with which the human world may have nothing to compare" `(p.255 / GA pp.371 / pdf 139)`, accessible "only if we consider it in a deconstructive [*abbauenden*] gaze."

## §7. The reversal of stimulation — §61a

Heidegger inverts the standard physiological order. Irritability (*Reizbarkeit*) had been named the mark of "living substance" (Johannes Müller's *Handbuch*), but "the stimulus and the process of being stimulated is not the condition of possibility for the disinhibition" of an instinctual drive. On the contrary, he holds, "it is only where there is disinhibition and intrinsic encirclement that stimulation is possible" `(p.255 / GA pp.372 / pdf 140)`. This explains why a given species is "restricted to a quite specific manifold of possible stimuli": "however strong or intense a stimulus is . . . a particular animal may be utterly unresponsive" — not because of a barrier but because "in the unity of its captivation" it "does not have any intrinsic drives that are oriented in this direction" `(p.256 / GA pp.373-74 / pdf 140)`. **Positively expressed, "the organism is the capability for behaviour in the unity of captivation"** `(p.257 / GA pp.374 / pdf 141)`. Against Buytendijk's claim that the animal's bond to environment is "almost as intimate as the unity of the body itself," Heidegger insists "the unity of the animal's body is grounded . . . in the unity of captivation" — "Captivation is the fundamental essence of the organism" `(p.258 / GA pp.376 / pdf 141)`. He summarizes captivation in **six structural moments**: "[1.] withholding; [2.] being taken; [3.] absorption; [4.] openness for something else; [5.] the structure of encirclement thus given; and … [6.] … captivation is the condition of the possibility of any kind of behaviour" `(p.259 / GA pp.377-79 / pdf 142)`.

## §8. Two essential steps in biology: Driesch and Uexküll — §61b

Heidegger names "two decisive steps . . . taken in biology" `(p.261 / GA pp.380 / pdf 143)`. The **first step** is "the recognition of the holistic character of the organism" — *wholeness* (*Ganzheit*): the organism "is not an aggregate" but governed by wholeness "in each and every stage." This grew from **Hans Driesch's** "pioneering investigations into the embryos of sea-urchins" (*Die Lokalisation morphogenetischer Vorgänge*, 1899): once a cell-group's development is determined "in the context of the whole," it "proceeds independently of the environment" `(p.262 / GA pp.382 / pdf 143)` — "the breakthrough of the idea of the whole." But this success "also represents a great danger": the experiments seemed to confirm purposiveness, and "Driesch was driven … to adopt his biological theory, known as neovitalism," a vitalism "characterized by the appeal to a certain force or entelechy." Heidegger's verdict: "vitalism is just as dangerous as mechanism" — "vitalism tries to solve the problem too hastily" `(p.262 / GA pp.382 / pdf 143)`. Driesch's result is "no longer conclusive today" (superseded by Spemann); what is essential is only "that the organism as such asserts itself at every stage of life." Yet Driesch's wholeness "coincides as it were with the external surface of the animal's body" — it leaves out the animal's *relation to environment* `(p.262 / GA pp.382 / pdf 143)`.

The **second step**, taken by "the near contemporary investigations of **Uexküll**," supplies precisely that relation `(p.263 / GA pp.383 / pdf 144)`. Ecology (from *oikos*, "house") investigates "where and how animals are at home in the world," but Darwinism took this externally as *adaptation*, on the "fundamentally misconceived idea that the animal is present at hand, and then subsequently adapts itself." Uexküll's value lies "less" in his "theoretical-philosophical interpretation" than in "the astonishing sureness and abundance of his observations." Heidegger's most-cited move follows: when Uexküll speaks of an *Umwelt* (and even an *Innenwelt*), "in fact he means nothing other than what we have characterized as the disinhibiting ring" `(p.263 / GA pp.383 / pdf 144)`. He cautions that "the whole approach does become philosophically problematic if we proceed to talk about the human world in the same manner," and credits Uexküll as the biologist "who has repeatedly pointed out … that what the animal stands in relation to is given for it in a different way than … for the human being" `(p.263 / GA pp.385 / pdf 144)`. The engagement is endorsed as "one of the most fruitful things that philosophy can learn from contemporary biology." The decisive problem, though, is not otherness of degree or kind but "whether the animal can apprehend something as something, something as a being, at all." If it cannot, Heidegger says, "then the animal is separated from man by an abyss" `(p.264 / GA pp.384 / pdf 144)`.

## §9. The incompleteness: captivation as motility — §61c

Heidegger flags that the interpretation is "incomplete" in an *essential* respect: it has omitted "the essence of motility belonging to the living being" `(p.264 / GA pp.386 / pdf 145)`. Life is "just as essentially process," "formally speaking motion" — birth, growth, aging, death. But "this motility determines the being of the animal as such": "Captivation is not a static condition . . . but rather an intrinsically determinate motility which continually unfolds or atrophies." "Captivation is at the same time motility" `(p.265 / GA pp.385-86 / pdf 145)`. Genetic inheritance, the species-character, and above all *death* are raised as the touchstone: because captivation belongs to the animal's essence, "the animal cannot die in the sense in which dying is ascribed to human beings but can only come to an end" `(p.267 / GA pp.388 / pdf 145)`. (Boveri's "organisms as historical beings" is invoked here only to be held in question.)

## §10. Being-open as a not-having of world — §62

The unit's pay-off for the world-question. World "means the accessibility of beings," and the animal "does have access to beings" — "the nest that is sought out, the prey that is seized, is surely a being" `(p.268 / GA pp.390 / pdf 146)`. But the formula must be tightened: "world does not mean the accessibility of beings but rather . . . the accessibility of beings *as such*" `(p.269 / GA pp.391 / pdf 146)`. Since in captivation "the possibility for the manifestness of beings" is withheld, "the animal essentially cannot have world at all, although that which it relates to can always be experienced as a being in our experience." This not-having, however, "does not force the animal alongside the stone," which "does not even have this possibility," for "not attending to . . . presupposes a being open" `(p.269 / GA pp.391 / pdf 146)`. Hence the animal shows no simultaneous having and not-having of world, but rather, Heidegger writes, "a not-having of world in the having of openness for whatever disinhibits" `(p.270 / GA pp.391-93 / pdf 147)` — "a not-having . . . on the basis of a having" that the stone wholly lacks.

## §11. The self-objection and its removal — §63

Heidegger raises his own most serious misgiving. "The animal could only be deprived of world if it at least knew something of world" — "but this is precisely what we have denied" `(p.270 / GA pp.392 / pdf 147)`. So "if the world is essentially closed to the animal then we can indeed talk of a not-having," yet "we are never permitted to understand this as a deprivation"; the poverty-thesis "goes too far" and "is being conceived by comparison with man" `(p.270 / GA pp.393 / pdf 147)`. The removal: he ranks the grounding. Asking whether captivation grounds poverty-in-world or vice versa, "obviously the first is the case: Captivation is the condition of the possibility of poverty in world" `(p.272 / GA pp.396 / pdf 148)`. He then "further weaken[s] even this proposition": captivation "is the condition of the possibility of a *merely comparative* definition of animality in terms of poverty in world," one reached only "insofar as the animal is viewed from the perspective of man" `(p.272 / GA pp.396 / pdf 148)`. The thesis is therefore "far from being a, let alone the, fundamental metaphysical principle of the essence of animality" — "at best . . . a proposition that follows from the essential determinations of animality" only "if the animal is regarded in comparison with humanity" `(p.272 / GA pp.396 / pdf 148)`. Yet the thesis must be *retained as a problem*: "that which disinhibits . . . brings an essential disruption into the essence of the animal" `(p.273 / GA pp.396 / pdf 148)`, and biology's silence about creaturely suffering "is no counter-argument against metaphysics." Heidegger closes by re-binding the whole analysis to the boredom spine: we developed the not-having of world while "in this context … we compared the animal's not-having of world with our own having of world," measured against "that 'as a whole' which profound boredom itself manifests to us" `(p.273 / GA pp.396 / pdf 148)`.

## §12. Structural summary for the index

This unit performs four index functions. (a) It fixes the **behaviour/comportment** pair (*Benehmen* vs *Verhalten*) and grounds behaviour in **captivation** (*Benommenheit*) as the inner possibility of animal being. (b) It establishes captivation's defining negation — **the withholding of the "as"**: the animal apprehends nothing *as* a being and so "does not stand within a manifestness of beings," which entails the *eliminative* character of all behaviour. (c) It introduces the **disinhibiting ring** (*Enthemmungsring*) and the inverted stimulus-order (disinhibition grounds stimulation), yielding the positive definition "the organism is the capability for behaviour in the unity of captivation" and the **six structural moments**. (d) It credits **Driesch** (wholeness → neovitalism, rejected) and **Uexküll** (*Umwelt* = disinhibiting ring; observations endorsed, philosophy cautioned), then resolves the world-aporia: **captivation grounds poverty-in-world**, which is "merely comparative" from the human standpoint.
