# FCM Unit fcm-10 — The Animal Is Poor in World (*weltarm*)

**Work.** Martin Heidegger, *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics: World, Finitude, Solitude* (GA 29/30), trans. William McNeill & Nicholas Walker (Indiana University Press, 1995). Freiburg lecture course, winter semester 1929–30.

**Coverage.** Part Two, Chapter Three — *The Animal Is Poor in World*, §§45–48, book pp. 186–200 (GA [274–294], pdf 105–112).

**Locus convention.** Triple locus `(p.NN / GA pp.NN / pdf NN)`; `pdf = floor((book_page + 24) / 2)`; GA bracket read from the running heads (non-linear within a spread).

## §1. The propositional character of the thesis; metaphysics and the positive sciences; the circle of philosophy (§45, p.186)

Heidegger opens by asking what *kind* of proposition "the animal is poor in world" is — and the same of its two companions (the stone is worldless, man is world-forming). The animal is zoology's object, so one expects the thesis to be "borrowed from zoology" (p.186 / GA pp.274 / pdf 105). He blocks that inference. The thesis is broader than any zoological generalization (it covers amoebae, infusoria, sea urchins — "every animal"), and its breadth is symptomatic of something deeper: it intends *animality as such*, "a statement of essence" (*Wesensaussage*). The decisive reversal: it is "not a statement of essence simply because it holds true for all animals … Rather, it is the other way around: it holds true for all animals because it is a statement of essence" (p.187 / GA pp.275 / pdf 105). Universal validity is a *result* of essential knowledge, never its ground.

From this the methodological knot follows. Because zoology deals with animals, the thesis "cannot be a result of zoological investigation; rather, it must be its presupposition" (p.187 / GA pp.275 / pdf 105) — it antecedently delimits the field within which any positive investigation of animals can move. Yet it is neither arbitrary nor a hypothesis confirmable by a specific investigation: it "does not derive from zoology, but it cannot be elucidated independently of zoology either" (p.187 / GA pp.275–76 / pdf 105). Ordinary understanding, which can only "perceive and grasp what lies straight in front of it" and calls movement-in-a-straight-line "progress," finds this circularity objectionable and reads it as a sign of impossibility. Heidegger reclaims the circle: "The essential feature of the circular movement of philosophy does not lie in running around the periphery … It lies in that view of the centre that this circular course alone can provide" (p.187 / GA pp.276 / pdf 105). The centre — "the middle and ground" — discloses itself only *in and for* the circling. Dialectic, the recurrent attempt to level off this circularity and ambiguity, is "only the expression of an embarrassment."

The long middle of §45 (pp.188–191) develops the metaphysics/science relation as a "matter of fate" (*Geschick*), not an organized operation. Biology's contemporary struggle is "to restore autonomy to 'life'" against "the tyranny of physics and chemistry"; *life cannot in principle be grasped* from within physico-chemical perspectives (p.188 / GA pp.278 / pdf 106). The slogans — "vitalism," "the teleological perspective of life," "the struggle against mechanism" — name the right negative tendency but are "burdened with misunderstandings as great as those that beset the mechanistic conception of life" (p.188 / GA pp.278 / pdf 106). Crucially, metaphysics and positive research are *not* "two consecutive phases of a production process," the one "supplying the facts and the latter providing the fundamental concepts" (p.189 / GA pp.279 / pdf 106) — a phrase Heidegger returns to twice as the very picture to be refused. He closes the digression by re-placing the thesis "between the other two, which assert that the stone is worldless and that man is world-forming" (p.192 / GA pp.282 / pdf 108).

## §2. Poverty-in-world vs world-formation; no hierarchy; poverty as deprivation (§46, p.192)

§46 first lets the *seemingly* obvious reading stand, then dismantles it. Set beside world-formation, "poor in world implies poverty as opposed to richness; poverty implies less as opposed to more" (p.192 / GA pp.284 / pdf 108). The bee "has its hive, its cells, the blossoms it seeks out"; its world "is limited to a specific domain and is strictly circumscribed," as are the worlds of frog and chaffinch. Even the *manner* of penetration is limited: the worker bee knows the blossom's colour and scent "but it does not know the stamens of these blossoms as stamens" (p.193 / GA pp.285 / pdf 108). Man's world, by contrast, is richer, "constantly extendable not only in its range … but also in respect to the manner in which we can penetrate ever more deeply" — "this is why we speak of man as world-forming" (p.193 / GA pp.285 / pdf 108). On this reading the distinction is one "of degree in terms of levels of completeness with respect to the accessibility of beings," and world "initially signifies the sum total of beings accessible to man or animals alike" (p.193 / GA pp.285–86 / pdf 108). The reading is "so obvious that there is no need to discuss it any further" — which is exactly Heidegger's warning sign. It is "suspiciously self-evident."

Here enters the unit's first major correction: the comparison "allows no evaluative ranking or assessment with respect to completeness or incompleteness" (p.194 / GA pp.286 / pdf 109). The falcon's eye, the canine nose, outstrip ours; "man can sink lower than any animal" — "no animal can become depraved in the same way as man." Within the animal realm too it is "a fundamental mistake to suppose that amoebae or infusoria are more imperfect or incomplete animals than elephants or apes"; "every animal and every species of animal as such is just as perfect and complete as any other" (p.194 / GA pp.286 / pdf 109). Poverty-in-world and world-formation "must not be taken as a hierarchical evaluation."

The second and pivotal correction is the recasting of *poverty itself*. Drawing on the German, Heidegger separates the weak comparative sense of "poor" (the "poor flow of water in a stream") from the proper sense heard through *Mut* (mood): "being poor means being deprived [*Entbehren*]" (p.195 / GA pp.287 / pdf 109). Deprivation admits of different ways "depending on how whatever is poor … comports itself in its deprivation, how it responds … how it takes this deprivation" — i.e., the way "in which it is in a mood [*zu Mute*] — poverty in mood [*Ar-mut*]" (p.195 / GA pp.287 / pdf 109), by analogy with *schwermutig* (melancholy) and *demutig* (humility). Poverty is thus "not merely a characteristic property, but the very way in which man comports and bears himself" — and as a *Zumutesein* it is the "preeminent kind of having in which we seem not to have" (p.195 / GA pp.287–88 / pdf 109). The consequence for the thesis is explicit: the poverty in question "does not express a purely quantitative difference," and therefore in both expressions "the term 'world' itself cannot express quantity, sum total, or degree with respect to the accessibility of beings" (p.195 / GA pp.288 / pdf 109). This dismantles the §46 self-evident reading from within: world is not a quantity of which the animal has less.

## §3. Poverty-in-world vs worldlessness; world as the accessibility of beings (§47, p.196)

With poverty defined as deprivation, the thesis can be paraphrased: "the animal is deprived of world," "the animal has no world" — which "also helps to define our second thesis in relation to the third … For man does have a world" (p.196 / GA pp.289 / pdf 110). But this threatens to collapse the animal into the stone: "Neither the stone nor the animal has world." Heidegger insists the not-having "is not to be understood in the same sense in each case" (p.196 / GA pp.289 / pdf 110). The distinction: "Poverty in world implies a deprivation of world. Worldlessness … is constitutive of the stone in the sense that the stone cannot even be deprived" of world at all (p.196 / GA pp.290 / pdf 110). Mere not-having is insufficient; "the possibility of being deprived of world requires further conditions."

Then the provisional definition that anchors the whole unit: "Let us provisionally define world as those beings which are in each case accessible and may be dealt with" — accessible such "that dealing with such beings is possible or necessary for the kind of being pertaining to a particular being" (p.196 / GA pp.289 / pdf 110). The stone illustrates the lower limit by privation. It "lies upon the earth but does not touch it" in the strong sense; the earth "is not given for the stone as an underlying support … let alone given as earth" (p.196 / GA pp.290 / pdf 110). Cast into meadow, ditch, or water, "everything present around it remains essentially inaccessible to the stone itself." Because it has "no possible access to anything else around it … it cannot possibly be said to be deprived of anything either" (p.197 / GA pp.290 / pdf 110). Hence the formal definition: "The worldlessness of a being can now be defined as its having no access to those beings (as beings) amongst which this particular being … is" (p.197 / GA pp.290 / pdf 110). And the anti-privative twist: "It is beside the point to regard the fact that the stone has no access as some kind of lack" — rather, "having no access is precisely what makes possible its specific kind of being," the realm "of physical and material nature and the laws governing it" (p.197 / GA pp.290 / pdf 110).

The lizard then marks the contrast with the stone (pp.197–198). It "has sought out this stone and is accustomed to doing so"; removed, "it starts looking for its stone again" (p.197 / GA pp.290–91 / pdf 110). One must guard against "'empathetically' projecting our own feelings"; the rock is not given to the lizard "as rock," the sun not "as sun" such that it could ask astrophysics of it — yet the lizard does not "merely crop up as present at hand beside the rock" the way the nearby stone does (p.198 / GA pp.291 / pdf 111). The famous device: "we ought to cross out the word 'rock'" to indicate that what the lizard lies on "is certainly given in some way for the lizard, and yet is not known to the lizard as a rock" — not that it is *another* thing, but that "whatever it is is not accessible to it as a being" (p.198 / GA pp.291 / pdf 111). Likewise "the blade of grass that the beetle crawls up … is not a blade of grass for it at all"; it is "simply a beetle-path on which the beetle specifically seeks beetle-nourishment" (p.198 / GA pp.292 / pdf 111). Every animal has "a specific set of relationships to its sources of nourishment, its prey, its enemies, its sexual mates," and maintains itself in a specific element; it is "confined to its environmental world, immured as it were within a fixed sphere that is incapable of further expansion or contraction" (p.198 / GA pp.292 / pdf 111) — the controlled (Uexkull-derived) *Umwelt* claim. The closing pressure of §47: "if we understand world as the accessibility of beings, how can we possibly claim that the animal is poor in world" — "especially if poverty implies being deprived — when it is obvious that the animal does have access of some kind?" (p.198 / GA pp.291–92 / pdf 111).

## §4. The sense in which the animal has and does not have world (§48, p.199)

§48 draws the comparative examination to its productive impasse. The three theses are clarified, but the guiding problem — "the question concerning the concept of world" — has only "become more acute" (p.199 / GA pp.294 / pdf 111). The contradiction is stated squarely: if world is "beings in their accessibility in each case," and "being a living being means having access to other beings, then the animal stands on the side of man." But if "poverty represents deprivation and deprivation in turn means not having something, then the animal stands on the side of the stone." So "the animal thus reveals itself as a being which both has and does not have world. This is contradictory and thus logically impossible" (p.199 / GA pp.292–94 / pdf 111). Heidegger refuses to dissolve the contradiction by retreat to common logic: "metaphysics and everything essential has a logic quite different from that of sound common understanding" (p.199 / GA pp.294 / pdf 111). If the having/not-having propositions are legitimate, "then we must be employing the ideas of world and accessibility of beings in a different sense in each case" — which is to say "the concept of world has still not been clarified" (p.199 / GA pp.294 / pdf 111).

The yield is methodological-positional rather than doctrinal: "we have found the place where such elucidation must begin and have identified the knot which we must first strive to undo" (p.199 / GA pp.294 / pdf 111). The animal's intermediate position is where "the two extremes of worldlessness and world-formation are, as it were, intertwined with one another"; only by solving this "in an original fashion" will it become visible "what world means" (p.199 / GA pp.294 / pdf 111). The unit ends by refusing two false simplifications recorded on p.200: the animal's world "is not simply a degree or species of the world of man," nor is the animal "a being that is simply present at hand"; "its specific manner of being is defined by the fact that it has access of some kind" (p.200 / GA pp.294 / pdf 112). The next question — what the animal relates to, and how — opens Chapter Four's organism analysis via the methodological question of transposition (*sich versetzen*) into another being.

## §5. Synthesis: the controlling result

The unit's load-bearing achievement is a single equation reached by triple contrast: **world = the accessibility of beings as beings.** The stone is *weltlos* because it has no access at all (and so cannot even be deprived); the animal is *weltarm* because its access is a confined, deprivational openness (it has and does not have world); man is *weltbildend* because his access is extendable in range and in depth. Two corrections make the result non-trivial. First, the comparison is *not hierarchical*: completeness-talk fails (man can sink below any animal; every species is complete as such). Second, poverty is *not quantitative*: being-poor is being-deprived (*Entbehren*), a mode of comportment, so "world" in the thesis names neither sum-total nor degree. The animal therefore cannot be slotted on a single scale between stone and man; it occupies a genuinely intermediate manner of being whose elucidation requires a new (organism) analysis rather than a ranking.

## §6. Bridge relevance

The equation **world = accessibility of beings as beings** is FCM's most compact statement of *world-as-disclosedness*, and it is significant for the boredom spine and the downstream pipeline precisely because it is reached by *graded openness*. Poverty-as-deprivation supplies a structural model directly transferable to the attunement analysis: just as the animal's poverty is not an absence of world (a mere "less") but a determinate mode of being-held within a restricted disclosure, so profound boredom is not a mere absence of stimulation but a determinate attunement in which beings-as-a-whole withdraw while still being disclosed *as* withdrawing. The accessibility/disclosure axis is thus the conceptual hinge between the animal chapter and the boredom chapters: both make "having world / being disclosed to beings" a matter of *how* beings are made accessible, not *how many*. For the empirical bridge, this licenses treating a "boring" stimulus condition as a mode of constrained disclosure (a graded openness to be interpreted) rather than as a stimulus deficit to be measured on a single intensity scale — the same anti-quantitative move Heidegger executes here against the "sum total of accessible beings" reading.
