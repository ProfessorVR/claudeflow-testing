# FCM Unit fcm-11 — The Essence of the Organism / Animality (Part Two, Ch. Four, §§49–57)

**Work:** Heidegger, *The Fundamental Concepts of Metaphysics: World, Finitude, Solitude* (GA 29/30), trans. McNeill & Walker, Indiana UP 1995.
**Coverage:** Part Two, Chapter Four, §§49–57 — book pp.201–235 / GA [294–343] / pdf 112–129.
**Locus form:** `(p.NN / GA pp.NN / pdf NN)`, where `pdf = floor((book_page+24)/2)` and GA is read from the running heads.
**Mode:** condensed "map" unit. Boredom content is minimal and indirect; this chapter is the *structural background* to captivation (*Benommenheit*).

## §1. The chapter's place in the boredom spine

Chapter Four interrupts the comparative march toward "world." Before the animal's *poverty in world* (*Weltarmut*) can be filled in, Heidegger must establish what an *animal* and an *organism* are. The detour is not incidental: the self-regulating, instinct-governed, world-poor organism worked out here is the ontological substrate on which the *next* chapter erects *captivation*. For the boredom spine the relevance is contrastive — the human Dasein that profound boredom discloses (held in an attunement that *opens* world and its emptiness) is sharpened by setting it against a living being held in *capacity without world*. Heidegger flags the destination at the very end: the organismic character "points back toward a more originary structure of animality" to be reached by a regressive analysis `(p.235 / GA pp.342-43 / pdf 129)`.

## §2. §49 — *Sichversetzen*: the method that is a substance-question

The chapter opens with the methodological question of whether we can "transpose ourselves" (*sich versetzen*) into another being — animal, stone, or man `(p.201 / GA pp.294 / pdf 112)`. Heidegger's first move is to deny that this is "merely" methodological: "this methodological question is a substantive one," because what is at issue *is* accessibility itself — the specific manner of being that "permit[s], resist[s], or possibly forbid[s]" self-transposition `(p.202 / GA pp.296 / pdf 113)`.

*Sichversetzen* is carefully cleared of its false senses. It is neither a factical transference of one being into the interior of another, nor a substitution, nor — the widespread philosophical error — a mere thought-experiment, an "as-if" in which "we merely act as if we were the other being" `(p.203 / GA pp.299 / pdf 113)`. The decisive *positive* moment is the opposite: it "consists precisely in we ourselves being precisely ourselves, and only in this way first bringing about the possibility" of going-along-with the other while it remains other `(p.204 / GA pp.300 / pdf 114)`. Heidegger glosses the better word as *going-along-with* and explicitly retires "empathy" (*Einfuehlung*), which smuggles in the picture of our being "outside" in the first place — a "guiding thread for a whole range of fundamentally mistaken theories" `(p.203 / GA pp.298 / pdf 113)`.

The three questions then receive asymmetric answers. Transposing into the **animal** is *in principle* possible for man; only its factical realization is questionable `(p.205 / GA pp.300 / pdf 114)`. Transposing into the **stone** is impossible in principle: the stone "offers no sphere intrinsically belonging to its being" `(p.204 / GA pp.299 / pdf 114)`. Transposing into another **human being** is a *superfluous*, even "nonsensical," question, "because it is fundamentally redundant" — being human already means *being-with*, being "transposed into other human beings" as a structure of Dasein `(p.205 / GA pp.301 / pdf 114)`. An excursus (pp.207–208) traces the contrary picture — man as isolated subject/consciousness — to Descartes, its Kantian transformation, and Hegel's absolutization into absolute idealism, with a cross-reference to *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* on finitude `(p.207 / GA pp.303 / pdf 115)`.

## §3. §50 — Poverty as not-having-yet-able-to-have

§50 converts the transposition result into the *deprivation* thesis. The domestic animal is the worked example: the dog "lives" with us, "is with us," goes up the stairs with us — "a transposedness, and yet not" `(p.210 / GA pp.307 / pdf 117)`. The decisive formula: "Only where there is a having do we find a not-having. And not-having in being able to have is precisely deprivation, is poverty" `(p.211 / GA pp.307 / pdf 117)`. The animal "intrinsically displays a sphere of transposability" — indeed "the animal itself is this sphere" — "one which nonetheless refuses any going along with" `(p.211 / GA pp.309 / pdf 117)`. So the animal "both has something and does not have something." Crucially, this poverty cannot be "conjure[d] up… from the formal concept of deprivation": "We can grasp this poverty only if we first know what world is" `(p.211 / GA pp.309 / pdf 117)`. That deferral is what licenses the whole organism-analysis: to say what the animal *lacks*, we must first characterize animality positively.

## §4. §51 — Organ ≠ instrument, organism ≠ machine

§51 launches the positive clarification by contesting the dominant picture. "The word 'organ' derives from the Greek *organon* or 'instrument'. The Greek word *ergon* is the same as the German word *Werk*" `(p.212 / GA pp.310 / pdf 118)`. Wilhelm **Roux** had accordingly "defined the organism as a complex of instruments" `(p.212 / GA pp.310 / pdf 118)`. This triggers a cascade of distinctions among *equipment* (*Zeug*), *instrument* (*Werkzeug*), and *machine* (*Maschine*): not every piece of equipment is an instrument (a fountain-pen, a sledge); "every machine is a piece of equipment although not every piece of equipment is a machine" `(p.215 / GA pp.314 / pdf 119)`; and the machine-character lies not in complexity but in "the autonomous functioning of a structure designed for specific dynamic operations" `(p.215 / GA pp.315 / pdf 119)`. All equipment is an *Erzeugnis* — a product of human *world-formation* (with a cross-reference to *Being and Time* §15ff) `(p.214 / GA pp.313 / pdf 118)`.

The verdict: if the organism constitutes living beings as such, then Roux's "complex of instruments" "must certainly collapse altogether," and we likewise cannot follow **von Uexkuell** in calling the machine "an 'imperfect organism'" `(p.215 / GA pp.315 / pdf 119)`. Subsection (b) extends the critique to the *mechanistic conception of vital movement*: the living being "presents itself as something in motion," and "the organism reveals itself as a bundle of reflex arcs" — but representing the animal as such "does nothing to prove that we have… grasped it as an organism at all" `(p.216 / GA pp.317 / pdf 118)`. The summary names the task: "organs are not merely instruments and… the organism is not merely a machine" `(p.217 / GA pp.318 / pdf 118)`.

## §5. §52 — Capacity (*Faehigkeit*) vs readiness (*Fertigkeit*)

§52 isolates the difference at the level of *serviceability itself*. The eye "is for seeing"; this "is not some arbitrary property… but is the essence of the eye" `(p.218 / GA pp.320 / pdf 121)`. The hinge question: "Can the animal see because it has eyes, or does it have eyes because it can see?" — answer: "Only because it can see… Possessing eyes and being able to see are not the same thing" `(p.219 / GA pp.320 / pdf 121)`. Equipment "has a certain readiness" (*Fertigkeit*); the organ "has a capacity" (*Faehigkeit*). The pen "is ready for writing, but it has no capacity for writing" `(p.221 / GA pp.322 / pdf 122)`. Heidegger then tightens the screw: "An eye taken independently is not an eye at all"; it "is never first an instrument which subsequently also gets incorporated into something else"; rather "the eye belongs to the organism and emerges from the organism" `(p.221 / GA pp.323 / pdf 122)`. Hence the inversion that governs the rest of the chapter: "It is not the organ which has a capacity but the organism which has capacities… It is the capability which procures organs for itself" `(p.221 / GA pp.323 / pdf 122)`. Organs "grow from it and are absorbed into it, they remain within it and come to an end with it."

## §6. §53 — Subservience (*Diensthaftigkeit*) and the temporality of the organ

§53 names the organ's distinctive belonging: "the capacity takes the organ into its service." Where the finished product is *serviceable for…*, "the organ which arises in and through the capacity is subservient." "*Serviceability* [*Dienlichkeit*] and *subservience* [*Diensthaftigkeit*] are not the same" `(p.226 / GA pp.330 / pdf 124)`. The capacity itself "must… possess an originarily subservient character." The contrast with equipment is driven home: the finished hammer "lies outside the possible act of hammering"; "the being of the hammer is not an urge toward hammering" `(p.226 / GA pp.330 / pdf 124)`. The eye, belonging to a capacity, subserves seeing "only because the capacity is intrinsically subservient."

A **temporal** argument anchors the distinction empirically (TEMPORAL-STRUCTURE). The unicellular amoeba forms *temporary organs*: around the food "an aperture which first becomes a mouth, then a stomach, then an intestine and finally an anal tract" (citing von Uexkuell, *Theoretische Biologie* 1928, p.98) `(p.224 / GA pp.327 / pdf 124)`. "It is the mouth which emerges first and then the intestine, not the intestine first and then the mouth" — which "conclusively shows that the capacities for feeding and for digesting are prior to the organs" `(p.224 / GA pp.328 / pdf 124)`. "Organ and equipment relate precisely to time in fundamentally different ways," and "the temporal aspect is metaphysically central for each manner of being" `(p.225 / GA pp.328 / pdf 124)`. Organs are bound not to an objective lifespan but "into… the temporal span which the animal is capable of sustaining as a living being."

## §7. §§54–55 — Self-driving drive and the achievement of the organ

§54 supplies the *motor* of capacity: drive (*Trieb*). Whereas equipment is governed by an external *prescription* derived from its production-plan, "something which is capable… is intrinsically regulative and regulates itself"; "it drives itself toward its own capability for…" `(p.228 / GA pp.332 / pdf 126)`. "Capacity is only to be found where there is drive" `(p.228 / GA pp.333 / pdf 126)`. Drives (*Antriebe*) "always permeate and drive on the whole movement in advance," so they are "never simply mechanical, even if they can be isolated in this way"; indeed "there is no mathematical expression for this instinctual structure of drives" `(p.229 / GA pp.334 / pdf 126)`. Throughout, "we must completely set aside any idea of consciousness or soul, as well as the idea of purposiveness" `(p.229 / GA pp.334 / pdf 126)`.

§55 then warns against reading the *capacity* off the *organ's achievement*. The worked example is an "insect eye… approached through a most remarkable experiment": a photographed retinal image of a glow-worm shows a window with mullion and transom, "a large letter R" on the pane, even the church tower beyond `(p.231 / GA pp.337 / pdf 127)`. "But can we infer from this what the glow worm sees? By no means" — "we cannot at all determine the capacity of vision" from the organ's achievement `(p.231 / GA pp.337 / pdf 127)`. Achievement is intelligible only *from* the prior capacity, never the reverse; animal seeing "is a capacity, whereas our potentiality to see… possesses a quite different manner of being" `(p.231 / GA pp.337 / pdf 127)`.

## §8. §§56–57 — Proper peculiarity and the holistic organism

§56 turns to the *self*-character. One cannot infer "from the organ as the known term to the organism as the unknown term," since the organ already *belongs to* the organism; the organism has been "in view all the time" `(p.232 / GA pp.338 / pdf 127)`. Here Heidegger credits **Husserl**: the questioning that takes its stand "before all argumentation… was brought back to life — and that always means radicalized — through Husserl," "the decisive characteristic of phenomenology" `(p.232 / GA pp.338 / pdf 127)`. The capacity's *self*-character is then defined austerely: "in this instinctual 'toward', the capacity as such becomes and remains proper to itself … and does so without any so-called self-consciousness or any reflection at all" `(p.233 / GA pp.339 / pdf 128)`. "Self" and selfhood are *reserved* for human peculiarity; the animal's mode is *Eigentum* (proper being), as the kingdom (*Koenigtum*) belongs to the king `(p.233 / GA pp.339 / pdf 128)`. A translator's footnote unpacks *Eigentum* / *eigentuemlich* / *Eigentuemlichkeit* and their link to *eigentlich* ("authentic" in *Being and Time*) `(p.233 / GA pp.339 / pdf 128)`. This retention-within-itself "represents a higher determination metaphysically speaking," not a deficiency relative to independent equipment `(p.228 / GA pp.332 / pdf 126)`.

§57 states the chapter's culminating formula. The animal "never merely displays a single capacity but rather several" — assimilation, growth, propulsion, struggle — and the point is "the specific nature of the unity in this multiplicity" `(p.234 / GA pp.340 / pdf 128)`. "The organ is not equipped with capacities but rather the capacities create organs for themselves" `(p.234 / GA pp.340 / pdf 128)`. To say "the animal is organized" means "the animal is rendered capable [*befaehigt*]… Being organized means being capable" `(p.235 / GA pp.342 / pdf 129)`. The verdict: the organism is "neither 'a complex of instruments', nor a union of organs, nor indeed a bundle of capacities"; "organism" "designates a particular and fundamental manner of being… a proper peculiarity with the capability to create organs" `(p.235 / GA pp.342 / pdf 129)`. Finally, against the schema of possibility/actuality: "it is just this actuality of the actual animal that we are trying to determine"; "being capable… belongs to the animal's being actual, to the essence of life" `(p.235 / GA pp.342-43 / pdf 129)`.

## §9. Boredom relevance (structural background)

There is no direct boredom analysis in §§49–57. Its presence is structural: this chapter constructs the *animal's* manner of being — captivated within a self-driving, world-poor circuit of capacity that is *proper to itself without selfhood* — precisely the term against which captivation (next chapter) and, behind it, the human openness disclosed in profound boredom acquire their contour.
