## 2. Working-Note Material Richer Than A

### The P1-P5 Propositions (C pp. 16-18)

C contains a propositional analysis of emotion-phantasia-movement that is significantly richer than A's narrative treatment:

| Prop | Content | Status in A |
|------|---------|-------------|
| P1 | Emotion is a form of motion in the soul | Present (sec. 1.5.1, "emotion is motion") |
| P2 | All motion involves a mover and a moved | Implicit but not propositionally stated |
| P3 | Phantasia provides the "mover" for emotional motion by presenting the object "as" something | Present (sec. 1.5.2) but not in propositional form |
| P4 | The "moved" is the soul insofar as it is disposed to be affected | Implicit in A's pathos discussion |
| P5 | Emotional response = phantasia (mover) + pathetic disposition (moved) + kinesis (motion) | Not explicitly stated in A |

**Assessment**: A's narrative prose is more readable, but C's propositional structure is more rigorous. Consider adding P5 (or a version of it) to A as a formal statement of the phantasia-emotion mechanism.

### The Gibsonian Framework (C pp. 16-18)

> "Perception is for action; it is not the construction of a world-model but the detection of possibilities for movement"

**Absent from A entirely**. This Gibsonian ecological perception framework maps well onto the phantasia account:
- Phantasia doesn't construct a "world-model" — it presents affordances (appearances "as" something actionable)
- The T-stage sequence (especially T4→T5→T6) IS Gibson's perception-for-action loop
- The outfielder example is implicitly Gibsonian but A doesn't make the connection

**Assessment**: The Gibsonian connection is a missed opportunity. It would strengthen the argument that phantasia is not merely "imagination" (passive, representational) but an active, action-oriented faculty.

### Aisthesis/Perception Distinction (C pp. 16-18)

> "Aristotle doesn't seem to distinguish between aisthesis, direct sensation, and perception proper"

**Absent from A**. This observation is important: if Aristotle blurs the sensation/perception boundary, then the T1→T2 transition is more complex than A presents. Is T1 sensation or perception? Is T2 perception or something else?

**Assessment**: This unresolved question in C was likely sidestepped rather than resolved in A. The author may have deliberately simplified by treating aisthesis as a single process.
