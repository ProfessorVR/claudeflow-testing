## 1. Theoretical Foundation

### 1.1 Core Premise
Calleja argues that the experience of gameplay cannot be adequately captured by vague concepts like "fun" or singular notions of "immersion." Instead, player involvement is a **multidimensional, multi-phase** phenomenon that can be analytically decomposed into six interacting dimensions, each operating across two temporal phases. The model rejects the notion that involvement is a monolithic state, proposing instead that it "will emerge from the combination of these forms of involvement" (Ch 3, p35).

### 1.2 Against Immersion/Presence
The PIM is developed as a corrective to four problems with existing immersion/presence frameworks:
1. **Involvement vs. Transportation**: Most frameworks conflate being absorbed *in* an activity (involvement) with feeling transported *to* another place (presence/immersion-as-transportation). These are distinct phenomena.
2. **Medium Specificity**: The concept must be specific to ergodic media (digital games), not borrowed wholesale from film or literature theory.
3. **Technological Determinism**: The framework must avoid assuming technology alone creates the experience — player agency and disposition are co-constitutive.
4. **Multidimensionality**: The experience is not singular but comprises multiple interacting dimensions that combine differently across games and players.

### 1.3 Ergodicity as Prerequisite
Drawing on Aarseth, Calleja grounds his model in **ergodicity**: "nontrivial effort" required to traverse a text/environment. Game involvement is indicated "not simply by the direct input of the player or the display of such an action on the screen, but by the player's cognitive effort, which is not necessarily registered as a form of input" (Ch 3, p42). This includes planning, readiness to act, and strategic thinking — not just button presses.

## 2. The Six Dimensions

The PIM identifies six dimensions of involvement. These are **layered and transparent**: "one dimension influences how another is perceived and interacted with. The dimensions are transparent in that their layering does not occlude what lies beneath, but changes the perception of both" (Ch 3, p38). Not all dimensions are equally relevant to every game; the model's power lies in its ability to map differential intensities.

### 2.1 Kinesthetic Involvement
**Definition**: Engagement with all modes of avatar or game piece control, "ranging from learning controls to the fluency of internalized movement" (Ch 3, p43).

**Macro Phase (Agency)**: The player's anticipation of and disposition toward the control scheme. Awareness of what actions are possible. Interest in the type of agency the game affords (first-person shooter vs. strategy vs. puzzle). Pre-play expectations about control freedom and responsiveness.

**Micro Phase (Control)**: Moment-to-moment engagement with input mechanics. This dimension "requires more conscious attention when the controls make themselves present, either because the player hasn't fully mastered them or because a situation demands a complex sequence of actions" (Ch 3, p43). As mastery develops, controls recede from consciousness — the transition from *learning* to *fluency* is the kinesthetic equivalent of internalization.

**Analytical Questions**:
- How does the control scheme announce itself to the player? When does it become transparent?
- What is the learning curve, and how does it map onto the game's difficulty progression?
- Does the game exploit the gap between conscious and internalized control (e.g., through sudden difficulty spikes, new mechanics, or control disruptions)?
- How much freedom of action does the control scheme afford?

### 2.2 Spatial Involvement
**Definition**: "Players' engagement with the spatial qualities of a virtual environment in terms of spatial control, navigation, and exploration. It accounts for the process of internalizing game spaces that is a powerful factor in engaging players and giving them the sense that they are inhabiting a place, rather than merely perceiving a representation of space" (Ch 3, p43).

**Macro Phase (Exploration Disposition)**: The player's desire to explore, their prior knowledge of the game world, anticipation of spatial discovery. Interest in the type of environments the game promises.

**Micro Phase (Spatial Internalization)**: "As cognitive maps of game environments improve, the player's spatial disposition to them shifts from the conceptual to the inhabited. As the lay of the land is memorized, less investment of conscious attention is required to orient oneself in the virtual environment" (Ch 5, p87). The process of forming **cognitive maps** (Tolman, 1948) — "encoding, storing, and manipulating experienced and sensed information that can be spatially referenced" — is central to this dimension. The critical transition is from navigating space *conceptually* (using maps, waypoints) to navigating *intuitively* (landmark-based, embodied orientation).

**Analytical Questions**:
- Does the game present space as navigable environment or as conceptual map? Can the player get meaningfully lost?
- How does spatial knowledge accumulate? Is the world revealed progressively or all at once?
- Does the game create a sense of "worldness" — spatial contingency where the environment feels like it extends beyond what is immediately visible?
- How does spatial mastery affect other dimensions (e.g., does knowing the map change narrative or ludic engagement)?

### 2.3 Shared Involvement
**Definition**: "Engagement derived from players' awareness of and interaction with other agents in a game environment. These agents can be human- or computer-controlled, and the interactions can be thought of in terms of cohabitation, cooperation, and competition" (Ch 3, p43).

**Macro Phase (Community/Reputation)**: Players' sense of belonging to a community, reputation accumulated across sessions, guild politics, social obligations. "In MMOGs, characters exist over an extended period of time... Consequently, players accumulate a reputation, positive or negative, among other players on the same server" (Ch 6, p103). This extends outside the game itself — forum participation, strategy discussion, social bonds.

**Micro Phase (In-Game Social Interaction)**: Moment-to-moment awareness of and interaction with other entities. Three sub-modes:
- **Cohabitation**: Simply being aware that other agents populate the world, whether human or AI. "It somehow makes the experience more immersive/intense knowing that there's a real person behind the avatar" (Ch 6, p101).
- **Cooperation**: Working with others toward shared goals. "The cooperative aspect of shared involvement seems to become more engaging the greater the number of people working together" (Ch 6, p107). The satisfaction of successful teamwork becomes a powerful attractor.
- **Competition**: Adversarial interaction ranging from casual PvP to organized clan/tournament play. Shifts perception of skill in relation to others.

**Analytical Questions**:
- Does the game acknowledge the player's presence within a social field? How?
- What forms of shared involvement does the game afford — cohabitation, cooperation, competition, or some combination?
- How does the game's social architecture shape reputation, obligation, and community formation?
- Do AI agents generate meaningful shared involvement, or is it primarily a multiplayer phenomenon in this game?

### 2.4 Narrative Involvement
**Definition**: "Engagement with story elements that have been written into a game as well as those that emerge from the player's interaction with the game" (Ch 7, p133).

**Two Narrative Modes** (critical distinction):
- **Scripted Narrative**: Pre-authored story elements designed by developers — cutscenes, dialogue, environmental storytelling, audio logs, quest text. These exist independently of any particular player's actions.
- **Alterbiography**: "The story generated by the moment-to-moment actions within the game environment" — the player's personal history of actions, decisions, encounters, and consequences. This is unique to each playthrough and emerges from the interaction between player agency and game systems.

**Macro Phase (Narrative Disposition)**: Interest in the game's story, anticipation of narrative developments, reflection on narrative events between sessions.

**Micro Phase (Narrative Unfolding)**: The interweaving of scripted narrative and alterbiography during play. "If the player cares about the scripted narrative enough to absorb it into his alterbiography, narrative involvement is deepened" (Ch 7, p133). The key analytical move is tracking how the two narrative modes interact — does the scripted narrative create context for the alterbiography, or do they run in parallel?

**Analytical Questions**:
- What is the ratio and relationship between scripted narrative and alterbiography in this game?
- Does the game's narrative architecture enable the player to absorb scripted elements into their personal alterbiography?
- How are narrative elements delivered — through interruption (cutscenes) or through environmental/emergent channels?
- Can the game generate meaningful narrative involvement purely through alterbiography (emergent narrative), or does it depend on scripted content?

### 2.5 Affective Involvement
**Definition**: Engagement with the emotional and aesthetic dimensions of the game experience — atmosphere, mood, visual style, sound design, and the emotional responses these generate.

**Macro Phase (Aesthetic Disposition/Taste)**: The player's pre-existing preferences for genres, visual styles, settings, and emotional registers. "Players look for different sorts of affective experiences in games: the pleasure of aesthetically beautiful and peaceful places... the appeal of visual styles borrowed from other popular media... or the exhilaration brought on by the startling effects of horror games" (Ch 8, p146). This is the dimension most shaped by the player's broader cultural and aesthetic sensibilities.

**Micro Phase (In-Game Affect)**: Moment-to-moment emotional and aesthetic responses during play. This includes:
- **Atmosphere/Mood**: The ambient emotional tonality created by environment, sound, lighting, and pace.
- **Aesthetic Reward**: Beauty, spectacle, or stylistic distinction as intrinsically involving. "A game like Flower explicitly uses aesthetic beauty to reward players: when a sequence of flowers is completed, the surroundings burst into color" (Ch 9, p165).
- **Rhetorical Strategies of Affect**: How the game uses "the aesthetic and systemic dimensions" together to create emotional impact (Ch 8, p146). Horror games exemplify this through the combination of dark spaces, impending danger, and collaborative pressure.

**Analytical Questions**:
- What is the game's dominant affective register? How does it modulate across the experience?
- Does the game use affective involvement as reward, motivation, or atmosphere?
- How do aesthetic and systemic dimensions combine to produce emotional impact?
- Does the game accommodate different affective dispositions, or does it target a specific emotional register?

### 2.6 Ludic Involvement
**Definition**: "Players' engagement with the choices made in the game and the repercussions of those choices" (Ch 9, p165). This is the dimension most closely tied to games as *systems* — the structural mechanics of goals, rules, challenges, and consequences.

**Macro Phase (Life Goals vs. Game Goals)**: The player's broader motivations for playing — competitive ambition, completionist drive, social status, skill development. Calleja distinguishes between goals that emerge from the game's design and goals that players bring from their life context.

**Micro Phase (Plans, Goals, Rewards)**: "Game designer Sid Meier described games as 'a set of interesting choices'... Ludic involvement in the micro phase concerns engagement with the decision-making process and the perceived consequences of such actions" (Ch 9, p156). The core loop: **planning → execution → feedback → replanning**. "Seasoned game players understand that well-balanced game systems emphasize the opportunity cost of any particular action taken. Without repercussions, actions lose their meaning" (Ch 3, p44).

**Analytical Questions**:
- What is the game's decision architecture? How are choices presented, and what are their consequences?
- Does the game emphasize opportunity cost — does choosing one path meaningfully preclude others?
- What is the feedback loop between action and consequence? Is it immediate, delayed, or cumulative?
- How does the game balance challenge and reward across its difficulty curve?
- Does the ludic system create emergent complexity beyond what individual rules prescribe?

## 3. The Two Phases: Macro and Micro

### 3.1 Macro Phase (Outside Gameplay)
The macro phase encompasses all involvement that occurs **outside the moment of gameplay**: anticipation, reflection, planning, discussion, community participation, and disposition formation. "There are a number of factors that shape the player's opinion and disposition toward the game that derive from thoughts, plans, feelings, and expectations both prior to and following the game experience" (Ch 3, p39). Examples:
- Discussing strategies on forums
- Planning a character build between sessions
- Feeling attachment to a guild community
- Anticipating narrative developments
- Reflecting on in-game events

### 3.2 Micro Phase (During Gameplay)
The micro phase is the moment-to-moment experience of play itself — the real-time interaction with the game's systems, spaces, agents, and narratives. Each dimension operates simultaneously during micro-phase involvement, with attentional resources distributed across them dynamically.

### 3.3 The Macro-Micro Transition
A key analytical insight: each dimension has a characteristic **internalization trajectory** from macro to micro. For spatial involvement, this is the shift from conceptual to inhabited space. For kinesthetic involvement, it is the shift from conscious control to fluent embodiment. For narrative involvement, it is the absorption of scripted narrative into alterbiography. Tracking these internalization trajectories across dimensions reveals how a game builds (or fails to build) deep involvement over time.

## 4. Incorporation: The Culminating Experience

### 4.1 Definition
Incorporation is Calleja's replacement for immersion/presence. It operates on a **double axis**:

> "Incorporation thus operates on a double axis: the player incorporates (in the sense of internalizing or assimilating) the game environment into consciousness while simultaneously being incorporated through the avatar into that environment. The simultaneous occurrence of these two processes is a necessary condition for the experience of incorporation." (Ch 10, p169)

Formally: **"the absorption of a virtual environment into consciousness, yielding a sense of habitation, which is supported by the systemically upheld embodiment of the player in a single location, as represented by the avatar"** (Ch 10, p169).

### 4.2 Requirements for Incorporation
1. The medium must **acknowledge the player's presence** within the virtual world.
2. The medium must offer the player **agency** within that world.
3. The player must be **embodied** in a single systemic location (typically via avatar).
4. Multiple dimensions of involvement must be operating at sufficient intensity to generate the composite experience.

### 4.3 Incorporation vs. Immersion
- **Immersion** implies a here/there dichotomy — the player *leaves* reality and *enters* a virtual world. This misrepresents the phenomenology.
- **Incorporation** treats virtual and physical as "both aspects of what we perceive as real" — "domains continuous with the media-saturated reality of everyday life" (Ch 10, p183). The virtual environment is assimilated into consciousness as an extension of immediate surroundings, not a replacement for them.

### 5.2 Dimensional Interaction Analysis
Map how dimensions **interact and influence each other**. Key questions:
- Which dimensions are tightly coupled in this game? (e.g., spatial + narrative in open-world RPGs; kinesthetic + ludic in fighting games)
- Does intensity in one dimension compensate for weakness in another?
- Are there moments where dimensional interaction produces emergent involvement not reducible to any single dimension?
- Do certain dimensions serve as prerequisites for others? (e.g., kinesthetic fluency as prerequisite for ludic engagement in action games)

### 5.3 Incorporation Assessment
Evaluate whether the game affords incorporation:
- Does the game acknowledge the player's presence within a single location?
- Is the player embodied via avatar in a navigable spatial environment?
- Do multiple dimensions reach sufficient intensity simultaneously to generate the composite experience?
- Does the game maintain continuity between virtual and physical experience, or does it enforce a here/there split?

### 5.4 Temporal Analysis
Track how involvement evolves across the **lifecycle of play**:
- **First encounter**: Which dimensions dominate? What is the entry point?
- **Learning phase**: How do internalization trajectories develop? When do controls become transparent? When does space become inhabited?
- **Mastery phase**: Which dimensions sustain long-term engagement? How does the macro-micro relationship stabilize?
- **Community phase** (for multiplayer): How does shared involvement interact with other dimensions over extended play?

### 5.5 Analyzing User Notes
When applying this framework to notes provided about a specific game experience:
1. **Identify which dimensions** the notes foreground (explicitly or implicitly).
2. **Classify observations** as macro or micro phase.
3. **Track the scripted/alterbiography distinction** in any narrative observations.
4. **Note dimensional interactions** — where do notes describe experiences that span multiple dimensions?
5. **Assess incorporation markers** — do the notes describe habitation, embodiment, or assimilation of the game world into consciousness?
6. **Identify gaps** — which dimensions are absent from the notes? This may indicate either low relevance or unexamined aspects of the experience.
