\section*{5. \textit{Red Dead Redemption 2} (§1: The Tutorial)}

Where \textit{Gnomes \& Goblins} withholds instruction and lets the player discover, \textit{Red Dead Redemption 2} withholds freedom and scripts the discovery — and the inversion is the whole difference between the two poles of the medium. The virtual-reality world trusts the player to find meaning in an open surround, the candle and the goblin and the fallen fruit each yielding only to a hand that works at them and none of it explained. The screen-based world takes the opposite wager, placing the player in a prepared corridor and running a full curriculum through it, mechanics and ethics alike, before the world opens at all. The rails are the tutorial — not its scaffolding and not its constraint, but its argument.

That argument is built upon a \textit{hexis} (\gk{ἕξις}), or rather upon two of them, woven from the first interactive clip so tightly that to pull one loose is to unravel the other. The first is a \textit{technē}-hexis of the craftsman-outlaw — horsemanship, gunplay, the tracker's eye, and the heist-man's deliberate hand — installed across three hours of structured play and then left to run untutored. The second is an ethical hexis of the desiring, dwelling, gang-loyal, morally-scored agent — laid down in Dutch's grief-speech before the controller arrives, deepened by camp labor and morning greetings, calibrated by the honor machine across five consecutive resolutions in a single clip, and finally spoken aloud by the man himself in a half-lit confession to one he has just spared and just threatened. Neither hexis precedes the other; they are not two programs run in sequence but one bundle cultivated at once, each conditioning what the other can become.

Three times across that cultivation the governing desire — the \textit{orekton} (\gk{ὀρεκτόν}) that orients the whole — is re-pitched to higher ground. Blizzard-survival names it first: warmth, food, and shelter for the gang, the bare needful goods against a storm that is as much curriculum as weather. Standing-and-belonging names it second: being of use inside Dutch's world, earning one's place, mattering to the people of Horseshoe Overlook. The outlaw economy names it third: the fence-unlock that turns a one-off robbery into a standing livelihood, the gang's prosperity made the shared object of desire. Each pitch carries the same hexis-bundle forward and adds to it a layer the next will draw upon, so that the player who begins as a patient in a cutscene — an unnamed body in a blizzard, led by the reins — is the very player who, hours later, acts in silence with a bow drawn on legendary prey and needs no narrator to name what is happening. It begins, before any mechanic, with a name.

\bigskip

\subsection*{The Opening Cutscene: Identity Before Embodiment}

The chapter opens not on a player but on a spectator: for nearly six minutes the controller does nothing, the game is a film, and the player watches. This is the tutorial's first lesson, and it names itself as no lesson at all — for before the first mechanic is installed, before a horse is ridden or a rifle raised, the cutscene builds the ethical horizon within which every mechanic will afterward be used, planting a \textit{hexis} of gang-loyalty as the orienting good before the player has had any occasion to act upon it. What the section establishes is the 2D pole's founding reversal: on the flat screen, identity is given before embodiment, the name before the deed.

The opening frames set the condition of patiency. The Van der Linde gang flees through the Grizzlies in a blizzard — on horseback, on foot, carrying its wounded — and the player watches without acting, made patient by the medium's own conditions. This is \textit{bia} (\gk{βία}) in its softest and most total form, not the sudden seizure of an ambush but the structural withholding the cinematic form itself enacts. And it arrives where the data is thinnest, for what the blizzard-screen delivers — white static, wind, indistinct shapes laboring through snow — is very nearly nothing. Near-zero data is where the high-\textit{phantasia}-load pole announces itself most loudly: everything the player registers of this world, the felt cold and the gang's desperation and the depth of the ravine they cross, is supplied not by the screen but by the player's own image-making (\textit{phantasia}, \gk{φαντασία}) from the backlog of prior perception. The screen primes; the player fills.

Into that primed reservoir Dutch van der Linde speaks. Between 04:11 and 05:01, in a speech the gang does not interrupt, he names the dead — ``Davy… Jenny…'' — grieves them, and rallies the living: ``Stay with me. We ain't done yet'' (clip 1, 05:18). What the speech installs, before the player has moved a thumb, is the ethical hexis of gang-loyalty: the disposition to hold the gang's survival as the governing good, to count its losses as losses that matter, and to follow Dutch because Dutch is the one still standing and still steering. ``There was nothing more you could have done'' (clip 1, 03:42) — the exculpatory frame opens here and will run the length of the campaign, attributing every loss to circumstance rather than to any agent's failure, to the storm and the Blackwater catastrophe and a world arrayed against the gang. This is the Burkean scene–act ratio running for the first time, the scene-term absorbing the moral weight that might otherwise land on the agent; the player is handed the lens before ever being handed its object.

Dutch's charged face, the gathered gang behind him, the grief and the rally together compose the \textit{phantasma} that will orient every loyalty-\textit{doxa} the player forms across the game's duration — and they compose it by the high-phantasia-load method in its purest form. On a 2D screen a speech is exactly where the data-discrepancy is total, flat image and audio with no body to enact anything, and exactly where phantasia-load runs to its maximum: Dutch's authority, the gang's need, the whole moral economy of survival are supplied from the player's own image-making in response to a voice and a face on glass. The master phantasma of Dutch-as-father-of-the-cause is built entirely so, and built before the player has done anything at all.

One detail seals the reading. At 05:47 Dutch says ``We've got some work to do'' and the controller becomes active — but Arthur has already been named, already addressed as ``the only one I can rely on to stay strong right now'' (clip 1, 01:13). On the 2D pole identity precedes embodiment: the player is told who he is before he is given anything to do, and where a head-mounted world would place a body in space and leave it to orient itself from the ground of sensory presence, the flat screen gives the name first and the body second. Arthur exists before the player inhabits him; the ethical horizon exists before the mechanic does. The off-screen Blackwater catastrophe drives the point home — ``What really went down back there on that boat?'' / ``We missed you. That's what happened'' (clip 1, 06:00) — for the originating event, the job that killed Davy and Jenny, is never shown, and \textit{phantasia} must constitute it entire from dialogue and tone and the grief inhabiting Dutch's face. Maximal data-discrepancy is here a narrative method: the game withholds the origin and conscripts the player to supply it, so that he begins already inside a crisis, already mourning people he never met, already indebted to a man whose speech he has merely watched. The controller arrives second.

\subsection*{The Leash: What the Blizzard Hides}

The clip that follows the cutscene runs twenty-nine seconds, and it is the tutorial's most important negative space — important not for what it does but for what it withholds, and for what the withholding installs. What the section establishes is that on the 2D pole the very thinness of the image, the medium's standing handicap, can be turned into an instrument of concealment.

The player rides out of Colter in a column with the gang, into a screen that is near-total white. ``This goddamn weather,'' a companion says; ``Been two days or more like this now'' (clip 2, 00:00). The data-discrepancy is here at its maximum for a flat screen, the visual field reduced to a blizzard that is nearly nothing, so that depth and terrain and the cold weight of the air and the gang's silhouettes resolving from the white must all be supplied by \textit{phantasia} from the player's own reservoir of hard mornings. Where a head-mounted display would deliver stereoscopic depth, the vestibular confirmation of a body in space, and the heft of cold through haptics, the 2D screen gives white static and wind. The world is maximally phantasia-borne, the 2D pole's standing condition carried to its extreme.

At 00:11 the player strays from the scripted trail, and the world declines to yield. ``I think you're going the wrong way'' — the sentence falls, companion and NPC and the voice of the designed path at once, and the player is turned back. No wall is visible, for the blizzard has hidden it; no instruction is offered, for the world simply will not give way to the off-track vector. The designed scene exerts what Burke called the recalcitrance of the situation, an ambient resistance that, as Rickert reads it, belongs to the material environment and not only to the persuading speaker (Rickert, \textit{Ambient Rhetoric}, 254). It is also a soft \textit{bia}, a micro-inversion of agency by which the agent is made briefly patient to the scene's insistence on its own path. No force is applied and no cutscene strips control; the companion's line stands in for the invisible wall, and the player re-corrects.

What the blizzard does for the leash is precisely what the leash cannot do for itself: it conceals the corral. Where virtual reality would betray a hard boundary by stereoscopic depth — the wall seen as a wall, the horizon held at a distance the body cannot ignore — the 2D blizzard withholds the geometry entire. The high-phantasia-load that is the pole's standing challenge becomes its instrument of concealment, since a boundary that cannot be seen can be read as the world's weather rather than the design's constraint. The medium's very limitation, its inability to render the depth that would reveal the wall, is what enables the tutorial's first sleight of hand.

What settles across these twenty-nine seconds is not a skill. No A₄ completes, nothing is finished, the \textit{orekton} is posited but not satisfied. What settles is something more basic and more lasting — the \textit{hexis} of being-led, the disposition of path-adherence, the trained readiness to stay with the column and go where the column goes. It is a privative deposit, not a positive capacity but a negative one, defining the horizon's edges by enforcing them and so making the later open world legible as release: the player must feel the leash to feel the moment it is loosed, and the blizzard, in hiding the wall, makes that lesson more effective than any visible boundary could.

\subsection*{The Colter Corridor: A Curriculum Under Fire}

After the leash comes the corridor, and the corridor makes the chapter's widest claim — that \textit{Red Dead Redemption 2}'s tutorial installs not one \textit{hexis} but two, not in sequence but together, in the same clip and the same thirty-eight minutes of on-rails play. The Colter sequence threads a full control curriculum — mounted movement, free-look, combat, inventory, melee grapple, horse-handling, tracking, Eagle Eye, weapon-selection — through a narrative that requires each mechanic to justify itself as a story-need, so that no menu announces itself and every skill is introduced by the world demanding it. But the curriculum carries a shadow curriculum, and the shadow is ethical: alongside each skill the player learns, the world deposits a disposition. (The clip's interleaved fork, capture 3a, is the choke/spare beat that occurs within this same in-game session but was beatmapped separately. It returns at the section's close as the curriculum's diagnostic zero.)

The corridor opens with movement, and with movement's constraint. ``Stay close. We'll do our best to stick to the trail,'' a companion says; ``Careful over this bridge here'' (clip 3, 00:28–00:30). Companion patter scripts every footfall, and navigational agency belongs to the gang and not the player. The leash of clip 2 returns now in narrative form, the only way being Dutch's way, and the \textit{hexis} of being-led deepens through story where before it deepened through rebuff. At 04:17 the camera opens — Dutch places Arthur in the cattle shed and the player finds the first mechanic of the gaze, the free-look, the 2D surrogate for the turned head — and where a head-mounted player turns to look with the whole body, here the look is a stick-driven symbolic act, the whole phantasia-load of ``being there'' resting on a camera's movement through a flat frame.

The first combat arrives at 07:08 with a recognition and an eruption — ``It's goddamn Dutch Van der Linde!'' / ``Shit! Look out! There's more of the bastards!'' — and the O'Driscoll ambush routes embodiment through \textit{phantasia} rather than through a headset. The involuntary reflex at the on-screen muzzle-flash, the body pulling back from a threat on a flat surface, is the 2D pole's defining register of embodiment, the felt body of danger substituting for the spatial surround the headset would supply. It fires here for the first time. The armed O'Driscoll becomes the \textit{phantasma} charged with threat; the committal \textit{doxa} (\gk{δόξα}) — \textit{this is an enemy, to be met with force} — forms and concretizes; the shot closes the chain. So the \textit{technē}-hexis of combat seeds itself not by instruction but by an attack the player must survive — which is already the section's lesson in miniature, that the skill and the disposition are installed in one motion.

Inventory arrives under looting's cover. ``Turn the place upside down. Grab as many supplies as you can'' (clip 3, 09:00), and the satchel-UI becomes the tutorial's first pure data-discrepancy beat, the act of rifling a dead man's home — the texture of wood, the smell of blood, the weight of a tin — thinned to a menu of icons and a text prompt. And inside the looting a single line catches and will not be cleared away: ``Poor bastard was married too'' (clip 3, 13:52). It is no mechanic but a moral residue the Burkean scene-term must absorb and paper over; the scene absorbs it, and the line remains.

At 14:52 the barn grapple reverses the combat's structure, and the player is seized: an O'Driscoll lunges from behind, control is momentarily stripped, and the player is made patient by the world rather than active in it — \textit{bia} suffered, the agent-to-patient inversion in its acute form, recovered only by counter-input. Then, within thirty seconds, its mirror, as the prisoner is beaten for information (clip 3, 15:30): \textit{bia} inflicted, the player now the agent of force. Both poles of the \textit{bia}-arc fall inside a single half-minute, neither scored by the honor machine and neither a deliberated act, the kinesthetic grammar of violence installed at the tutorial's speed.

What follows is where the dual-hexis claim becomes most precise. Dutch hands Arthur lethal discretion — ``Do what you want with him. I don't care. But bring that horse when you're done'' (clip 3, 16:00) — the prisoner begs, and Arthur releases him: ``Get the hell outta here! Go!'' This is the first \textit{prohairesis} (\gk{προαίρεσις}) the player makes in the game, a freely chosen mercy over a fully realized patient of \textit{bia}, a man on his knees asking to live. And the honor machine scores the act and attributes it to the agent — not to the scene, not to Dutch's permission, but to Arthur's character. The \textit{technē}-hexis and the ethical hexis are thus installed in the same clip, the same half-hour, the same breathing sequence of mechanics: the player who learns to shoot at 07:08 is the player who learns to spare at 16:00, and the curriculum is dual from its outset.

Sadie Adler's rescue follows at 18:00, where Micah manhandles a hidden woman — ``Get away from me!'' — and Arthur and Dutch intervene: ``Leave her alone. She ain't one of them'' (clip 3, 18:00). Micah's \textit{bia} against Sadie is met by Arthur's agency-restoring force, and the gang's self-understanding arrives in a single line — ``We're bad men. We ain't them'' — so that the ethical hexis of in-group distinction, defined not against a positive ideal but against the O'Driscolls, is planted in the space between that line and the deed it names. Horse-handling, tracking, Eagle Eye, and the weapon-wheel arrive between 17:00 and 29:00 under the same narrative cover, each mechanic a story-need. And Eagle Eye, toggled at 24:00, is the tutorial's first explicit perception-prosthesis, a vision-mode supplying in highlighted tracks and scat what a trained tracker's gaze would otherwise hold as bodily knowledge without a UI. The 2D screen cannot deliver the trained eye, so it grants a filter that stands in for the eye. At the canyon traversal (29:00–31:00) the companion patter tightens the leash once more — ``It's slippery. Be careful. / Stay close to the wall. / Get up here'' — and soft \textit{bia} returns as the sustained scripting of every footfall. Then, at 33:00, the wolves: A₂→A₃→A₄ on repeat under time-pressure, the lunging animal as \textit{phantasma}, the snap \textit{doxa} (\textit{threat, kill it}), the shot — and the flinch firing again, sharper now, because the wolves lunge and the screen can place the body nowhere but here. ``Good work, Arthur'' (clip 3, 38:10): the act attributed to the agent's competence, the \textit{technē}-hexis of combat-under-duress named and affirmed.

Against this whole sequence the choke/spare fork stands as the tutorial's diagnostic zero. In the interleaved beat (capture 3a) Dutch hands Arthur the same lethal discretion, and this time the honor machine scores nothing: a choke and a spare are mechanically identical, and the only phenomenal residue of a life-or-death act is the tolling of a bell (clip 3a, 11:31–11:45). Set beside the prisoner release, where mercy was scored and credited to the agent, this fork is machine-inert — the most intimate and most morally charged choice in the tutorial falling silent — and what the bell does is exactly what the meter declines to do: it carries the moral weight aesthetically, affectively, without attribution. So the honor machine's selectivity is established from the outset. The witnessed, publicly legible mercy scores; the intimate maximal choice, made in private with a pleading man and no witnesses, leaves only a bell-toll and a silence.

\subsection*{The Hunt: A Desire Made Legible}

Against the corridor's density and pace the hunting tutorial is something rarer — a chain that Charles narrates aloud, stage by stage, so that the actualization of desire can be watched as it happens. The clip is useful not because the chain requires narration to run, for it runs whether or not anyone speaks it, but because Charles verbalizes each stage and so makes the apparatus's operation, everywhere else smuggled under story, unusually legible. That legibility is the section's reason for being.

The chain begins before any animal appears, at the horizon. ``A few cans of food and a rabbit… for 10, 12 people?'' (clip 4, 00:58) — the scarcity is designed, and the design is plain: the camp cannot be fed on what it has, and so someone must go. The \textit{orekton} — food, survival, the gang provisioned against the storm — is installed before the hunt begins, so that the kill will read, from the moment it is made possible, as provision and not as the agent's bloodlust. The motive is pre-assigned, the act licensed in advance, coded as a scene-and-purpose deed the honor machine will not need to stir for. Charles hands the bow at 02:24 — ``You take this. I can't use it and you'll have to'' — and the weapon specifies the \textit{technē}: a silent kill, the bow chosen because the gun would scatter the prey. They climb the mountain, Charles reads the terrain aloud, and the scarcity-horizon follows them up into the trees.

At 06:10 the trained perception the analysis calls A₁, the uptake of the world's offered residues, is modeled by the tutor before the player performs it: ``There's deer been here. Recently. How can you tell? / How can you not?'' The Eagle Eye vision-mode is toggled, the screen's track-highlights supplying what a trained hunter's gaze would otherwise hold as body-knowledge without a UI — the 2D pole's perception-prosthesis at its most explicit, since the tracker does not develop a tracker's eye over years in a single session but presses a button and the game paints the ground. ``It's easier in the snow, but once you get your eye in, you'll be able to track them nearly as well in grass and woods'' (clip 4, 06:28): the \textit{hexis} is named as portable, a disposition that will carry from terrain to terrain, the game framing aloud the very thing it is cultivating.

``Good tension on the string before releasing. Just don't overdo it. Now, Arthur'' (clip 4, 07:52). The bow draws, the tension-indicator runs, the reticle steadies, the arrow flies. In four seconds the full chain closes — A₂ (the deer as \textit{phantasma} charged with the \textit{orekton} of the kill), A₃ (the \textit{doxa}: \textit{this is the shot, take it}), A₄ (the kill). The felt body of the drawn bow — the tension across the back, the steadied shoulder, the held breath — is entirely \textit{phantasia}-supplied behind a button-hold and an analog stick. The mechanic abstracts the act, \textit{phantasia} supplies its body, and four seconds of screen-time close what three minutes of narrated preparation had opened. ``Let's try for another'' (clip 4, 08:04): the loop is set to repeat, because repetition is how a \textit{hexis} sediments.

``Easier when they ain't shooting back'' (clip 4, 12:35) is a throwaway line and also the clip's own placement of itself on the phantasia-load axis, for the hunting tutorial is the low-flinch end of the spectrum. The deer does not charge, no lunging threat arrives, the flinch does not fire. The embodiment here is quiet and accumulated rather than sudden and involuntary, the patient building of a disposition by repetition rather than the acute registration of danger — and the contrast with the wolf-attack, and with the bear still to come, is precisely the axis between the low-data-discrepancy end and the high. The carcasses go to Pearson at 19:19, the kill dissolving into the camp economy and the A₄ act depositing its residue into the shared A₀; the \textit{orekton} was always the camp's, merely routed through Arthur's hand. And then the tutor names the \textit{hexis} outright: ``Takes a lifetime of practice to master'' (clip 4, 19:09) — not a skill-set but a time-built disposition, not what one can do after thirty minutes with a bow but what one becomes after a lifetime of using one. The bow-clip is the moment the tutorial says aloud what, everywhere else, it only does.

\subsection*{The Heist: An Acting-Together}

Against the blizzard-corridor and the solitary hunt, the Cornwall train robbery is the tutorial's collective event — the first time the full actualization of desire draws not on the player's solitary hand but on the gang's distributed agency, so that what is achieved cannot be credited to Arthur alone. Here Burke's consubstantiation becomes literal — ``a way of life is an acting-together'' (Burke, \textit{Rhetoric}, 21), and the gang's way of life is enacted in the shared act — and here, too, the second A₀, no longer survival but belonging, no longer the bare needful good but the earned standing, is planted before it is named. What the section establishes is that the ethical hexis has a collective register the solitary clips could not reach: the ``we'' that can itself source a deed.

The sequence opens at Colter, already past the blizzard horizon, with Dutch sublimating Colm O'Driscoll's personal revenge into collective provision: ``much less fun to rob him if he never finds out about it'' (clip 5, 06:21). The re-attribution is Burke's act–purpose ratio run deliberately, the act reframed from attitude (revenge) to purpose (plunder for the camp) so that the honor machine may score it without penalty. The approach, the cover-positioning, and the firefight through the lake camp are each a \textit{technē}-beat, each a phase of a planned collective act in which the gang moves together and Arthur is one part of the moving whole. The Cornwall job is the fullest \textit{technē}-lesson after the Colter corridor: Arthur kneels and unspools detonator-wire across frozen ground, the manual handling abstracted to a button-sequence while \textit{phantasia} supplies the tension of the fuse and the cold of the metal (clip 5, 40:01). The detonator misfires — Bill's error, scored to the agent, ``you're pathetic'' (clip 5, 41:00) — and the consequence is immediate: board the moving train or lose the score. The leap to the train (clip 5, 42:42) is the clip's flinch-peak and its highest phantasia-load moment, the rush of ground beneath the car and the teetering body and the slip — ``I'm slipping! Pull me up!'' — all supplied by \textit{phantasia} behind a stick and a button that perform the grab, for on a flat screen the vertigo of a moving platform is wholly image-borne, there being no vestibular organ to feel the sway. The flinch fires anyway.

At the private car (clip 5, 51:00) Dutch hands Arthur a hostage choice — ``Kill them, leave them here, take them with you — just make sure they don't send no folk after us'' — and the honor machine scores this fork, the playthrough taking the non-lethal path, marching the workers down the train and keeping them silent without killing. \textit{Prohairesis} again: the deliberated act that finds the option the ethical hexis can live with. But here, unlike the bell-toll choke/spare, the honor meter responds, the act is attributed, the agent is scored — and the difference tells, for the machine sees what it is built to calibrate: not private mercy toward a begging man but publicly coherent restraint under Dutch's command. Attribution, that is, is not equal to visibility; it is equal to what the machine is designed to recognize.

``Proud of you boys. Not a man down. Outlaws for life'' (clip 5, 25:29): the ethical hexis of in-group fidelity is named as a creed, not described but affirmed, the gang's shared substance offered up as an identity, the ``outlaw for life'' as the kind-of-thing-one-is that carries across acts. And the Kieran Duffy episode (clip 5, 27:25) deposits that creed's dark underside, the player now the agent of the lasso and the drag and the ``I will break every bone in your body'' addressed to a man on his knees — \textit{bia} inflicted, agent upon patient, and no honor penalty, because the O'Driscoll is an enemy of the camp. The honor machine calibrates to in-group and out-group; the creed names the line; Kieran is the line's proof. Horseshoe Overlook arrives at 65:00 with the full A₀ re-pitch carried in four words — ``This place is perfect'' — and the home-base shifts and the \textit{orekton} shifts with it, from survival to prosperity, from Dutch's grief in the blizzard to Dutch's sparkling eyes over a valley he means to hold. ``The camp gets its slice'' (clip 5, 65:40) is the new governing horizon, the residue of the heist depositing into a new A₀, and the tutorial passes into its second phase.

\subsection*{The Camp: Dwelling Before Desire}

There is a kind of being-in-a-world that has no goal — not the absence of desire but its satiation, a phase in which the \textit{orekton} is simply to \textit{stay} and to \textit{be here}. The Horseshoe Overlook chore clip is this rest, a dwelling within the A₀ the heist deposited: the player grinds no quest, but chops wood, drinks coffee, and is hailed by name. What the section establishes is that a \textit{hexis} can be deposited by sheer repetition, without any act the meter would score — that membership is laid down not in deeds but in showing up.

``Morning, Mr. Morgan'' (clip 6, 00:00). The greeting is the shared-channel belonging-engine, registering not what Arthur does but that the camp \textit{knows} him, knows him by name and acknowledges his standing at the day's first light. Burke's motive-attribution machine is here at its quietest, no scored act and no honor delta, only the agent-term pressed by a companion's bark. It is recognition of standing, not credit for a deed — and standing is exactly what the ethical hexis of belonging is, not the act but the deposit of prior acts, the accumulated residue a gang hails when it says the agent's name at dawn. The coffee runs A₂→A₄ in three seconds: the pot at the fire becomes a \textit{phantasma} of warmth, the \textit{doxa} forms — \textit{this is my morning} — the button is pressed, the animation runs. And the warmth of the cup, the bitterness, the radiated heat are supplied wholly by \textit{phantasia} across the screen's data-discrepancy, the felt body at rest and at home furnished by image-making from the backlog of every morning the player has ever spent before a fire. The woodpile runs the same arc at a slower register, the axe swinging and the log splitting and the contribution ticking, and the game barely scores it — no honor pulse, no dramatic residue, only work done for the camp, labor as membership rather than as scored act.

It is here that Calleja's apparatus earns its place most plainly, for the channel-profile of camp-dwelling is unlike any combat beat the tutorial has run. The spatial, kinesthetic, and affective channels are dominant — the player moving through a bounded place, doing the things the place asks, and being seen to do them (Calleja, \textit{In-Game}, 36–38) — while the ludic channel, the engagement with consequential choice, falls to near-zero, no goal pressing and nothing won or lost. ``Good work, Mr. Morgan,'' should the greeting fire mid-chore, is the agent-term once more, character recognized not from a single act but from the pattern of showing up. Across the long ambient loops no new \textit{orekton} opens. The clip cultivates not a desire but a disposition, the readiness to be present and to do the labor that dwelling requires and to receive the greeting that proves one belongs — the \textit{hexis} of membership, depositing quietly and by repetition alone, since one chore deposits nothing and a hundred chores deposit everything. The blizzard-survival horizon has closed; the standing-and-belonging horizon is now the ground.

Heidegger's second form of boredom bears directly on this stretch, and on that dominant channel-profile — not the boredom of waiting for something but the being-bored-\textit{with} a situation one nonetheless remains in, the being-held in an empty time that yet discloses the world as a whole (Heidegger, \textit{Fundamental Concepts}, 120–124). The looping ``Morning, Mr. Morgan,'' the chore-prompt, the woodpile, the fire: this is exactly that texture, the camp as a world one is bored-with and yet does not leave, because remaining is itself the disposition the tutorial is building. The second-form boredom is not the analysis's target but the phenomenal register in which the \textit{hexis} of dwelling sediments — the ludic channel near silent, the spatial and affective channels carrying the whole.

\subsection*{The Town: Honor as Motive-Attribution Machine}

If the camp is where the ethical \textit{hexis} is cultivated quietly, the Valentine trip is where the calibration of the honor machine is put on open display, and where its design-assumptions are brought to the surface. Within thirty minutes the player is handed five consecutive honor-positive events, and in the spaces between them Arthur twice disclaims the very virtue the machine has just credited him — and these two movements, the scoring and the disclaiming, are what the section is built to set against each other.

The first event opens the sequence: a stranger, feigning lumbago, needs his horse retrieved, and Arthur retrieves it and returns it, the meter bumping. ``You're a gentleman, sir.'' And Arthur: ``No, not really. I was just trying to impress the women'' (clip 7, 07:45). This is the Burkean scene–agent ratio made explicit by the agent himself, the honorable act credited by the machine to his character and re-credited by the character to an audience — the women the scene-condition without which he would have robbed the man. The machine scores what it sees; Arthur reports what moved him; and neither attribution is false, nor is either complete. Then, a beat later: ``If you lot hadn't been here, I probably would have robbed them'' (clip 7, 08:40). Uncle supplies the scene-attribution in the very form the honor machine elides, for the machine cannot read the subjunctive — the robbery that did not happen because the witnesses were watching — and so scores only the act that did, and scores it to the agent. The dispositions on display are not identical to the scores that track them: this is the clip's first payload, that honor-attribution and moral truth diverge, and that the character names the divergence for the player while the machine files its report.

Tilly's grab (clip 7, 23:00), Karen's beating (clip 7, 24:16), and Jimmy's dangling plea (clip 7, 28:38) are three \textit{bia} events, three de-inversions, three honor-gains, the structure rhythmic — patient to rescuer, each act coded to the agent's standing. But the acts themselves are mixed: intervening for Tilly is protective and uncalculated, a scene-reflex. Beating the john in the hotel room is violent in excess of the need, Arthur striking him ``a lot harder'' than he hit Karen, as Karen reports afterward (clip 7, 26:50). The honor machine scores both alike, because it cannot read degree. The motive-attribution pentad has a slot for act and a slot for agent, and what falls into each is fixed by the geometry of the scene and not by the player's conscience.

Jimmy Brooks is the clip's keystone. The player has chased him down, lassoed him from horseback, and hauled him to a ledge — all \textit{bia} inflicted, the player the agent of force against a begging man — and then at 30:00 Arthur hauls him up rather than dropping him, and the meter rewards it. The speech that follows is the tutorial's most complete pentad in a single breath: ``I'm not a good man, Jimmy Brooks… I was in Blackwater. I kill people. And maybe I should have killed you… I never saw you. We have an understanding'' (clip 7, 30:00) — scene (Blackwater, the fugitive life), agent (Arthur naming himself a killer), act (spare and intimidate), purpose (the gang's safety), attitude (mercy edged with menace). The honor machine scores the spare as positive, and the player's conscience must supply the weight of the coercion that surrounds it. One terminal is moral credit, the other an asymmetric power relation enforced by the threat of death, and the machine scores the one and is silent on the other. At 32:10 Arthur returns the commandeered horse — ``You really were just borrowing it. Appreciate it'' — and the loop closes. The ethical \textit{hexis} of these thirty minutes is not clean virtue but its approximation under particular scene-conditions, witnesses and women and a fugitive who can name the gang. Strip those conditions away, and Arthur is his own narrator's confession, ``I probably would have robbed them.'' What the machine deposits is a score. What the chapter cultivates is what the score can only approximate — the disposition to act honorably when the scene calls for it, tracked by a machine that cannot read the gap between the disposition and its measure.

\subsection*{The Legendary Hunt: The \textit{Technē} at Ceiling}

Where the hunting tutorial was legible because Charles narrated every stage — ``get your eye in,'' ``good tension on the string,'' ``let's try for another'' — the legendary hunt is diagnostic precisely because it is silent. Its transcript is nothing but the Whisper model's ``you,'' repeated every thirty seconds for twenty-five minutes. No tutor speaks, no stage is named, and the \textit{hexis} seeded under Charles's narration is now enacted without it — the absence of narration being the very mark that the disposition has settled. What the section establishes is what a mature \textit{hexis} looks like from the outside: not instruction, only enacted competence.

The player whistles for the horse and the horse comes. Eagle Eye is toggled and the quarry resolves from the residue. Dead Eye is drawn and time slows and the painted marks land — and none of it is taught here, for all of it was taught in the corridor and the bow-clip and the heist and has since sedimented, the act now running untutored. The horse-bond contributes what the hunting tutorial did not, a care toward an animal that sits between \textit{technē} and ethics: the grooming and feeding of the mount (clip 9, 05:00–07:30) build no toward-the-hunt capability, yet the bond they cultivate is what lets the horse respond at ceiling — the smooth gait, the obeyed rein, the re-attuned spook. The bond is the ethical hinge the honor machine never scores, because the care-act is private and unnested and received only by an animal. Yet it modulates the \textit{technē} throughout, the hunter's ground-cover and the mounted traversal both running differently on a bonded horse, the unscored act depositing into the scored performance.

The bear charges (clip 9, 11:00): the image lunges at the screen, and the player's body pulls back. This is the pure specimen the analysis has been building toward — the involuntary recoil, embodiment as flinch, the felt body on a flat screen supplied by \textit{phantasia} with no headset in the circuit. The bear does not move toward the player, for there is no spatial ``toward''; the flat image advances within its frame, and still the player ducks. Here is the 2D pole's deepest claim: that \textit{phantasia} scales to the gap, supplying what the medium withholds, and that at the moment of a charging animal it supplies \textit{enough} to move the body — not as much as a headset predator would, which would place the charge in space and deliver it vestibularly, but enough to fire the recoil through a screen. Against the bear, the legendary hunt itself (clip 9, 16:30–22:30) stacks three \textit{phantasia}-prostheses the pole cannot do without. The first is Eagle Eye, the tracking-vision filter that supplies in perception-highlight what the trained body would hold as a gaze. The second is the legendary aura, the world-tint that marks the quarry's range as special, supplying in UI-desaturation what the experienced hunter might hold as a felt strangeness. The third is Dead Eye, the time-slow, composure rendered as mechanic, supplying in slowed frames what the practiced shooter holds as steadied attention. Three eidetic and affective states that a body in a headset might begin to hold proprioceptively are here, on the flat screen, converted each to a filter, a tint, or a slowdown — the 2D pole's instruments of compensation made visible all at once in a single encounter. The legendary pelt goes onto the horse, the trophy-\textit{orekton} taken and the mastery-\textit{orekton} consummated, and the clip ends in silence: no one names what happened, no ``Good work, Arthur,'' no ``takes a lifetime of practice to master.'' The \textit{technē}-hexis at ceiling is its own testimony.

\subsection*{The Drunk Night: Loosened Agency}

Against the hunt's concentrated mastery, the drunk night with Lenny puts a different claim on the table — that \textit{bia} need not be violent to be analytically productive, and that the 2D pole's highest \textit{phantasia}-load is not the predator's charge but the corrupted screen. A caveat is owed at the outset: this clip's transcript was wholly irrecoverable, rated in its beatmap as ``100\% whisper hallucination'' with no coherent English dialogue, and so its mechanics — the drunk-cam and the brawl-arc described below — are analytically inferred from the clip's role and framing, pending frame-verification, rather than grounded in a transcript as its siblings are.

The herbalist challenge opens the clip — Eagle Eye scanning the field for herbs, the hold-to-pick animation returning the foraging competence the hunting clips installed — a \textit{technē}-hexis of collection, thin and quiet, A₀→A₄ in a slow loop, the herb a \textit{phantasma} charged with the completionist \textit{orekton}, the \textit{doxa} ``this counts,'' the pick, the counter's tick. The game paints the ground with highlights, the player reads the painting, and the camp-provision grows by increment, nothing rupturing and nothing flinching. Then come the saloon, and Lenny, and the drinks. Intoxication in \textit{Red Dead Redemption 2} is rendered not through the avatar but through the display: the screen blurs, sways, doubles, and trails — a drunk-cam applied not to the character's eyes but to the player's own viewport — so that the sober player must process a drunk image. This is the pole's most explicit gambit, for the felt body of drunkenness, the heaviness and the narrowed field and the unreliable coordination, cannot be delivered through a flat display by any other means. The game delivers instead the \textit{appearance} of intoxication to the player's perception and relies on \textit{phantasia} to supply the rest, high data-discrepancy translated now not into perceptual thinness but into perceptual distortion — the gap no longer between screen and world but between the sober eye and the drunk image it is made to read.

What loosens is agency. The controls fight back, the camera drifts, aim and movement degrade to stumbling — \textit{bia} in its softest and most consensual register, not the O'Driscoll ambush stripping control without warning but a gradual, player-initiated, willingly undertaken cession of mastery. The \textit{orekton} is oblivion and companionship. The player drinks because drinking is the act the world is soliciting, because Lenny is there and the night is warm and the camp has just proven itself. And the \textit{hexis} cultivated here is neither combat-readiness nor the hunter's competence but the male solidarity of shared misrule — camaraderie as \textit{hexis}, belonging as letting-go. Burke's pentad raises a question the scene leaves open: when the drunken antics accrue — the stumbling, the brawling, the saloon commotion incited — to which term does the honor machine attribute them, to the agent (Arthur's character) or to the agency (the drink, the scene)? The transcript can give no answer, and the attribution can be settled only from frames. But the question is itself the point, for the machine's calibration, which the Valentine clip displayed in its positive direction, is here exposed on its negative side — a machine that cannot read the scene-condition of intoxication must either score the agent or ignore the act, and whichever it does tells us whose motive it was built to track. The morning returns Arthur to sobriety and the camp to its routines, the Lenny bond deepened and another A₄ deposited into the ethical-hexis bundle. But the pole has shown what neither camp nor town could — that \textit{phantasia} may be conscripted by corrupting the image as readily as by withholding the data, that the high-load end of the spectrum holds the drunk-cam no less than the bear-charge, and that the felt body of altered consciousness is, on a flat screen, entirely the player's own supply.

\subsection*{The Disposition Built}

Three hours of tutorial; three re-pitchings of the governing desire; one dual hexis-bundle deposited across combat and camp-chores and heist and solitary hunt and a drunk saloon night with a friend. What the tutorial has built is neither a skill-set nor a personality but a \textit{paideia} (\gk{παιδεία}) — a standing readiness to act in certain ways in certain kinds of situation, cultivated by repetition until it runs without instruction. The \textit{technē}-hexis runs untutored in the legendary hunt because it was tutored in the corridor and the bow-clip and the heist and has sedimented. The ethical hexis runs across the camp and the town and the drunk night without any explicit lesson in virtue because Dutch's grief-speech seeded the orienting good and each subsequent resolution has deepened its furrow. Neither hexis was set by a curriculum; the tutorial designed a world, and the world cultivated the disposition.

This is what the RODA analysis discloses when it is run across the whole tutorial arc: not a series of discrete events but a protracted hexis-bundle, a single compound disposition deposited incrementally, each clip adding a layer the next draws upon, the whole intelligible only from its end looking back. The opening cutscene names an Arthur before the player can act; the legendary hunt shows what that Arthur has become once the player has acted for three hours. Between those poles lies the tutorial, a \textit{paideia} on rails, building the disposition that will run the rest of the game. The two strands are by now one bundle — the \textit{technē}-hexis that draws a bow in silence and the ethical hexis that spares a begging man under the eyes of witnesses — and the bundle is the prepared agent the open world will inherit.

It was built, moreover, by the route the flat screen makes necessary, and that route is the chapter's contribution to the differential the two cases compose. Where the head-mounted world of \textit{Gnomes \& Goblins} hands the body its surround and asks little of the image, the screen withholds the surround and asks everything — the cold of the blizzard, the heft of the drawn bow, the lurch of the leaping train, the recoil from the charging bear, each supplied by the player's own \textit{phantasia} across a data-discrepancy the medium never closes. And where the eye alone will not suffice, the design lends prostheses — Eagle Eye for the tracker's gaze, Dead Eye for the marksman's composure, the drunk-cam for the swimming head — the 2D pole's standing instruments of compensation. The disposition deposited is the same kind of thing the virtual-reality world deposited at the other pole, an incorporation that sediments into a \textit{hexis}. What differs is only the road to it, and that road, in the screen-based case, runs through the image and not the body. The VR pole reaches its dwelling by giving the body its world; the 2D pole reaches the same dwelling by making the player imagine one.

The rails will come off. The world will open, the camp economy will expand, Valentine will become a hub, and the seeds planted in the heist and the town will begin to bear. Everything the tutorial deposited will be drawn upon across the forty hours that follow — and tested, when the gang the loyalty-hexis was built around begins to come apart, against Micah's corrosive creed and Dutch's slow erosion and the moment in the game's last act when the disposition is all that remains. But those tests belong to the disruption, and the disruption is the subject of the section the footage will let me write. What the tutorial built was the world that is made to break.
