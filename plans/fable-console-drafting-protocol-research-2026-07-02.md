## 5. Self-Critique, Gates, and LLM-as-Judge Evaluation

### 5.1 LLM-as-Judge Reliability and Techniques

LLM-as-judge has become a dominant paradigm for evaluating the outputs of other LLM systems, including for tasks such as answer relevancy, faithfulness, bias, and coherence.[7][18] The core idea is to employ one LLM as a “judge” that scores or ranks the outputs of another model against specified criteria, often using chain-of-thought prompting and structured rubrics to elicit reliable evaluations.[7][18] Research on GPT-4 as a judge, for example, shows up to approximately 85% agreement with human annotators, which is higher than the ~81% agreement rate that humans achieve with each other on the same tasks.[7][18] This level of agreement suggests that LLM judges can be quite reliable for high-level decisions, especially when guided by clear criteria and examples.

Several techniques have been identified as particularly effective at improving LLM-as-judge performance. Chain-of-thought prompting encourages the judge model to articulate detailed reasoning steps rather than issuing opaque scores, reducing arbitrariness and making errors more interpretable.[7][18] Few-shot examples anchored to the rubric help calibrate judgments toward human expectations. Swapping positions in pairwise comparisons mitigates position biases when the judge is asked to choose between two outputs. DAG-based evaluations structure judgments as directed acyclic graphs of decisions, breaking complex evaluative tasks into binary sub-decisions that are easier to adjudicate reliably. Question-answer generation (QAG) is used to compute fine-grained evaluation metrics by transforming subjective criteria into close-ended questions, effectively turning evaluation into a set of yes/no or multiple-choice interrogations.[7][18]

Your Stage G quality gauntlet functions as an evaluation framework, but currently relies primarily on mechanical checks (e.g., stylometric measurements, exact quote matching, greps for terminology, and explicit evidence-grade adherence) and a human checklist for anti-degradation. You do not yet explicitly use LLM-as-judge methods for semantic criteria such as coherence, helpfulness, or foundation fidelity. Given the demonstrated reliability of LLM judges when supported by chain-of-thought, DAG, and QAG structures, incorporating an LLM-judged layer into some of your gates could enhance their sensitivity and reduce the burden on human review.[7][18]

### 5.2 Structured Rubrics and Decision Trees for Gate Evaluation

Many of your gates are naturally amenable to DAG-based LLM evaluation. The foundation-fidelity gate, for instance, requires that the author’s original claims not be silently dropped, altered, or strengthened beyond their evidence grade. This can be operationalized as a series of binary questions: For each foundation claim, does the draft retain the claim? Does it alter its strength or modality beyond what is indicated by the evidence grade? Does it introduce new claims that appear to exceed the evidence bank’s support? An LLM judge can be prompted with the foundation draft, the evidence bank, and the generated movement, and asked to answer these questions sequentially, thereby implementing a DAG-based fidelity evaluation.[7][18]

Similarly, an anti-degradation checklist can be framed as QAG prompts: Does the revised draft maintain or improve clarity compared to the previous iteration? Does it preserve all quoted material and citations without corruption? Does it introduce any new unsupported assertions? Each checklist item becomes a binary or ordinal question that the LLM judge answers with a chain-of-thought explanation.[7] For citation-rigor, even before introducing a full CiteCheck pipeline, you could use a judge model to flag obvious citation anomalies (e.g., mismatched years, obviously non-existent titles) by comparing draft citations with a simple external retrieval or with your own reference lists, following QAG-driven verification methods.[7][9]

By structuring these evaluations as DAGs and QAG sequences, you reduce the cognitive load on both the judge model and yourself. Each gate becomes a set of small, tractable decisions rather than a single holistic verdict. This aligns with recommendations that breaking subjective judgments into binary sub-decisions and confining them via decision trees improves the accuracy and consistency of LLM-as-judge evaluations.[7][18]

### 5.3 Revision Cycles, Degradation, and Model Separation

You asked specifically how many revision cycles help before degradation occurs, and whether the judge pass should use a different context or model instance than the drafter. There is limited direct empirical work on the optimal number of self-refinement cycles, but practitioners often observe diminishing returns after a small number of iterations. LLM-as-judge studies emphasize scalability and reliability of single-pass or few-pass evaluations; repeated generative revisions guided by the same model can risk overfitting to its own biases or introducing unnatural verbose corrections.[7][18] In your protocol, you already limit targeted revision loops to a maximum of three cycles before human review. This is sensible and consistent with informal evidence that beyond two or three revision cycles, LLM-generated texts may start to exhibit over-regularization, loss of nuance, or drift away from the original authorial voice.

As for model separation, using a different model instance or a different context configuration for judging than for drafting is advisable. If the same instance that drafted the text also judges it, especially with access to the same Stage P pack, there is a risk of rationalization: the judge may be biased toward defending its own outputs or may rely too heavily on the founding context rather than on the evaluation rubric.[7] Instead, best practice is to employ the same or a similar model in “judge mode” with a different prompt and a minimized context that includes only the draft, the rubric, and any necessary foundation or evidence segments.[7][18] Anthropic’s guidance on context engineering also suggests keeping evaluation contexts lean, focusing the judge on relevant information rather than exposing it to the full generative context.[14]

Thus, your judge passes should ideally run as separate model calls, with carefully crafted evaluation prompts, chain-of-thought rationales, and DAG/QAG structures, rather than as inline self-critiques in the drafting conversations. This separation supports clearer provenance for judgments and reduces the chance of runaway self-correction loops.

### 5.4 Amendments for Self-Critique and Gate Design

Research on LLM-as-judge methods supports several concrete amendments to your gate system.

First, you can instantiate an LLM-judged foundation-fidelity gate. For each movement, a judge model would receive the relevant portion of the foundation draft, the evidence items assigned in the Stage P pack, and the generated movement, and then answer QAG-styled questions about whether each foundation claim has been retained, whether any claims have been strengthened beyond their evidence grade, and whether any new claims lack evidence support.[7][18] The judge’s chain-of-thought explanations would be recorded, and movements found to violate fidelity constraints would be routed back for targeted revision.

Second, your anti-degradation checklist can be converted into a structured evaluation prompt for a judge model. The model would compare successive versions of each movement and answer specific questions—clarity, faithfulness, stylistic congruence—using DAG-based reasoning. This turns the checklist into a formal evaluation stage, reducing your manual burden while providing explicit rationales for each judgment.[7]

Third, you should maintain your three-cycle limit for revisions but ensure that each cycle is guided by fresh judge evaluations rather than by self-reflection from the drafter model. Judge and drafter should be separated in terms of prompts and, ideally, context scope. This aligns your protocol with evidence that separate judge models, working with structured rubrics and chain-of-thought, achieve higher agreement with humans and avoid the pathologies of self-referential revision loops.[7][18]

These amendments would leave your mechanical gates intact but augment them with semantic and epistemic checks mediated by LLM judges, thereby enhancing the overall robustness of your quality gauntlet.

## 6. Context Engineering: Single Authoritative Pack versus Retrieval

### 6.1 Limits of Long-Context Processing and “Lost in the Middle”

Long-context language models promise to ingest tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens, but empirical studies have shown that their ability to use information at arbitrary positions within the context remains limited. “Lost in the Middle” analyzes performance on tasks requiring identification of relevant information in long input contexts, such as multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval.[16] The study finds that model performance can degrade significantly when the position of relevant information is changed, with performance often highest when relevant information appears at the beginning or end of the context and significantly worse when it is located in the middle.[16] This phenomenon persists even for explicitly long-context models and underscores that simply increasing context length does not guarantee robust attention to all parts of the input.

Your Stage P pack design—assembling a single authoritative context block before any prose—is consistent with treating context as a curated resource. However, if the pack is very long and dense, “Lost in the Middle” implies that portions of it, especially those in the middle, may be underutilized or mis-attended during drafting.[16] Important elements such as locked terminology, evidence grades, or negative constraints could end up in positions where the model’s attention is weaker, leading to violations or omissions despite their presence in the input.

Anthropic’s guidance on effective context engineering for AI agents additionally emphasizes that context should be structured to foreground the most important information for task success, often by placing critical instructions and constraints at the beginning and by using references or indexes to recall less central material as needed.[14] They advocate strategies such as modular context segments, explicit labeling of sections, and local contexts tailored to specific sub-tasks, rather than relying on a monolithic global context.[14]

Taken together, these findings suggest that while a single authoritative pack is a strong conceptual anchor, its practical usage in long-context models may require careful ordering and modularization to mitigate “lost in the middle” effects.[16][14]

### 6.2 Curated Packs versus RAG for Faithfulness

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) aims to reduce hallucinations by grounding responses in external context, but recent benchmarking work indicates that RAG systems still exhibit hallucination behaviors and that effectively managing retrieved context is non-trivial.[8] FaithJudge, for example, proposes a framework for benchmarking LLM faithfulness in RAG settings, using labelled hallucination annotations from diverse LLM generations to evaluate how often models hallucinate when summarizing or responding to queries based on retrieved articles.[8] The results indicate that while RAG can improve faithfulness relative to models answering without external context, it also introduces new failure modes: models may over-trust or misinterpret noisy or irrelevant retrieved passages, leading to hallucinated content that appears well-grounded but is epistemically false.[8]

You have banned RAG during drafting precisely because, in your experiments, it tended to override the carefully curated Stage P pack with “junk”—irrelevant or low-quality material from retrieval that distracted or misled the model. This choice is supported by FaithJudge’s observation that not all retrieval is beneficial and that the composition and quality of retrieved context strongly influence hallucination behavior.[8] For a humanities dissertation, where the citation ecology is complex and interpretive nuance matters, letting an LLM indiscriminately pull in external text during drafting can easily derail argument structure and style.

That said, the research also shows that context curation—selecting and structuring relevant passages—can mitigate hallucinations and improve faithfulness.[8][14] The key is not whether context comes from retrieval or pre-loaded packs but whether it is filtered and organized in a way that the model can reliably use. Your Stage P pack is essentially a curated micro-corpus. The main risk, given “lost in the middle,” is that its size and structure may inadvertently bury critical elements. Your ban on RAG is sensible for drafting; RAG should be reserved for post-hoc verification stages such as citation checking and cross-referencing.

### 6.3 Pack Structure, Ordering, and Stage-Specific Contexts

To make your single authoritative pack more effective, research suggests several structural adjustments. First, you should place the most critical instructions and constraints—task specification, stylometric targets, terminology locks, negative constraints—at the beginning of the pack. “Lost in the middle” and Anthropic’s context engineering guidance both imply that models attend more reliably to early content, especially when tasked with following instructions over long generations.[16][14] Explicit labels such as “TASK,” “STYLE TARGET,” “TERMINOLOGY LOCKS,” and “NEGATIVE CONSTRAINTS” can further help the model parse these sections.

Second, less critical but voluminous content, such as the full quotation bank, can be placed toward the end of the pack or referenced via an index. For instance, you might include a compact catalog of quote IDs and short descriptions near the beginning and store the full text of quotes near the end. During D2, the model is instructed to embed quotes using IDs or short descriptors, and you or downstream scripts substitute full text, which also interacts with the constrained copy strategies discussed earlier.[19][11] This reduces the need for the model to scan full quote content during drafting and alleviates positional attentional burdens.

Third, instead of using the same Stage P pack for all sub-stages, you can employ stage-specific sub-packs. For D1 planning, the context might only include the task spec, foundation draft, evidence bank, theoretical apparatus locks, and high-level style targets. For D1.5 skeletoning, you might add shorthand descriptions of quotes and locked terminology. For D2 drafting, you would incorporate the skeleton, detailed rhythm directives, and a reference to where quotes will be substituted, leaving out some global instructions that are no longer central. Anthropic’s blog advocates this kind of “effective context engineering,” in which each agent or sub-task receives a tailored context slice rather than the entire global context.[14]

Finally, internal referencing mechanisms can help. Explicitly marking the locations of key sections within the pack and reminding the model how to interpret them (“The following section contains terminology locks; do not alter these definitions.”) can compensate for long-context weaknesses. By pairing positional ordering (beginning and end placement) with labels and stage-specific slicing, you create a context environment more compatible with the model’s attentional profile.[16][14]

### 6.4 Amendments for Context Management

The evidence on long-context processing and context engineering leads to several amendments.

You should restructure your Stage P pack so that task specification, style targets, terminology locks, theoretical apparatus, and negative constraints appear at the very beginning with clear labels. This ensures that the most critical instructions are not “lost in the middle” of a long context and are more likely to be obeyed during drafting.[16][14]

You can then separate the quotation bank into two layers: a compact index of quote IDs and short paraphrastic descriptions early in the pack, and the full text of quotes later. Combined with constrained copy mechanisms where drafts reference quote IDs rather than full text, this will reduce the cognitive burden on the model and align with Copy-as-Decode principles.[19][11]

Additionally, you should adopt stage-specific sub-packs. D1 would use a reduced pack emphasizing argument and evidence, D1.5 skeletoning would add skeleton-specific structures and quote indexes, and D2 would integrate skeletons, rhythm directives, and quote IDs while omitting some global metadata. This modular approach leverages Anthropic’s context engineering recommendations and mitigates “lost in the middle” effects.[14][16]

These amendments preserve your curated, non-RAG drafting environment while making it more compatible with the known limitations of long-context attention.

## 7. Human-in-the-Loop Scholarly Workflows and Ethics

### 7.1 Empirical Findings on LLM Reliability in Scholarly Writing

Studies in medical and scientific domains have highlighted both the promise and limitations of LLMs for scholarly writing. The JMIR evaluation of ChatGPT’s citation behavior found substantial inaccuracies in citations and references, underscoring that LLM outputs cannot be trusted without human verification in academic contexts.[10] A related study comparing ChatGPT and Bard in performing systematic reviews found notable hallucination rates as well as reference inaccuracies, reinforcing the need for careful human oversight when models are used for literature-based tasks.[20] These findings generalize to humanities dissertations: while LLMs can assist with drafting, they are liable to fabricate or distort citations and evidence if not strictly constrained and checked.

Moreover, recent work on how LLMs handle ambiguous real-world questions shows that models struggle when faced with interpretive ambiguity and often fail to balance accurate answer generation with reliable source citation.[6] This is particularly relevant for humanities fields like philosophy and game studies, where ambiguity and contestable interpretations are central. Models may produce confident-seeming arguments that gloss over ambiguity or misrepresent scholarly disputes, requiring human scholars to correct or contextualize them.[6]

Your protocol’s insistence on human review paragraph-by-paragraph and on author sign-off for every committed text aligns with emerging consensus that LLM-assisted scholarly writing must be fundamentally human-controlled. LLMs can be tools for drafting and revising but should not replace human judgment about interpretation, evidence weighting, and argumentative commitments.[10][20][6]

### 7.2 Approval Points, Diff-Based Review, and Provenance Tracking

In human-in-the-loop workflows, best practices emphasize multiple approval points and transparent provenance tracking. For dissertations, this means not only that the author reviews drafts but also that the lineage of each passage is traceable: which segments were written by the LLM, which by the author, which by revision, and what sources support them. Although formal research on provenance tracking is still developing, the logic of retrieval-grounded verification and citation checking supports treating each passage as a node in a provenance graph linking text segments to sources and to generative agents.[8][9][15]

Diff-based review tools, commonly used in software development, can be repurposed for dissertation drafting. Each LLM-assisted revision could be recorded as a diff against previous drafts, enabling you to see exactly what changed and to approve or reject changes at a granular level. Combined with your foundation-fidelity gate, which ensures that original claims are not silently altered or strengthened beyond their evidence grade, diff-based review would make the process of LLM-assisted rewriting transparent and controllable.[8]

Your current protocol already incorporates human paragraph-by-paragraph review and author sign-off. To align more fully with best practices, you might systematically record revision diffs and annotate them with gate pass/fail results and judge explanations, effectively creating a provenance record for each movement. This record would support any future need to demonstrate integrity and authorship, for example in dissertation defenses or publication contexts.

### 7.3 Ethics and Disclosure Norms for LLM-Assisted Dissertations

Academic ethics around LLM use are evolving, but several norms are emerging. Many journals and institutions now require authors to disclose any substantive use of LLMs in drafting manuscripts, particularly in generating text, analyzing data, or performing literature reviews. While formal guidelines vary, the underlying principles are transparency, accountability, and preservation of scholarly responsibility. LLMs cannot be co-authors; they are tools. Authors must remain responsible for the content, including all claims, interpretations, and citations.

For dissertations, similar norms are likely to prevail. You should anticipate that your department or institution may require a statement disclosing that LLMs were used as drafting aids under a controlled protocol, with human review and sign-off at every stage. Given the documented hallucination and citation inaccuracies in LLM outputs, ethical use requires explicit acknowledgment of these limitations and demonstration that you addressed them through rigorous gates and verification.[10][20][9][15]

Your protocol’s emphasis on evidence grades, foundation fidelity, and quote-citation constraints is ethically oriented: it aims to prevent the model from introducing unsupported claims or fabricated references. To strengthen the ethical dimension, you might formalize an “LLM usage statement” template as part of Stage P, specifying where the model is allowed to contribute (e.g., phrasing, organization) and where it is strictly barred (e.g., providing new claims, inventing citations). This statement could later be adapted into a dissertation disclosure.

## 8. Additional Quality Levers: Discourse Structure, Counterargument, and Consistency

### 8.1 Discourse Structure Modeling with Rhetorical Structure Theory

Rhetorical Structure Theory (RST) offers a formal framework for modeling text structure in terms of nuclei and satellites connected by rhetorical relations such as evidence, elaboration, concession, and contrast.[4] In RST, each region of text has a central nuclear part and one or more satellite parts that relate to it, creating a hierarchical discourse tree.[4] Early work on discourse structures for text generation demonstrated that incorporating RST-based planning into text generation systems yielded more coherent and rhetorically well-formed outputs than systems that operated purely at the sentence level.[4]

For long-form scholarly argument, RST is particularly relevant. A dissertation is not merely a sequence of sentences but a complex discourse structure in which claims must be supported by evidence, counterarguments must be acknowledged and addressed, and transitions must signal rhetorical shifts. Current LLM planning research emphasizes outlines and skeletons but often does not explicitly encode rhetorical relations at the level of nuclei and satellites.[2][3][12] By integrating RST concepts into your skeleton stage, you can encourage the model to think about discourse structure explicitly rather than implicitly.

Practically, this could mean that D1.5 skeletons specify, for each movement, a hierarchy of nucleus claims and satellite supports, labeled with RST relations. For example, in a movement arguing for a particular interpretation of a game mechanic, the nucleus might be the central claim about the mechanic’s rhetorical function, while satellites include textual evidence (evidence relation), theoretical framing (elaboration), and treatment of competing readings (concession or contrast). Encoding these relations in skeletons would push the model toward more coherent discourse structures during D2 drafting.[4]

### 8.2 Counterargument Modeling and Dialectical Rigor

Dissertations in rhetoric and philosophy must engage with counterarguments to demonstrate dialectical rigor. While your protocol does not explicitly mention a counterargument pass, the foundation-fidelity gate and evidence bank implicitly address argumentative strength. LLMs can be used not only to draft arguments but also to generate potential counterarguments, provided that their suggestions are filtered through human judgment.

Research on LLM evaluation in ambiguous or contentious domains indicates that models often underspecify ambiguity and overstate confidence, a tendency that can be leveraged to generate straw counterarguments but must be corrected to avoid caricature.[6] A structured counterargument pass could involve prompting the model, using the Stage P pack and the D1 plan, to generate plausible objections or alternative readings for each major claim, and to suggest ways to address them. You would then vet these counterarguments, incorporate those that are epistemically and historically plausible, and revise your D1 skeletons and D2 drafts accordingly.

Incorporating counterargument explicitly into your planning and drafting stages would promote dialectical integrity and align with scholarly norms. It would also allow you to use LLM-as-judge methods to evaluate how well the draft addresses identified counterarguments, via QAG prompts such as “Does Movement X adequately respond to Counterargument Y? If not, where is the gap?”[7][18]

### 8.3 Consistency Checking for Entities, Claims, and Evidence

Consistency across a long dissertation—regarding terminology, entity descriptions, and claim-evidence relationships—is a major challenge. RAG faithfulness benchmarks and hallucination studies highlight that models can contradict themselves or misalign claims and evidence even within single documents, especially when dealing with evolving contexts.[8][6] FaithJudge, for instance, emphasizes the importance of tracking hallucinations across multiple generations for the same articles or queries, which is conceptually similar to tracking claim consistency across a dissertation.[8]

Your evidence bank with confidence grades and terminology-lock gate already enforce some consistency: terms must be used as locked, and evidence must be asserted only at its given confidence grade. However, the system might still allow subtle inconsistencies, such as shifting interpretations of a key concept or changes in how a theorist is characterized. To address this, you could implement a consistency check stage in which an LLM judge, aided by your Stage P pack, scans the draft for contradictions or drift in entity descriptions and claim-evidence mappings.

This consistency check could be structured as QAG prompts: “For term T, are all usages consistent with the locked definition?” “For claim C, is the supporting evidence cited consistently across movements?” “Are there any movements that contradict earlier claims without explicit acknowledgment or revision?”[7][8] The judge’s chain-of-thought responses would help you identify and correct inconsistencies before finalizing the dissertation.

### 8.4 Amendments for Discourse Structure, Counterargument, and Consistency

The discourse and consistency research suggests three additions to your protocol.

You should explicitly integrate RST-inspired discourse planning into a D1.5 skeleton stage, labeling nucleus claims and satellite supports with rhetorical relations. This will guide D2 drafting toward coherent, rhetorically structured argumentation, building on the foundation provided by your Stage P pack and D1 planning.[4]

You should add a counterargument generation and integration pass, where the model suggests potential objections and alternative interpretations, and you decide which to incorporate. These counterarguments should be reflected in skeletons and drafts, and their treatment can be evaluated by a judge model using QAG prompts.[6][7]

Finally, you should incorporate a consistency check stage where an LLM judge scans the draft for terminological consistency and claim-evidence alignment across movements, reporting contradictions or drifts for correction. This would enhance the epistemic stability of your dissertation and align with faithfulness-focused evaluation frameworks.[8][6]
